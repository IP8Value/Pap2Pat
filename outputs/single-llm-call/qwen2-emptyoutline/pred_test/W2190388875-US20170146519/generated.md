## BACKGROUND

The innate immune system plays a critical role in the body's first line of defense against pathogens. Pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) are key components of this system, recognizing and responding to microbe- and danger-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs and DAMPs). These patterns are often constituents or replication intermediates of intracellular pathogens, particularly viruses. Upon engagement, PRRs initiate signaling pathways that lead to the expression or processing of cytokines, chemokines, and effector molecules, which both directly inhibit microbial replication and facilitate adaptive immune responses.

Interferon (IFN) proteins, including multiple subtypes of IFNα and β (type I IFN) and IFNλ1-3 (type III IFN), are primary outcomes of PRR-mediated signaling. These IFNs act via cell surface receptors, triggering a phosphorylation cascade involving Janus and tyrosine kinases (Jak1, Tyk2) and signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 and 2 (STAT1/2) transcription factors. This amplifies the expression of antiviral effector and other immune stimulatory genes, collectively known as IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs).

PRR-mediated expression of IFNβ is particularly well-characterized and requires the phosphorylation of the transcription factor IFN regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) by serine kinases TANK Binding Kinase 1 (TBK1) and I Kappa B Kinase ε (IKKε). This occurs primarily through pathways utilizing specific adaptor proteins. For example, TIR-domain-containing adaptor-inducing IFNβ (TRIF; also called TICAM1) is required for signals initiated by Toll-like receptors (TLRs) 3 and 4, while IFN promoter stimulator 1 (IPS-1; also called MAVS, VISA, Cardif) is employed by RIG-I and MDA5, which sense cytoplasmic dsRNA. Stimulator of IFN genes (STING; also called MITA, TMEM173, MPYS, ERIS) acts as both a PRR for cyclic dinucleotides (CDNs) and an adaptor molecule for multiple cytoplasmic receptors of dsDNA.

Given the importance of these pathways for innate immune activation and antimicrobial protection, they have been the focus of extensive research aimed at understanding their physiological effects and harnessing their potential for therapeutic purposes. Pharmacologic IFN stimulation has been suggested as a broad-spectrum antiviral strategy, and factors capable of yielding therapeutic effects via activation of IRF3-mediated responses have been identified and biologically validated. However, most synthetic small molecules identified thus far have only exhibited suitable efficacy in mouse models due to their strict specificity for the murine STING ortholog. There is a significant need for human-specific synthetic small molecules that can activate the STING pathway.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a novel small molecule capable of stimulating IRF3 phosphorylation and IFN production in human cells, thereby preventing replication of Alphaviruses. The molecule, referred to as G10, is the first synthetic compound definitively capable of activating the human STING-dependent innate immune response. The invention provides methods and compositions for using G10 to treat or prevent viral infections, particularly those caused by Alphaviruses such as Chikungunya virus (CHIKV) and Venezuelan Equine Encephalitis Virus (VEEV).

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Terms

- **Alphavirus**: A genus of viruses in the family Togaviridae, including species such as Chikungunya virus (CHIKV) and Venezuelan Equine Encephalitis Virus (VEEV).
- **Cyclic Dinucleotides (CDNs)**: Small molecules that serve as second messengers in cellular signaling pathways, particularly in the activation of the STING pathway.
- **IFN (Interferon)**: A group of signaling proteins made and released by host cells in response to the presence of several viruses. They allow communication between cells to trigger the protective defenses of the immune system.
- **IRF3 (Interferon Regulatory Factor 3)**: A transcription factor that plays a crucial role in the induction of type I interferons in response to viral infection.
- **STING (Stimulator of Interferon Genes)**: An adaptor protein that mediates the activation of IRF3 and the subsequent production of type I interferons in response to cytosolic DNA and cyclic dinucleotides.
- **STAT1 (Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 1)**: A transcription factor that is activated by interferons and plays a key role in the expression of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs).

### Compounds:

The invention features a novel small molecule, G10, which is defined by the chemical formula:
\[ \text{4-(2-chloro-6-fluorobenzyl)-N-(furan-2-ylmethyl)-3-oxo-3,4-dihydro-2H-benzo[b][1,4]thiazine-6-carboxamide} \]
G10 is capable of inducing IRF3 phosphorylation and IFN production in human cells, leading to the expression of antiviral effector genes and the establishment of an antiviral state. The molecule is particularly effective against Alphaviruses, including CHIKV and VEEV.

### Pharmaceutical Compositions

Pharmaceutical compositions of the invention include G10 formulated for administration to a subject. The compositions may be in the form of tablets, capsules, solutions, suspensions, emulsions, or injectable solutions. The compositions may further include pharmaceutically acceptable excipients, carriers, and adjuvants. Suitable excipients include, but are not limited to, lactose, starch, cellulose, magnesium stearate, and mannitol. The compositions may be formulated for oral, intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous, or topical administration.

### Treatment

The invention provides methods for treating or preventing viral infections, particularly those caused by Alphaviruses, using G10. The methods involve administering a therapeutically effective amount of G10 to a subject in need thereof. The dosage and frequency of administration will depend on the specific condition being treated, the severity of the infection, and the response of the subject. In general, a daily dose of G10 ranging from about 1 mg to about 100 mg per kg of body weight may be administered.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1 Identification of a Novel IFN/IRF3-Inducing Molecule by High Throughput In Vitro Screening

We sought to discover novel small molecules capable of stimulating innate immune signaling and effector activity in human cells. For this, we employed human fibroblasts stably transfected with constitutively expressed human telomerase reverse transcriptase (THF) and luciferase (LUC) driven by a promoter element reactive to type I IFN-dependent and IRF3-dependent transcription (THF-ISRE). Using a 384-well high-throughput in vitro screening platform, we examined 51,632 chemically diverse compounds in duplicate for their ability to significantly stimulate LUC expression. Sixteen positive control (1000 U/mL IFNβ) and negative control (1% DMSO) LUC readings were obtained for each plate. Readings for individual compounds (R) on a single plate were designated as significant if \( R > (\mu_P - \mu_N) * 0.5 \). The compound that exhibited the third highest signal of all those screened was 4-(2-chloro-6-fluorobenzyl)-N-(furan-2-ylmethyl)-3-oxo-3,4-dihydro-2H-benzo[b][1,4]thiazine-6-carboxamide, which we termed G10. The two molecules eliciting stronger responses are currently being characterized. G10 exhibited low cytotoxicity even at high concentrations and was selected for more comprehensive examination.

### Example 2—G10 Induces IFN/IRF3- but not NF-κB-Dependent Transcription in Human Fibroblasts

We validated the G10-mediated induction of IFN/IRF3-dependent LUC in reporter cells by exposing them to a range of concentrations. G10 induced LUC expression in a dose-dependent manner. We verified that the molecule could trigger the expression of endogenous genes transcribed in response to IRF3- and IFN-dependent processes using semi-quantitative RT-PCR. Exposure to G10 led to the transcription of cellular genes triggered by IRF3/IFN-dependent signaling, similar to that induced by exposure to human cytomegalovirus rendered replicationally inactive by UV irradiation (UV-CMV). However, G10 did not stimulate NF-κB-dependent transcription, as evidenced by the lack of LUC expression in cells stably transduced with LUC driven by an NF-κB-dependent promoter and the absence of mRNA synthesis of endogenous NF-κB-dependent genes (IL8, IL1β, and MIP1α).

### Example 3—G10 Elicits Antiviral Activity Against New and Old World Alphaviruses

We examined whether G10 could generate a cellular state refractory to virus replication in vitro. Pre-exposure of THF cells to G10 reduced the replication of CHIKV by over three logs (IC90 = 8.01 μM) and VEEV by over three logs (IC90 = 24.57 μM). The relative inability of G10 to render cells as resistant to WNV or VACV replication may reflect the differential susceptibilities of the virus types to the specific antiviral genes induced by G10 or innate evasion phenotypes exhibited by the different viruses.

### Example 4—IRF3 is Essential to G10-Induced Transcription and Anti-Alphaviral Activity

We constructed THF-ISRE cells from which either the STAT1 or IRF3 protein was stably removed via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene disruption. G10 induced LUC expression in the absence of STAT1 but not in the absence of IRF3. IRF3 activation, as evidenced by phosphorylation of IRF3 residue S386, was also observed in response to G10. The anti-Alphaviral activity elicited by G10 was lost in cells lacking IRF3, indicating that the innate immune stimulation observed in response to G10 is dependent on the IRF3 transcription factor.

### Example 5—G10-Mediated IRF3 Activation and Anti-Alphaviral Activity Occur Independently of IPS-1/MAVS-Dependent Signaling

We constructed THF cells lacking the IPS-1 protein via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene disruption. G10-induced IRF3 phosphorylation and anti-Alphaviral activity were functional in these cells, indicating that the molecule stimulates a response that does not require components of the IPS-1 signaling apparatus.

### Example 6—STING is Required for G10-Mediated IRF3 Activation, Gene Expression, and Anti-Alphaviral Activity

We constructed THF-ISRE cells from which the STING protein was eliminated via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene disruption. G10-induced IRF3 phosphorylation, LUC induction, and transcription of IRF3-dependent endogenous host genes were abolished in the absence of STING. The anti-Alphaviral activity elicited by G10 was also lost in cells lacking STING, indicating that the STING pathway is crucial to the IRF3-dependent innate antiviral activity induced by G10.

### Example 7—G10 Induces STING-Dependent Synthesis and Secretion of Bioactive Interferon

Exposure of THF-ISRE to G10 led to the transcription of both IFNβ and IFNλ1 mRNAs. G10 also induced the expression of known IFNα/β-dependent genes Mx2 and OAS, consistent with the secretion of IFN in response to treatment with G10. Using a cell-based reporter assay, we detected the secretion of bioactive IFN species capable of initiating Jak/STAT signaling and ISG expression in response to G10.

### Example 8—STAT1 is Required for G10-Mediated Anti-Alphaviral Activity and ISG Expression

Replication of CHIKV, VEEV, and SINV in cells treated with G10 from which STAT1 was deleted was similar to that seen in the presence of DMSO alone. IFNβ appeared to induce detectable yet significant antiviral effects even in the absence of STAT1. G10 was unable to stimulate the expression of Mx2 and ISG56 proteins in STAT1-deficient cells, indicating that STAT1 is required for G10-mediated anti-Alphaviral activity and ISG expression.

### Example 9—G10 Triggers IRF3-Dependent Activity with Less Potency than 2′3′-cGAMP

We compared the dose dependence of G10-induced IRF3 phosphorylation and gene expression to that of 2′3′-cGAMP, an established STING ligand. While G10 may trigger earlier IRF3 phosphorylation, it triggers levels of IRF3-dependent gene expression with overall less potency than 2′3′-cGAMP. Specifically, 2′3′-cGAMP induced higher levels of IRF3- and IFN-dependent mRNA expression at lower concentrations than G10.

### Example 10—G10 Induces Innate Antiviral mRNA Expression in Primary Human Cells

We examined the transcription of IRF3-, IFN-, and NF-κB-dependent genes in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and primary human umbilical microvascular endothelial cells. G10 triggered the expression of IFIT1, IFIT2, IFNβ, ISG15, OAS, and Viperin to degrees proportional to compound concentration. NF-κB-dependent genes MIP1α and IL1β were also transcriptionally induced by G10 in PBMCs and endothelial cells, demonstrating that innate immune induction by G10 is not an effect specific to the cell model and suggesting that in vivo stimulation by G10 is likely feasible.

### Example 11—STING Agonism Inhibits CHIKV Replication In Vivo

We used a mouse model of CHIKV infection to evaluate the in vivo efficacy of STING agonism. Mice were pre-treated with DMXAA (25 mg/kg) intraperitoneally at 3 hours pre-infection with CHIKV (1000 PFU). Treatment with DMXAA resulted in undetectable serum-associated virus at 72 hours post-infection. Post-infection treatment with DMXAA at 6 hours and 24 hours also reduced viral titers, with the latter treatment showing diminished but statistically significant efficacy. These data indicate that artificial stimulation of STING-dependent signaling in vivo within an appropriate temporal window can block CHIKV replication.
