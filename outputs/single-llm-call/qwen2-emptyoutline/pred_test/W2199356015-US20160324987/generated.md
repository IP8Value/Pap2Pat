## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of gene therapy, specifically to methods and compositions for treating autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa (adRP) using CRISPR/Cas9 technology. More particularly, the invention provides a method for selectively ablating the mutant allele of the rhodopsin (Rho) gene in vivo, thereby preventing retinal degeneration and preserving visual function.

## BACKGROUND

Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is a group of inherited retinal degenerative disorders characterized by the progressive loss of photoreceptors, leading to blindness. Autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa (adRP) is caused by gain-of-function mutations in the rhodopsin (Rho) gene. These mutations result in the production of a toxic mutant rhodopsin protein (RHO S334) that accumulates in photoreceptor cell bodies, leading to cell death and retinal degeneration.

Traditional gene therapy approaches for RP have focused on gene replacement strategies, which are effective for recessive forms of the disease but are not applicable to adRP. In adRP, the presence of a single mutant allele is sufficient to cause disease, and simply adding more wild-type (WT) Rho does not address the toxic effects of the mutant protein. Therefore, there is a critical need for a therapeutic approach that can specifically target and eliminate the mutant Rho allele without affecting the wild-type allele.

Recent advances in genome editing, particularly the CRISPR/Cas9 system, offer a promising solution to this challenge. CRISPR/Cas9 allows for precise and targeted modifications to the genome, making it a potential tool for correcting genetic mutations. However, achieving allele-specific targeting in vivo remains a significant challenge.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for treating autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa (adRP) by selectively ablating the mutant allele of the rhodopsin (Rho) gene using CRISPR/Cas9 technology. The method involves designing a guide RNA (gRNA) that targets a specific sequence in the mutant Rho allele, which differs from the wild-type allele by a single nucleotide. The gRNA, in combination with the Cas9 nuclease, is delivered to the retina, where it selectively disrupts the mutant Rho allele, thereby preventing the production of the toxic mutant protein and allowing the wild-type Rho to function normally.

The invention also encompasses compositions comprising the gRNA and Cas9 nuclease, as well as methods for delivering these components to the retina, such as subretinal injection followed by electroporation. The invention further includes animal models and methods for evaluating the efficacy of the treatment.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Genomic Editing

Genomic editing using the CRISPR/Cas9 system involves the use of a guide RNA (gRNA) and the Cas9 nuclease to introduce targeted double-strand breaks in the DNA. The gRNA is designed to bind to a specific sequence in the genome, guiding the Cas9 nuclease to the target site. Once bound, the Cas9 nuclease cleaves the DNA, creating a double-strand break. The cell's repair mechanisms then attempt to fix the break, often leading to insertions or deletions (indels) that can disrupt the target gene.

In the context of the present invention, the gRNA is designed to target a sequence in the mutant Rho allele that differs from the wild-type allele by a single nucleotide. This single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) allows the gRNA to specifically recognize and bind to the mutant allele, while avoiding the wild-type allele. The Cas9 nuclease then cleaves the DNA at the target site, leading to the disruption of the mutant Rho gene.

### Animal Model

The S334ter-3 rat model is used to study the efficacy of the CRISPR/Cas9-based treatment for adRP. This model carries a transgenic insertion of the mouse Rho gene with a serine-to-stop codon mutation at position 334 (Rho S334), which results in the production of a truncated and toxic rhodopsin protein. The S334ter-3 rats exhibit rapid and progressive photoreceptor loss, leading to retinal degeneration and vision decline.

### CRISPR/Cas9 Constructs, Generally

The CRISPR/Cas9 constructs used in the present invention consist of a plasmid vector encoding the Cas9 nuclease and a gRNA. The gRNA is designed to target a specific sequence in the mutant Rho allele, which differs from the wild-type allele by a single nucleotide. The plasmid vector also includes a promoter to drive the expression of the Cas9 nuclease and the gRNA, as well as a reporter gene (e.g., mCherry) to track the expression of the construct.

### CRISPR/Cas9 Constructs, Specifically

For the S334ter-3 rat model, a 20-nucleotide gRNA (gRNA TRGT) was designed to target a region in exon 1 of the Rho S334 allele immediately upstream of a protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) unique to the mutant allele. The gRNA TRGT binds to a sequence that differs from the wild-type Rho allele by a single nucleotide at position 10/20. This single nucleotide difference allows the gRNA TRGT to specifically recognize and bind to the mutant Rho allele, while avoiding the wild-type allele.

A control gRNA (gRNA CNTRL) was also designed to target a sequence in exon 2 of the Rho S334 allele with four nucleotide mismatches from the wild-type allele. The gRNA CNTRL was used to confirm the specificity of the gRNA TRGT and to serve as a negative control in in vivo experiments.

### Methods

#### In Vitro Validation

To validate the specificity and efficacy of the gRNA TRGT, bone marrow-derived stromal cells (MSCs) from S334ter-3 rats were transfected with the gRNA TRGT construct. The cells were sorted based on mCherry expression, and genomic DNA was extracted and sequenced to confirm the presence of indels at the target site. Sequencing results showed multiple genomic sequences resulting from Rho S334-selective cleavage downstream of the Cas9 cleavage site, confirming the specificity of the gRNA TRGT.

#### In Vivo Treatment

To evaluate the efficacy of the gRNA TRGT in vivo, S334ter-3 rats received a single unilateral subretinal injection of the gRNA TRGT construct at postnatal day 0 (P0), followed by electroporation to facilitate plasmid uptake by photoreceptors. Retinas were harvested at P4, and mCherry-positive cells were sorted and analyzed for genomic disruption. Sequencing results confirmed the presence of indels at the Rho S334 locus, but not at the wild-type Rho locus, indicating successful and specific ablation of the mutant allele.

#### Phenotypic Rescue

To assess the phenotypic rescue, gRNA TRGT and gRNA CNTRL treatments were evaluated by immunohistology at P33. Extensive retinal preservation was observed in gRNA TRGT-injected eyes, with up to eight layers of rescued photoreceptors, in contrast to the single layer of photoreceptors in gRNA CNTRL-treated retinas. Immunostaining confirmed the absence of RHO S334 from photoreceptor cell bodies in the rescued outer nuclear layer (ONL), while RHO WT was strictly confined to photoreceptor outer segments (POS), indicating proper trafficking and function of the wild-type protein.

#### Visual Function Assessment

Visual acuity was assessed at P39 using the optokinetic response. Rats treated with gRNA TRGT showed a 53% higher visual acuity compared to those treated with gRNA CNTRL. Using the contralateral eye as an internal control, visual acuity was 35% higher in the gRNA TRGT-treated eye compared to the untreated eye, while gRNA CNTRL-injection reduced visual acuity by 2.3%.

#### Quantification of Photoreceptor Density

Cross-sectional analysis of retinal flat-mounts at P33 showed that the density of photoreceptors in mCherry-positive regions from gRNA TRGT treatment was 307 ± 82 photoreceptor nuclei/100 µm, which was ninefold greater than from gRNA CNTRL treatment (33 ± 3 nuclei/100 µm) and was similarly higher when compared with comparable regions of untreated control (27 ± 13 nuclei/100 µm). The density of photoreceptor nuclei was not different between gRNA CNTRL and untreated groups.

### Conclusion

The present invention provides a method for treating autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa (adRP) by selectively ablating the mutant allele of the rhodopsin (Rho) gene using CRISPR/Cas9 technology. The method effectively prevents the production of the toxic mutant protein, allowing the wild-type Rho to function normally and preserve retinal structure and visual function. The invention represents a significant advance in the treatment of adRP and offers a promising therapeutic approach for other genetic diseases with mutational heterogeneity.
