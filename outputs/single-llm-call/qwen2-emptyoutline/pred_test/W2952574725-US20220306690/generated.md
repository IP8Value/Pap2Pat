# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) is a retrovirus that causes acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS). The virus infects vital cells in the human immune system, such as helper T cells (specifically CD4+ T cells), macrophages, and dendritic cells. Over time, HIV infection leads to a progressive decline in the number of CD4+ T cells, resulting in a weakened immune system and increased susceptibility to opportunistic infections and cancers. Combination antiretroviral therapy (cART) has been the cornerstone of HIV treatment, significantly reducing morbidity and mortality. However, the emergence of drug-resistant mutations (DRMs) and the side effects of approved anti-HIV drugs pose ongoing challenges.

The HIV-1 capsid (CA) protein plays a crucial role in the viral life cycle. It is composed of two structurally distinct domains: the N-terminal domain (CA-NTD) and the C-terminal domain (CA-CTD), connected by a flexible linker. The mature capsid core, which contains approximately 250 hexamers and 12 pentamers, is involved in multiple steps of HIV replication, including viral entry, uncoating, nuclear entry, and integration of viral DNA into the host genome. Given its multifaceted role, the CA protein is an attractive target for the development of new antiviral agents.

Several compounds that bind to HIV-1 CA have been reported, including benzodiazepines (BD), benzimidazoles (BM), pyrrolopyrazolones (BI-1 and BI-2), and the small molecule PF-3450074 (PF74). These compounds have been shown to interfere with CA function, but none have advanced to clinical trials. Recently, the compounds GS-CA1 and GS-6207 have demonstrated greater potency against HIV-1 compared to currently approved anti-HIV drugs. GS-CA1 and GS-6207 bind to a pocket between the CA-NTD and CA-CTD, similar to PF74, CPSF6, and NUP153. However, GS-CA1 and GS-6207 exhibit higher binding affinity and efficacy, making them promising candidates for therapeutic intervention.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to novel cyclic and linear peptides that inhibit the function of the HIV-1 capsid (CA) protein. Specifically, the invention provides a cyclic peptide, designated as Pep-1, which binds to the CA protein with high affinity and inhibits HIV-1 replication. The invention also encompasses methods for designing and synthesizing such peptides, as well as their use in treating HIV-1 infection.

The cyclic peptide Pep-1 was designed based on the structural features of known CA inhibitors, including PF74, BI-2, CPSF6, and NUP153. Computational modeling and docking studies revealed that Pep-1 binds to the same pocket on the CA protein as these inhibitors, with a binding affinity that is approximately seven times greater than that of PF74. The high binding affinity of Pep-1 suggests that it has the potential to be a potent antiviral agent.

The invention further includes methods for using the cyclic and linear peptides to inhibit HIV-1 replication in vitro and in vivo. The peptides can be administered alone or in combination with other antiretroviral agents to enhance their therapeutic efficacy. The invention also covers pharmaceutical compositions comprising the peptides and methods for their preparation and administration.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Definitions

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms are defined as follows:

- **HIV-1 Capsid (CA) Protein**: A viral protein that forms the conical capsid core of the HIV-1 virus. The CA protein is composed of two domains, the N-terminal domain (CA-NTD) and the C-terminal domain (CA-CTD), connected by a flexible linker.
- **Cyclic Peptide**: A peptide in which the amino acid residues are linked in a closed loop, typically through a covalent bond between the amino and carboxyl termini.
- **Linear Peptide**: A peptide in which the amino acid residues are arranged in a straight chain, without forming a closed loop.
- **Binding Affinity**: A measure of the strength of the interaction between two molecules, typically expressed as the dissociation constant (Kd).
- **MicroScale Thermophoresis (MST)**: A technique used to measure the binding affinity of molecules by detecting changes in their movement in a temperature gradient.

### Methods for Designing and Synthesizing the Peptides

The cyclic and linear peptides of the present invention can be designed and synthesized using standard techniques in peptide chemistry. #### Computational Modeling and Docking Studies

Computational modeling and docking studies were used to design the peptides. The crystal structures of PF74, BI-2, CPSF6, and NUP153 bound to the CA protein were used as templates. The Induced-Fit Docking (IFD) module of the Schrödinger Suite was used to predict the binding modes of the peptides to the CA protein. The IFD module optimizes the side chain conformation of the CA protein to best determine the docking poses of the peptides.

The best scoring complexes of the CA protein with the peptides were selected for further analysis. The binding affinities of the peptides to the CA protein were determined using MicroScale thermophoresis (MST) assays. The binding isotherms obtained by plotting the difference in normalized fluorescence against increasing peptide concentration revealed that the binding affinity of Pep-1 (Kd.Pep-1) is 32 ± 3 nM and the binding affinity of Pep-2 (Kd.Pep-2) is 50 ± 5 nM.

#### Peptide Synthesis

The cyclic and linear peptides can be synthesized using standard solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) techniques. For the synthesis of cyclic peptides, the amino and carboxyl termini of the peptide are coupled using a suitable coupling reagent, such as 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS). The coupling reaction is typically carried out in a suitable solvent, such as dimethylformamide (DMF), and the reaction mixture is allowed to stir for several hours.

For the synthesis of linear peptides, the amino acid residues are sequentially coupled to a solid support using a suitable coupling reagent, such as HBTU (2-(1H-benzotriazol-1-yl)-1,1,3,3-tetramethyluronium hexafluorophosphate) and DIPEA (N,N-diisopropylethylamine). The solid support is then cleaved from the peptide using a suitable cleavage reagent, such as trifluoroacetic acid (TFA).
