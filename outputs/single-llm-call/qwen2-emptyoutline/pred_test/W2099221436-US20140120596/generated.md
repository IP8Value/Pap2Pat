# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of biomass pretreatment for the production of fermentable sugars and biofuels. More specifically, the invention concerns a novel method of pretreating lignocellulosic biomass using an alkaline twin-screw extrusion (ATSE) process. This method is designed to enhance the enzymatic digestibility of the biomass, thereby facilitating the conversion of lignocellulosic materials into valuable products such as bioethanol.

## BACKGROUND

The depletion of fossil fuel resources and the increasing environmental concerns have driven the search for sustainable and renewable energy sources. Lignocellulosic biomass, derived from agricultural and forestry residues, is a promising feedstock for the production of biofuels and chemicals. However, the conversion of lignocellulosic biomass into fermentable sugars is challenging due to the recalcitrant nature of the material. The complex structure of lignocellulosic biomass, characterized by the tight association of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, poses significant barriers to enzymatic hydrolysis.

Various pretreatment methods have been developed to overcome these challenges, including chemical, mechanical, thermo-chemical, and biochemical approaches. These methods aim to disrupt the lignin-carbohydrate complex and increase the accessibility of enzymes to the cellulose and hemicellulose components. However, many of these pretreatment techniques suffer from limitations such as high operational costs, long processing times, and environmental concerns related to chemical usage and waste management.

Extrusion pretreatment is a relatively novel approach that combines physical and chemical treatments to enhance the digestibility of lignocellulosic biomass. This method involves the use of an extruder to subject the biomass to heat, compression, and shear forces, leading to physical disruption and chemical modifications. While extrusion pretreatment has shown promise, there is a need for further optimization to improve its efficiency and economic viability.

The present invention addresses these challenges by providing an improved ATSE pretreatment method that effectively disrupts the lignocellulosic structure and enhances the enzymatic digestibility of the biomass. The method is designed to be conducted at a relatively low temperature and with a high biomass-to-liquid ratio, making it more cost-effective and environmentally friendly compared to existing pretreatment technologies.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for pretreating lignocellulosic biomass to improve its enzymatic digestibility. The method comprises the steps of:

1. Feeding lignocellulosic biomass into a twin-screw extruder.
2. Introducing an alkaline solution into the extruder, wherein the alkaline solution is mixed with the biomass under conditions of heat, compression, and shear forces.
3. Extruding the biomass-alkali mixture through the extruder to achieve physical disruption and chemical modification of the biomass.
4. Preserving the extruded biomass at a specified temperature for a predetermined period to further enhance the pretreatment effects.
5. Washing the preserved biomass to remove residual alkali and other soluble components.
6. Optionally, recovering lignin from the wash liquor for further use or disposal.

The method of the present invention is particularly effective in reducing the lignin content of the biomass, thereby improving the accessibility of enzymes to the cellulose and hemicellulose components. The pretreatment conditions, including the concentration of the alkaline solution, the temperature, and the preservation time, can be optimized to achieve the desired level of lignin removal and enzymatic digestibility.

In one embodiment, the lignocellulosic biomass is corn stover, and the alkaline solution is sodium hydroxide (NaOH). The NaOH concentration is preferably in the range of 0.04 to 0.1 g/g biomass, and the preservation time is in the range of 1 to 10 hours. The extrusion process is conducted at a temperature of about 99°C, and the biomass-to-liquid ratio is 1:2 (w/w).

The invention also encompasses the use of the pretreated biomass for the production of fermentable sugars and biofuels, such as bioethanol. The pretreated biomass can be subjected to enzymatic hydrolysis to convert the cellulose and hemicellulose into monomeric sugars, which can then be fermented to produce bioethanol or other valuable chemicals.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a method for pretreating lignocellulosic biomass to enhance its enzymatic digestibility. The method, referred to as Alkaline Twin-Screw Extrusion (ATSE), involves the use of a twin-screw extruder to subject the biomass to a combination of physical and chemical treatments. The ATSE process is designed to disrupt the lignin-carbohydrate complex and improve the accessibility of enzymes to the cellulose and hemicellulose components, thereby facilitating the conversion of the biomass into fermentable sugars and biofuels.

### Feedstock Preparation

The lignocellulosic biomass used in the ATSE process can be derived from various sources, including agricultural residues such as corn stover, rice straw, wheat straw, and bagasse, as well as forestry residues such as sawdust and wood chips. Prior to pretreatment, the biomass is typically cut into small pieces, preferably 2-5 cm in length, to facilitate uniform processing in the extruder. The biomass is then fed into the extruder continuously at a rate of about 200 kg/h.

### Alkaline Solution

An alkaline solution is introduced into the extruder to chemically modify the biomass. The preferred alkaline solution is sodium hydroxide (NaOH), although other alkaline agents such as potassium hydroxide (KOH) or sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) can also be used. The concentration of the alkaline solution is a critical parameter that affects the effectiveness of the pretreatment. In one embodiment, the NaOH concentration is in the range of 0.04 to 0.1 g/g biomass. Higher concentrations of NaOH can lead to more extensive lignin removal but may also result in the degradation of carbohydrates, which can reduce the overall sugar yield.

### Extrusion Process

The biomass and alkaline solution are mixed and subjected to heat, compression, and shear forces within the twin-screw extruder. The extruder is designed with a series of transport screw elements (TSE) and reversed screw elements (RSE) to achieve mechanical crushing and mixing of the biomass. The TSE pushes the biomass forward, while the RSE creates a backpressure that compresses and crushes the biomass. This process generates significant mechanical forces, including frictional and shearing forces, which cause fibrillation and shortening of the biomass fibers. The mechanical forces also generate heat, raising the temperature of the biomass to about 99°C, which facilitates the chemical reactions between the alkaline solution and the biomass.

### Heat Preservation

After extrusion, the biomass-alkali mixture is sealed in plastic bags and preserved at ambient temperature for a specified period. The preservation time is another critical parameter that affects the effectiveness of the pretreatment. In one embodiment, the preservation time is in the range of 1 to 10 hours. During this period, the alkaline solution continues to react with the biomass, further disrupting the lignin-carbohydrate complex and enhancing the enzymatic digestibility. Longer preservation times can lead to more extensive lignin removal but may also increase the risk of carbohydrate degradation.

### Washing and Lignin Recovery

After the preservation step, the biomass is washed with tap water to remove residual alkali and other soluble components. The washing is typically performed through a 300 mesh nylon cloth until the wash water reaches neutrality. The washed biomass can then be used for enzymatic hydrolysis to convert the cellulose and hemicellulose into monomeric sugars. The wash liquor, which contains dissolved lignin and other soluble components, can be recovered and further processed. For example, the lignin can be recovered using a spray dryer and used as a value-added product, such as a concrete additive or a component in phenol-formaldehyde resins.

### Enzymatic Hydrolysis

The pretreated biomass is subjected to enzymatic hydrolysis to convert the cellulose and hemicellulose into fermentable sugars. The hydrolysis is typically performed using a mixture of cellulase and β-glucosidase enzymes. The enzymes are added to the pretreated biomass at a solid loading of about 2% (w/v) and the mixture is incubated at 50°C for 48 hours. The released sugars are analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to determine the glucan and xylan conversion rates.

### Optimization of Pretreatment Conditions

The effectiveness of the ATSE pretreatment can be optimized by adjusting the concentration of the alkaline solution, the preservation time, and other process parameters. For example, increasing the NaOH concentration from 0.04 to 0.06 g/g biomass can significantly improve the lignin removal and enzymatic digestibility. However, higher concentrations of NaOH (above 0.1 g/g biomass) can lead to excessive carbohydrate degradation, reducing the overall sugar yield. Similarly, extending the preservation time beyond 3 hours has a limited impact on the enzymatic digestibility but can increase the operational costs.

### Advantages of ATSE Pretreatment

The ATSE pretreatment method offers several advantages over existing pretreatment technologies. First, the method can be conducted at a relatively low temperature (99°C) without the need for additional heating, making it more energy-efficient. Second, the high biomass-to-liquid ratio (1:2 w/w) reduces the volume of waste water generated and simplifies the waste water treatment process. Third, the ATSE process can be integrated into existing alkali-based pulp mills, leveraging mature technologies for chemical recovery and waste water treatment. Finally, the ATSE method is scalable and can be implemented at a commercial scale with minimal technological barriers.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

#### Objective

To evaluate the effect of different NaOH concentrations on the composition changes and enzymatic digestibility of corn stover after ATSE pretreatment.

#### Method

Corn stover was pretreated using the ATSE process with NaOH concentrations of 0.04, 0.06, 0.08, and 0.1 g/g biomass. The preservation time was set to 1 hour. The pretreated biomass was washed and analyzed for glucan, xylan, and lignin content using the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) Analytical Procedure. The enzymatic digestibility of the pretreated biomass was assessed by measuring the glucan and xylan conversion rates after 48 hours of hydrolysis.

#### Results

The results showed that the ATSE pretreatment significantly altered the composition of the corn stover. The glucan content was well-preserved, with about 96% recovery across all NaOH concentrations. The xylan content decreased with increasing NaOH concentration, with losses ranging from 21.7% to 39.3%. The lignin content was significantly reduced, with reductions ranging from 14% to 83%. The enzymatic digestibility of the pretreated biomass was positively correlated with the NaOH concentration, with glucan conversion rates increasing from 42% to 83% and xylan conversion rates increasing from 29% to 89%.

### Example 2

#### Objective

To evaluate the effect of different preservation times on the composition changes and enzymatic digestibility of corn stover after ATSE pretreatment.

#### Method

Corn stover was pretreated using the ATSE process with a NaOH concentration of 0.06 g/g biomass. The preservation time was varied from 0 to 10 hours. The pretreated biomass was washed and analyzed for glucan, xylan, and lignin content using the NREL Analytical Procedure. The enzymatic digestibility of the pretreated biomass was assessed by measuring the glucan and xylan conversion rates after 48 hours of hydrolysis.

#### Results

The results showed that the preservation time had a significant impact on the composition changes and enzymatic digestibility of the corn stover. The lignin content was reduced by 71% after 1 hour of preservation and by 76% after 10 hours of preservation. The glucan and xylan conversion rates increased with longer preservation times, with the highest conversion rates (83% for glucan and 89% for xylan) observed after 10 hours of preservation. However, extending the preservation time beyond 3 hours had a limited impact on the enzymatic digestibility.

### Example 3

#### Objective

To evaluate the overall mass balance and sugar yield of the ATSE pretreatment process.

#### Method

Corn stover was pretreated using the ATSE process with a NaOH concentration of 0.06 g/g biomass and a preservation time of 1 hour. The pretreated biomass was washed, and the lignin was recovered using a spray dryer. The washed biomass was subjected to enzymatic hydrolysis to convert the cellulose and hemicellulose into monomeric sugars. The sugar yield and mass balance were calculated based on the experimental data.

#### Results

The mass balance analysis showed that the ATSE pretreatment effectively conserved about 90% of the original polysaccharides in the pretreated solid, with a lignin reduction of 71%. The total sugar yield from the enzymatic hydrolysis was 467 g/kg of original biomass, corresponding to about 78% of the potential carbohydrates in the raw biomass. The lignin recovered from the wash liquor had a molecular weight (Mw) of 22,780, a number-average molecular weight (Mn) of 16,430, and a polydispersity index (PDI) of 1.386, indicating that the recovered lignin was suitable for use in value-added applications.

These examples demonstrate the effectiveness of the ATSE pretreatment method in improving the enzymatic digestibility of lignocellulosic biomass and highlight the potential of this method for the production of fermentable sugars and biofuels.
