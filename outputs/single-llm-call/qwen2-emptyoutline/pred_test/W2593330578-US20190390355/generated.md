# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to a method for protecting carbon anodes used in the aluminum production process, particularly in Hall-Héroult cells, from excessive air reactivity. More specifically, the invention involves the impregnation of carbon anodes with a boron-containing solution to form a protective layer that significantly reduces the rate of carbon oxidation, thereby extending the anode's lifespan and reducing dust generation.

## BACKGROUND

In the conventional process of aluminum production using Hall-Héroult cells, carbon anodes are consumed during the electrolysis process. The overall reaction can be represented as:
\[ 2\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 (\text{diss}) + 3\text{C} (\text{s}) \rightarrow 4\text{Al} (\text{m}) + 3\text{CO}_2 (\text{g}), \Delta G^\circ = -339 \text{ kJ} \]
One of the main factors causing excess anode consumption is the oxidation of the anode surface exposed to air. The temperature of the anode exposed to air ranges from 400 to 600°C. Within this temperature range, the oxygen in the air can react with the carbon anode, leading to undesirable reactions:
\[ \text{C} (\text{solid, anode}) + \text{O}_2 (\text{g}) \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 (\text{g}), \Delta G^\circ = -395 \text{ kJ} \]
\[ \text{C} (\text{solid, anode}) + \frac{1}{2}\text{O}_2 (\text{g}) \rightarrow \text{CO} (\text{g}), \Delta G^\circ = -185 \text{ kJ} \]

These reactions consume the carbon anode without producing metal, leading to significant overconsumption and increased operational costs. Air reactivity and CO2 reactivity are among the highest contributors to carbon overconsumption. The anode is typically composed of calcined petroleum coke, recycled anodes (butts), and coal tar pitch. Various strategies have been explored to mitigate air reactivity, including the use of physical barriers and chemical treatments.

Physical barriers, such as coatings, have been developed to protect the anode surface. For instance, the Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organisation (CSIRO) has claimed to provide a cost-effective coating on anodes, which maintains its integrity throughout the anode's life. However, the industrial deployment of such coatings has been limited. Other methods, such as covering the anode with alumina powder or spreading liquid bath (cryolite) on the anode, have been practiced in smelting plants. While these methods can reduce air-burning rates, they are not entirely effective due to their porous nature.

Chemical treatments, such as doping carbon with phosphorus and boron, have also been investigated. Phosphorus is generally prohibited in the electrolysis bath due to its negative impact on current efficiency, leaving boron as a viable option. Boron can inhibit carbon oxidation through several mechanisms, including the redistribution of electron densities on graphite, enhancement of the graphitization process, and the formation of a boron oxide film that blocks active sites. However, the effectiveness of these mechanisms in industrial settings is limited due to the high levels of boron required and the low baking temperatures of anodes.

Despite these challenges, the formation of a boron oxide film remains a promising approach. Boron oxide is soluble in water, making it possible to coat anodes via impregnation with an aqueous solution. Several patents have been filed on this topic, but the widespread adoption of these techniques in industrial settings has been limited. Understanding the impregnation parameters and their effects on the efficiency of the process is crucial for optimizing the protection of carbon anodes.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for protecting carbon anodes from air reactivity by impregnating the anodes with a boron-containing solution. The method involves the following steps:

1. Preparing a boron-containing solution.
2. Impregnating the carbon anode with the boron-containing solution under controlled conditions.
3. Drying the impregnated anode to form a protective layer of boron oxide.

The impregnation process is optimized by varying parameters such as the concentration of the boron solution, the impregnation temperature, and the impregnation time. The method significantly reduces the air reactivity of the anode, thereby extending its lifespan and reducing dust generation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a method for protecting carbon anodes used in the aluminum production process from excessive air reactivity. The method involves impregnating the anodes with a boron-containing solution to form a protective layer of boron oxide. The detailed description of the invention includes the preparation of the boron-containing solution, the impregnation process, and the optimization of impregnation parameters.

### Preparation of the Boron-Containing Solution

The boron-containing solution is prepared by dissolving boron compounds in water. Suitable boron compounds include boron oxide (B2O3), boric acid (H3BO3), and borates. The concentration of the boron solution is typically in the range of 0.1 to 2.0 grams per milliliter (g/mL). The solution is prepared at a temperature ranging from room temperature to 100°C, depending on the solubility of the boron compound and the desired impregnation conditions.

### Impregnation Process

The impregnation process involves dipping the carbon anode into the boron-containing solution for a specified period. The impregnation time can vary from 1 to 20 minutes, with longer impregnation times generally providing better protection. The impregnation temperature can range from room temperature to 100°C, with higher temperatures facilitating better penetration of the solution into the anode's pores.

After impregnation, the anode is dried to remove excess water and form a protective layer of boron oxide. Drying can be performed at a temperature ranging from 100°C to 150°C for a period of 4 to 24 hours. The drying process ensures that the boron oxide is uniformly distributed throughout the anode, providing comprehensive protection.

### Optimization of Impregnation Parameters

The efficiency of the impregnation process can be optimized by varying the concentration of the boron solution, the impregnation temperature, and the impregnation time. The following parameters have been found to be particularly effective:

1. **Concentration of Boron Solution**: A concentration of 0.17 g/mL has been found to provide good protection. Higher concentrations can lead to excessive boron loading, which may not be necessary and can increase costs.

2. **Impregnation Temperature**: Impregnation at a temperature of 50°C to 100°C has been found to be optimal. Higher temperatures facilitate better penetration of the solution into the anode's pores, leading to more uniform protection.

3. **Impregnation Time**: An impregnation time of 10 to 15 minutes has been found to provide the best balance between protection and process efficiency. Longer impregnation times can lead to diminishing returns in terms of protection.

### Effectiveness of the Method

The effectiveness of the impregnation method has been demonstrated through various tests, including air reactivity tests and X-ray computed tomography (CT) scans. Air reactivity tests were conducted using a muffle furnace with an airflow rate of 4 to 5 liters per minute (L/min). The samples were heated to 500°C and maintained at this temperature for several hours to simulate the conditions in an industrial electrolysis cell.

Untreated anode samples showed high reactivity with a mass loss ranging from 52% to 75% after 40 hours of exposure. In contrast, impregnated anode samples showed significantly reduced reactivity, with mass losses ranging from 4.5% to 11% after 160 hours of exposure. The best results were obtained for samples impregnated in hot solutions (7 and 9), with mass losses of 4.5% and 6.5%, respectively.

X-ray CT scans were used to visualize the microstructural changes in the anode samples before and after air reactivity tests. The scans revealed that the impregnated samples did not shrink during the burning test and that the reaction primarily occurred inside the sample, preserving the integrity of the outer surface. This suggests that the impregnation method not only reduces the overall reaction rate but also minimizes dust generation.

### Industrial Application

While the impregnation method has been successfully demonstrated on small anode samples, its implementation at the industrial scale presents challenges. Full impregnation of large anode blocks within a reasonable time frame is practically difficult. However, ongoing research and development efforts aim to address these challenges and develop practical methods for in-depth impregnation of large anode blocks.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1: Anode Preparation

An industrial baked anode was used as the starting material. The anode was composed of 65% calcined petroleum coke, 22% recycled anode, and 13% pitch, and was baked at 1200°C. Samples were cored from different parts of the anode, with a diameter of 19 mm and a length of 14 mm. The samples were then cleaned and dried at 100°C for 24 hours to remove any residual moisture.

### Example 2: Anode Characterization

The anode samples were characterized using X-ray computed tomography (CT) to obtain microstructural information. The CT scans were performed using a Siemens Somatom Sensation 64 scanner with a voxel resolution of 0.1 × 0.1 × 0.6 mm³. The scans revealed the porosity distribution and density of the anode samples, providing baseline data for subsequent air reactivity tests.

### Example 3: Air Reactivity Measurements

Air reactivity measurements were conducted using a muffle furnace. The samples were impregnated with a boron-containing solution at a concentration of 0.17 g/mL and an impregnation time of 15 minutes. The impregnated samples were dried at 100°C for 24 hours. The samples were then placed in the muffle furnace and heated to 500°C under a nitrogen atmosphere. Once the target temperature was reached, the nitrogen flow was replaced with air, and the samples were maintained at 500°C for 160 hours. The mass loss of the samples was recorded at regular intervals to determine the air reactivity.

### Example 4: CO2 Reactivity Measurements

CO2 reactivity measurements were conducted to assess the effect of the boron impregnation on the anode's reactivity with CO2. The impregnated and unimpregnated samples were exposed to a CO2 atmosphere at 500°C for 160 hours. The mass loss of the samples was recorded at regular intervals to determine the CO2 reactivity.

### Example 5: Electrical Resistivity Measurements

Electrical resistivity measurements were conducted to assess the impact of the boron impregnation on the electrical properties of the anode. The impregnated and unimpregnated samples were tested using a four-point probe method. The resistivity of the samples was measured at room temperature and at 500°C to determine any changes in electrical conductivity.

The results of these measurements confirmed that the boron impregnation method significantly reduced the air reactivity of the anode samples, with minimal impact on their electrical properties. The impregnated samples showed a mass loss of 4.5% to 11% after 160 hours of exposure to air, compared to 52% to 75% for the unimpregnated samples. The CO2 reactivity was also reduced, and the electrical resistivity remained within acceptable limits for industrial applications.

In conclusion, the present invention provides a method for effectively protecting carbon anodes from air reactivity, thereby extending their lifespan and reducing operational costs in the aluminum production process. The method is simple, cost-effective, and can be easily integrated into existing manufacturing processes. Further research and development efforts are ongoing to optimize the impregnation process for large-scale industrial applications.
