## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION
The field of therapeutic delivery systems for cancer treatment has seen significant advancements in recent years, particularly with the use of oligonucleotides and peptide nucleic acids (PNAs). However, a major challenge remains in the efficient and targeted delivery of these therapeutic agents into cancer cells. Traditional methods often suffer from poor cellular uptake and limited nuclear localization, which are critical for the effectiveness of therapies targeting transcription factors such as STAT3.

Cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) have emerged as a promising solution to this problem. CPPs can facilitate the delivery of various cargo molecules, including PNAs, into cells. The design of CPPs can be tailored to achieve cell specificity and organelle targeting, such as nuclear localization. One approach involves using the nuclear localization signals (NLS) of transcription factors, which can guide the therapeutic payload to the nucleus where it can exert its effect.

Despite the potential of CPPs, there are still challenges in optimizing their design for efficient and specific delivery. For instance, the use of the NLS of the Oct6 transcription factor has shown promise, but its effectiveness can be limited by factors such as endosomal sequestration and insufficient uptake. The present invention addresses these limitations by introducing a novel CPP that combines a glutamate-rich peptide with the Oct6 NLS, resulting in enhanced cellular uptake and nuclear localization.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION
The present invention provides a novel cell-penetrating peptide (CPP) for the delivery of therapeutic payloads, particularly peptide nucleic acids (PNAs), into cancer cells. The CPP comprises a glutamate-rich peptide (EEEAA) fused to the NLS of the Oct6 transcription factor. This combination results in a CPP with significantly improved cellular uptake and nuclear localization properties compared to the individual components.

The invention also includes methods for using the novel CPP to deliver therapeutic payloads, such as anti-STAT3 PNAs, into cancer cells. The delivered PNAs can effectively inhibit the expression of target genes, leading to significant apoptosis in cancer cell lines. The novel CPP is particularly effective in prostate and pancreatic cancer cell lines, making it a valuable tool for developing targeted cancer therapies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION
### Composition of the Novel CPP
The novel CPP of the present invention is composed of a glutamate-rich peptide (EEEAA) fused to the NLS of the Oct6 transcription factor. The glutamate-rich peptide is positioned at the N-terminus of the Oct6 NLS to enhance cellular uptake and nuclear localization. The specific sequence of the novel CPP is as follows: EEEAA-NLS-Oct6, where NLS-Oct6 represents the NLS sequence of the Oct6 transcription factor.

### Mechanism of Action
The glutamate-rich peptide (EEEAA) plays a crucial role in enhancing the cellular uptake of the CPP. The negatively charged glutamate residues interact with positively charged cell surface molecules, facilitating the initial binding and internalization of the CPP. Once inside the cell, the NLS of the Oct6 transcription factor guides the CPP to the nucleus, ensuring that the therapeutic payload reaches its target site.

### Synthesis of the Novel CPP
The novel CPP can be synthesized using standard solid-phase peptide synthesis techniques. The glutamate-rich peptide (EEEAA) and the NLS of the Oct6 transcription factor are synthesized separately and then conjugated to form the final CPP. The purity and integrity of the CPP can be confirmed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF).

### Delivery of Therapeutic Payloads
The novel CPP can be conjugated to various therapeutic payloads, such as PNAs, to facilitate their delivery into cancer cells. For example, an anti-STAT3 PNA can be conjugated to the C-terminus of the novel CPP to create a hybrid molecule. The hybrid molecule is then incubated with cancer cell lines, such as DU-145 and PANC-1, to evaluate its effectiveness in inducing apoptosis.

### Experimental Validation
#### Cellular Uptake and Nuclear Localization
The novel CPP was tested for its ability to enter and localize to the nucleus of cancer cells. Flow cytometry and imaging flow cytometry were used to quantify the uptake and distribution of the CPP in DU-145 and PANC-1 cells. The results showed that the novel CPP entered the cells rapidly and efficiently, with a significant portion localizing to the nucleus.

#### Temperature Dependence and ATP Requirement
The uptake of the novel CPP was found to be temperature-dependent, with optimal uptake occurring at 37°C. Additionally, the uptake of the CPP was inhibited by NaN3, indicating that it is an ATP-dependent process. This suggests that the CPP enters the cells via an endocytic pathway.

#### Therapeutic Efficacy
The novel CPP was conjugated to an anti-STAT3 PNA and tested for its ability to induce apoptosis in cancer cell lines. The results showed that the hybrid molecule significantly increased apoptosis in both DU-145 and PANC-1 cells, demonstrating the therapeutic potential of the novel CPP.

The results showed that the hybrid molecule significantly increased apoptosis in both cell lines, demonstrating its therapeutic potential.

## Experimental Procedures
### Synthesis of Peptides and Peptide-PNA
Peptides used in the experiments are listed in Table 1 and are referred to by their synthesis numbers for convenience. The carboxyfluorescein and carboxydansyl amino acids were purchased from Bachem. All peptides were synthesized by the Molecular Resources Facility at the University of Medicine and Dentistry, New Jersey Medical School (Newark, NJ) on an Applied Biosystems model 433 peptide synthesizer using FMoc chemistry. After cleavage and deprotection, the peptides were purified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and analyzed by both HPLC and sequencing on an Applied Biosystems Procise 494C sequenator. The FITC-labeled peptide-PNAs FITC-PNA-EEE, FITC-TAT-PNA, and Glu-Oct6-PNA-FITC were synthesized by BioSynthesis (Lewisville, TX). They were purified by HPLC and their structures verified by MALDI-TOF. Because of the requirements of PNA chemistry, FITC was added to the C-terminus of Glu-Oct6-PNA as lysyl FITC.

### Cells
DU-145 and LNCaP cells were obtained from Dr. James Turkson (University of Central Florida, Orlando, FL). DU-145 cells were grown in DMEM/Ham's F12 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) plus 10% newborn bovine serum (Hyclone, Logan, UT). LNCaP cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 (Invitrogen) plus fetal bovine serum (Hyclone). PANC-1 cells were obtained from Dr. James Freeman, University of Texas Health Sciences Center, San Antonio TX. They were grown in the same medium as the DU-145 cells. Cell viabilities were determined using fluorescein diacetate (Sigma Chemical Co., St. Louis, MO) and a Universal RIII fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

### Uptake/Fluorescence Quantification and Nuclear Colocalization Studies
Peptides were added to subconfluent cultures of cells at various times, temperatures, and concentrations. Concentrations ranging from 0 to 1000 nM were assayed. Fluorescence was normalized using calibration beads (Becton-Dickinson; BD). Cells were analyzed in the presence of 5 mM 7-AAD (eBioscience, San Diego CA) to gate on live events. Fluorescence was quantified, after cells were harvested, using a BD FACScan flow cytometer. At least 10,000 events were acquired using CellQuest Pro software and an Apple Macintosh G4 dual coprocessor computer running OS X 10.3.9. Fluorescence detectors on the instrument were standardized prior to each acquisition run, so that fluorescence intensities from different days could be compared. Because the FACScan employs logarithmic amplifiers on the fluorescence detectors, the more accurate parameter with which to compare fluorescence intensities for different samples is the geometric mean fluorescence intensity (geometric MFI). For the studies examining uptake of carboxyfluorescein peptides plus carboxydansyl peptides, fluorescence was quantified on a BD LSR II flow cytometer. In nuclear colocalization studies, cells were stained with the nuclear stain DRAQ5 (Axxora, San Diego, CA) at 5 mM final concentration following uptake of peptides. Fixed cells (4% paraformaldehyde/DPBS) were then analyzed on an Amnis ImageStream 200 imaging cytometer using IDEAS 3.0 software. Four to five thousand events were collected for analysis.

### Live Cell Imaging Studies
A Zeiss Axiovert 200 inverted phase-contrast microscope outfitted with epifluorescence was used for live imaging studies. Subconfluent cultures of cells in 12-well plates were incubated with 500 nM peptides as indicated. DRAQ5 (Axxora; 10 mM final concentration) was added for the last hour of incubation, then the cells were washed twice with warm phenol red-free buffer. Cells were examined in phenol red-free buffer plus 10% serum. Images were acquired and analyzed using Zeiss Axiovision software.

### Apoptosis Studies
DU-145 and PANC-1 cells were incubated with PNAs Glu-Oct6-13410a or 13410a (no CPP attached) at 0, 300, 600, or 1000 nM for 48 hr. Cells were harvested, then stained with FITC-annexin V (Abcam; Cambridge, MA) and counterstained with propidium iodide (Sigma, St. Louis, MO). Cells were analyzed on a BD FACScan for fluorescence in the FL1 and FL3 channels; CellQuest Pro software was used to quantify fluorescence and determine the extent of apoptosis.

### Statistical Analysis
The graphing program Kaleidagraph 4.2 (Synergy Software, Reading, PA) and the statistical program InStat3 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) were used for data analyses unless otherwise indicated.
