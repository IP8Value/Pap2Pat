# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to a method for the comprehensive analysis and comparison of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in donkey meat from different strains using gas chromatography–ion mobility spectroscopy (GC–IMS) combined with multivariate analysis. Specifically, the invention provides a novel strategy for the authentication and differentiation of donkey meat from SanFen (SF) and WuTou (WT) donkeys, which is crucial for ensuring the quality and authenticity of donkey meat products in the market.

## BACKGROUND

Donkey husbandry is a significant industry in China, contributing to the optimization of animal farming practices, diversification of animal products, and rural revitalization. Donkey meat, known for its nutritional value and distinctive flavor, has gained increasing popularity due to its high protein and polyunsaturated fatty acid content. The flavor of meat is primarily determined by the combination of volatile organic compounds (VOCs), which are sensed by smell and taste. Over 1,000 VOCs have been identified in various meats, contributing to their characteristic flavors and influencing consumer preferences.

Traditional methods for identifying VOCs in meat, such as gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) and gas chromatography–olfactometry–mass spectrometry (GC–O–MS), require complex sample pre-treatments and lengthy detection times, leading to untimely and inaccurate results. Gas chromatography–ion mobility spectroscopy (GC–IMS) offers a rapid, stable, and convenient alternative for detecting VOCs without the need for extensive sample preparation. GC–IMS has been successfully applied to differentiate various meats, such as beef, mutton, and chicken, and to identify significant differences in VOCs in bacon from different pork breeds and in dry-cured pork with varying salt contents.

However, the application of GC–IMS combined with multivariate analysis for the identification and analysis of donkey meat has not been extensively explored. The present invention addresses this gap by providing a method to comprehensively analyze and compare the VOC profiles of donkey meat from different strains, facilitating a better understanding of the characteristic VOCs and offering a novel strategy for the authentication of donkey meat.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for the comprehensive analysis and comparison of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in donkey meat from different strains using gas chromatography–ion mobility spectroscopy (GC–IMS) combined with multivariate analysis. The method involves the following steps:

1. **Sample Collection**: Collecting donkey meat samples from SanFen (SF) and WuTou (WT) donkeys, ensuring that the samples are representative and stored under controlled conditions to maintain their integrity.
2. **VOC Analysis**: Analyzing the VOCs in the donkey meat samples using a GC–IMS unit equipped with a capillary column and an automatic headspace sampling unit. The samples are incubated at a specific temperature, and the headspace gas is injected into the apparatus for analysis.
3. **VOC Identification**: Identifying the VOCs by comparing their retention indices (RIs) and drift times (DTs) with those of standard compounds in the NIST 2014 library and GC–IMS database.
4. **Statistical Analysis**: Performing statistical analysis to evaluate the differences in VOC profiles between the two strains of donkey meat. This includes one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), Tukey’s test, and multivariate analysis techniques such as principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and orthogonal PLS-DA (OPLS-DA).

The invention also provides a method for the authentication of donkey meat by identifying and characterizing differential VOCs that can serve as potential markers to distinguish between different strains of donkey meat. The method facilitates a better understanding of the characteristic VOCs of donkey meat and offers a novel strategy for ensuring the quality and authenticity of donkey meat products in the market.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE EMBODIMENTS

### Sample Collection

The method begins with the collection of donkey meat samples from SanFen (SF) and WuTou (WT) donkeys. A total of 12 healthy two-year-old Dezhou donkeys, including 6 SF donkeys and 6 WT donkeys, with an equal distribution of males and females, are obtained from a local farm in Liaocheng, Shandong, China. The donkeys are fed the same diet and raised under similar conditions to ensure consistency. They are transported to a local slaughterhouse and killed according to international standards (CAC/RCP 41-1993 and ISO/TS 34700:2016) after a 12-hour starvation period. The longissimus dorsi muscle, located between the 17th and 18th ribs, is immediately excised, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80°C in a refrigerator for subsequent GC–IMS analysis. The animal experiments are approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Liaocheng University.

### VOC Analysis

The volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in the donkey meat samples are analyzed using a FlavourSpec® GC–IMS unit equipped with a capillary column (MXT-5, 15 m × 0.53 mm × 1.0 μm) and an automatic headspace sampling unit (CTC-PAL, CTC Analytics AG, Zwingen, Switzerland). A meat sample weighing 1.5 g is placed into a 20 mL headspace glass bottle and incubated at 60°C for 15 minutes with spinning at 500 rpm. Subsequently, 500 μL of the headspace gas is automatically injected into the apparatus. The temperature of the injector is set to 85°C, and the GC column temperature is maintained at 40°C. The carrier gas, which is nitrogen with a purity of ≥99.999%, flows at a programmed rate: 0–2 minutes, 2 mL/min; 2–10 minutes, 2–20 mL/min; 10–20 minutes, 20–100 mL/min. The drift tube of the IMS instrument is 9.8 cm long, and the drift and tube temperatures are set to 60°C and 45°C, respectively. The voltage of the drift tube is 5 kV, and the drift gas, also nitrogen with a purity of ≥99.999%, flows at a rate of 150 mL/min. 3H ionization is performed in positive ion mode.

### VOC Identification

The retention indices (RIs) of the volatiles are compared with those of C4–C9 n-ketones obtained under the same analytical conditions. The RIs and drift times (DTs) of the standards in the NIST 2014 library and GC–IMS database are used to identify the VOCs. This step ensures the accurate identification of the VOCs present in the donkey meat samples.

### Statistical Analysis

Each sample is analyzed in triplicate, and the data are represented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The results are analyzed using SPSS version 24.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey’s test to evaluate the differences among the samples. A significance level of p < 0.05 is considered statistically significant. The spectra and fingerprints are processed using the Reporter plug-in and Gallery Plot plug-in, respectively. Principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), orthogonal PLS-DA (OPLS-DA), and heatmap analysis are performed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 online software. Differential VOCs are determined based on a variable importance in projection (VIP) value of >1 and a p-value of <0.05.

### Results and Discussion

#### VOC Profiles of Donkey Meats

The GC–IMS analysis reveals the presence of 45 VOCs in the donkey meat samples, including 15 ketones, 13 alcohols, 9 aldehydes, 1 heterocycle, and 7 unidentified compounds. A total of 38 VOCs are identified in both strains of donkey meat. Ketones, aldehydes, and alcohols are the most abundant VOCs, with ketones and alcohols being significantly more abundant in SF donkey meat (p < 0.001; p < 0.05) and aldehydes being more abundant in WT donkey meat (p < 0.001).

#### Comparison of VOCs from the Two Strains

Topographic plots of the GC–IMS results show good repeatability, and significant differences are observed between the fingerprints of the two strains. Specific VOCs, such as isopropyl alcohol, ethanol, acetone, benzaldehyde, 2-pentanone-d, 2-pentanone-m, oct-1-en-3-ol, 3-octenal, pentanal-d, pentanal-m, methyl isobutyl ketone-d, methyl isobutyl ketone-m, heptanal-d, and heptanal-m, exhibit different signals and represent differential components in the fingerprint regions of the donkey meats from the two strains.

#### Multivariate Analysis of VOCs

Multivariate analysis, including PCA, PLS-DA, and OPLS-DA, is performed to better present and distinguish the differences between the donkey meats from the two strains. The results show that the donkey meat samples are well differentiated according to strain using PCA, PLS-DA, and OPLS-DA. The OPLS-DA model is robust, reliable, and free from over-fitting, as indicated by the R2 and Q2 intercept values. A total of 17 differential VOCs are identified between the two strains, with 7 downregulated and 10 upregulated VOCs in SF compared to WT. These differential VOCs belong to four categories: 7 alcohols, 7 aldehydes, 1 heterocycle, and 2 ketones. The levels of tetrahydrofurane, hexanal-m, pentan-1-ol-d, pentan-1-ol-m, 3-octenal, oct-1-en-3-ol, pentanal-d, (e)-hept-2-enal, pentanal-m, and hexanal-d are significantly lower in SF than in WT (p < 0.05), while 2-butanol, 2-methyl-1-propanol, benzaldehyde, ethanol, isopropyl alcohol, acetone, and 2-pentanone-m show the opposite trend (p < 0.05).

### Conclusion

The present invention provides a method for the comprehensive analysis and comparison of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in donkey meat from different strains using gas chromatography–ion mobility spectroscopy (GC–IMS) combined with multivariate analysis. The method identifies 38 VOCs in donkey meat and differentiates the meat from SF and WT donkeys based on their VOC fingerprints and multivariate analysis. A total of 17 differential VOCs are identified as potential markers to distinguish between the two strains of donkey meat. This method facilitates a better understanding of the characteristic VOCs of donkey meat and offers a novel strategy for its authentication, ensuring the quality and authenticity of donkey meat products in the market. Further studies are required to clarify the characteristic VOCs of donkey meat and to explore their formation mechanisms.
