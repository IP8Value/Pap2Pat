# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Blunt trauma is a significant cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, affecting various organs and tissues. The pathophysiology of blunt injuries, particularly those involving the brain and liver, is complex and not fully understood. Traditional animal models have been used to study these injuries, but they often fail to provide a clear understanding of the underlying cellular mechanisms due to the complexity of interpreting animal models and the lack of standardized testing procedures. Recent advancements in in vitro platforms have shown promise in addressing these limitations by allowing researchers to investigate the cellular and molecular mechanisms of blunt injuries in a controlled environment. However, these platforms face technical challenges in accurately applying injury-relevant forces to cell populations and maintaining consistent in vitro conditions during experiments.

The present invention addresses these challenges by providing a novel in vitro platform for studying blunt injury mechanisms. This platform integrates a drop-tower-based system with a multiplexed live cell-imaging instrument, enabling the application of well-characterized mechanical impacts to live cell populations while maintaining consistent in vitro conditions. The platform also allows for real-time monitoring of cell behavior before and after impact, ensuring statistical significance and reproducibility of results.

## BRIEF DESCRIPTION

The invention relates to a new in vitro platform for studying the cellular and molecular mechanisms of blunt injuries. The platform includes a drop-tower-based system for applying controlled mechanical impacts to live cell populations and a multiplexed live cell-imaging instrument for real-time monitoring of cell behavior. The platform is designed to maintain consistent in vitro conditions during experiments and to accommodate multiple cell populations, ensuring statistical significance and reproducibility of results. The invention also includes methods for quantifying the acceleration- and cavitation-induced pressure during impact and for assessing cell damage.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### In Vitro Setup for Blunt Injury Mechanisms

The in vitro setup for blunt injury mechanisms is designed to apply a wide range of biologically relevant mechanical impacts to live cell populations while maintaining consistent in vitro conditions. The setup integrates a drop-tower-based system with a multiplexed live cell-imaging instrument, allowing for real-time monitoring of cell behavior before and after impact.

The system consists of a conventional drop tower impact system, two high-speed cameras, a cell culture setup, accelerometers, and a data acquisition system. The drop tower impact system is used to apply controlled mechanical impacts to the cell culture setup by releasing a weight from a specific height. Upon release, the weight impacts a holder with an integrated cell culture setup, causing the cells and cell culture media to rapidly accelerate. The velocity, acceleration, and impact force are quantified using various sensors, while the cell culture setup is optically monitored using high-speed cameras.

The cell culture setup includes a commercially available 35-mm cell culture petri dish, aluminum plates, and a transparent silicone film. The petri dish is assembled and disassembled with the cell culture setup using standard bolts and nuts. Two major technical challenges for any in vitro experimental platform are environmental control and cell heterogeneity. The cell culture setup is designed to address these challenges by integrating with a multiplexed live cell-imaging instrument, ensuring consistent in vitro conditions and minimizing batch-to-batch and population-to-population variations in cell response.

The holder is rigid enough to sustain impacts while preventing direct impact to the cell culture setup. Soft foam layers are used at the top and bottom of the holder to achieve desired impact characteristics. The top layer is thinner and stiffer to prevent direct solid-solid surface impact, while the bottom layer is thicker and softer to allow vertical movement against the small stiffness of the layer. These parameters are specifically chosen to simulate common blunt injury scenarios, such as those involving the abdomen or skull.

### Multiplexed In Vitro Cell Cultures

The in vitro platform supports multiplexed cell cultures, allowing the simultaneous monitoring of multiple cell populations. This feature is crucial for probing the injury mechanisms of cells while minimizing batch-to-batch and population-to-population variations in cell response. The platform can accommodate up to 12 separate cell culture setups, each of which can be monitored in real-time using live cell microscopy.

For the experimental study of cell damage due to mechanical impact, multiple cell culture petri dishes are prepared and monitored using live cell imaging capability in an incubator. After the cells reach a specific target stage, such as 35-40% confluency in the cell growth curve, additional cell culture media is added to the dish before assembling with a holder for the drop tower experiments. Two dishes are dedicated for control experiments, while other dishes are used for drop tower experiments. The confluency of each dish is continuously monitored to quantify cell population and to ensure consistent in vitro conditions during experiments.

### Quantifying the Blunt Injury

The critical blunt injury conditions that result in cell damage are quantified by subjecting cell cultures to a range of drop tower heights and monitoring the average and local confluency of the cell populations. The platform allows for the precise control of impact conditions, enabling the identification of the critical input acceleration associated with cell damage.

Experimental data show that the critical drop height associated with noticeable loss of cell population is greater than 30 cm. The maximum amplitude of input acceleration for 30 cm-drop is 990-1,000 g, significantly larger than the commonly used criteria for blunt head injury. The critical acceleration for cell damage is influenced by the size of the biological system, with smaller systems requiring higher accelerations to trigger cell damage.

Another unexpected observation is the sudden increase in localized cell death for 40 cm-drops, where the corresponding amplitude of acceleration is 1,280-1,370 g. High-speed camera images show the formation of cavitation bubbles at the bottom of the petri dish, which are likely responsible for localized cell damage. The dynamics of cavitation bubbles are stochastic and localized, resulting in significant loss of cell population in randomly distributed, localized spots on the 2D cell culture plate.

To further investigate the main injury mechanism, the platform includes methods for measuring time-varying pressure at the bottom of the cell culture chamber. Experimental data show that the amplitude of cavitation-induced pressure is considerably larger than acceleration-induced pressure, and the rate of pressure changes for cavitation-induced pressure is significantly faster. The sudden changes in pressure are mainly associated with the dynamics of cavitation bubbles, particularly cavitation collapse.

### The Main Injury Mechanism: Cavitation

The main injury mechanism associated with mechanical impact is cavitation. Cavitation bubbles form and collapse rapidly, applying localized compressive and tensile pressure cycles to cells. The amplitude of cavitation-induced pressure is much larger than acceleration-induced pressure, and the rate of pressure changes is significantly faster. The dynamics of cavitation bubbles are stochastic and localized, resulting in sudden and localized cell death.

Experimental data show that cavitation-induced pressure increases from -72.9 to 259.2 kPa within 0.05 ms, with a rate of pressure change of 6,642 kPa/ms. In contrast, acceleration-induced pressure gradually increases from 0 to 118.6 kPa within 0.70 ms, with a rate of pressure change of 169 kPa/ms. The localized and rapid nature of cavitation-induced pressure changes is responsible for the sudden and localized cell damage observed in the experiments.

### Conclusion

The in vitro platform for studying blunt injury mechanisms provides a novel and powerful tool for understanding the cellular and molecular mechanisms of blunt injuries. The platform integrates a drop-tower-based system with a multiplexed live cell-imaging instrument, enabling the application of well-characterized mechanical impacts to live cell populations while maintaining consistent in vitro conditions. The platform allows for real-time monitoring of cell behavior before and after impact, ensuring statistical significance and reproducibility of results.

The critical blunt injury conditions are quantified by subjecting cell cultures to a range of drop tower heights and monitoring the average and local confluency of the cell populations. The main injury mechanism is cavitation, which applies localized compressive and tensile pressure cycles to cells, resulting in sudden and localized cell death. The platform offers a novel window into fundamental mechanisms of cell injury due to impact and can be expanded to a plethora of cell lines and in vitro conditions, paving the way for innovative protective equipment designs and effective strategies for post-injury treatment.
