# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Over a million adults are annually diagnosed with brain diseases or disorders in the United States alone. The Alzheimer’s Association reported that in 2016, the national cost for dementias was approximately $236 billion. A major contributor to this high treatment cost is the late-stage failure of promising drug candidates. Only 8% of central nervous system drug candidates that reach initial Phase 1 human safety testing eventually achieve regulatory approval due to either toxicity or ineffectiveness. This estimate does not include the number of initially promising treatments pursued during pre-clinical in vitro and in vivo studies. The shortage of effective therapies and low success rate of investigational drugs are in part due to the lack of human-equivalent models. Some current 2D systems may not accurately mimic human physiology because they do not possess the three-dimensional organization of tissues and often consist of cell lines, lacking physiologically relevant ratios of all cell types present in the organ. More recently established 3D blood-brain barrier (BBB) models have provided an understanding of size exclusion, selectivity, and many other important aspects such as the expression of efflux and transport proteins. However, these models often lack the complexity of the human neurovascular unit (NVU), which includes pericytes, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons. The interaction between these cell types is crucial for maintaining the integrity and function of the BBB. Therefore, the development of a 3D in vitro system that contains all major cell types found in the adult human brain cortex may provide a platform that can be used to understand the fundamental principles at play with the BBB, its function, and the effects of chemical substances that cross the BBB.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a 3D in vitro human neurovascular unit (NVU) organoid model that contains the six constituent cell types found within the brain cortex: human brain microvascular endothelial cells (HBMEC), human pericytes (HBVP), human astrocytes (HA), human microglia (HM), human oligodendrocytes (HO), and human neurons (HN). The organoid model is designed to mimic the physiological microenvironment of the human brain and can be used to study the formation, maintenance, and function of the blood-brain barrier (BBB). The model can be used in toxicity assessment studies for molecules that have the potential to cross or open the BBB, and to understand the intercellular dynamics once molecules cross the barrier. The invention also provides methods for making and using the 3D NVU organoid model, including methods for assessing the integrity and selectivity of the BBB, and methods for evaluating the effects of chemical substances on the NVU.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

### 1. Blood, Brain Barrier Models and Methods of Making the Same.

The present invention provides a 3D in vitro human neurovascular unit (NVU) organoid model that contains the six constituent cell types found within the brain cortex: human brain microvascular endothelial cells (HBMEC), human pericytes (HBVP), human astrocytes (HA), human microglia (HM), human oligodendrocytes (HO), and human neurons (HN). The organoid model is designed to mimic the physiological microenvironment of the human brain and can be used to study the formation, maintenance, and function of the blood-brain barrier (BBB).

#### 1.1. Cell Types and Culture Conditions

The organoid model is composed of the following cell types:
- **Human Brain Microvascular Endothelial Cells (HBMEC)**: These cells form the inner lining of the blood vessels and are crucial for the formation of the BBB.
- **Human Pericytes (HBVP)**: These cells are located in the walls of capillaries and play a role in the regulation of blood flow and the maintenance of the BBB.
- **Human Astrocytes (HA)**: These cells provide support and nutrients to neurons and are involved in the regulation of the BBB.
- **Human Microglia (HM)**: These cells are the resident immune cells of the brain and play a role in the regulation of the BBB and the response to inflammation.
- **Human Oligodendrocytes (HO)**: These cells produce myelin, which insulates axons and is essential for the proper functioning of the nervous system.
- **Human Neurons (HN)**: These cells are the primary functional units of the nervous system and are involved in the transmission of signals.

Each cell type is cultured under specific conditions to ensure optimal growth and differentiation. The cells are expanded and maintained in culture media that supports their specific needs. For example, HBMEC are cultured in complete classic medium supplemented with CultureBoost™ and attachment factor, while HA are cultured in astrocyte medium containing 2% FBS, astrocyte growth supplement, and penicillin-streptomycin.

#### 1.2. Organoid Formation

The organoid model is formed using a hanging drop culture method. The cell types are mixed in a specific ratio and placed in a hanging drop culture plate. The cells are allowed to self-assemble into a 3D structure over a period of 96 hours. The organoids are then transferred to a 96-well plate for long-term culture. The medium is changed every 48 hours to ensure the continued growth and maintenance of the organoids.

#### 1.3. Staged Assembly Method

To improve the coverage of the endothelial cells on the surface of the organoids, a staged assembly method is used. In this method, the organoids are first formed with the four cell types (HA, HM, HO, and HN) and allowed to mature for 48 hours. The HBMEC and HBVP are then added to the organoids to form a more cohesive coating on the surface. This method ensures that the endothelial cells fully cover the outer surface of the organoids, thereby enhancing the integrity of the BBB.

### 2. Methods of Use.

The 3D NVU organoid model can be used in various applications, including the assessment of the integrity and selectivity of the BBB, the evaluation of the effects of chemical substances on the NVU, and the study of the intercellular dynamics within the NVU.

#### 2.1. Assessment of BBB Integrity and Selectivity

The integrity and selectivity of the BBB in the organoid model can be assessed using various methods. One method involves the use of FITC-labeled IgG to evaluate the permeability of the BBB. The organoids are incubated with FITC-labeled IgG, and the permeability is assessed using confocal microscopy. The presence of a functional BBB is indicated by low permeability of the FITC-labeled IgG to the core of the organoids. The BBB can also be transiently opened using histamine, and the permeability can be assessed again to confirm the presence of a functional barrier.

Another method involves the use of inorganic mercury (Hg2+) to assess the charge selectivity of the BBB. Hg2+ does not cross the BBB due to its positive charge. The organoids are treated with HgCl2, and the cell viability is assessed using an ATP production assay. Organoids with a functional BBB show higher cell viability compared to organoids without a BBB, indicating the low permeability of Hg2+ through the BBB.

#### 2.2. Evaluation of Chemical Substance Effects

The organoid model can be used to evaluate the effects of chemical substances on the NVU. For example, the model can be used to assess the neurotoxicity of MPTP and MPP+. MPTP is a lipophilic small molecule that crosses the BBB and is metabolized into the toxic positively charged MPP+ by monoamine oxidase B present in glial cells. MPP+ causes ATP depletion in dopaminergic neurons, leading to cell death. The organoids are treated with MPTP and MPP+, and the cell viability is assessed using an ATP production assay. Organoids with a functional BBB show reduced cell viability when treated with MPTP but not with MPP+, indicating the selectivity of the BBB.

#### 2.3. Study of Intercellular Dynamics

The organoid model can be used to study the intercellular dynamics within the NVU. For example, the model can be used to assess the interaction between pericytes and endothelial cells, the influence of astrocytes on the integrity of the BBB, and the role of microglia in BBB regulation. The model can also be used to study the effects of hypoxia on the expression and distribution of tight junction proteins and adherens junction proteins. The organoids are cultured under hypoxic conditions, and the expression and distribution of these proteins are assessed using immunofluorescent staining and confocal microscopy.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

**Confirmation of Assembly with Barrier Formation**

To demonstrate the assembly and cellular organization of organoids with four cell types (HBMEC, HBVP, HA, and HCN-2), the cells were pre-treated with a long-term cell labeling dye. The cell mixture was placed in a hanging drop culture environment. After 96 hours of culture, the assembled organoids were imaged using confocal microscopy. The images confirmed that HBMEC and HBVP localized to the periphery of the organoid, with HA localized near the surface and neuronal cells forming the core. The presence of a BBB in the organoid model was confirmed by testing the expression of the tight junction protein ZO-1 using immunofluorescent labeling and confocal microscopy. The permeability of the BBB was assessed by incubating the organoids with FITC-labeled IgG. The permeability was first evaluated in untreated organoids and then in organoids pretreated with histamine to transiently open the BBB. Confocal microscopy showed higher FITC fluorescence at the core of treated organoids compared to untreated organoids, confirming the presence of a functional BBB.

### Example 2. Human Cortex Model with Spheroid Culture System

**Six Cell Type Human Cortical Organoid**

A 3D in vitro human cortical organoid model containing all six cell types (HBMEC, HBVP, HA, HM, HO, and HN) was constructed using a single self-assembly method. The organoids maintained high cell viability for up to 21 days in vitro. Immunofluorescent staining was conducted to confirm the expression of cell-specific markers, including CD31 for HBMEC, PDGFR for pericytes, GFAP for astrocytes, Iba1 for microglia, MBP and O4 for oligodendrocytes, and MAP2 and tyrosine hydroxylase for neurons. The expression of tight junction proteins (ZO-1 and Claudin-5) and transport proteins (P-gp and GLUT1) was also confirmed. The organoids were further characterized by assessing the permeability of FITC-labeled IgG and the effects of hypoxia on the expression and distribution of tight junction and adherens junction proteins.

### Example 3: Bioprint Functional Cortical Tissue

**Staged Assembly Method for Enhanced Coverage**

To improve the coverage of endothelial cells on the surface of the organoids, a staged assembly method was used. The organoids were first formed with the four cell types (HA, HM, HO, and HN) and allowed to mature for 48 hours. The HBMEC and HBVP were then added to the organoids to form a more cohesive coating on the surface. The organoids were imaged using confocal microscopy to confirm the improved coverage of the endothelial cells. The expression of cell-specific markers and tight junction proteins was assessed using immunofluorescent staining. The permeability of the BBB was evaluated using FITC-labeled IgG, and the effects of hypoxia on the expression and distribution of tight junction and adherens junction proteins were assessed.

### Example 4. Three-Dimensional Bioprinting of the Human Neurovascular Unit

**Hypoxia Affects Junctional Protein Distribution**

To evaluate the effect of hypoxia on the expression and distribution of tight junction and adherens junction proteins, the organoids were cultured under hypoxic conditions (37°C, 0.1% O2) for 24 hours. The organoids were then fixed and immunostained for claudin-5, ZO-1, β-catenin, and VE-cadherin. Confocal microscopy showed that the localization of these proteins was disrupted under hypoxic conditions compared to normoxic conditions. The fluorescence intensity of claudin-5, ZO-1, β-catenin, and VE-cadherin was quantified and normalized to DAPI fluorescence. The results showed a significant reduction in the relative amount of β-catenin and VE-cadherin under hypoxic conditions compared to normoxic conditions, indicating that hypoxia disrupts the integrity of the BBB.

## CONCLUSION

The 3D in vitro human neurovascular unit (NVU) organoid model described herein forms a functional BBB that is charge-selective and shows relevant responses to physiologic conditional changes. This model may be useful not only in drug discovery for novel therapeutics but also to evaluate the ability of such drug candidates to cross the BBB and their ensuing sequelae such as general cytotoxicity, glial toxicity, and neurotoxicity. The model can also be used to study the intercellular dynamics within the NVU and to understand the combined capabilities of a novel drug to cross the BBB and its effect on microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons. The improvement reported herein warrants this model as a possible alternative to existing in vitro models.
