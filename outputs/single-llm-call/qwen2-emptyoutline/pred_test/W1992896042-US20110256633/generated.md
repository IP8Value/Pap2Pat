# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of cellular biology, particularly to the identification and characterization of large dense-core vesicles (LDCVs) in astrocytes. Astrocytes, a type of glial cell, play a crucial role in the central nervous system (CNS) by providing support and regulation to neurons. They are involved in various functions, including the maintenance of the blood-brain barrier, ion homeostasis, and the modulation of synaptic transmission. Recent studies have revealed that astrocytes also participate in cell-to-cell communication through the release of signaling molecules, known as gliotransmitters, which are stored in secretory vesicles.

Traditionally, the regulatory secretory pathway in astrocytes has been thought to involve small synaptic-like vesicles, similar to those found in neurons. However, the presence and function of large dense-core vesicles (LDCVs) in astrocytes have been less explored. LDCVs are typically associated with neuroendocrine cells and are known to store and release a variety of signaling molecules, including regulatory peptides and neurotransmitters, in a Ca²⁺-dependent manner. The identification of LDCVs in astrocytes and their role in intracellular Ca²⁺ signaling and exocytosis is a significant advancement in understanding the complex communication network within the CNS.

The present invention provides a method for identifying and characterizing large dense-core vesicles in astrocytes, demonstrating that these vesicles are indeed secretory granules. The invention further reveals the presence of chromogranin B (CGB) and secretogranin II (SgII) in these secretory granules, as well as all three isoforms of inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptors (IP3Rs). This discovery highlights the potential of these secretory granules to function as major IP3-sensitive intracellular Ca²⁺ stores in astrocytes, which is crucial for the regulation of exocytotic processes and cell-to-cell communication.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THIS INVENTION

The present invention is directed to the identification and characterization of large dense-core vesicles (LDCVs) in astrocytes, which are shown to be secretory granules. The invention provides a method for detecting the presence of chromogranin B (CGB) and secretogranin II (SgII) in these secretory granules, as well as the localization of all three isoforms of inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptors (IP3Rs) in the membranes of these granules. This method involves the use of immunogold electron microscopy to label and visualize the specific proteins within the secretory granules of astrocytes.

### EXAMPLES

#### Example 1: Identification of Large Dense-Core Vesicles in Astrocytes

In this example, the presence and distribution of large dense-core vesicles (LDCVs) in astrocytes were investigated using immunogold electron microscopy. Human brain tissue samples were obtained from patients undergoing surgical treatments, and the tissues were processed for electron microscopy. The tissue samples were fixed, embedded, and sectioned to prepare ultrathin sections for immunogold labeling.

The ultrathin sections were incubated with primary antibodies specific to chromogranin B (CGB) and secretogranin II (SgII), followed by incubation with gold-conjugated secondary antibodies. The sections were then stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate for visualization under a transmission electron microscope.

Results showed that CGB- and SgII-labeling gold particles were localized inside the large dense-core vesicles, confirming the presence of these secretory granule marker proteins in the LDCVs. The number of CGB- and SgII-labeling gold particles per µm² area of secretory granules was significantly higher compared to the background levels in mitochondria, further validating the identity of these vesicles as secretory granules.

#### Example 2: Localization of IP3R Isoforms in Astrocyte Secretory Granules

In this example, the presence of inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptors (IP3Rs) in the secretory granules of astrocytes was investigated. The same human brain tissue samples used in Example 1 were processed for immunogold electron microscopy. The ultrathin sections were incubated with primary antibodies specific to IP3R1, IP3R2, and IP3R3, followed by incubation with gold-conjugated secondary antibodies.

Results showed that IP3R1-, IP3R2-, and IP3R3-labeling gold particles were localized in the membranes of secretory granules, but not in mitochondria. The number of IP3R1-, IP3R2-, and IP3R3-labeling gold particles per µm of secretory granule membrane was significantly higher compared to the background levels in mitochondrial membranes, confirming the presence of all three IP3R isoforms in the secretory granules of astrocytes.

### Results

The results of the immunogold electron microscopy experiments demonstrated the presence of chromogranin B (CGB) and secretogranin II (SgII) in the large dense-core vesicles (LDCVs) of astrocytes. The number of CGB- and SgII-labeling gold particles per µm² area of secretory granules was 12.38 and 10.31, respectively, while the background levels in mitochondria were 0.37 and 0.20, respectively. These findings confirm the identity of LDCVs as secretory granules in astrocytes.

Additionally, the results showed the presence of all three isoforms of inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptors (IP3Rs) in the membranes of astrocyte secretory granules. The number of IP3R1-, IP3R2-, and IP3R3-labeling gold particles per µm of secretory granule membrane was 0.872, 0.586, and 0.719, respectively, while the background levels in mitochondrial membranes were 0.035, 0.022, and 0.018, respectively. These findings indicate that astrocyte secretory granules function as major IP3-sensitive intracellular Ca²⁺ stores.

### Discussion

The present invention provides a significant advancement in the understanding of the regulatory secretory pathway in astrocytes. The identification of large dense-core vesicles (LDCVs) as secretory granules in astrocytes, and the presence of chromogranin B (CGB) and secretogranin II (SgII) in these granules, confirms the secretory nature of astrocytes. The localization of all three isoforms of inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptors (IP3Rs) in the membranes of these secretory granules further supports the hypothesis that these granules serve as major IP3-sensitive intracellular Ca²⁺ stores in astrocytes.

The presence of secretory granules in astrocytes is consistent with the increasing evidence of the secretory activity of these cells in the brain. Astrocytes are known to release a variety of signaling molecules, including glutamate, ATP, D-serine, and neuropeptide Y (NPY), which are stored in secretory vesicles and released in a Ca²⁺-dependent manner. The regulated exocytosis in astrocytes is crucial for cell-to-cell communication and the modulation of synaptic transmission.

The IP3-sensitive intracellular Ca²⁺ stores in astrocyte secretory granules are likely to play a key role in the initiation of exocytotic processes and the release of gliotransmitters. The high IP3 sensitivity of the secretory granule IP3R/Ca²⁺ channels ensures that these granules can respond to low concentrations of IP3, thereby contributing to the rapid and efficient release of signaling molecules. This mechanism is particularly important in the context of cell-to-cell communication in the brain, where precise and timely signaling is essential for proper neural function.

Moreover, the presence of secretory granules in the cell processes of astrocytes suggests that these granules may play a role in the fine-tuning of intracellular Ca²⁺ signaling and exocytotic activity in specific regions of the cell. This is particularly relevant for the regulation of synaptic transmission and the maintenance of neural network stability.

In conclusion, the present invention provides a method for identifying and characterizing large dense-core vesicles (LDCVs) in astrocytes, demonstrating their identity as secretory granules and their role as major IP3-sensitive intracellular Ca²⁺ stores. This discovery has significant implications for understanding the regulatory secretory pathway in astrocytes and the broader role of these cells in the central nervous system.
