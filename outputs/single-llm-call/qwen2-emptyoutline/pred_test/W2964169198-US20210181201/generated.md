# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of extracellular vesicle (EV) research and diagnostics. More specifically, the invention pertains to recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) that serve as biological reference materials for the accurate calibration, normalization, and method development in EV research and biomedical applications. The rEV are designed to resemble the physical and biochemical characteristics of endogenous EV, thereby facilitating precise and reliable measurements and comparisons in various experimental conditions.

## BACKGROUND

Extracellular vesicles (EV) are small membrane-bound particles released by cells and play crucial roles in intercellular communication, disease pathogenesis, and therapeutic applications. Despite their significance, the study and application of EV are hampered by the lack of appropriate reference materials for accurate calibration, normalization, and method development. Current reference materials, such as polystyrene beads, silica beads, or liposomes, do not possess the EV-like properties necessary for precise measurements. Biological reference materials derived from biofluids or cell cultures are available but lack specificity, making them less suitable for quality control applications.

To address these challenges, the present invention introduces recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) as a trackable biological reference material that closely mimics the physical and biochemical characteristics of endogenous EV. The rEV are generated using the gag polyprotein from HIV-1, which hijacks the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT) pathway to produce nanometer-sized immature virus-like particles (VLP) with a lipid bilayer and enriched for gag molecules and EV-associated proteins. This invention provides a robust and versatile tool for EV research and biomedical applications, ensuring rigorous and accurate analysis of sample EV.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### EXAMPLES

#### Example 1: Recombinant Extracellular Vesicles (rEV) Share Physical and Biochemical Characteristics with Endogenous Sample Extracellular Vesicles (Sample EV)

Recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) were produced in a well-characterized HEK293T cell culture model by transient transfection with a retroviral gag polyprotein C-terminally fused to enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP). The rEV were separated from the conditioned medium (CM) 72 hours post-transfection using OptiPrep density gradient (ODG) centrifugation and consecutive pelleting, resulting in approximately 5 × 10^11 rEV.

The physical and biochemical characteristics of rEV were evaluated and compared to those of sample EV derived from various sources, including urine, plasma, and medium conditioned by breast cancer cells (MCF7 and 4T1) and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF). The buoyant density of rEV was equivalent to that of sample EV, ranging from 1.086 to 1.119 g/mL. The size distribution (108.6 ± 9.5 nm) and zeta potential (−32.3 ± 1.6 mV) of rEV did not significantly differ from those of sample EV. The refractive index (RI) of rEV, calculated using Mie theory, was approximately 1.37, corresponding to the RI of sample EV.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that rEV exhibit a cup-like morphology characteristic of sample EV. Western blot analysis and mass spectrometry-based proteomics confirmed that rEV contain luminal and membrane-associated proteins, including ALIX, TSG101, flotillin-1, syntenin-1, CD81, and CD9. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering and differential protein analysis further indicated a high degree of similarity in protein composition between rEV and sample EV.

#### Example 2: Identification of rEV Detection Methods

Fluorescence-, RNA-, and protein-based technologies were evaluated for their suitability to quantify rEV and distinguish rEV from sample EV. Fluorescence intensity was sufficient to quantify and distinguish rEV from non-fluorescent particles using fluorescent nanoparticle tracking analysis (fNTA) and fluorescence-triggered high-resolution flow cytometry (HR-FC). Both fNTA and HR-FC measured equivalent relative rEV concentrations.

Fluorescence-based microplate reader, p24 ELISA, western blot, and RT-qPCR were also assessed for rEV quantification. The microplate reader allowed linear deduction of rEV numbers in a defined concentration range of 5 × 10^8 to 1 × 10^10 rEV. The p24 ELISA showed a linear correlation in a defined concentration range of 1 × 10^6 to 1 × 10^7 rEV. Western blot analysis for gag-EGFP protein allowed visualization of rEV with a lower detection limit of 7 × 10^7 rEV, while densitometry analysis revealed a linear correlation in a defined concentration range of 4 × 10^8 to 1 × 10^9 rEV. RT-qPCR analysis of EGFP mRNA concentrations behaved in a semi-logarithmic manner in a range from 1 × 10^7 to 1 × 10^9 rEV.

#### Example 3: Identification of Optimized Storage Conditions of rEV

The structural and biological stability of rEV under different storage conditions was assessed. Freezing at −80 °C for 6 and 12 months, as well as storage at 4 °C for up to 7 days, did not affect the density, fluorescence intensity, number, and size distribution of rEV, as evaluated by ODG centrifugation and fNTA. Immunoprecipitation with an antibody recognizing the extravesicular loop of the tetraspanin CD81 remained unaffected following a freeze-thaw cycle, indicating the correct orientation of the rEV membrane.

Lyophilization of rEV in PBS supplemented with 5% trehalose did not affect their morphology, fluorescence intensity, number, size distribution, and density, as assessed by TEM, HR-FC, fNTA, and ODG centrifugation. The presence of 5% trehalose was necessary and sufficient to maintain rEV integrity, as its absence resulted in reduced rEV numbers, increased size, and membrane disruption.

#### Example 4: The Use of rEV to Estimate the Efficiency of EV Isolation Methods

To assess the performance of EV separation methods, 5 × 10^10 rEV were spiked into PBS, non-cell exposed culture medium (DMEM), plasma, and urine, followed by size-, immune affinity-, and density-based separation of rEV from corresponding biofluids. Western blot analysis for gag-EGFP protein and EV-associated proteins revealed that rEV elute in identical 1 mL elution volume fractions as sample EV upon size exclusion chromatography (SEC). Similar to sample EV from urine and plasma, rEV co-precipitated with antibodies directed against the large extravesicular loop of CD81 or CD63. rEV spiked in urine or culture medium floated at equivalent density (1.086–1.119 g/mL) as sample EV when separated by ODG centrifugation.

However, rEV spiked in plasma shifted to higher densities (1.141–1.215 g/mL) compared to sample EV (1.086–1.119 g/mL). This density shift was reversible upon proteinase K (PK) treatment, which did not affect the fluorescence intensity and integrity of rEV or the number and quality of sample EV. To enable rEV segregation from sample EV in any biofluid using any separation method, rEV were modified by post-insertion PEGylation to avoid protein corona formation. Surface masking of rEV by DMPE-PEG increased the mean size distribution by 10 nm but did not alter fluorescence intensity, preventing the interaction with corona proteins and providing the unique opportunity for proteinase-independent segregation of spiked rEV from sample EV.

#### Example 5: rEV Allows for an Accurate Comparison of EV Numbers in Plasma from Cancer Patients Versus Healthy Individuals

Sample EV and spiked rEV (2 × 10^10) were separated by ODG centrifugation from 2 mL of plasma of breast cancer patients (n = 26) and sex-matched healthy volunteers (n = 11), and measured by NTA and fNTA, respectively. The average, normalized sample EV concentration per mL plasma was increased by 2.5-fold (1.46 × 10^11 vs 5.83 × 10^10) in breast cancer patients compared to healthy individuals. In contrast, absolute counts did not reveal a significant difference (3.91 × 10^10 vs 2.47 × 10^10). Normalization was independent of the rEV quantification method used, as implementation of a p24 ELISA to quantify rEV provided equivalent results in breast cancer patients compared to healthy individuals.

#### Example 6: Implementing the PSI Sequence to Load EGFP mRNA in rEV

To enhance the functionality of rEV, the PSI (packaging signal) sequence was introduced to facilitate the efficient loading of EGFP mRNA into rEV. The PSI sequence, located in the 5' untranslated region of the HIV-1 genome, is essential for the encapsidation of viral RNA into VLP. By incorporating the PSI sequence into the gag-EGFP construct, the production of rEV was optimized to ensure high levels of EGFP mRNA encapsulation. This approach not only improves the trackability of rEV but also opens possibilities for customizing rEV with various exogenous RNAs and proteins, expanding their utility in diagnostic and therapeutic applications.

In summary, the present invention provides recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) that serve as a robust and versatile biological reference material for EV research and biomedical applications. The rEV closely mimic the physical and biochemical characteristics of endogenous EV, enabling accurate calibration, normalization, and method development. The invention addresses the critical need for appropriate reference materials in the field of EV research, ensuring rigorous and reliable analysis of sample EV.
