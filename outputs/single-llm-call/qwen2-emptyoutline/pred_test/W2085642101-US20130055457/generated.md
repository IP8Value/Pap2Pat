## FIELD OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present disclosure relates to the field of biotechnology and agriculture, specifically to the genetic engineering of plants to enhance yield and drought tolerance. More particularly, the disclosure pertains to the over-expression of the ZAR1 (Zea mays ARGOS) gene in maize to improve plant growth, organ size, and agronomic traits related to drought stress.

## BACKGROUND OF THE DISCLOSURE

Engineered crops with traits such as insect control and herbicide tolerance have been widely adopted and have successfully protected or stabilized crop yields. However, there is a growing interest in using biotechnology to address complex agronomic traits such as grain yield, drought-stress tolerance, and nitrogen use efficiency. These traits are typically quantitative, controlled by multiple genes with small individual effects, and are influenced by environmental factors.

Traditional breeding has significantly increased maize yield over the decades, but the mechanisms behind this continuous improvement are not fully understood. One approach to enhancing yield and drought tolerance involves the manipulation of genes that control organ size, known as intrinsic yield genes. The ZAR1 (Zea mays ARGOS) gene, a putative maize orthologue of the Arabidopsis ARGOS gene, has been identified as a candidate for improving these traits.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present disclosure provides methods and compositions for enhancing plant growth, organ size, and agronomic traits related to drought stress in maize by over-expressing the ZAR1 gene. Specifically, the disclosure describes the use of recombinant expression cassettes containing the ZAR1 gene to produce transgenic maize plants with improved yield and drought tolerance.

The ZAR1 gene, when over-expressed, increases plant and organ size by promoting cell proliferation rather than cell enlargement. Additionally, the transgenic plants exhibit improved traits related to drought stress, such as reduced tip kernel abortion, anthesis-silking interval (ASI), and barrenness. The transgene's efficacy is influenced by environmental conditions, with significant yield increases observed under hotter and drier environments.

The disclosure also includes methods for producing transgenic maize plants with heterozygous combinations of ZAR1 alleles, which exhibit higher yield increases and more stable performance across different environments compared to plants with single alleles.

The ZAR1 gene is expressed in various tissues, with the highest expression observed in root and pericarp tissues. The nucleic acid molecules of the disclosure can be isolated from natural sources, synthesized, or modified to optimize expression in maize. ### Recombinant Expression Cassettes

Recombinant expression cassettes of the present disclosure are designed to drive the expression of the ZAR1 gene in maize. The expression cassettes typically include a promoter operably linked to the ZAR1 coding sequence and a terminator sequence. The promoter can be constitutive, inducible, or tissue-specific, depending on the desired expression pattern.

For constitutive expression, the maize ubiquitin promoter (ProZmUBI) is a suitable choice. Other constitutive promoters, such as the cauliflower mosaic virus 35S promoter (CaMV 35S), can also be used. Inducible promoters, such as those responsive to environmental stress or chemical inducers, can be employed to control ZAR1 expression under specific conditions. Tissue-specific promoters, such as those active in roots or leaves, can be used to target ZAR1 expression to specific tissues.

The expression cassettes can also include additional regulatory elements, such as enhancers, to increase expression levels. For example, the CaMV 35S enhancer can be incorporated upstream of the promoter to enhance ZAR1 expression.

### Expression of Proteins in Host Cells

The recombinant expression cassettes are introduced into host cells, such as maize embryogenic callus, using standard transformation methods. Agrobacterium-mediated transformation is a preferred method for introducing the expression cassettes into maize cells. Alternatively, biolistic transformation methods, such as particle bombardment, can be used.

Transformed cells are selected using selectable markers, such as antibiotic resistance genes. The transformed cells are then cultured under conditions that promote regeneration into whole plants. The resulting transgenic plants are characterized for the presence and expression of the ZAR1 gene using molecular and biochemical techniques, such as PCR, RT-PCR, and Western blotting.

Plants that express the ZAR1 gene at high levels are selected for further characterization and field testing.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

#### Construction of Recombinant Expression Cassette

A recombinant expression cassette was constructed to constitutively express the ZAR1 gene in maize. The maize ubiquitin promoter (ProZmUBI) was cloned upstream of the ZAR1 coding sequence, and the PINII terminator was cloned downstream. The expression cassette was inserted into a binary vector, pCAMBIA1301, which also contained the nptII gene for kanamycin resistance.

### Example 2

#### Agrobacterium-Mediated Transformation

The binary vector containing the ZAR1 expression cassette was introduced into Agrobacterium strain LBA4404. Maize embryos from the inbred line Hi-II were transformed with the Agrobacterium strain using standard protocols. Transformed embryos were selected on medium containing 50 mg/L kanamycin. Selected embryos were cultured on regeneration medium to produce transgenic plants.

### Example 3

#### Molecular Characterization of Transgenic Plants

Transgenic plants were analyzed for the presence of the ZAR1 gene using PCR. Genomic DNA was extracted from leaf tissue, and PCR was performed using primers specific to the ZAR1 coding sequence. The presence of the ZAR1 gene was confirmed by the amplification of a 1.5 kb fragment.

### Example 4

#### Expression Analysis of ZAR1 Gene

The expression of the ZAR1 gene in transgenic plants was analyzed using RT-PCR. Total RNA was extracted from leaf tissue, and cDNA was synthesized using reverse transcriptase. RT-PCR was performed using primers specific to the ZAR1 coding sequence. The expression of the ZAR1 gene was normalized to the expression of the endogenous reference gene eIF4g.

### Example 5

#### Phenotypic Analysis of Transgenic Plants

Transgenic plants were grown in the greenhouse and field to evaluate their phenotypic characteristics. Plant height, internode length, leaf area, and biomass were measured. Transgenic plants over-expressing the ZAR1 gene showed increased plant height, primarily due to increased upper internode length. The transgenic plants also had larger leaves and increased biomass compared to non-transgenic controls.

### Example 6

#### Drought Stress Tolerance

Transgenic plants were subjected to drought stress conditions to evaluate their tolerance. Plants were grown under controlled conditions and subjected to water deprivation during the grain-filling stage. Transgenic plants over-expressing the ZAR1 gene showed reduced tip kernel abortion, anthesis-silking interval (ASI), and barrenness compared to non-transgenic controls. The transgenic plants also had higher grain yield under drought stress conditions.

### Example 7

#### Yield Testing

Transgenic maize hybrid plants over-expressing the ZAR1 gene were yield-tested in multiple field locations across the U.S. Central Corn Belt. The transgenic hybrids exhibited significant yield increases in hotter and drier environments (Temperate Dry and Temperate environments) but showed yield reductions in cooler and wetter environments (Temperate Humid and High Latitude environments). The yield data indicated that the ZAR1 transgene's efficacy is influenced by environmental conditions.

### Example 8

#### Natural Variation of ZAR1 Alleles

The natural variation of the ZAR1 gene in maize breeding germplasm was investigated. Two major alleles, SS1 and NS1, were identified and characterized. The SS1 allele was predominantly found in Stiff Stalk (SS) inbred lines, while the NS1 allele was predominantly found in Non Stiff Stalk (NS) inbred lines. The two alleles differed in their promoter and protein coding regions, leading to differential expression and protein structure.

### Example 9

#### Heterozygous Combination of ZAR1 Alleles

Vectors were designed to test the transgenic heterozygous combination of the SS1 and NS1 alleles of ZAR1. Transgenic plants with the molecularly stacked heterozygous alleles (SS1+NS1) were produced and yield-tested in multiple field locations. The transgenic plants with the heterozygous allele stack showed a significant yield increase in more locations and had a more stable performance across different environments compared to plants with single alleles. The data indicated that the heterozygous combination of ZAR1 alleles enhances yield and stability.
