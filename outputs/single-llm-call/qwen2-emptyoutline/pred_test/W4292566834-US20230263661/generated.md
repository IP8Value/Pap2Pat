# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNOLOGICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of ophthalmology and, more specifically, to a method and device for noninvasively manipulating and controlling the movement of particulate matter within the anterior chamber of the eye using acoustic waves. The invention provides a novel approach to managing and preventing complications associated with the presence of particulate matter in the aqueous humor (AH), such as open-angle glaucoma, corneal staining, and visual axis obstruction.

## BACKGROUND

Aqueous humor (AH) is a clear, low-viscosity fluid that plays a crucial role in maintaining the health and function of the eye. It is secreted by the ciliary body into the posterior chamber and flows to the anterior chamber, where it is drained into the systemic cardiovascular circulation. The proper circulation and drainage of AH are essential for the vitalization and protection of non-vascularized tissues such as the trabecular meshwork (TM), lens, and corneal endothelium. Disruptions in the synthesis, circulation, or drainage of AH can lead to various ocular pathologies, including glaucoma, which is characterized by elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) and can result in irreversible vision loss.

Particulate matter of varying shapes, sizes, and characteristics can form or disperse into the AH due to various conditions. These particles can obscure the visual axis, cause secondary complications such as open-angle glaucoma, and deposit in the corneal endothelium, leading to Krukenberg’s spindle or corneal staining. Despite the availability of medical and surgical treatments for these complications, effective preventive options are limited. Current clinical practices often involve a "wait and see" approach, where treatment is initiated only after secondary complications arise.

Acoustic waves have been utilized in medicine for various applications, including imaging and tissue displacement. High-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) is used for tissue-destructive purposes, such as thermic ablation of the ciliary body to treat glaucoma. However, the use of acoustic waves to control and manipulate intraocular particles has not been extensively explored. The present invention addresses this gap by introducing a method and device for noninvasively controlling the movement of particulate matter within the anterior chamber using acoustic radiation forces.

## GENERAL DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a method and device for noninvasively manipulating and controlling the movement of particulate matter within the anterior chamber of the eye using acoustic waves. The method involves generating standing acoustic waves within the anterior chamber, which drive the particles to specific nodal areas where they can be concentrated, organized, or removed. The device includes an acoustic transducer that can be placed in close proximity to the eye and activated to generate the acoustic waves.

The invention offers several advantages over existing methods for managing particulate matter in the eye. It is noninvasive, reducing the risk of complications associated with surgical interventions. The method can be used to clear the visual axis, protect the trabecular meshwork from particle accumulation, and prevent the development of secondary glaucoma. Additionally, the technique has potential applications in targeted drug delivery, differential diagnosis of intraocular particles, and the implantation of injected endothelial corneal cells.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

### Acoustic Transducer

The acoustic transducer used in the present invention is a Type-II (lead zirconate titanate (PZT)) acoustic radial resonator with specific dimensions and properties. The resonator has an inner diameter of 22 mm, an outer diameter of 26 mm, and a length of 20 mm. It is designed to operate at a center resonance frequency of 1.1 ± 0.1 MHz. The resonator is driven by a signal generator capable of producing a continuous sine wave with an amplitude ranging from 1 to 10 volts peak-to-peak (Vpp) and a resistance of 50 Ohms. A power amplifier is used to amplify the signal to a maximum of 20 Vpp, resulting in an effective transducer intensity of 20–80 mW/cm².

To ensure biocompatibility and prevent direct contact with the eye, the resonator is coated with a biocompatible soft coating. The coating is made of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) and is applied by dipping the resonator in a Sylgard TM184 silicon elastomer base and a curing agent mixed at a 10:1 weight ratio. The curing process is performed at 80°C for 2 hours, resulting in a coating thickness of approximately 70 µm.

### Particulate Matter

The method of the present invention can be applied to various types of particulate matter found in the anterior chamber of the eye. These particles can be categorized into pathologic and therapeutic particulate matter. Pathologic particulate matter includes porcine pigment particles, which are created by homogenizing iris tissues retrieved from enucleated porcine eyes. These particles range in size from approximately 50 µm to sub-micron particles and have a density greater than 1 g/cm³. Therapeutic particulate matter includes triamcinolone acetonide (TA), a pharmacologic treatment with a milky appearance that is easy to visualize in contrast to the dark brown iris of pig eyes. TA particles have an average size of 11.5 µm and a density of 1.3 ± 0.1 g/cm³. Both types of particles have a positive acoustic contrast factor in water and are expected to be driven towards the nodes of the acoustic wave.

### In Vitro Model

An in vitro model was developed to evaluate and quantify the effect of the acoustic field on the micro particles. The piezoelectric cylinder was adhered to a glass plate using high vacuum silicon grease, and the reservoir was filled with 1.5 mL of deionized water containing 1.2 mg/mL of TA. An optical microscope equipped with a CMOS camera was positioned below the reservoir to capture and record pattern formations after activation of the acoustic wave. The widths of the ring structures were measured at various time intervals (50, 70, 110, 150, 180, and 240 seconds) at three different wave amplitudes (2.5 Vpp, 5 Vpp, and 10 Vpp).

### Ex Vivo Model

An ex vivo model using enucleated porcine eyes was employed to further evaluate the safety and effectiveness of the acoustic manipulation technique. Twelve porcine eyes were retrieved from a local abattoir within 4 hours postmortem and fixed in place using a custom-cut Styrofoam holder. The piezoelectric resonator was placed securely on the eye, and normal saline solution was poured into the resonator to cover the eye completely.

Six eyes were used for safety evaluation, with two serving as controls. Acoustic waves generated with amplitudes of 10 and 20 Vpp were applied for durations of 1 and 5 minutes. The safety of the technique was assessed using multiple methods, including gross evaluation by an optical microscope, thermal imaging to measure temperature changes, optical coherence tomography (OCT) scans to detect signs of stromal changes, and histologic evaluation of the eyes after immersion in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 hours.

The remaining six eyes were used to determine the effect of the acoustic wave on intraocular micro particles. TA or iris pigment particles were injected into the anterior segment of each eye through a clear corneal incision with a 30G needle before placing the piezoelectric resonator. An optical microscope equipped with a CMOS camera was positioned above the apparatus to capture and record the pattern formations within the anterior chamber after activation of the acoustic wave.

### Results

#### In Vitro

Without the acoustic waves, the TA particles dispersed in the cylindrical resonator sedimented over time and covered the glass slide in a homogeneous manner. Upon introduction of the acoustic waves, the particles formed concentric rings corresponding to the nodal areas of the standing acoustic wave. The mean distance between the rings was 0.75 ± 0.1 mm. The thickness of the rings increased over time as the TA particles sedimented and aggregated at the nodal areas. Stronger acoustic fields (higher amplitudes) resulted in narrower and denser rings.

#### Ex Vivo

Without acoustic waves, the particles sedimented over time to form a homogeneous layer over the iris and retina. Upon acoustic manipulation, both the pigment and TA particles in the anterior chamber were rearranged into concentric rings similar to those observed in the in vitro model. The pigment particles arranged more slowly than the TA particles due to their smaller size. The concentric rings remained stable after deactivation of the acoustic wave, with minimal changes observed after more than 1 hour.

Safety evaluations showed no macroscopic or microscopic signs of tissue damage during or immediately after activation of the acoustic wave, even at the highest energy levels (20 Vpp). Thermal imaging measurements indicated a total temperature increase of less than 0.5°C after 60 seconds at 10 Vpp and 1.5–2.5°C after 60 seconds at 20 Vpp. OCT scans and histologic evaluations revealed no adverse effects on the corneal endothelium, stroma, or other anterior segment tissues.

### Discussion

The present invention demonstrates the feasibility and safety of using acoustic manipulation to control the movement of particulate matter within the anterior chamber of the eye. The method leverages the principles of standing acoustic waves, which drive particles with a positive acoustic contrast factor to nodal areas where they can be concentrated and organized. This noninvasive technique has the potential to address various ocular conditions caused by the presence of particulate matter, such as visual axis obstruction, open-angle glaucoma, and corneal staining.

The acoustic manipulation technique is particularly advantageous because it operates at intensities below the threshold for diagnostic ultrasound in ophthalmology, ensuring minimal thermal and mechanical damage to ocular tissues. The method can be tailored to specific applications by adjusting the amplitude of the acoustic waves, which affects the speed and density of the formed structures.

Potential clinical applications of the invention include clearing the visual axis, protecting the trabecular meshwork from particle accumulation, and preventing the development of secondary glaucoma. The technique can also facilitate targeted drug delivery, assist in the differential diagnosis of intraocular particles, and aid in the implantation of injected endothelial corneal cells. Future studies will focus on optimizing the technique for in vivo use and exploring its broader applications in ophthalmology.

### Conclusion

The present invention provides a novel method and device for noninvasively manipulating and controlling the movement of particulate matter within the anterior chamber of the eye using acoustic waves. The invention offers a safe and effective alternative to current treatment options for managing ocular conditions associated with particulate matter. Further research and development are expected to expand the scope and utility of this innovative technique in ophthalmic care.
