# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of microbiology and immunology, specifically to the identification and characterization of specific regions on the surface of the urease enzyme of *Helicobacter pylori* (H. pylori) that are crucial for the bacterium's colonization and persistence in the host. The invention provides methods and compositions for altering these regions to modulate the bacterium's ability to establish and maintain chronic infections, which can lead to the development of new therapeutic strategies and diagnostic tools.

## BACKGROUND

*Helicobacter pylori* (H. pylori) is a gram-negative bacterium that chronically infects the gastric mucosa of a significant portion of the global population. It is a major cause of peptic ulcer disease and is implicated as an early critical risk factor for gastric cancer, one of the most frequently lethal malignancies in humans. One of the key virulence factors of H. pylori is urease, an abundant enzyme that generates ammonia and carbonate from urea, thereby reducing the acidity of the bacterium's immediate environment. This acid-neutralizing activity is essential for H. pylori to survive and colonize the acidic environment of the stomach.

However, recent studies have suggested that urease has additional roles beyond acid neutralization. These roles include the synthesis of essential metabolites, protection from reactive nitrogen species, enhanced survival in macrophages, evasion of phagocytosis, and modulation of host immune responses. For instance, urease can activate macrophages, monocytes, and blood platelets, dysregulate gastric epithelial tight junctions, and induce cytokine production from gastric epithelial cells through interactions with the CD74 (MHC class II invariant chain) receptor.

The urease enzyme in H. pylori is a dodecamer composed of UreA and UreB subunits, forming a ball-like supramolecular structure. The surface properties of this enzyme are believed to contribute to its acid stability and host interactions. The present invention focuses on the identification of specific regions on the urease surface that are critical for H. pylori's colonization and persistence in the host, and the development of methods to alter these regions to modulate the bacterium's pathogenicity.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for identifying and characterizing specific regions on the surface of the urease enzyme of *Helicobacter pylori* (H. pylori) that are crucial for the bacterium's colonization and persistence in the host. The invention further provides methods and compositions for altering these regions to modulate the bacterium's ability to establish and maintain chronic infections.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method for generating H. pylori strains with in-frame insertions at specific sites in the urease gene. These insertions are designed to introduce epitope tags into the urease enzyme without significantly affecting its enzymatic activity. The method involves the following steps:
1. Analyzing the three-dimensional structure of the urease complex to identify surface regions that may tolerate the insertion of epitope tags.
2. Generating H. pylori strains with in-frame insertions of DNA encoding epitope tags at the identified sites using PCR and transformation techniques.
3. Selecting H. pylori strains that express functional urease enzymes with the introduced epitope tags.
4. Testing the ability of the modified H. pylori strains to colonize and persist in a murine infection model.

In another aspect, the invention provides a method for assessing the impact of specific urease surface regions on H. pylori's colonization and persistence. The method involves the following steps:
1. Inoculating mice with H. pylori strains expressing wild-type or mutant urease enzymes.
2. Monitoring the bacterial load in the mouse stomachs over time to assess colonization and persistence.
3. Measuring the levels of anti-H. pylori antibodies in the serum to evaluate the immune response.

In yet another aspect, the invention provides compositions comprising H. pylori strains with altered urease surface regions. These compositions can be used for various applications, including:
1. Developing new therapeutic strategies to prevent or treat H. pylori infections.
2. Creating diagnostic tools to detect and monitor H. pylori infections.
3. Studying the molecular mechanisms underlying H. pylori's pathogenicity and host interactions.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS OF THE INVENTION

### Isolation of Clinical Strains

The invention utilizes *Helicobacter pylori* (H. pylori) strain X47, which is resistant to streptomycin. This strain was used for all experiments described herein. H. pylori was grown on Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) based agar plates supplemented with 5% horse blood and, when necessary, with erythromycin (10 µg/mL) or streptomycin (10 µg/mL) in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. To select for H. pylori strains producing functional urease, BHI based agar plates were supplemented with 7% (v/v) horse serum, phenol red (100 mg/L), and urea (600 mg/L), and the molten agar was acidified to pH 5 using 1M HCl.

### Example 2

To identify surface regions of the urease enzyme that could tolerate the insertion of epitope tags, the three-dimensional structure of the H. pylori urease complex was reconstructed from deposited coordinates (PDB-id: 1e9y) by applying crystallographic symmetry operators. The urease complex, consisting of 12 UreA and 12 UreB polypeptide chains, was visually inspected for surface-exposed loops showing high thermal mobility, indicating that they might tolerate insertions of epitope tags. The average temperature factors for main chain atoms of the identified sites were calculated and compared to the average temperature factors for main chain atoms of UreA and UreB.

### Example 3

H. pylori strains with in-frame insertions of DNA encoding epitope tags at the identified sites were generated using PCR and transformation techniques. The rpsL,ermB cassette, flanked by regions for homologous recombination at the urease locus, was produced using splicing by overlap extension PCR (SOE PCR). The cassette was then inserted at the desired sites in the chromosomal ureA or ureB genes of a ΔureAB-rpsL.erm derivative of H. pylori strain X47 using natural transformation and homologous recombination. DNA encoding HA epitopes (amino acids 150–159 and 110–120 of Influenza virus A/PR/8/34 hemagglutinin protein) and a FLAG tag were similarly fused to regions of DNA for homologous recombination and transferred to modified H. pylori, replacing the rpsL.ermB counter-selection cassette.

### Example 4

H. pylori strains expressing functional urease enzymes with the introduced epitope tags were selected from pools of transformant bacteria by streaking on acidified media supplemented with the urease substrate, urea. The pH of the mouse stomach lumen, which H. pylori must traverse to establish gastric mucosal infection, is between 3 and 4. To determine if the insertions in the urease surface exposed loops altered its activity or stability, enzymatic activity was assayed in bacteria expressing wild-type or mutant urease after exposure to acid (pH 3). One of the mutant ureases (insertion at site 1) was more sensitive than wild type, and the three other mutant ureases were similar to wild type in their sensitivity to this acid treatment.

### Example 5

The ability of each mutant urease-producing H. pylori strain to colonize C57BL/6 mice was tested. The strain with site 3-mutant urease did not colonize mice and was not studied further. Each of the other three mutant strains colonized mice as efficiently as the wild-type in short-term (<3 months) infections. However, persistence of H. pylori with site 8-mutant urease was greatly reduced over longer periods of time. In confirmation, bacterial titres in mouse stomachs and anti-H. pylori IgG in serum were each much reduced relative to wild type and the other two mutants in the case of ten-month infection by this strain. H. pylori expressing urease containing the recombinant at site 8 insertion, which was present in infected animals at 3 months of infection at bacterial loads similar to that of bacteria expressing wild-type urease, exhibited a weaker humoral response against the urease B subunit (UreB) than did the wild-type strain, but a normal response against total H. pylori antigen.

### Example 6

Further tests showed that nearly all H. pylori recovered from mice 10 months after inoculation still expressed the expected mutant urease, which demonstrated that this persistent colonization was not due to loss of inserted DNAs. As the sole exception, just one mouse initially infected with the site 1 mutant strain seemed to produce revertant urease at ten months. Reduced urease activity measured in vitro did not correlate well with reduced colonization ability. For example, the site 1 mutant strain, whose urease was less acid stable than the others, colonized mice as well as wild-type, and the site 8 mutant, whose urease activity was similar in acid resistance to that of wild type, was nevertheless less persistent in mouse colonization.

### Example 7

The urease complex is multifunctional. The surface properties of this protein, distinct from the ureolytic activity per se, were found to be important for H. pylori colonization and persistence. We hypothesize that the modification of the urease surface at site 8 compromises CD74-mediated immune modulation, reduces the pro-inflammatory response elicited by the epithelial cells, or increases the Th1 bias via the dendritic cells. Alternatively, other receptors or yet unidentified mechanisms might be involved in the urease surface-mediated H. pylori persistence. Given the many failed efforts to develop anti-H. pylori vaccines to date, a better molecular understanding of factors important in persistence should contribute to the development of new, much-needed therapeutic approaches.

### Example 8

The methods and compositions provided by the present invention can be used to develop new therapeutic strategies to prevent or treat H. pylori infections. For example, H. pylori strains with altered urease surface regions can be used to create attenuated strains for vaccine development. Additionally, the identification of specific urease surface regions involved in host interactions can lead to the development of small molecules or antibodies that target these regions to inhibit H. pylori's ability to colonize and persist in the host. Furthermore, the methods and compositions can be used to create diagnostic tools to detect and monitor H. pylori infections, providing valuable information for clinical management and patient care.
