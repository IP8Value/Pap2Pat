## BACKGROUND
The three-dimensional structure of proteins can be studied in solution using various methods of covalent and noncovalent labeling, collectively known as protein ‘footprinting’. These methods typically involve measuring the solvent accessibility of either the peptide backbone (hydrogen deuterium exchange) or amino acid side chains (covalent modification and cross-linking reagents). After labeling, proteins are analyzed using tandem mass spectrometry (MS), and linkages or adducts are quantitatively mapped to specific regions, revealing areas and degrees of intermolecular interactions or solvent accessibility. The emergence of the protein therapeutics industry has generated a need for easy, sensitive, and reproducible methods for detecting changes in protein conformation, as part of the rigorous quality control process required for clinical use and for quickly detecting the regions of proteins involved in their binding to ligands and/or protein-protein interactions.

One of the more prevalent means of noncovalent protein footprinting is Hydrogen Deuterium Exchange (HDX), which utilizes deuterium exchanged with hydrogen atoms along the peptide backbone to measure solvent accessibility. HDX is widely used, and automated systems have been developed to improve throughput and reproducibility. However, HDX is technically challenging: time-sensitive back-exchange between deuterium and naturally abundant hydrogen occurs, so the technique must be rapidly performed at very low temperatures. In addition, HDX relies on acidic conditions for quenching the exchange reaction, necessitating the use of the non-specific protease, pepsin, for digestion. The solvent accessibility information obtained is tied to the peptide backbone, rather than to amino acid side chains, and while important, may not provide a complete picture of the protein’s three-dimensional state.

Protein footprinting performed with chemical reagents results in covalent modification of amino acid side chains. Several reagents are available for this purpose, such as EDC/GEE for carboxyl labeling, trypsin-based methods for detecting changes in accessibility to proteolytic enzymes, highly reactive carbenes, and hydroxyl radicals. Hydroxyl radical footprinting (HRF) for protein structure analysis is mainly performed using two well-known methods: Fast Photochemical Oxidation of Proteins (FPOP) and synchrotron-based radiolysis of water. FPOP uses a 248 nm excimer laser to generate radicals from hydrogen peroxide added to a sample prior to exposure. Synchrotron experiments generate hydroxyl radicals via radiolysis of water and do not involve added chemicals, but they require access to major synchrotron radiation source facilities and labeling occurs on a millisecond timescale. FPOP can be employed on a benchtop scale but requires hydrogen peroxide and exposure to a 248 nm laser, which can potentially perturb protein structure.

In this context, the present invention provides a novel technique termed PLIMB (Plasma-Induced Modification of Biomolecules), which performs HRF using a plasma to generate microsecond (μs) pulses of hydroxyl radicals from water in solution. This technique addresses the limitations of existing methods by being benchtop-friendly, applicable to a range of protein concentrations and sizes, and generating μs bursts of hydroxyl radicals without added chemicals.

## SUMMARY
The present invention relates to a method and system for performing protein footprinting using plasma-induced modification of biomolecules (PLIMB). Specifically, the invention involves generating μs pulses of hydroxyl radicals from water in solution using a plasma, and using these radicals to label proteins for solvent accessibility measurements. The method includes the steps of generating a plasma, directing the plasma through a liquid sample containing the protein of interest, and analyzing the labeled protein using mass spectrometry to determine solvent accessibility. The invention also encompasses kits and compositions of matter for performing the method.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION
### Methods
The present invention provides a method for performing protein footprinting using plasma-induced modification of biomolecules (PLIMB). The method involves generating μs pulses of hydroxyl radicals from water in solution using a plasma and using these radicals to label proteins for solvent accessibility measurements. The method includes the following steps:

1. **Generating a Plasma**: An instrument is constructed that generates a plasma using a dielectric-barrier discharge. The instrument includes a grounded cooling plate and a needle fixed approximately 1 mm above the surface of the sample. A time-varying high voltage signal is applied to the needle to create a plasma. The system can be operated over a range of high voltages from 1 kV to 31 kV and a range of frequencies from 0 to 15 kHz. Between pulses, there is sufficient time for radicals to recombine and dissipate.

2. **Directing the Plasma Through the Sample**: The plasma is directed through a liquid sample containing the protein of interest. The sample is placed in a 0.25 mL PCR tube, and the volume is 100 μL. The sample is kept on ice prior to and following plasma treatment, and for treatment, it is transferred to a Peltier cooling block set to cool to between 2° and 4°C.

3. **Analyzing the Labeled Protein**: After plasma treatment, the protein is analyzed using mass spectrometry to determine solvent accessibility. For example, the protein can be precipitated with trichloroacetic acid (TCA), resolubilized, and digested with trypsin and Lys-C. The resulting peptides are desalted and concentrated using Omix tips, and the peptides are analyzed using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS).

### Systems
The present invention also provides a system for performing protein footprinting using PLIMB. The system includes:

1. **Plasma Generator**: An instrument that generates a plasma using a dielectric-barrier discharge. The instrument includes a grounded cooling plate and a needle fixed approximately 1 mm above the surface of the sample. A time-varying high voltage signal is applied to the needle to create a plasma. The system can be operated over a range of high voltages from 1 kV to 31 kV and a range of frequencies from 0 to 15 kHz.

2. **Sample Holder**: A device for holding the liquid sample containing the protein of interest. The sample holder is designed to maintain the sample at a low temperature (2° to 4°C) during plasma treatment.

3. **Mass Spectrometer**: An instrument for analyzing the labeled protein. The mass spectrometer can be an electrospray ionization time-of-flight (ESI-TOF) mass spectrometer or an Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometer.

### Compositions of Matter
The present invention also provides compositions of matter for performing protein footprinting using PLIMB. The compositions include:

1. **Buffer Solutions**: Buffer solutions for maintaining the pH and ionic strength of the liquid sample. For example, 50 mM ammonium acetate or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) can be used.

2. **Proteases**: Enzymes for digesting the protein into peptides. For example, trypsin and Lys-C can be used.

3. **Desalting and Concentration Reagents**: Reagents for desalting and concentrating the peptides. For example, Omix tips can be used.

### Kits

## EXAMPLE 1

### Labeling Cytochrome C
Cytochrome C, a well-characterized protein, was used to test the PLIMB method. Cytochrome C was dissolved in 50 mM ammonium acetate at a concentration of 50 μM and treated with a dose curve of plasma from 0 to 60 seconds. The treated samples were analyzed using ESI-TOF mass spectrometry. The results showed that cytochrome C was labeled in a dose-dependent manner, with the appearance of modified products linearly increasing with plasma exposure time. This demonstrated that PLIMB effectively labels proteins in solution.

## EXAMPLE 2

### Labeling Bovine Serum Albumin
Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) was used to further test the PLIMB method. BSA was dissolved in PBS at a concentration of 50 μM and treated with plasma for 0, 15, 30, or 60 seconds. The treated samples were precipitated with TCA, resolubilized, and digested with trypsin and Lys-C. The resulting peptides were desalted and concentrated using Omix tips and analyzed using LC-MS. The results showed that BSA was labeled in a dose-dependent manner, with the appearance of modified products increasing with plasma exposure time. Additionally, the protein structure was maintained throughout the treatment, as evidenced by the lack of significant changes in the peptide map.

## EXAMPLE 3

### Breakdown of DNA in Size-Dependent and Exposure-Dose-Dependent Fashion
To test the effect of PLIMB on nucleic acids, double-stranded DNA was treated with plasma for varying exposure times. The treated DNA was analyzed using gel electrophoresis. The results showed that the DNA was broken down in a size-dependent and exposure-dose-dependent fashion, with longer exposure times resulting in smaller fragments. This demonstrated that PLIMB can be used to study the effects of hydroxyl radicals on nucleic acids.

## EXAMPLE 4

### Protein Labeling in Intact/Live Cells
To test the applicability of PLIMB to live cells, HeLa cells were treated with plasma for varying exposure times. The treated cells were lysed, and the proteins were analyzed using LC-MS. The results showed that proteins in intact cells were labeled in a dose-dependent manner, with the appearance of modified products increasing with plasma exposure time. This demonstrated that PLIMB can be used to study protein conformation and interactions in live cells.

### EXAMPLE 5
To further validate the PLIMB method, the ecto domain of human epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) was treated with plasma before and after binding its ligand, epidermal growth factor (EGF). The treated samples were analyzed using LC-MS. The results showed that specific regions of EGFR exhibited decreased oxidation upon EGF binding, indicating reduced solvent accessibility. This demonstrated that PLIMB can be used to map protein-protein interaction domains and structural changes in solution.

### EXAMPLE 6
To test the sensitivity and reproducibility of the PLIMB method, the same protein (BSA) was treated with plasma multiple times under identical conditions. The treated samples were analyzed using LC-MS. The results showed high reproducibility, with consistent labeling patterns across multiple replicates. This demonstrated that PLIMB is a reliable and reproducible method for protein footprinting.

### EXAMPLE 7
To test the applicability of PLIMB to a wide range of protein concentrations, BSA was treated with plasma at concentrations ranging from 1 μM to 100 μM. The treated samples were analyzed using LC-MS. The results showed that PLIMB effectively labeled proteins across the entire concentration range, demonstrating its versatility and broad applicability.
