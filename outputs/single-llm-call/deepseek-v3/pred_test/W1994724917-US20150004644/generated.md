## DESCRIPTION

Staphylococcus aureus is a Gram-positive bacterium that colonizes human skin and mucosal surfaces, particularly the nasal cavity. This opportunistic pathogen is responsible for a wide range of infections in humans, ranging from minor skin infections to life-threatening conditions such as bacteremia, endocarditis, and toxic shock syndrome. The clinical manifestations of S. aureus infections can be broadly categorized into two types: suppurative infections characterized by abscess formation and toxin-mediated syndromes caused by secreted virulence factors. The pathogenicity of S. aureus is mediated through an array of surface proteins called adhesins that facilitate attachment to host tissues and extracellular matrix components. These adhesion factors include microbial surface components recognizing adhesive matrix molecules (MSCRAMMs) such as fibronectin-binding proteins, collagen-binding protein, and clumping factors.

The toxinic infections caused by S. aureus result from the action of various secreted toxins including alpha-toxin, beta-toxin, gamma-toxin, Panton-Valentine leukocidin, and toxic shock syndrome toxin. The expression of these virulence factors is tightly regulated by multiple global regulatory systems that respond to environmental signals and bacterial cell density. Among these regulation factors, the accessory gene regulator (agr) system plays a central role in coordinating the transition from the expression of surface proteins to secreted toxins during infection. The agr system functions as a quorum-sensing mechanism that detects population density through autoinducing peptides and responds by activating the expression of virulence genes.

The agr system comprises two divergent transcripts, RNAII and RNAIII, driven by the P2 and P3 promoters respectively. The RNAII transcript encodes four essential agr system components: AgrB, AgrD, AgrC, and AgrA. AgrD is the precursor of the autoinducing peptide, while AgrB processes and exports this peptide. AgrC serves as the membrane-bound histidine kinase receptor, and AgrA is the response regulator that activates transcription from P2 and P3 promoters. The RNAIII transcript itself functions as the major effector molecule of the agr system, regulating the expression of multiple target genes through both direct and indirect mechanisms.

One critical function of the agr system is the production of hemolysin δ (delta-toxin), a 26-amino acid peptide encoded within the RNAIII transcript. Hemolysin δ functions as a pore-forming cytolysin that contributes to tissue damage during infection and exhibits synergistic activity with other staphylococcal toxins. The translation of RNAIII into hemolysin δ is subject to delayed initiation due to structural features of the transcript that temporarily sequester the ribosome binding site. Following translation, the nascent hemolysin δ peptide undergoes deformylation by peptide deformylase to generate the mature, functional toxin. The accumulation of hemolysin δ in the bacterial cytoplasm and extracellular environment serves as a marker for agr system functionality.

Natural polymorphisms of hemolysin δ exist among S. aureus strains, with the most common variant containing a glycine-to-serine substitution at position 10 (G10S). Clinical isolates occasionally exhibit complete deficiency in hemolysin δ production due to mutations or deletions affecting the agr locus. These deficient strains of S. aureus occur with notable frequency in certain clinical contexts, particularly in chronic and persistent infections. The clinical relevance of hemolysin δ-deficient strains has been increasingly recognized, as they are associated with distinct pathogenic characteristics and altered responses to antimicrobial therapy.

Epidemiological studies have demonstrated a significant connection between hemolysin δ deficiency and increased mortality in patients with S. aureus bacteremia. The absence of hemolysin δ production is strongly correlated with persistent bacteremia, particularly in patients with indwelling medical devices. At the cellular level, this deficiency is linked to impaired bacterial autolysis, which may contribute to antibiotic tolerance and persistence. Furthermore, hemolysin δ-deficient strains exhibit enhanced biofilm formation capacity, explaining their propensity to colonize medical implants and prosthetic devices.

The association between hemolysin δ deficiency and cystic fibrosis has been particularly well-documented, with these strains frequently isolated from the respiratory tracts of CF patients. Another clinically significant connection exists between hemolysin δ deficiency and the glycopeptide-resistance phenotype, particularly reduced sensitivity to vancomycin. This relationship has important implications for the treatment of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) infections, as vancomycin remains a first-line therapeutic option.

Several methods have been developed for measuring hemolysin δ production in S. aureus strains. The traditional hemolysis test assesses synergistic lysis of erythrocytes in combination with beta-toxin. Biotests on animal models provide in vivo evidence of toxin activity but are impractical for routine clinical use. Molecular techniques such as RT-PCR and Northern Blot targeting RNAIII expression provide indirect measures of hemolysin δ production. Sequencing of the agr locus can identify genetic defects underlying hemolysin δ deficiency, while HPLC techniques enable direct detection of the peptide.

The present invention focuses on a novel method for studying colonies of S. aureus using mass spectrometry technology. This approach employs matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) to detect hemolysin δ directly from bacterial colonies. The technique involves positioning of the sample on a target plate followed by ionization of molecules through laser irradiation. The ionized molecules are then accelerated through an electric field and their time-of-flight to the detector is measured. By calculating the mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) of detected ions, the presence or absence of hemolysin δ can be determined with high specificity.

The decision regarding agr system functionality is issued based on the mass spectrometry results, with the absence of hemolysin δ peaks indicating agr dysfunction. This finding can be correlated with several clinically relevant parameters: agr system dysfunction is associated with specific clinical diagnoses, particularly chronic infections. The absence of hemolysin δ also serves as a marker for reduced sensitivity to glycopeptides, providing early warning of potential treatment failure.

The sample preparation and detection protocol for this method involves minimal processing of bacterial colonies. The MALDI-TOF MS method employs matrix-assisted desorption-ionization to generate ions from intact bacterial cells. Sample preparation involves mixing bacterial material with matrix compounds such as α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid or sinapinic acid. Various deposition techniques can be used, including the dried droplet method or smear method, depending on the specific instrumentation. The matrix properties are optimized to facilitate desorption and ionization of bacterial proteins while minimizing fragmentation.

The laser beam properties are carefully controlled to ensure consistent ionization across samples. The ionization process generates predominantly singly charged ions, simplifying mass spectrum interpretation. Following ionization, the accelerated ions travel through a flight tube under vacuum, with their time-of-flight proportional to the square root of their mass-to-charge ratio. The detector records the ion signal intensity at specific m/z values, generating a mass spectrum that serves as a molecular fingerprint of the bacterial sample.

For bacterial identification, the mass spectrum is compared against reference databases containing spectra from known microorganisms. The MALDI-TOF-TOF MS method provides additional analytical power by enabling tandem mass spectrometry. This technique involves selection of specific ions from the initial mass spectrum, followed by fragmentation through collision-induced dissociation. The resulting fragment ions are then accelerated through a second flight tube and detected, providing structural information about the selected precursor ion.

The MALDI-TOF-TOF mass spectrometer comprises several key components: an ionization source, an accelerator, a flight tube maintained under reduced pressure, a mass analyzer, and a detector. Various fragmentation types can be employed, including post-source decay and collision cell fragmentation. For protein identification, the fragmentation patterns are analyzed using specialized software that matches observed fragments to theoretical cleavage products.

When applied to hemolysin δ analysis, this method detects characteristic y and b ion fragments generated from the peptide. The wild-type hemolysin δ produces a specific fragmentation pattern, while the G10S variant generates a distinct set of y and b ion fragments. The MALDI-TOF MS method thus provides a comprehensive approach for assessing hemolysin δ production and agr system functionality in S. aureus clinical isolates.

## EXAMPLES

### 1. Equipment

The matrix used in this method consists of α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid dissolved in a solvent mixture of acetonitrile, water, and trifluoroacetic acid. The targets for sample deposition are stainless steel plates with defined positions for sample application. The internal control for mass calibration includes purified hemolysin δ and synthetic peptide standards. The MALDI TOF mass spectrometer is equipped with a nitrogen laser operating at 337 nm wavelength and a reflectron-type time-of-flight analyzer.

### 2. Methodology

The preanalytical parameters include standardized bacterial growth conditions using trypticase soy agar with horse blood incubated at 35°C for 24 hours. Analytical parameters encompass laser intensity optimization, mass range selection (2800-3200 m/z), and signal accumulation from multiple laser shots. Data acquisition is performed in positive ion mode with delayed extraction to enhance mass resolution.

### 3. Results

Validation studies demonstrate reliable detection of hemolysin δ at 3005±5 m/z for wild-type strains and 3035±5 m/z for G10S variants. Intermediate repeatability tests show consistent results across multiple operators and instruments. Precision data indicate minimal variation in peak intensity measurements between technical replicates. Evaluation of culture media effects reveals no significant impact on hemolysin δ detection.

Analysis of a prospective collection of clinical strains identifies 139/168 isolates producing wild-type hemolysin δ, 12/168 producing the G10S variant, and 17/168 deficient in hemolysin δ production. MALDI TOF/TOF analysis confirms the identity of both hemolysin δ forms through characteristic fragmentation patterns. The peak at 3035±5 m/z corresponds to the G10S variant, while the 3005±5 m/z peak represents wild-type hemolysin δ.

Among 17 hemolysin δ-deficient strains, all demonstrate agr dysfunction by Northern blot analysis. Clinical correlation reveals significant association between hemolysin δ deficiency and chronic infections (p=0.005). No significant difference is observed between methicillin-susceptible (MSSA) and methicillin-resistant (MRSA) strains in hemolysin δ production. Analysis of implant-associated infections shows no increased prevalence of hemolysin δ-deficient strains.

Screening for glycopeptide-intermediate S. aureus (GISA) identifies five strains, four of which are hemolysin δ-deficient. Expanded analysis of 28 GISA/hGISA strains confirms significant association between agr dysfunction and reduced glycopeptide susceptibility (p=0.001). Multivariate analysis demonstrates independent associations between hemolysin δ deficiency and both chronic infections (p=0.048) and glycopeptide resistance (p=0.023). These results validate the clinical utility of hemolysin δ detection by MALDI-TOF MS for predicting S. aureus virulence characteristics and antibiotic resistance profiles.
