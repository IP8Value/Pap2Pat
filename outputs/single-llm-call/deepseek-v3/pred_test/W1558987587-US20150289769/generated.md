# DESCRIPTION  

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention relates to the field of cardiac health, specifically to methods and systems for assessing cardiac function, particularly diastolic function, using cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging. The invention provides a novel approach for determining the risk of cardiac failure, including heart failure (HF) and atrial fibrillation (AF), by analyzing myocardial deformation during left ventricular (LV) relaxation. The disclosed method involves the computation of a strain relaxation index (SRI), which integrates early diastolic strain rates and post-systolic strain peaks to provide a comprehensive assessment of diastolic dysfunction.  

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION  

Cardiac health remains a critical area of medical research due to the high prevalence of heart failure and related conditions. Current diagnostic methods for assessing diastolic function primarily rely on echocardiography, which, while useful, has limitations in terms of reproducibility and dependency on operator expertise. Existing CMR-based techniques, such as those measuring circumferential strain, do not fully capture the complex dynamics of myocardial relaxation, particularly during early diastole. Prior art lacks a robust, quantitative index that combines both tissue compliance and relaxation timing to predict cardiac events accurately.  

A significant limitation of conventional CMR methods is their inability to account for the interplay between post-systolic strain and early diastolic strain rates, both of which are critical indicators of diastolic dysfunction. Furthermore, current techniques often fail to normalize strain measurements relative to the cardiac cycle, leading to potential inaccuracies in risk stratification. There is thus a pressing need for an improved diagnostic tool that leverages CMR data to provide a more reliable predictor of cardiac failure risk.  

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention introduces a novel method for assessing cardiac function by computing a strain relaxation index (SRI) derived from CMR imaging. The SRI is calculated as the difference between post-systolic and systolic strain peaks, divided by the early diastolic strain rate peak, and normalized by the total relaxation time. This index provides a quantitative measure of diastolic dysfunction by integrating both myocardial relaxation and tissue compliance.  

One embodiment of the invention involves the use of tagged CMR to acquire mid-ventricular mid-wall circumferential strains and strain rates. The method further includes harmonic phase analysis to derive deformation curves, from which systolic, post-systolic, and early diastolic strain peaks are identified. The SRI is then computed and used to determine the level of risk for cardiac failure, including HF and AF.  

Another embodiment of the invention comprises a system for implementing the method, including a non-transitory computer-readable medium storing executable instructions for SRI computation, a user interface for displaying deformation curves and risk assessments, and an imaging modality for acquiring CMR data. The system is designed to facilitate seamless integration with existing clinical workflows, providing clinicians with a reliable tool for early detection of cardiac dysfunction.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

The present patent application describes a method and system for determining cardiac events, particularly HF and AF, using a novel strain relaxation index (SRI) derived from CMR imaging. The invention addresses the limitations of prior art by providing a comprehensive approach to assessing diastolic function, incorporating both myocardial relaxation dynamics and tissue compliance.  

The system and method involve the acquisition of cardiac images using CMR, followed by analysis of circumferential strain and strain rates. Key parameters include systolic strain peaks, post-systolic strain peaks, and early diastolic strain rate peaks (E peaks). The SRI is calculated as the temporal difference between post-systolic and systolic strain peaks, divided by the E peak amplitude, and normalized by the total relaxation time (RR interval minus systolic interval). This normalization ensures that the index accounts for variations in heart rate, enhancing its diagnostic accuracy.  

Current CMR methods for assessing diastolic function are limited by their inability to capture the full spectrum of myocardial relaxation dynamics. The present invention overcomes this by introducing circumferential strain and strain rates as primary metrics, with particular emphasis on post-systolic strain and early diastolic strain rates. The isovolumic relaxation time (IVRT) is also considered, as it provides additional insight into LV relaxation properties.  

The development of the SRI was motivated by the need for a robust, quantitative measure of diastolic dysfunction that correlates with established echocardiographic parameters, such as E/e' and tissue Doppler e' waves. The SRI algorithm is designed to integrate these parameters into a single index, enabling more accurate risk stratification. A flow diagram illustrating the method is provided, detailing the steps from image acquisition to SRI computation and risk assessment.  

The system components include a CMR imaging device, a processor for executing the SRI algorithm, and a display for visualizing deformation curves and risk scores. The non-transitory computer-readable medium stores instructions for performing harmonic phase analysis, strain rate computation, and SRI derivation. The user interface allows clinicians to interact with the system, adjusting parameters and reviewing results.  

An example study involving participants from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) demonstrates the efficacy of the SRI. The study included 125 participants who underwent simultaneous echo and tagged CMR assessments. Results showed strong correlations between SRI and established diastolic function parameters, including mass-to-volume ratio (MVR) and E/e'. The SRI also exhibited a significant trend across diastolic function ratings, confirming its utility as a predictor of HF and AF.  

In conclusion, the SRI represents a significant advancement in cardiac risk assessment, providing a reliable, CMR-based index for early detection of diastolic dysfunction. Its integration into clinical practice has the potential to improve patient outcomes by enabling timely intervention.  

### EXAMPLE  

An example study was conducted to validate the strain relaxation index (SRI) as a predictor of heart failure (HF) and atrial fibrillation (AF). The study cohort comprised participants from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA), who underwent concurrent echocardiography and tagged CMR imaging. Data acquisition involved harmonic phase analysis of mid-ventricular mid-wall circumferential strains and strain rates.  

The SRI was calculated as the temporal difference between post-systolic and systolic strain peaks, divided by the early diastolic strain rate peak (E peak), and normalized by the total relaxation time. Additional risk factors, including NT-proBNP levels and coronary calcium scores, were also assessed. Event classification was based on predefined criteria for HF and AF, with a combined endpoint used for statistical analysis.  

Statistical methods included Cox regression for hazard ratio estimation, Harrell's C-statistic for model discrimination, and the integrated discrimination index (IDI) and net reclassification index (NRI) for incremental predictive value. Secondary analyses incorporated established risk factors, such as hypertension and diabetes, to evaluate model calibration. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated to illustrate survival probabilities based on SRI tertiles.  

Baseline characteristics of the cohort included a mean age of 61 years, with 41% males and a balanced racial distribution. Over a median follow-up of 10 years, 18 incident HF cases and 22 AF cases were recorded. The SRI demonstrated a strong association with both outcomes, with higher values indicating increased risk. Univariate and multivariate analyses confirmed the independent predictive value of the SRI, with hazard ratios of 1.8 (95% CI: 1.3–2.5) for HF and 1.6 (95% CI: 1.2–2.1) for AF.  

The IDI and NRI results indicated significant improvement in risk prediction when the SRI was added to conventional models. Specifically, the IDI increased by 0.12 (p < 0.01), and the NRI showed a net reclassification improvement of 0.25 (p < 0.05). These findings underscore the clinical utility of the SRI as a standalone or adjunctive tool for cardiac risk assessment.  

In summary, the example study validates the SRI as a robust predictor of HF and AF, offering superior discriminative ability compared to existing biomarkers. Its integration into routine CMR protocols has the potential to enhance early detection and management of cardiac dysfunction.
