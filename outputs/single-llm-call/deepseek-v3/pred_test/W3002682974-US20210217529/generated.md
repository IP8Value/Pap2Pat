# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND  

The field of healthcare technology has advanced significantly with the development of predictive models that assist clinicians in assessing patient risk for adverse medical outcomes. These models analyze patient data to generate risk scores that inform clinical decision-making. However, the reliability of such predictions can vary substantially across different patient subgroups, creating challenges for clinicians who must determine when to trust a model's output. Existing approaches for evaluating prediction reliability either depend on the specific model architecture or require access to the original training data, limiting their practical utility in clinical settings. There remains an unmet need for a model-independent reliability assessment method that can proactively identify unreliable predictions without requiring retraining or access to sensitive patient data.

## SUMMARY  

The present invention relates to systems and methods for assessing the reliability of clinical risk predictions. A clinical decision support system is disclosed that incorporates a reliability assessment module configured to evaluate the trustworthiness of risk scores generated by predictive models. The method involves comparing a patient's risk score from a primary predictive model against an alternative risk estimate derived using generative modeling techniques. Discrepancies between these estimates indicate unreliable predictions where the model's output may be misleading.  

The reliability assessment module operates by first segregating electronic medical record (EMR) data into positive and negative outcome sets. Generative models are trained on each subset to characterize the distribution of patient features associated with different outcomes. These models are used to sample synthetic patient data and compute likelihood ratios that inform an alternative risk estimate. The absolute difference between the primary risk score and this alternative estimate quantifies prediction unreliability.  

Several embodiments and variations are disclosed, including implementations using neuromorphic systems, neural networks, and cloud computing architectures. The system may modify clinician interfaces to display reliability indicators, issue alerts for unreliable predictions, and facilitate additional review for high-risk patients. Performance evaluations demonstrate the method's effectiveness in identifying patient subgroups where conventional risk models exhibit reduced accuracy and discriminatory ability.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

The clinical decision support system disclosed herein addresses a critical challenge in healthcare technology: determining when predictive models yield unreliable risk assessments. Conventional approaches for evaluating prediction reliability fall into two categories: model-dependent methods that report confidence intervals based on parameter uncertainty, and model-independent methods that assess reliability through retraining or similarity metrics. Both approaches have significant limitations that hinder clinical adoption.  

Model-dependent reliability measures are inherently tied to specific classifier architectures. For example, neural networks may evaluate whether sufficient training data exists for a given prediction, while regression models might calculate confidence intervals through least squares estimation. These techniques cannot be generalized across different predictive models, forcing clinicians to use multiple reliability assessment methods for different risk scores.  

Model-independent approaches, while more flexible, typically require access to the original training dataset to evaluate reliability. Some methods retrain the predictive model using augmented datasets, while others compute similarity metrics between test patients and training examples. These techniques raise privacy concerns and impose impractical computational burdens in clinical environments where rapid risk assessment is required.  

The trust score represents a recently proposed model-independent approach that measures agreement between a classifier and a nearest-neighbor analysis. While avoiding retraining, this method still depends on access to the original training data and has shown inconsistent performance in real-world evaluations. Notably, it fails to reliably identify subgroups where predictive models exhibit poor calibration or discriminatory ability.  

The disclosed system introduces a proactive reliability assessment method that overcomes these limitations. The approach is model-independent, requiring neither retraining nor direct access to sensitive training data. Instead, it leverages generative modeling to estimate prediction reliability based on summary statistics from the original training distribution. This enables practical implementation across healthcare systems while maintaining patient privacy.  

A key embodiment of the invention comprises a clinical decision support system with integrated reliability assessment capabilities. The system includes data processing components that receive patient EMR data and interface with predictive models to generate clinical risk scores. A reliability assessment module compares these scores against alternative estimates derived through generative modeling techniques.  

The system defines a clinical risk score as the output of any predictive model that estimates the probability of an adverse medical outcome. These scores may originate from established risk calculators (e.g., GRACE score for cardiovascular events) or machine learning models trained on institutional data. The reliability assessment module processes these scores alongside patient feature vectors to determine prediction reliability.  

The method for determining reliability involves several computational steps. First, generative models are trained to characterize the distribution of patient features in positive and negative outcome classes. These models may employ multivariate normal distributions, Gaussian mixture models, or other probabilistic frameworks to approximate the underlying data distributions. Using these trained models, the system samples synthetic patient data and processes these samples through the predictive model to estimate likelihood functions.  

Bayes' theorem is applied to derive an expression for the alternative risk score. The system estimates likelihoods using the generative models, computing the probability that a given risk score would be observed in positive versus negative outcome classes. These likelihoods are combined with prior outcome probabilities to calculate a posterior probability estimate through Bayesian inference.  

An alternative formula for the probability function incorporates relative likelihood ratios that compare how likely a risk score is under each outcome class. The absolute difference between the original risk score and this alternative estimate quantifies prediction unreliability. Large discrepancies indicate cases where the training data provides insufficient basis for a robust prediction.  

System architecture embodiments include server and database components that support these computations. A clinician's prior decisions database may store historical risk assessments and outcomes to inform reliability estimates. The system can be implemented across various computing environments, including traditional server architectures and cloud-based deployments.  

Neuromorphic system embodiments leverage neural network functionality for efficient reliability assessment. These implementations may employ specialized hardware optimized for parallel processing of generative models and likelihood calculations. The neural networks can be trained to approximate the reliability assessment function, enabling rapid evaluation of prediction trustworthiness.  

The method for assessing reliability involves segregating EMR data into positive and negative outcome sets. Generative models are trained separately on each subset to capture distinct feature distributions. These models then sample synthetic patient data that preserves the statistical properties of each outcome class.  

The system processes these synthetic samples through the predictive classifier to generate risk score distributions. Numerical methods determine convergence of these distributions through iterative sampling and statistical testing. Relative likelihoods are calculated by comparing how frequently specific risk scores appear in each synthetic distribution.  

These likelihoods inform the calculation of an alternative risk score through Bayesian probability estimation. The absolute difference between this score and the original prediction quantifies unreliability. Thresholds can be set to classify predictions as reliable or unreliable based on clinical requirements.  

The system matches patient features with reliability determinations to identify high-risk subgroups. User interface modifications display reliability indicators alongside risk scores, alerting clinicians to potentially misleading predictions. For unreliable assessments, the system may trigger additional review processes including consultation with primary care physicians via electronic communication.  

Integration of unreliability scores into clinical decision making provides several advantages. Clinicians can weigh risk predictions against their reliability estimates, focusing attention on cases where models may perform poorly. The system's proactive identification of unreliable predictions helps prevent over-reliance on automated assessments in high-risk scenarios.  

Performance evaluation using real-world data demonstrates the method's effectiveness. Analysis of the GRACE risk score shows that unreliable predictions correspond to patient subgroups where the model exhibits poor calibration and reduced discriminatory ability. The unreliability metric successfully identifies cases where predicted risks diverge substantially from observed outcomes.  

Computing the unreliability metric reveals its dependence on class imbalance in the training data. When positive outcomes are rare, unreliable predictions cluster among high-risk assessments where limited training examples reduce model robustness. This pattern aligns with clinical expectations regarding model performance in underrepresented patient groups.  

Comparative analysis against the trust score highlights advantages of the disclosed method. While both approaches identify some unreliable predictions, the disclosed unreliability metric more consistently detects subgroups with reduced model performance. Evaluation metrics including normalized Brier scores and AUC analysis confirm these findings across different clinical outcomes.  

Extension to stroke risk prediction demonstrates the method's generalizability. The unreliability metric effectively identifies poorly calibrated predictions in this distinct clinical context, maintaining performance despite significant class imbalance. This suggests broad applicability across various medical prediction tasks.
