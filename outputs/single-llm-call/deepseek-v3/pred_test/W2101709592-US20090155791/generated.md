# DESCRIPTION

The present invention relates to methods for detecting methylated CpG-containing nucleic acids, particularly through improved primer design strategies that correct for PCR bias in methylation analysis.

## FIELD OF INVENTION

The invention relates to a method of detecting methylated CpG-containing nucleic acids through optimized primer design and amplification techniques that compensate for PCR bias in methylation analysis.

## BACKGROUND OF INVENTION

### Importance of DNA Methylation

DNA methylation represents a fundamental epigenetic mechanism that regulates gene expression and maintains genomic stability. The process involves the covalent addition of a methyl group to the fifth carbon position of cytosine residues, primarily occurring at CpG dinucleotides. This methylation reaction is catalyzed by DNA methyltransferases and plays crucial roles in cellular differentiation, genomic imprinting, X-chromosome inactivation, and suppression of transposable elements.

CpG islands, which are regions of high CpG density, are frequently found in gene promoter regions. The methylation status of these CpG islands serves as a critical regulator of gene expression, with hypermethylation typically associated with gene silencing and hypomethylation linked to transcriptional activation. In cancer cells, abnormal methylation patterns emerge as a hallmark feature, characterized by global hypomethylation accompanied by localized hypermethylation of tumor suppressor gene promoters.

The detection of DNA methylation patterns has become increasingly important for cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment monitoring. Existing methodologies for methylation analysis include methylation-specific PCR (MSP), combined bisulfite restriction analysis (COBRA), bisulfite sequencing, and methylation-sensitive high resolution melting (MS-HRM). However, these techniques suffer from limitations, particularly PCR bias that favors amplification of unmethylated alleles, leading to inaccurate representation of true methylation status in samples.

## SUMMARY OF INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method for detecting methylated CpG-containing nucleic acids that overcomes PCR bias through strategic primer design. Key aspects include the incorporation of limited CpG dinucleotides in primer sequences, optimization of annealing temperatures, and careful positioning of CpG sites within primers to achieve proportional amplification of methylated and unmethylated templates.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention provides an improved method for DNA methylation pattern identification through optimized primer design and amplification conditions.

### DEFINITIONS

Amplification refers to the process of increasing the number of copies of a specific nucleic acid sequence through enzymatic replication. The template copying process involves using a DNA polymerase to synthesize new strands complementary to the target sequence. A double stranded polynucleotide consists of two complementary nucleic acid strands bound together by hydrogen bonds between base pairs.

A gene represents a functional unit of heredity composed of DNA that encodes a specific product. Nucleotides are the basic building blocks of nucleic acids, each consisting of a nitrogenous base, a pentose sugar, and a phosphate group. The nucleotide structure enables base pairing through hydrogen bonds between complementary bases (A-T and C-G).

An oligonucleotide is a short nucleic acid polymer, typically 15-50 nucleotides in length, with defined sequence specificity. Polynucleotides are longer nucleic acid chains that may contain thousands of nucleotides. A dinucleotide consists of two nucleotide units linked by a phosphodiester bond, with the CpG dinucleotide being particularly significant in methylation analysis.

Methylation status refers to the presence or absence of methyl groups at specific cytosine residues within a DNA sequence. Detection of methylation status involves distinguishing between methylated and unmethylated cytosines. PCR bias describes the preferential amplification of certain sequences over others during PCR, which can significantly affect methylation analysis results.

### Samples

Suitable samples for methylation analysis include tissue biopsies, blood, saliva, urine, and other biological fluids containing nucleic acids. Preferred sample sources include tumor tissue, circulating tumor cells, and cell-free DNA from plasma. Nucleic acid extraction may be performed using commercially available kits or traditional phenol-chloroform methods.

Target nucleic acids may exist in various forms including genomic DNA, cDNA, or amplified products. The invention accommodates nucleic acid mixtures containing both methylated and unmethylated sequences. For genomic nucleic acid preparation, typical amounts range from 10 ng to 1 μg, with optimal performance achieved with 50-200 ng input DNA.

### Modification of DNA

The DNA modification step involves treatment with sodium bisulfite, which converts unmethylated cytosines to uracil while leaving methylated cytosines unchanged. Subsequent PCR amplification then converts uracils to thymines, creating sequence differences between originally methylated and unmethylated templates that can be detected.

### Methylation-Independent Primer

A methylation-independent primer in this context refers to an oligonucleotide designed to amplify both methylated and unmethylated sequences while incorporating strategic CpG dinucleotides to compensate for PCR bias. These primers hybridize to bisulfite-converted DNA regardless of methylation status at most positions, but include carefully placed CpGs to modulate amplification efficiency.

Primer design considerations include length (typically 18-30 nucleotides), with specific examples being 20-25 nucleotides optimal for most applications. The method for determining methylation status involves using these specially designed primers in conjunction with melting curve analysis of PCR products.

CpG dinucleotides are strategically positioned within primers, preferably near the 5' end rather than the 3' end to maintain amplification of both methylated and unmethylated templates. The 5'-end location of CpG dinucleotides allows modulation of hybridization stringency through temperature adjustment while minimizing complete selectivity for methylated sequences.

Primer identity to target template is maintained at non-CpG positions, with controlled mismatches at CpG sites to enable bias correction. Annealing temperature modulation between 50-65°C allows adjustment of stringency to compensate for PCR bias. Buffer composition and salt concentration are optimized to maintain primer-template interaction stability.

Specific primer sequences are provided as SEQ ID NOs 128-151, with preferred embodiments including SEQ ID NOs 138-139, 142-143, and 144-151. These primers target various genes including MGMT, BNIP3, and other tumor suppressor genes implicated in cancer development.

### Amplifying Step

The amplifying step involves polymerase chain reaction using methylation-independent oligonucleotide primers. Suitable enzymes include Taq polymerase and its variants, with PCR being the preferred amplification method. The process includes melting at 94-98°C for 15-60 seconds, annealing at optimized temperatures (typically 50-65°C) for 30-90 seconds, and elongation at 72°C for 30-60 seconds.

Cycling typically involves 35-50 repetitions of these steps. Real-time PCR instruments equipped with thermal cyclers and fluorescence detection capabilities are preferred. Dyes such as SYBR Green or EvaGreen enable monitoring of amplification and subsequent melting analysis.

### Analysis of Amplified CpG-Containing Nucleic Acids

Analysis involves comparing the sequence differences between methylated and unmethylated templates after bisulfite conversion and amplification. Methods include melting curve analysis, high resolution melting analysis, sequencing, and restriction enzyme digestion.

Melting curve analysis monitors fluorescence changes as temperature increases, with methylated and unmethylated sequences displaying distinct melting profiles due to their different base compositions. High resolution melting provides enhanced discrimination through precise temperature control and sensitive fluorescence detection.

### Nucleic Acid Sequencing

Nucleic acid sequencing, particularly the dideoxy (Sanger) method, provides definitive determination of methylation status by directly reading the sequence of amplified products and identifying C-to-T conversions indicative of original methylation status.

### Primer Extension

Primer extension methods involve hybridizing a primer adjacent to the methylation site of interest, followed by limited extension with nucleotides including chain terminators to detect sequence variations resulting from bisulfite conversion.

### Restriction Enzyme Digestion

Restriction enzyme digestion takes advantage of sequence differences created by bisulfite conversion, where certain restriction sites are preserved in methylated sequences but lost in unmethylated ones. Enzymes such as BstUI or TaqI can discriminate between methylated and unmethylated sequences after bisulfite treatment.

## Disorders

Abnormal CpG methylation patterns are indicative of various disorders, particularly cancers. Protooncogene hypomethylation and tumor suppressor gene hypermethylation contribute to cancer development. Specific cancers associated with methylation abnormalities include breast, bladder, ovarian, prostate, lung, colon, endometrial, leukemia, gastric, and cervical cancers, as well as melanoma and imprinting disorders.

## Kit

The invention includes a kit comprising methylation-independent oligonucleotide primers, reference samples with known methylation status, control CpG-containing nucleic acids, necessary reagents for amplification and detection, detailed instructions for performance and interpretation, and software for data analysis.

## Use

The method is used for detecting methylation status of CpG-containing nucleic acids in diagnostic, prognostic, and research applications, particularly in cancer management and epigenetic studies.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

CpG-containing nucleic acids were modified by bisulfite conversion and amplified using redesigned primers incorporating limited CpG sites. Testing revealed PCR bias toward unmethylated alleles with conventional primers, while redesigned primers showed improved sensitivity through strategic CpG placement and annealing temperature optimization. Melting profiles demonstrated distinct patterns for methylated and unmethylated sequences.

### Example 2

Validation using four assays with defined mixtures of methylated and unmethylated DNA demonstrated proportional amplification with redesigned primers. The importance of primer design in achieving accurate methylation assessment was confirmed, highlighting advantages of the modified methodology over conventional approaches.

### Example 3

Methylation-sensitive high resolution melting (MS-HRM) was developed as a sensitive, high-throughput methylation assessment method. DNA samples from colorectal cancer and cell lines were analyzed, demonstrating accurate detection of methylation levels compared to reference methods. Primer redesign for shorter amplicons improved performance, with validation against MethylLight assays confirming accuracy.

### Example 4

DNA methylation analysis protocols were optimized, emphasizing bisulfite modification, PCR amplification with carefully designed primers, and high resolution melting analysis. Detailed protocols for bisulfite conversion, primer design, PCR optimization, and melting analysis were established, addressing common challenges in methylation detection.

### Example 5

A comprehensive MS-HRM protocol was developed, including PCR amplification of bisulfite-modified DNA and high resolution melting analysis. Primer design guidelines specifically addressed PCR bias correction through strategic CpG placement and annealing temperature optimization. The protocol included detailed reagent specifications, equipment requirements, and troubleshooting guidance.
