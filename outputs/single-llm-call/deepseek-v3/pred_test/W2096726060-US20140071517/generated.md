## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to optical materials and more specifically to nanomaterials capable of photon upconversion. The invention particularly concerns transition metal-doped nanocrystals coupled with organic dye optical antennas for efficient infrared-to-visible wavelength conversion.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Photon upconversion refers to nonlinear optical processes where sequential absorption of two or more lower energy photons leads to emission of higher energy radiation. This phenomenon has shown promise for applications including solid-state lasers, biological imaging, advanced lighting systems, solar energy conversion, and silicon detector enhancement. Conventional upconversion materials based on lanthanide-doped crystals suffer from several limitations including narrow absorption bandwidths, low absorption cross-sections, and inefficient energy transfer. The Laporte-forbidden f-f transitions in lanthanide ions result in long excited state lifetimes but extremely weak absorption coefficients (ε ∼ 0.5-5 M⁻¹cm⁻¹). While low phonon energy host materials like NaYF₄ can reduce nonradiative relaxation, the fundamental constraints of atomic transitions restrict practical implementation. Recent attempts to improve efficiency through energy clustering at the sublattice level still fail to address the core limitations of narrow bandwidth and weak absorption.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides an optical upconversion material comprising transition metal-doped nanocrystals coupled with specifically designed organic dye optical antennas. The nanocrystal host material contains energy transfer facilitating dopants and emitter dopants that together enable efficient multiphoton upconversion processes. The optical antenna, chemically bonded to the nanocrystal surface, dramatically increases light absorption cross-section (typically 200-400 L g⁻¹cm⁻¹) and spectral bandwidth (≥100 nm) compared to direct atomic absorption. Energy transfer between the antenna and nanocrystal occurs primarily through Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) when the components are spaced approximately 5 nm apart.

Key advantages of the invention include a 10-fold increase in spectral response width and a 10-fold enhancement in monochromatic upconversion efficiency compared to conventional materials. The broad absorption characteristics of the dye antennas eliminate the need for monochromatic excitation sources. The invention enables multiple applications including: enhanced photovoltaic response in solar cells by converting unused infrared radiation to visible wavelengths detectable by silicon; expanded detection range for silicon-based imaging systems; visible laser threat warning systems through distinctive emission signatures; and covert marking applications utilizing tailored upconversion signatures.

The optical upconverting nanomaterial comprises transition metal-doped crystalline nanoparticles, preferably NaYF₄ host crystals doped with combinations of Yb³⁺, Er³⁺, Ho³⁺, and Tm³⁺. Energy transfer facilitating dopants like Yb³⁺ (20-25% doping) efficiently capture and transfer excitation energy, while emitter dopants like Er³⁺ (2.5%), Ho³⁺ (2%), or Tm³⁺ (0.3%) produce the upconverted emission. In some embodiments, a single element serves as both energy transfer facilitator and emitter (e.g., Er³⁺ at 15% doping). The system supports multiple parallel upconversion pathways within a single nanocrystal, enabling response to distinct input wavelengths and emission at different output wavelengths.

The optical antenna consists of infrared-absorbing organic dyes selected from heptamethines, dithiones, squaryliums, tris azos, or Dyomics series dyes. These dyes feature functional groups (—NH₂, —CO₂M′, —SH, —OH) for covalent bonding to the nanocrystal surface through metal ions (M = H₂, Cu, Co, CoR₃, Ni, SiR₃R₄, AlR₃, Zn, Fe, FeR₃, Mn) and R₄ groups (—R₃, —Cl, —CH₃, —H, -Ph, —OCH₃, —OCH₂CH₃). Preferred embodiments use NHS-ester functionalized dyes like DY-831 or DyL800 that form stable amide bonds with amine-functionalized nanoparticle surfaces. An encapsulating silica shell provides protection and enables additional ligand attachment for dispersion in composite materials.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

For purposes of this invention, "optical means" refers to electromagnetic radiation spanning ultraviolet (200-400 nm), visible (400-700 nm), and infrared (700 nm-1 mm) wavelengths. The near infrared (NIR) region (700-1400 nm) is of particular importance for upconversion applications. "Upconversion" denotes the nonlinear optical process where sequential absorption of lower energy photons leads to emission of higher energy radiation. "Förster resonance energy transfer" (FRET) describes the nonradiative energy transfer mechanism involving dipole-dipole interactions between donor (dye) and acceptor (nanocrystal) species.

Transition metals refer to elements in groups 3-12 of the periodic table, while rare earth elements comprise the lanthanide series plus scandium and yttrium. Organic dyes are aromatic compounds exhibiting strong electronic transitions, with sensitizers specifically absorbing light and transferring energy to other components. Energy transfer facilitating materials promote efficient migration of excitation energy within the nanocrystal lattice.

The optical upconverting nanomaterial consists of crystalline nanoparticles, preferably hexagonal-phase NaYF₄, doped with precise combinations of transition metals. The nanocrystals exhibit dimensions of 15-25 nm with well-defined crystalline structure confirmed by TEM and XRD analysis. Key compositional parameters include:

### X=—NH₂, —CO₂M′, —SH, —OH

These functional groups enable covalent attachment of dye molecules to the nanoparticle surface. The amine (—NH₂) group specifically reacts with NHS-ester functionalized dyes to form stable amide linkages. Carboxylic acid (—CO₂M′) groups provide alternative attachment points through metal ion coordination.

### M=H₂, Cu, Co, CoR₃, Ni, SiR₃R₄, AlR₃, Zn, Fe, FeR₃, Mn

These metal ions facilitate bonding between organic dyes and the nanocrystal surface. The selection of metal ion influences bond strength and energy transfer efficiency. Cobalt and nickel complexes often provide optimal balance between stability and FRET efficiency.

### R₄=—R₃, —Cl, —CH₃, —H, -Ph, —OCH₃, —OCH₂CH₃

These substituent groups modify the electronic properties of the dye-nanocrystal interface, tuning energy transfer characteristics. Phenyl (-Ph) groups enhance π-conjugation while alkoxy groups (—OCH₃, —OCH₂CH₃) influence solubility and dispersion.

### M′=K, Li, Na, H

These counterions balance charge in carboxylate-functionalized dyes. Sodium and potassium salts typically provide superior water solubility for biological applications.

The optical antenna dyes include several important classes:

Heptamethines feature extended conjugation with strong NIR absorption. Dithiones offer excellent photostability and broad absorption profiles. Squaryliums provide large extinction coefficients and sharp emission bands. Tris azos enable multiple attachment points for controlled orientation on nanocrystal surfaces. The Dyomics series (e.g., DY-831, DyL800) combines high molar absorptivity (ε > 200,000 M⁻¹cm⁻¹) with functional groups for stable nanoparticle attachment.

Specific dye examples (1-25) include: DY-831 NHS ester, DyLight 800 NHS ester, DLS-776A carboxylic acid, NIR-797 isothiocyanate, IR-806, IR-820, IR-825, IR-832, IR-1061, ADS830A, ADS832A, ADS840A, ADS848A, ADS852A, ADS860A, ADS868A, ADS880A, ADS890A, ADS900A, Cy7, Cy7.5, Alexa Fluor 790, and indocyanine green.

Energy transfer facilitator dopants (e.g., Yb³⁺ at 20-25%) efficiently absorb and migrate excitation energy through the nanocrystal lattice. Emitter dopants (e.g., Er³⁺ at 2.5%, Ho³⁺ at 2%, Tm³⁺ at 0.3%) concentrate this energy to produce upconverted emission. The system operates through sequential photon absorption and energy transfer events:

1. Dye antenna absorbs incident IR photon (ε ≈ 270,000 M⁻¹cm⁻¹)
2. Excited dye transfers energy to nanocrystal via FRET (r ≈ 5 nm)
3. Energy migrates through facilitator dopants (Yb³⁺ → Yb³⁺*)
4. Sequential energy transfer to emitter dopant (Yb³⁺* → Er³⁺)
5. Emitter dopant reaches excited state through multiple transfers
6. Upconverted emission at visible wavelength (e.g., Er³⁺ → 550 nm)

The Dexter electron exchange mechanism supplements FRET at very short donor-acceptor distances (<2 nm). Composite materials integrate multiple parallel optical pathways within a single nanocrystal, enabling responses to different input wavelengths (e.g., 785 nm and 975 nm) and distinct output emissions (e.g., green and blue). This is achieved either through multiple dye antennas or single dyes with broad absorption profiles.

Distinct elements serving as energy transfer facilitators (Yb³⁺) and emitters (Er³⁺) provide design flexibility, while single-element systems (Er³⁺ at 15%) simplify fabrication. Energy transfer facilitating dopants are selected for optimal spectral overlap with dye emission, while emitter dopants are chosen for desired upconversion wavelengths. Multiple distinct input wavelengths can be addressed simultaneously (e.g., 785 nm + 975 nm), with either single or multiple output wavelengths generated.

Fabrication of optical nanomaterials involves three key steps:

## Synthesis of Lanthanide-Doped NaYF₄ Nanocrystals

The synthesis begins with preparation of lanthanide acetate salts (Y³⁺, Yb³⁺, Er³⁺, etc.) mixed with sodium acetate in oleic acid/octadecene solvent. Heating to 120°C forms a clear solution that is cooled before adding ammonium fluoride in methanol, producing a white precipitate. After methanol evaporation, the mixture is heated to 324°C under nitrogen to form hexagonal-phase nanocrystals. Particle size (15-25 nm) and crystallinity are controlled by reaction time (20-100 min) and temperature (300-324°C). Purification involves ethanol precipitation and centrifugation.

## Encapsulation of Attached Dye

NaYF₄:Ln³⁺ nanoparticles are dispersed in cyclohexane with surfactant (Igepal CO-520) to form a reverse microemulsion. Amine-containing siloxane monomer (APS) is added to form a thin silica shell (∼2 nm) around each nanoparticle. The dye (e.g., DY-831 NHS ester) is pre-attached to the siloxane before encapsulation or added afterward through amide bond formation with surface amines.

## Attachment of Dye to Encapsulated Nanoparticle

Alternately, silica encapsulation precedes dye attachment. Amine-functionalized nanoparticles are reacted with NHS-ester dyes (200:1 dye:nanoparticle ratio) in ethanol at room temperature. Excess dye is removed by washing until the eluent is clear. Typical loading reaches ∼60 dye molecules per 25 nm nanoparticle.

## Fabrication of Films

Upconverting films are fabricated by suspending nanoparticles in monomeric solutions (e.g., methyl methacrylate) followed by curing to form clear PMMA composites. Alternative methods include spin-coating nanoparticle dispersions or embedding in sol-gel matrices. Film transparency (>85% visible transmission) is maintained while providing strong IR absorption and upconverted emission.
