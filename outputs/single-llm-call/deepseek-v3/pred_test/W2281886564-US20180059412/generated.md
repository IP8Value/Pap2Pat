# DESCRIPTION  

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention relates generally to micro- and nano-photonic resonators, and more specifically to methods for precisely tuning the optical resonance properties of such photonic resonators. Photonic resonators are optical devices that confine light within small volumes through resonant modes, enabling strong light-matter interactions. These devices find applications across numerous fields including optical sensing, quantum optics, optical communications, and integrated photonic circuits. The invention particularly addresses the need for high-precision, permanent tuning of resonator properties after fabrication, while maintaining or improving the optical quality factors of the resonators.  

## PRIOR ART  

Traditional fabrication techniques for photonic resonators rely on clean room processes such as electron beam lithography and reactive ion etching. These methods suffer from inherent limitations in reproducibility, making it challenging to fabricate multiple resonators with identical optical properties. Even with advanced fabrication techniques, small variations in resonator dimensions inevitably lead to variations in resonant wavelengths, limiting the scalability of photonic devices requiring multiple identical resonators.  

Optical resonators have been extensively studied for applications in light-matter interaction research, serving as sensitive detectors, efficient light emitters, and fundamental components for optical information processing. Miniaturized optical resonators have enabled compact sensors, display devices, and optical transistors. However, the inability to precisely tune resonator properties post-fabrication has constrained their practical implementation in industrial settings.  

Several techniques have been developed to address resonator tuning. Nitrogen deposition has been used to shift resonant wavelengths through refractive index modification, but this method lacks permanence and spatial selectivity. Photochromic thin films offer reversible tuning but suffer from limited stability and precision. Mechanical action through piezoelectric actuators can tune resonators but introduces mechanical instability and complexity. Photo-addressable polyelectrolyte functionalization provides chemical tuning but lacks the precision required for high-quality resonators. Tension application techniques can modify resonator properties but are limited to specific geometries and materials.  

These prior art techniques share common limitations including lack of permanence, insufficient precision, poor scalability, and inability to simultaneously tune multiple resonators. They often degrade optical quality factors or require complex implementation incompatible with industrial production. The present invention overcomes these limitations through a novel photoelectrochemical etching approach that enables precise, permanent, and scalable tuning of photonic resonators.  

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION  

The invention provides an improved method for tuning optical resonators through resonant photoelectrochemical etching. The method involves injecting light into a resonator immersed in a fluid containing ions, where the light is at a resonance wavelength of the resonator. The resonant condition creates an enhanced light intensity within the resonator, driving a photoelectrochemical etching process that selectively reduces the resonator's dimensions. As the dimensions decrease, the resonance wavelength shifts, enabling precise tuning of the optical properties.  

The etching process can be controlled through consecutive wavelength sweeps, where each sweep both monitors the resonance position and induces a small, controlled amount of etching. The optical coupling to the resonator may be achieved through far-field or near-field techniques, with waveguide implementations providing particularly efficient evanescent wave coupling. The fluid containing ions enables the photoelectrochemical reactions while maintaining optical access to the resonator.  

Key advantages of the invention include the permanent nature of the tuning, scalability to industrial production, fast tuning speeds, and high precision down to picometer scale. The method is particularly suited for tuning multiple resonators simultaneously to identical resonance wavelengths, overcoming fabrication variations. The invention enables production of photonic devices with precisely controlled optical properties, suitable for applications requiring multiple identical resonators or exact resonance matching.  

The tuning method preserves or improves the quality factors of the resonators, in contrast to many prior art techniques that degrade optical performance. The spatial selectivity of the resonant etching process allows targeting specific resonators or resonator modes without affecting nearby structures. The invention is applicable to various resonator geometries including disks, rings, and photonic crystal cavities, and can be implemented with different semiconductor materials through appropriate choice of fluid and optical parameters.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

The invention relates to photonic devices incorporating optical resonators and methods for tuning their resonant properties. Optical resonators confine light through total internal reflection or periodic structures, creating standing wave patterns known as resonant modes. These modes are characterized by specific resonance wavelengths and quality factors quantifying optical loss. The invention enables post-fabrication adjustment of these properties with unprecedented precision.  

### The Photonic Device  

The photonic device comprises one or more optical resonators fabricated from semiconductor materials such as gallium arsenide. A typical resonator structure includes a disk-shaped region with dimensions on the order of micrometers in diameter and hundreds of nanometers in thickness. The disk is supported by a pedestal that provides mechanical stability while minimizing optical losses. The resonator material is chosen for its optical properties and compatibility with photoelectrochemical processes.  

Optical coupling to the resonator is achieved through waveguides positioned in proximity to the resonator. The waveguide may incorporate tapered sections to optimize evanescent coupling efficiency. The waveguide-resonator system is designed to maintain optical functionality when immersed in the tuning fluid, preserving light confinement despite the surrounding medium. The resonator dimensions and material properties determine the initial resonance wavelengths before tuning.  

The photonic device may include multiple resonators arranged along a common waveguide or through other coupling geometries. Additional components such as input/output couplers, spectral filters, or photodetectors may be integrated depending on the intended application. The device architecture enables simultaneous optical addressing of multiple resonators during the tuning process.  

### Installation Set-Up  

The tuning method requires optical access to the resonator while immersed in the photoelectrochemically active fluid. A spectroscopy setup injects light into the waveguide or directly to the resonator, with precise wavelength control around the resonance of interest. The transmission spectrum through the waveguide or reflection from the resonator is monitored in real-time during the tuning process.  

The optical setup includes a tunable laser source with narrow linewidth and precise wavelength control. Detection components measure the transmitted or reflected light intensity with sufficient sensitivity to resolve small changes in resonance properties. The fluid containment system maintains stable conditions around the resonator during etching while allowing optical access. Temperature and environmental controls ensure stable operation throughout the tuning procedure.  

The resonator transmission spectrum initially shows distinct resonance dips corresponding to various optical modes. The linewidth and depth of these resonances provide information about the quality factor and coupling efficiency. Monitoring these characteristics during tuning allows real-time optimization of the process parameters to achieve desired resonator properties.  

### Method for Tuning an Optical Resonator (FIG. 7)  

The photoelectrochemical etching process begins by immersing the resonator in a fluid containing appropriate ions, such as water or other electrolytes. Light is injected into the resonator at a wavelength corresponding to one of its optical resonances. The resonant condition creates a standing wave pattern with enhanced light intensity at specific locations within the resonator.  

This localized intensity drives a photoelectrochemical reaction at the resonator surface, preferentially etching regions of high optical intensity. The etching process gradually reduces the resonator dimensions, causing a blue shift in the resonance wavelength. As the resonance shifts, the laser wavelength is adjusted to maintain the resonant condition, sustaining the etching process.  

The etching can be performed through discrete wavelength sweeps, where each sweep both characterizes the current resonance position and induces a controlled amount of etching. Alternatively, continuous wavelength tracking can be employed for smoother tuning. The process is stopped when the desired resonance wavelength is achieved, at which point the resonator is removed from the fluid environment, permanently fixing its new optical properties.  

A preferred embodiment uses infrared light for etching, taking advantage of two-photon absorption processes and mid-gap states in the semiconductor material. This approach provides exceptional selectivity and control over the etching process. The resonant nature of the etching ensures spatial localization to the optical mode volume, preserving the overall resonator structure while precisely modifying its optical properties.  

### Method for Tuning Several Resonators at a Targeted Value (FIG. 8)  

The invention enables simultaneous tuning of multiple resonators to identical resonance wavelengths, overcoming fabrication variations. Initially, the resonators exhibit a distribution of resonance wavelengths due to small differences in their dimensions. The tuning process begins by addressing the resonator with the longest wavelength resonance.  

As this resonator is etched, its resonance shifts toward shorter wavelengths until it matches the next resonator in the distribution. At this point, both resonators are being etched simultaneously, with their resonances locked together. The process continues as the now-matched pair shifts toward the next resonator's resonance, progressively incorporating more resonators into the matched set.  

This method naturally scales to any number of resonators, requiring only a single laser source and wavelength sweep. The final result is a set of resonators with identical resonance wavelengths, despite their initial variations. The collective tuning does not require individual identification or separate addressing of each resonator, greatly simplifying the process for large arrays.  

### Different Embodiments for Varying the Laser Wavelength in Order to Tune Optical Resonators  

The wavelength variation during tuning can be implemented in different modes. Discrete sweeps involve rapid scanning back and forth across the resonance, with each scan inducing a small, controlled shift. This approach provides excellent precision and allows real-time monitoring of the tuning progress. Continuous tracking maintains the laser wavelength locked to the shifting resonance, enabling smoother tuning with potentially higher final precision.  

Both approaches are applicable to single resonator tuning or collective tuning of multiple resonators. The choice between discrete and continuous tuning depends on the required precision, available equipment, and time constraints. Hybrid approaches combining aspects of both methods may be employed to optimize the tuning process for specific applications.  

### Parameters and Experimental Data  

The fluid properties significantly influence the etching process. Factors such as ionic concentration, pH, and chemical composition affect the etching rate and selectivity. Water provides an effective medium for gallium arsenide resonators, with controlled additives enabling tuning of the etching characteristics. The fluid conductivity relates directly to the etching rate, allowing process control through electrolyte composition.  

The resonator material and geometry offer flexibility in implementation. While the examples focus on gallium arsenide disks, the method applies to various semiconductor materials including silicon, gallium nitride, and others, with appropriate choice of fluid. Different resonator geometries such as rings, spheres, or photonic crystals can be tuned following similar principles.  

Experimental data demonstrate precise control over etching rates through laser power and sweep parameters. The relationship between laser power and etching speed is substantially linear, enabling predictable tuning. Sweeping rates can be adjusted to balance tuning speed and precision, with typical etching speeds ranging from 0.5 to 1 nm per second for moderate optical powers.  

The tuned resonators exhibit excellent optical characteristics, with quality factors often improved compared to their pre-tuning values. The permanent nature of the tuning is confirmed through repeated measurements over extended periods. The precision of collective tuning matches that of individual resonator tuning, with alignment accuracies better than 10 pm demonstrated for multiple resonator systems.  

### Characteristics of the Tuned Optical Resonator  

Tuned resonators maintain or improve their optical quality factors, with demonstrated values exceeding 70,000. The tuning precision reaches picometer scale, corresponding to dimensional control below atomic monolayer levels. The permanent nature of the tuning ensures stable operation over time without degradation of optical properties.  

For collectively tuned resonator sets, the alignment precision matches that of individual tuning, enabling applications requiring multiple identical resonators. The spatial selectivity of the etching process preserves resonator integrity while precisely modifying optical properties. The method's applicability extends to various resonator types and materials, with maintained or improved quality factors across implementations.  

The tuned resonators exhibit excellent spectral characteristics with well-defined resonance lineshapes and stable positions. The precision and reproducibility of the tuning process enable implementation in demanding applications such as quantum optics, precision metrology, and advanced optical communications. The scalability of the method supports industrial production of photonic devices with precisely controlled optical properties.
