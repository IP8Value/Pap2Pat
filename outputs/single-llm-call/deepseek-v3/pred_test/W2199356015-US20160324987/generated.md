## FIELD OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention relates to the field of medicine, specifically to gene therapy for inherited retinal degenerative disorders. More particularly, the invention provides compositions and methods for treating autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa (adRP) using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated allele-specific genomic ablation.  

## BACKGROUND  

Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is a group of inherited retinal degenerative disorders characterized by progressive photoreceptor cell death, leading to vision loss and eventual blindness. RP manifests with symptoms including night blindness, peripheral vision loss, and diminished visual acuity, typically diagnosed through clinical examination, electroretinography, and genetic testing.  

The genetic causes of RP are heterogeneous, with mutations in over 24 genes implicated in autosomal dominant RP (adRP). Among these, mutations in the rhodopsin (RHO) gene account for the largest proportion of cases. Conventional therapeutic approaches for recessive RP, such as gene replacement strategies, are ineffective for adRP due to the dominant-negative effect of mutant alleles.  

CRISPR (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeat) technology has emerged as a powerful tool for precise genome editing. The CRISPR/Cas9 system enables targeted DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), facilitating gene disruption or correction. However, applying CRISPR in vivo to treat dominant retinal disorders requires allele-specific targeting to avoid disrupting the wild-type allele.  

Current limitations of conventional RP therapies include the inability to selectively silence mutant alleles without affecting wild-type gene function. In vivo CRISPR therapy presents a promising alternative by enabling allele-specific ablation of disease-causing mutations while preserving endogenous wild-type gene expression.  

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention provides a therapeutic method for treating autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa (adRP) through CRISPR/Cas9-mediated allele-specific genomic ablation. The method utilizes a CRISPR protein, such as Staphylococcus aureus Cas9 (SaCas9), and a guide RNA (gRNA) designed to target a mutant allele of the rhodopsin (RHO) gene while sparing the wild-type allele.  

The target polynucleotide sequence includes a protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) unique to the mutant RHO allele, enabling selective cleavage. The invention is particularly applicable to adRP caused by the RHO S334ter mutation, where the mutant allele contains a PAM sequence (5′-TGG-3′) distinct from the wild-type allele (5′-TGC-3′).  

The therapeutic vector comprises an adeno-associated virus (AAV) delivery system encoding the CRISPR protein and gRNA. Administration methods include subretinal injection, optionally combined with electroporation to enhance transfection efficiency. The genomic editing method involves generating a site-specific DSB in the mutant RHO allele, leading to non-homologous end joining (NHEJ)-mediated indel mutations that disrupt the mutant gene.

"Treat" encompasses therapeutic intervention to slow, halt, or reverse disease progression.  

Retinitis pigmentosa is characterized by progressive photoreceptor degeneration, with autosomal dominant forms caused by gain-of-function mutations in genes such as RHO. Current mutation-focused approaches, including RNA interference and gene replacement, are limited by the need to preserve wild-type gene function.  

Gene editing tools using CRISPR/Cas9 enable precise targeting of disease-causing mutations. In dominant negative forms of RP, such as those caused by the P23H RHO mutation, the mutant protein interferes with wild-type protein function. The S334ter mutation in RHO results in a truncated protein lacking critical C-terminal residues required for proper trafficking.  

Peripherin (RDS) gene mutations also cause adRP through similar dominant-negative mechanisms. While gene replacement strategies have shown promise for recessive RP, they are ineffective for dominant disorders due to persistent mutant protein interference.  

Allele-specific genomic ablation using CRISPR/Cas9 provides a solution by selectively disrupting the mutant allele. In transgenic S334ter rats, which model human class-I RHO mutations, the mutant protein (RHO S334) is mislocalized and causes photoreceptor toxicity. The invention hypothesizes that selective ablation of Rho S334 would eliminate toxic protein accumulation while allowing wild-type RHO (Rho WT) to maintain normal function.  

CRISPR/Cas9 disruption of Rho S334 is achieved by targeting a PAM sequence unique to the mutant allele. The system preserves unabated function of Rho WT, as even reduced wild-type expression (10-50% of normal levels) is sufficient to maintain photoreceptor viability.  

### Genomic Editing  

Genomic editing involves site-specific chromosomal integration or disruption of target sequences. Therapeutic gene cassettes can be designed to disrupt specific alleles, correct genetic mutations, or insert functional transgenes. Prior gene editing technologies, including zinc finger nucleases (ZFNs) and transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALENs), were limited by complex protein engineering requirements and reduced efficiency.  

The CRISPR-Cas system offers advantages including simplicity, high efficiency, and multiplexing capability. The system requires a guide RNA (gRNA) complementary to the target sequence and a CRISPR-associated (Cas) protein, such as Cas9. The gRNA directs Cas9 to the target DNA, where it induces a DSB adjacent to a PAM sequence.  

DSBs are repaired by either NHEJ, which often introduces indel mutations that disrupt gene function, or homology-directed repair (HDR) when a donor template is provided. Multiple gRNAs can be used simultaneously to edit several genetic loci.  

AAV vectors are preferred for in vivo delivery due to their safety profile and tropism for retinal cells. The AAV-CRISPR system can be optimized using smaller Cas9 orthologs like SaCas9, which offers efficient packaging into AAV particles.  

In the S334ter rat model, CRISPR/Cas9 gene correction prevents RP by ablating the mutant Rho S334 allele prophylactically. The method represents a novel treatment for dominant RP by selectively eliminating the disease-causing allele while preserving wild-type gene function.  

Therapeutic administration involves subretinal injection of AAV vectors encoding SaCas9 and allele-specific gRNAs. The PAM sequence (5′-TGG-3′) in Rho S334 enables discrimination from Rho WT (5′-TGC-3′), ensuring specific targeting.  

The invention treats diseases caused by single gene mutations, particularly dominant mutations where the mutant allele exerts a toxic gain-of-function. Recessive mutations may also be addressed by gene correction via HDR.  

For retinitis pigmentosa, the method targets mutations in RHO or peripherin (RDS) genes. Therapeutic vectors are delivered in vivo to generate DSBs in mutant alleles, with repair occurring via NHEJ or HDR.  

In vitro genomic editing methods similarly involve generating DSBs in target sequences, with applications in cell-based therapies or ex vivo gene correction.  

Compositions of the invention include CRISPR proteins complexed with gRNAs, or vectors encoding these components. gRNAs are designed to maximize on-target efficiency while minimizing off-target effects.  

### Animal Model  

The S334ter-line-3 rat model exhibits rapid photoreceptor degeneration resembling human class-I RHO mutations. These rats express a truncated RHO S334 protein that mislocalizes to photoreceptor cell bodies, causing toxicity and apoptosis.  

### CRISPR/Cas9 Constructs, Generally  

CRISPR/Cas9 is particularly suited for targeting RHO mutations due to the ability to design allele-specific gRNAs. The system induces targeted DSBs, leading to mutant allele disruption while sparing wild-type RHO.  

### CRISPR/Cas9 Constructs, Specifically  

The invention provides a gRNA targeting the RHO S334 mutation (gRNA TRGT), designed to bind upstream of the mutant-specific PAM. The gRNA TRGT sequence differs by one nucleotide from the wild-type sequence, ensuring allele discrimination.  

### Methods  

CRISPR/Cas9/gRNA constructs are delivered via subretinal injection in neonatal S334ter-3 rats. mCherry reporter expression confirms transfection efficiency. Retinal cells are sorted and analyzed by immunohistochemistry and genomic sequencing.  

Results demonstrate significant photoreceptor preservation in gRNA TRGT-treated retinas compared to controls. Visual function evaluation by optokinetic response shows improved acuity in treated animals.  

Cas9/gRNA vector design incorporates constitutive expression of SaCas9 and gRNA TRGT under appropriate promoters. Cleavage efficiency is determined by indel frequency at the target locus.  

Animal procedures include subretinal injection, electroporation, and longitudinal phenotypic assessment. Immunofluorescent staining and confocal microscopy evaluate photoreceptor survival and protein localization.  

Statistical analysis confirms the significance of therapeutic outcomes. Differential distribution of RHO S334 versus RHO WT is assessed by immunolabeling.  

gRNA vector design targets the S334 mutation while avoiding off-target effects. In vitro and in vivo experiments confirm Rho S334 disruption without affecting Rho WT.  

Results demonstrate that CRISPR/Cas9/gRNA TRGT treatment effectively ablates the mutant allele, preserving photoreceptors and visual function. Future investigations will optimize delivery methods and assess long-term efficacy.  

Histological evaluation shows rescue of photoreceptor layers in treated retinas. Genomic sequencing confirms allele-specific indels at the Rho S334 locus.  

Photoreceptor density and synaptic connectivity are preserved following Rho S334 ablation. Retinal phenotype analysis reveals maintained outer segment structure in treated regions.  

Quantitative assessment shows significantly higher photoreceptor density and visual acuity in gRNA TRGT-treated eyes compared to controls.  

Alternative delivery methods, including dual AAV vectors, exosomes, and liposome-encapsulated Cas9 protein/gRNA complexes, are proposed to enhance retinal transfection.  

The invention provides proof-of-principle for in vivo allele-specific ablation as therapy for dominant retinal disorders. Selective elimination of Rho S334 prevents retinal degeneration and preserves vision.  

Transition zones between treated and untreated retinal areas demonstrate the spatial extent of therapeutic effect. Long-term photoreceptor survival in degenerative environments suggests durable correction.  

This represents the first in vivo functional correction of an inherited dominant mutation using CRISPR. The approach can be extended to other adRP-linked mutations with targetable PAM sequences.  

Engineering Cas proteins with altered PAM specificities or using non-canonical Cas enzymes may broaden the range of treatable mutations. The invention thus expands therapeutic options for degenerative retinal diseases and other genetic disorders.  

Various embodiments and alternatives of the invention are contemplated, including different CRISPR systems, delivery methods, and target genes for treating inherited retinal diseases.
