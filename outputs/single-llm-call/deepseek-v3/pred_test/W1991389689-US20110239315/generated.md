# DESCRIPTION  

## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention relates generally to the field of molecular biology and genetic engineering. More specifically, the invention pertains to engineered transcription activator-like (TAL) effector proteins capable of programmable DNA-binding specificity and methods for their construction and use in modulating gene expression across diverse biological systems, including plant and mammalian cells. The invention further encompasses compositions comprising these engineered proteins, nucleic acids encoding them, and methods for their application in genome editing, gene regulation, and therapeutic interventions.  

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION  

Phytopathogenic bacteria such as Xanthomonas employ specialized virulence factors called TAL effectors to manipulate host gene expression during infection. These proteins contain central repeat domains that confer sequence-specific DNA binding through hypervariable regions (HVRs) within each repeat unit. The modular nature of TAL effectors enables specific recognition of extended DNA sequences, with each repeat unit binding a single nucleotide base pair.  

Current methods for creating custom DNA-binding proteins, such as zinc finger nucleases, suffer from limitations in predictability and modularity. While zinc finger domains can be engineered to recognize specific sequences, the complex interplay between adjacent fingers makes target site prediction unreliable. Furthermore, the construction of zinc finger arrays with novel specificities remains technically challenging.  

The present invention overcomes these limitations by leveraging the intrinsic modularity of TAL effector repeat domains. Each repeat unit functions independently to recognize a single base pair, enabling straightforward prediction of DNA-binding specificity based on repeat composition. The invention provides methods for constructing artificial TAL proteins with predetermined DNA-binding specificities, along with compositions and applications thereof in diverse biological systems.  

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION  

The invention provides a method for producing a polypeptide capable of selectively recognizing a target DNA sequence. The method involves assembling a repeat domain comprising multiple repeat units, where each repeat unit contains a hypervariable region that determines recognition of a specific base pair. The modular architecture allows combination of repeat units in any order to create polypeptides with custom DNA-binding specificities.  

The invention further encompasses polypeptides produced by this method, including fusion proteins combining the repeat domain with functional domains such as transcriptional activation domains (e.g., VP16), nuclear localization signals, or nuclease domains (e.g., FokI). DNA sequences encoding these polypeptides are provided, along with host cells and organisms transformed with such sequences.  

Methods for targeted modulation of gene expression are disclosed, comprising introducing into cells a polypeptide of the invention designed to bind a specific target sequence in or near a gene of interest. The invention further provides methods for directed modification of target DNA sequences, including creation of double-strand breaks to stimulate homologous recombination or other DNA repair processes.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION  

The invention is described with reference to the accompanying drawings and specific embodiments, although it will be understood that numerous variations and modifications may be made while remaining within the scope of the invention.  

A "repeat domain" as used herein refers to a polypeptide region comprising multiple tandem repeat units derived from TAL effectors. Each "repeat unit" typically consists of approximately 34 amino acids, with positions 12 and 13 forming a hypervariable region that determines base pair specificity. The "recognition code" refers to the correlation between specific amino acids at these positions and their corresponding recognized DNA base pairs, such as NI for A, HD for C, NN for G, and NG for T.  

An "effector" in the context of this invention refers to a polypeptide comprising a repeat domain capable of sequence-specific DNA binding, which may be naturally occurring or artificially constructed. The term "naturally occurring" refers to polypeptides or nucleic acids as found in nature, while "modulating expression" encompasses both increasing (activating) and decreasing (inhibiting) gene expression levels.  

The invention provides various "regulatory domains" that may be combined with the repeat domain, including transcriptional activation domains (e.g., VP16, GAL4), repression domains, and nuclease domains. These may be derived from diverse sources including viral proteins (e.g., herpes simplex virus VP16), yeast transcription factors, or bacterial restriction enzymes (e.g., FokI).  

A "gene" is defined as a DNA sequence encoding a functional product, including coding sequences and associated regulatory elements. The "target gene" refers to any gene whose expression or sequence is to be modified using the methods of the invention. In plant applications, the invention may be used to modulate genes in any plant tissue or cell type, creating "genetically modified plants" with altered traits.  

The DNA-binding specificity of engineered polypeptides is determined by the order and composition of repeat units in the repeat domain. The invention provides methods for constructing such polypeptides using a modular approach, exemplified by the "Golden TAL Technology" which employs type IIS restriction enzymes (e.g., BsaI, BpiI) for seamless assembly of repeat units.  

The invention further encompasses polynucleotide molecules encoding the subject polypeptides, including DNA, RNA, and analogs with modified backbone residues or linkages. These molecules may be delivered to cells as expression cassettes comprising operably linked transcriptional and translational control elements, or as pre-formed polypeptides introduced via various delivery methods.  

Applications of the invention include but are not limited to: targeted activation or repression of endogenous genes; creation of double-strand breaks at specific genomic loci to stimulate genome editing; labeling or isolation of specific DNA sequences; and construction of inducible gene expression systems. The engineered polypeptides may be used in diverse host systems including plant, mammalian, yeast, and bacterial cells.  

## EXAMPLES  

### Example 1  

The DNA-binding specificity of TAL effectors was systematically analyzed by examining repeat units from various Xanthomonas strains. AvrBs3 repeat units and their hypervariable amino acids were correlated with specific base pairs in target DNA sequences. A model of recognition specificity was developed and validated using AvrBs3 repeat deletion derivatives.  

Analysis of UPA-boxes in AvrBs3-induced pepper genes revealed conserved sequence patterns matching the predicted specificity. Similar analyses were performed for TAL effectors Hax3 and Hax4, confirming the recognition code through reporter gene activation assays. The importance of the first nucleotide in target sequences was demonstrated through permutation experiments.  

Artificial effectors containing 0.5 to 15.5 repeat units were constructed and tested for promoter activation capacity. These experiments established that at least 10.5 repeats are required for maximal activity, while effectors with different repeat numbers (e.g., 11.5 vs. 21.5 repeats) can show comparable transcriptional activation.  

### Example 2  

Functional analysis of hypervariable region (HVR) motifs was performed to identify repeat units specific for each nucleotide. Bioinformatic analysis of HVRs from diverse TAL effectors enabled prediction of DNA-binding specificities, which were experimentally validated using reporter assays. This work confirmed the modular nature of repeat units and their neighbor-independent function in DNA recognition.  

### Example 3  

The Golden Gate cloning strategy was developed to overcome challenges posed by the repetitive nature of TAL effector sequences. This method employs type IIS restriction enzyme BsaI in a two-stage ligation process involving insert plasmids, intermediate vectors, and acceptor vectors. Repeat units were designed with BsaI recognition sites allowing precise assembly while preserving amino acid sequences.  

The system enables construction of effectors with 1-24 repeat units through modular assembly, with the final array transferred to expression constructs via recombination. This approach maintains consistent codon usage across repeats and allows error-free assembly through carefully designed restriction overhangs differing at multiple positions.  

### Example 4  

Target DNA-specific nucleases were produced by fusing custom repeat domains to the FokI nuclease domain. In vitro assays demonstrated that these fusion proteins could create double-strand breaks at specified target sequences, as verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. The activity depended on both the DNA-binding specificity of the repeat domain and the catalytic function of the nuclease domain.  

### Example 5  

Recognition specificities were determined for additional amino acid pairs beyond the canonical NI, HD, NN, and NG repeats. DNA binding domains incorporating these novel specificities were constructed using Golden Gate Cloning and tested in binding assays. The results expanded the recognition code and enabled design of polypeptides with enhanced specificity and affinity for diverse target sequences.  

These examples demonstrate the broad utility of the invention for creating custom DNA-binding proteins with programmable specificity, along with methods for their construction and applications in genome engineering and gene regulation across biological systems.
