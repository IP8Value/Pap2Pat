## DESCRIPTION  

### TECHNICAL FIELD  

The present invention relates generally to semiconductor materials and fabrication methods. More specifically, the invention pertains to methods for growing single-crystal III-V semiconductor structures on amorphous substrates through templated liquid-phase (TLP) crystal growth techniques. The disclosed methods enable direct formation of optoelectronic-quality single-crystalline III-V semiconductors with controlled geometries on various substrate materials without requiring lattice-matched crystalline substrates.  

### BACKGROUND  

III-V semiconductors have become increasingly important materials for electronic and optoelectronic devices due to their superior electronic properties compared to silicon. These compound semiconductors, formed from elements in groups III and V of the periodic table, exhibit high electron mobility, direct bandgaps, and other advantageous characteristics that make them ideal for high-speed electronics, photodetectors, light-emitting diodes, and solar cells. However, conventional growth methods for III-V semiconductors typically require expensive single-crystal substrates that lattice-match the growing semiconductor to achieve high-quality crystalline material. This requirement significantly limits substrate choice and increases manufacturing costs.  

Traditional approaches for growing III-V semiconductors include molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) and metalorganic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD), both of which require high temperatures and lattice-matched crystalline substrates. These methods also typically produce polycrystalline material when attempted on amorphous substrates. Alternative approaches such as vapor-liquid-solid (VLS) growth of nanowires can produce single crystals on amorphous substrates but lack control over crystal geometry and positioning. There remains a significant need for methods that can produce high-quality single-crystal III-V semiconductors with controlled geometries on arbitrary substrates at lower temperatures and costs.  

### SUMMARY  

The present invention provides a method for fabricating single-crystal III-V semiconductor structures through templated liquid-phase (TLP) crystal growth. In one embodiment, the method involves patterning a boron group element (such as indium) on an amorphous substrate with a nucleation layer, encapsulating the patterned element with a ceramic material (such as silicon oxide), and heating the structure in the presence of a nitrogen group element gas (such as phosphine) to grow single-crystal semiconductor structures.  

An alternative embodiment involves growing doped III-V semiconductor structures by introducing dopant gases during the growth process. The method enables fabrication of single-crystal semiconductor structures comprising binary, ternary, and quaternary III-V semiconductors with controlled geometries. The resulting single-crystal structures can be incorporated into various semiconductor devices, including MOSFETs with top gates.  

### DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

The following detailed description provides a comprehensive explanation of the templated liquid-phase (TLP) crystal growth process for fabricating single-crystal III-V semiconductor structures on amorphous substrates. The description includes specific embodiments, process parameters, material choices, and characterization results that demonstrate the effectiveness and versatility of the invention.  

Prior approaches to growing single-crystal semiconductors on amorphous substrates have faced significant challenges. Conventional epitaxial growth methods require lattice-matched crystalline substrates to achieve high-quality single crystals. Non-epitaxial methods often produce polycrystalline material with poor electronic properties. The TLP crystal growth process disclosed herein overcomes these limitations by utilizing a templated approach that confines crystal nucleation and growth to predetermined locations while maintaining single-crystal quality.  

The TLP crystal growth process begins with preparing an amorphous substrate, such as silicon dioxide on silicon or borosilicate glass. A resist material is applied and patterned using lithographic techniques to define the desired semiconductor structure geometry. The resist dimensions typically range from nanometers to micrometers depending on the target application. The patterned resist creates a template that will determine the final shape of the semiconductor structure.  

A nucleation layer is then deposited on the substrate within the patterned areas. In one embodiment, the nucleation layer comprises molybdenum oxide (MoOx) with a thickness between 1 and 10 nanometers. The nucleation layer promotes subsequent crystal formation while maintaining the amorphous nature of the underlying substrate. Following nucleation layer deposition, a boron group element is deposited within the patterned areas. For indium phosphide (InP) growth, indium metal is used with thicknesses typically ranging from tens to hundreds of nanometers depending on the desired final semiconductor structure dimensions.  

The structure is then encapsulated with a ceramic material such as silicon oxide (SiOx) with thicknesses between 10 and 100 nanometers. The ceramic material serves multiple purposes: it mechanically confines the boron group element during subsequent processing, provides a diffusion path for the nitrogen group element, and maintains the predefined geometry of the growing crystal. After ceramic material deposition, the resist is removed, leaving the boron group element encapsulated by ceramic material on the substrate.  

Additional ceramic material may be deposited to completely cover the boron group element and ensure proper confinement during crystal growth. The structure is then heated in a furnace at temperatures between 500°C and 535°C in the presence of a nitrogen group element gas, such as phosphine (PH3) for InP growth, along with hydrogen gas. The heating transforms the boron group element into a liquid phase while remaining mechanically confined by the ceramic template.  

During heating, the nitrogen group element diffuses through the ceramic material and supersaturates the liquid boron group element, precipitating out as a nucleus of the III-V semiconductor compound. A key feature of this growth mode is the formation of a nitrogen group element depletion zone around each growing nucleus that prevents further nucleation within the template area. For properly sized templates, this depletion zone ensures that only a single crystal forms within each predefined area.  

The growth process typically continues for 10 to 20 minutes, during which time the single-crystal semiconductor structure forms with the same geometry as the original boron group element pattern. The resulting single-crystal structure maintains excellent crystallinity as confirmed by various characterization techniques including electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and X-ray diffraction spectroscopy.  

The dimensions of the resulting single-crystal semiconductor structure can be precisely controlled through the initial patterning process. Structures with lateral dimensions ranging from 150 nanometers to 20 micrometers have been successfully demonstrated while maintaining single-crystal quality. The thickness of the semiconductor structure corresponds to the initial boron group element thickness and can range from tens to hundreds of nanometers.  

Following crystal growth, the ceramic encapsulation material may be optionally removed using appropriate etching processes if needed for subsequent device fabrication. However, in many applications the ceramic material can remain as part of the final structure, serving as a dielectric layer or protective coating.  

The TLP crystal growth process offers several significant advantages over conventional III-V semiconductor growth techniques. First, it eliminates the requirement for lattice-matched crystalline substrates, enabling growth on a wide variety of amorphous materials including silicon dioxide, glass, and potentially flexible plastic substrates. Second, the process provides excellent control over semiconductor structure geometry through the initial patterning step, allowing creation of complex shapes and arrays with single-crystal quality. Third, the relatively low growth temperatures (500-535°C) compared to conventional epitaxial growth methods reduce energy costs and enable compatibility with temperature-sensitive substrates.  

The TLP crystal growth technique is highly compatible with traditional semiconductor device processing technology. The grown single-crystal structures can be directly incorporated into standard device fabrication flows for transistors, photodetectors, and other electronic and optoelectronic devices. The process also offers significant scalability advantages, as demonstrated by successful growth across full 4-inch wafers using simple furnace designs.  

In one specific embodiment, the method is used to fabricate single-crystal indium phosphide (InP) structures. The TLP growth of InP begins with lithographically patterning indium metal onto a silicon/silicon dioxide or glass substrate with a thin (1-10 nm) molybdenum oxide nucleation layer. The indium is encapsulated by evaporated silicon oxide, and growth is carried out in a low-pressure furnace at 500-535°C in the presence of phosphine and hydrogen gas.  

At the growth temperature, indium transforms into the liquid phase but remains mechanically confined by the silicon oxide template. Phosphorus diffuses through the silicon oxide cap and supersaturates the liquid indium, precipitating out as an InP nucleus. The phosphorous depletion zone that forms around each growing nucleus prevents further nucleation, enabling single-crystalline InP growth in user-defined geometries.  

Arrays of InP crystals shaped into circles, rings, squares, and other geometries have been successfully grown using this method. The stoichiometry of the films is confirmed by electron dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) to be 1:1 In:P ratio. X-ray diffraction spectroscopy displays only peaks corresponding to zincblende InP, indicating complete conversion of indium to InP. Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) mapping confirms that each individual pattern is a single crystal (excluding twinning) with preferential texturing in the (1 0 n) direction where n ranges between 1 and 2.  

The effect of growth conditions and InP feature size on crystal quality has been systematically studied. For InP circles with diameters varying from 3 to 20 micrometers, the number of grains per circle follows a quadratic relationship with diameter. The number of grains can be maintained near unity even for 20 micrometer diameters by optimizing phosphine partial pressure during growth. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) examination clearly shows the crystalline InP lattice sitting on top of the amorphous substrate with the nucleation layer in between, confirming the non-epitaxial nature of TLP crystal growth.  

The versatility of the TLP crystal growth technique has been demonstrated by creating complex patterns including micron-scale text and logos in crystalline InP. Geometric features with dimensions as small as 150 nanometers have been successfully fabricated while maintaining single-crystal quality. The technique also enables growth of multilayer structures by starting with substrates consisting of multilayer indium/silicon oxide stacks, opening possibilities for monolithic 3D electronics.  

A critical component of semiconductor growth is the ability to control doping concentrations to tune electrical and optical properties. The TLP crystal growth process enables in situ doping by introducing dopant gases during growth. For example, germanium doping of InP has been achieved by introducing germane (GeH4) gas into the growth chamber. Photoluminescence spectra show significant blue-shifting as germanium partial pressure increases, indicating electron concentrations up to approximately 1.3 × 10^19 cm^-3, near the upper limit for InP. Urbach tail parameters extracted from photoluminescence spectra indicate crystal quality comparable to commercial InP wafers at equivalent doping levels.  

The electronic quality of TLP-grown InP has been demonstrated through fabrication and characterization of MOSFET devices. Long-channel Schottky n-type MOSFETs with top gates exhibit excellent performance characteristics including ON-current of 120 μA/μm at 2V bias, ON/OFF current ratio >10^5, and peak extrinsic transconductance of 100 μS/μm. Effective electron mobility extracted from device simulations reaches 675 cm^2/V·s, comparable to unpassivated InP nanowire MOSFETs reported in literature.  

Top-gated InP photo-MOSFETs fabricated using the same process demonstrate strong photoresponse with peak responsivity of ~700 A/W and specific detectivity reaching ~8.4 × 10^11 Jones at room temperature, comparable to state-of-the-art epitaxial InGaAs detectors. These results confirm that TLP-grown InP maintains excellent electronic and optoelectronic properties despite being grown on amorphous substrates.  

The TLP crystal growth technique is not limited to InP but can be extended to other III-V semiconductors. Proof-of-concept demonstrations have shown successful growth of gallium phosphide (GaP) and indium antimonide (InSb) using similar methods. The thermodynamic and kinetic principles underlying TLP growth suggest broad applicability to other binary, ternary, and quaternary III-V compounds.  

In summary, the templated liquid-phase crystal growth method provides a versatile technique for fabricating high-quality single-crystal III-V semiconductor structures on amorphous substrates. The process enables precise control over semiconductor geometry while maintaining excellent crystallinity and electronic properties. The elimination of lattice-matched substrate requirements and relatively low growth temperatures offer significant advantages for commercial applications. The method is compatible with standard device processing flows and demonstrates excellent scalability potential for volume manufacturing.  

The resulting single-crystal III-V semiconductor structures can be incorporated into various electronic and optoelectronic devices including but not limited to field-effect transistors, photodetectors, light-emitting diodes, and solar cells. The ability to grow these high-performance semiconductor materials directly on amorphous substrates opens new possibilities for heterogeneous integration of III-V devices with silicon technology and other material systems.
