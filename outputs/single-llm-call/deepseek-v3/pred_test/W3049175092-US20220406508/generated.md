# DESCRIPTION  

## BACKGROUND  

The present invention relates to the field of spintronics and magneto-ionics, specifically concerning the Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interaction (DMI) in magnetic systems. The DMI is an antisymmetric exchange interaction arising in systems lacking inversion symmetry, first proposed by Dzyaloshinskii and later formalized by Moriya. This interaction plays a crucial role in stabilizing chiral spin textures, such as spin spirals, skyrmions, and chiral domain walls, which are of significant interest for next-generation spintronic devices.  

Experimental efforts have primarily focused on interfacial DMI in systems where magnetic layers are adjacent to heavy metals, such as hafnium, tantalum, tungsten, iridium, platinum, palladium, or ruthenium. These studies have revealed that the magnitude of DMI varies significantly depending on the degree of hybridization between 5d and 3d orbitals near the Fermi level. Additionally, sizable DMI has been observed at certain metal/oxide interfaces, suggesting a correlation between DMI and orbital angular momentum.  

Despite these advances, current approaches exhibit limitations, including the reliance on rare and expensive heavy metals and the inability to dynamically control DMI in a reversible manner. There is a pressing need for improved techniques that leverage more abundant materials and enable tunable DMI for practical applications in spintronics and magneto-ionics.  

## SUMMARY  

The present invention provides embodiments for inducing and controlling DMI through chemisorbed species on ferromagnetic surfaces. A key aspect involves the oxygen-induced DMI, where chemisorbed oxygen on ferromagnetic layers generates a substantial DMI comparable to that achieved with heavy metals. The magnitude and sign of the DMI can be tuned by adjusting the oxygen coverage on the surface, offering a versatile platform for manipulating chiral spin textures.  

The invention further describes an apparatus for generating DMI, comprising ferromagnetic materials such as nickel or cobalt, with a substance chemisorbed on their surface, such as oxygen, hydrogen, or organic molecules. The DMI can be controlled by varying the thickness of non-magnetic spacer layers (e.g., palladium or tungsten) or by adjusting the coverage of the chemisorbed substance.  

Specific embodiments include the generation of skyrmions by changing the coverage of chemisorbed oxygen, as well as the control of perpendicular magnetic anisotropy (PMA) through chemisorbed species. The invention also details methods for generating PMA by selecting appropriate ferromagnetic materials and chemisorbed substances.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

### DMI and PMA Induced by Chemisorbed Species on Ferromagnets  

The invention leverages the discovery that chemisorbed species, such as oxygen, hydrogen, or organic molecules, can induce significant DMI and PMA in ferromagnetic systems. For instance, chemisorbed oxygen on nickel or cobalt surfaces creates a strong electric dipole moment due to charge transfer between oxygen and the ferromagnetic material. This dipole moment, in turn, generates a sizable DMI that can stabilize chiral spin textures at room temperature.  

### Oxygen Coverage-Dependent Evolution of DW Chirality  

The chirality of domain walls (DWs) in ferromagnetic films can be systematically controlled by varying the oxygen coverage. Experiments demonstrate that increasing oxygen coverage transitions DW chirality from left-handed to achiral and eventually to right-handed. This evolution is attributed to the competition between the intrinsic DMI of the ferromagnetic multilayer and the additional DMI induced by chemisorbed oxygen.  

### Tuning Effective DMI via Spacer Layer Thickness  

The effective DMI in multilayer systems can be fine-tuned by adjusting the thickness of non-magnetic spacer layers, such as palladium or tungsten. For example, in Ni/Co/Pd/W(110) multilayers, the DMI sign switches from right-handed (tungsten-like) to left-handed (palladium-like) as the Pd layer thickness increases. This tunability provides a platform for quantifying unknown DMI contributions from newly introduced interfaces.  

### Quantification of Chemisorbed Oxygen-Induced DMI  

The DMI induced by chemisorbed oxygen is quantified by comparing the chirality transitions in systems with and without oxygen. Measurements reveal that the oxygen-induced DMI is comparable in magnitude to that achieved with heavy metals, despite oxygen's low atomic number. For instance, the DMI at the oxygen/nickel interface is approximately 0.63 ± 0.26 meV/atom for monolayer oxygen coverage.  

### Applications in Spintronics  

The large DMI induced by chemisorbed oxygen enables the tailoring of skyrmion winding numbers and domain wall types at room temperature. This capability is critical for applications such as racetrack memory and logic devices, where precise control of spin textures is essential. Additionally, the reversible adsorption and desorption of oxygen or hydrogen allow for dynamic switching of chirality, opening new avenues for gas-sensing and magneto-ionic devices.  

### Experimental and Theoretical Validation  

The invention is supported by spin-polarized low-energy electron microscopy (SPLEEM) measurements and density functional theory (DFT) calculations. SPLEEM imaging directly observes the chirality transitions induced by chemisorbed oxygen, while DFT calculations predict the DMI strength and its dependence on spacer layer thickness.  

### Device Embodiments  

A specific device embodiment includes a multilayer heterostructure with a reservoir layer (e.g., Pd or W), a ferromagnetic layer (e.g., Ni or Co), and a chemisorbed species (e.g., oxygen or hydrogen). The device enables reversible control of DMI and PMA through external stimuli, such as gas exposure or electric fields, facilitating applications in artificial synapses and neuromorphic computing.  

### Conclusion  

The invention demonstrates that chemisorbed species, particularly oxygen, can induce substantial DMI and PMA in ferromagnetic systems, rivaling the effects of heavy metals. This discovery provides a cost-effective and tunable platform for designing spintronic devices, with potential applications in memory, logic, and sensing technologies.
