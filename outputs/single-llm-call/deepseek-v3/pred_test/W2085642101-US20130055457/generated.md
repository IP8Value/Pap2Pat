## FIELD OF THE DISCLOSURE  

The present disclosure relates generally to plant biotechnology and crop improvement. More specifically, the disclosure provides compositions and methods for increasing crop yield through transgenic manipulation of yield-related genes and their allelic variants. The disclosure encompasses nucleic acid constructs, expression cassettes, transgenic plants, and methods for modulating plant growth and development to improve agronomic performance.  

## BACKGROUND OF THE DISCLOSURE  

The domestication of plants has been a cornerstone of human civilization, enabling the transition from hunter-gatherer societies to agricultural communities. Over millennia, selective breeding has generated cultivated varieties with improved yield, quality, and adaptability compared to their wild progenitors. Phenotypic variation in natural plant populations provides the raw material for breeding programs, with traits such as plant height, flowering time, and seed size showing continuous variation due to polygenic inheritance.  

Modern agriculture faces the challenge of meeting increasing global food demand while contending with limited arable land and changing climatic conditions. Traditional breeding approaches, while successful, are often time-consuming and limited by the genetic variation present within sexually compatible species. The identification of specific genes controlling yield-related traits has opened new avenues for crop improvement through biotechnology.  

Transgenic manipulation of genes involved in plant growth and development has demonstrated potential for enhancing agricultural productivity. Previous work has shown that overexpression of certain genes can lead to increased organ size, improved stress tolerance, or altered developmental timing. However, the effects of transgenes on complex agronomic traits such as yield are often inconsistent across different genetic backgrounds and environmental conditions.  

The concept of favorable alleles and heterosis (hybrid vigor) has been central to maize breeding programs. Elite inbred lines are typically divided into heterotic groups, and crosses between lines from different groups often produce hybrids with superior performance compared to either parent. While the molecular basis of heterosis remains incompletely understood, it is clear that specific allelic combinations can result in improved phenotypic performance.  

Breeding selection of high-yielding hybrids has traditionally relied on phenotypic evaluation across multiple environments. This empirical approach has successfully increased maize yields by approximately 1-2% per year over several decades. However, the integration of molecular genetics with traditional breeding holds promise for accelerating genetic gain. Understanding how specific alleles and their interactions contribute to yield improvement can inform both conventional breeding and transgenic strategies.  

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE DISCLOSURE  

The present disclosure provides novel approaches for optimizing transgene efficacy in crop plants. Building upon the observation that natural allelic variation contributes to yield improvement in conventional breeding, the disclosure describes methods for identifying and utilizing functional allelic variants of yield-related genes.  

A key aspect involves creating transgenic plants with heterozygous allelic combinations of yield improvement genes. This approach mimics the heterotic allelic combinations that have been selected through conventional breeding, potentially leading to more robust and consistent transgene performance. The disclosure demonstrates that engineered heterozygous combinations can provide greater yield improvement than either allele alone, particularly under stress conditions.  

The methods can be applied to genes from other plant species, facilitating the transfer of beneficial traits across taxonomic boundaries. The disclosure provides compositions including polynucleotides encoding yield improvement polypeptides, DNA constructs for plant transformation, and expression cassettes for modulating gene expression in specific tissues or developmental stages.  

Various plant parts containing the disclosed nucleic acids are provided, including seeds, pollen, ovules, and vegetative propagules. The disclosure encompasses methods for modulating yield improvement sequences in plants, including overexpression, suppression, and targeted modification approaches.

"Selectively hybridizes" refers to the ability of a nucleic acid to form a stable duplex with a complementary sequence under defined hybridization conditions while not forming stable duplexes with non-complementary sequences.  

"Stringent conditions" for hybridization refer to conditions under which nucleic acids with high sequence similarity will hybridize while those with lower similarity will not. Stringency can be low, moderate, or high depending on factors such as temperature, salt concentration, and presence of denaturing agents.  

Low stringency conditions might include hybridization at 42°C in 5× SSC, 0.1% SDS, 5× Denhardt's solution, and 50% formamide, followed by washing at 42°C in 2× SSC and 0.1% SDS.  

Moderate stringency conditions might include hybridization at 50°C in 5× SSC, 0.1% SDS, 5× Denhardt's solution, and 50% formamide, followed by washing at 50°C in 1× SSC and 0.1% SDS.  

High stringency conditions might include hybridization at 65°C in 5× SSC, 0.1% SDS, 5× Denhardt's solution, and 50% formamide, followed by washing at 65°C in 0.1× SSC and 0.1% SDS.  

The melting temperature (Tm) is the temperature at which 50% of a nucleic acid duplex dissociates into single strands. Tm can be calculated using formulas accounting for factors such as GC content, length, and salt concentration.  

Adjustment of Tm can be achieved by modifying hybridization or wash conditions, particularly temperature and salt concentration. Higher temperatures or lower salt concentrations increase stringency by requiring greater sequence similarity for stable hybridization.  

Hybridization and wash conditions can be optimized empirically to achieve desired specificity. Computer programs are available to predict optimal conditions based on sequence characteristics.  

A "transgenic plant" contains a heterologous polynucleotide stably integrated into its genome, which may be transmitted to progeny through normal Mendelian inheritance. The heterologous polynucleotide may be integrated into nuclear, chloroplast, or mitochondrial DNA.  

Integration of heterologous polynucleotides can occur through various mechanisms including homologous recombination, non-homologous end joining, or site-specific recombination.  

Transmission of heterologous polynucleotides to progeny follows standard genetic inheritance patterns, though factors such as insertion location and copy number may influence segregation ratios.  

"Transgenic" excludes plants modified solely through conventional breeding methods or naturally occurring genetic events such as spontaneous mutations or horizontal gene transfer.  

A "vector" is a nucleic acid molecule capable of transporting another nucleic acid to which it has been linked. An "expression vector" contains regulatory sequences necessary for expression of inserted coding sequences in a host cell.  

A "reference sequence" serves as a basis for sequence comparison, typically a known sequence from a database or a wild-type sequence.  

A "comparison window" refers to a contiguous segment of a sequence being compared to a reference sequence where an alignment is performed.  

"Sequence identity" refers to the percentage of identical residues in an alignment between two sequences over a specified comparison window. Percentage of sequence identity is calculated by dividing the number of identical residues by the total number of residues in the comparison window and multiplying by 100.  

Methods of sequence alignment are well-known in the art and include global alignment methods (e.g., Needleman-Wunsch) and local alignment methods (e.g., Smith-Waterman).  

Local homology algorithms identify regions of similarity between sequences without requiring global alignment.  

Homology alignment algorithms such as BLAST use heuristic methods to identify statistically significant matches between sequences.  

Search for similarity methods typically involve creating a matrix of possible alignments and identifying the optimal path through this matrix based on scoring systems that reward matches and penalize mismatches and gaps.  

Computerized implementations of alignment algorithms are widely available, including the CLUSTAL program for multiple sequence alignment.  

The PileUp program creates multiple sequence alignments using a progressive pairwise alignment approach.  

The BLAST family of programs (e.g., BLASTN, BLASTP) provides rapid sequence comparison capabilities using heuristic methods.  

The GAP program implements the Needleman-Wunsch algorithm for global alignment with user-defined gap creation and extension penalties.  

Gap creation penalty refers to the cost assigned to initiating a gap in an alignment, while gap extension penalty refers to the cost assigned for each additional residue in an existing gap.  

Figures of merit for alignments include percent identity, percent similarity, E-value, bit score, and alignment score.  

Sequence similarity refers to the degree of relatedness between sequences based on identical or functionally equivalent residues.  

Low-complexity filter programs can mask regions of sequences that might produce spurious matches due to simple repeats or compositional bias rather than true homology.  

Conservative substitutions in protein sequences are amino acid replacements that maintain similar physicochemical properties (e.g., replacing leucine with isoleucine).  

"Substantial identity" for nucleic acids typically means at least 70% sequence identity over at least 50 nucleotides, while for proteins it typically means at least 60% sequence identity over at least 30 amino acids.  

Hybridization under stringent conditions can serve as an alternative criterion for defining related nucleic acids when sequence identity is borderline.  

Immunological cross-reactivity between proteins can indicate structural similarity even when sequence identity is modest.

### Nucleic Acids  

Yield improvement polynucleotides include sequences encoding proteins involved in growth regulation, stress response, resource allocation, or developmental timing. These polynucleotides may be derived from the same species (cisgenic) or different species (transgenic).  

Codon preferences for expression in maize favor codons ending in G or C, particularly for highly expressed genes. Codon optimization can enhance expression of heterologous genes in maize.  

Specific identities of polynucleotides and polypeptides include SEQ ID NOs provided in the sequence listing, representing various alleles and variants of yield improvement genes.  

Construction of nucleic acids can be achieved using standard molecular biology techniques such as PCR, restriction enzyme digestion and ligation, or synthetic techniques including gene synthesis.  

Cloning vectors, expression vectors, adapters, and linkers are well-known tools for nucleic acid manipulation. Exemplary vectors include pUC derivatives, pBR322 derivatives, and binary vectors for plant transformation.  

Direct chemical synthesis of nucleic acids is available through commercial providers, allowing construction of genes up to several kilobases in length.  

Single-stranded oligonucleotides can be converted to double-stranded DNA through polymerase-mediated extension or annealing of complementary strands.  

Regulation of translational efficiency can be achieved through 5' untranslated region (UTR) elements that influence ribosome binding and initiation.  

Modification of codon usage for expression in desired hosts involves replacing rare codons with host-preferred codons while maintaining the encoded amino acid sequence.  

Analysis of codon usage frequency can be performed using software that compares a gene's codon usage to a reference set of highly expressed genes from the target organism.  

Sequence shuffling methods create libraries of variant sequences through recombination of homologous sequences, followed by selection of improved variants.  

Libraries of recombinant polynucleotides can be generated through error-prone PCR, DNA shuffling, or other directed evolution techniques.  

Selection of polynucleotides with desired characteristics can be performed through functional screens or selection markers linked to the desired phenotype.  

Properties of selected polynucleotides may include enhanced expression, improved protein folding, or altered regulatory characteristics.  

Increase in properties of selected polynucleotides can be quantified through assays measuring transcript levels, protein accumulation, or phenotypic effects.  

### Recombinant Expression Cassettes  

Recombinant expression cassettes contain all necessary elements for expression of a coding sequence in a host cell. In plants, these typically include a promoter, 5' UTR, coding sequence, and 3' termination signals.  

Construction of recombinant expression cassettes involves assembling these elements in a functional arrangement using standard cloning techniques.  

Plant expression vectors often include selectable markers for identification of transformed cells, such as antibiotic resistance genes or herbicide tolerance genes.  

Promoters for constitutive expression drive transcription in most tissues under most conditions. Examples include the cauliflower mosaic virus 35S promoter and maize ubiquitin promoter.  

Inducible promoters respond to specific stimuli such as chemicals, temperature, or light. Examples include ethanol-inducible and steroid-inducible systems.  

Tissue-specific promoters restrict expression to particular organs or cell types. Examples include root-specific, leaf-specific, and seed-specific promoters.  

Environmentally regulated promoters respond to abiotic factors such as drought, heat, or nutrient availability.  

Polyadenylation regions and 3' termination signals ensure proper mRNA processing and stability. Common examples include the nopaline synthase (NOS) terminator and cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) terminator.  

Intron sequences can increase gene expression through mechanisms such as enhanced mRNA processing or nuclear export. The maize Adh1 intron is frequently used for this purpose.  

Plant signal sequences direct proteins to specific subcellular compartments such as chloroplasts, mitochondria, or the secretory pathway.  

Signal peptides for targeting proteins may include N-terminal transit peptides or internal sorting signals recognized by the cellular trafficking machinery.  

Marker genes for selectable phenotypes allow identification of transformed cells. Common examples include genes conferring resistance to antibiotics like kanamycin or herbicides like glyphosate.  

Antibiotic resistance genes such as nptII (neomycin phosphotransferase) are widely used as selectable markers in plant transformation.  

Herbicide resistance genes such as bar (phosphinothricin acetyltransferase) or epsps (5-enolpyruvylshikimate-3-phosphate synthase) provide both selection capability and potential agronomic traits.  

### Expression of Proteins in Host Cells  

Proteins can be expressed in recombinantly engineered host cells through introduction of appropriate expression constructs. This allows production of proteins under non-natural conditions or at non-physiological levels.  

Genetically altering cells to produce proteins involves introducing nucleic acids encoding the desired proteins along with necessary regulatory elements.  

Introduction of nucleic acids into host cells can be achieved through various methods including electroporation, chemical transformation, viral transduction, or particle bombardment.  

Expression systems are available for both prokaryotic (e.g., E. coli) and eukaryotic (e.g., yeast, mammalian, plant) host cells, each with characteristic advantages.  

Operably linking DNA or cDNA to a promoter creates a functional transcription unit that can be incorporated into an expression vector.  

Typical expression vectors include elements such as an origin of replication, selectable marker, multiple cloning site, and transcription termination signals.  

Strong promoters drive high-level expression while weak promoters produce more moderate expression levels. Choice depends on the application and potential toxicity of the expressed protein.  

Modification of proteins for cloning, expression, or incorporation may include addition of affinity tags (e.g., His-tag, GST-tag), signal sequences, or protease cleavage sites.  

Expression in prokaryotes such as E. coli offers advantages of rapid growth and high yields for many proteins. Commonly used promoters include lac, trp, and T7 promoters.  

Bacterial vectors such as pET, pGEX, and pMAL series provide versatile platforms for protein expression in E. coli with various fusion tags and purification options.  

Infection with phage vector particles or transfection with naked DNA are alternative methods for introducing nucleic acids into bacterial cells.  

Expression in eukaryotes such as yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Pichia pastoris) provides post-translational modifications more similar to higher eukaryotes.  

Mammalian, insect, or plant cell expression systems may be preferred for proteins requiring complex post-translational modifications or proper folding.  

Plant transformation methods include Agrobacterium-mediated transformation and direct gene transfer methods such as particle bombardment or protoplast transformation.  

Increasing activity and/or level of yield improvement polypeptide can be achieved through overexpression, modification of regulatory elements, or stabilization of the protein.  

Methods to inhibit expression include polynucleotide-based approaches such as antisense RNA, RNA interference, or ribozymes, as well as polypeptide-based inhibition through dominant negative mutants or antibodies.  

### EXAMPLES  

#### Example 1  

Favorable alleles of maize growth enhancing genes were identified through genetic association analysis. Transgenic plants were generated to test transgene efficacy of these alleles. Vectors were constructed containing either favorable protein coding alleles or favorable promoter alleles of target genes.  

#### Example 2  

Different allele variants were molecularly stacked to create heterozygous allelic combinations in transgenic plants. These plants were evaluated for transgene efficacy compared to plants containing hemizygous or homozygous constructs. Phenotypic attributes including ear size, secondary ear growth, plant vigor, and productivity were measured and compared to non-transgenic controls.  

#### Example 3  

Sequence variation in ZmARGOS1 and ZmERECTA A genes was analyzed. Allele-specific expression regulation was examined through comparative analysis of different allelic variants.  

#### Example 4  

Immature maize embryos were prepared for transformation by particle bombardment. DNA was prepared and coated onto gold particles for bombardment. Embryos were cultured on selection medium containing hygromycin to identify transformed callus clones. Plants were regenerated from transformed callus and evaluated for increased drought tolerance and kernel-earing capacity under drought conditions.  

#### Example 5  

Immature maize embryos were infected with Agrobacterium containing transformation vectors. After co-culture, embryos were rested to eliminate Agrobacterium before selection for transformed callus. Plants were regenerated from transformed callus and analyzed for transgene integration and expression.  

#### Example 6  

The function of ZmARGOS gene was tested using transgenic plants. Transgene expression was confirmed by RT-PCR. Field evaluation of transgenic plants included measurements of plant height, biomass accumulation, stem diameter, flowering time, and grain yield. Results were confirmed in advanced generations.  

#### Example 7  

Somatic embryos were induced from soybean cotyledons and maintained as embryogenic suspension cultures. These cultures were transformed using particle gun bombardment and selected using hygromycin. Whole plants were regenerated from transformed tissue and maintained as suspension cultures for further analysis.  

#### Example 8  

Sunflower meristem tissues were transformed through microprojectile bombardment or Agrobacterium infection. Transformed shoots were grafted to seedling rootstock and acclimatized to greenhouse conditions. Transgenic sectors were identified and analyzed for ARGOS activity in leaf extracts.  

#### Example 9  

Variant nucleotide sequences of ARGOS/ERECTA were generated through site-directed mutagenesis. Conserved and variable regions were identified through protein alignment. Artificial protein sequences with 80-100% identity to wild-type were created and tested for functionality in transgenic plants.
