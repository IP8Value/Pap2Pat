## BACKGROUND

Trauma remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide, with impact-related pathologies varying widely in severity, diagnostic accuracy, and temporal manifestation. While animal models have traditionally been employed to study blunt impact injuries through endpoint assays like immunohistochemistry, these approaches suffer from significant limitations in interpreting complex injury mechanisms at the cellular level. The inherent complexity of animal systems and lack of standardized testing procedures have hindered progress in understanding post-trauma cellular damage and death.

Current in-vitro platforms attempting to study cellular injury mechanisms face substantial technical challenges in applying biologically relevant pressure forces to cell populations while maintaining tight environmental control. The human body routinely experiences linear accelerations ranging from 80-250 g during mechanical collisions such as sports impacts or military operations, leading to various traumatic injuries including blunt organ trauma and traumatic brain injury. While acceleration serves as the most common criterion for blunt injury assessment, this approach fails to account for system-size dependent effects on injury mechanisms.

Recent research has identified dynamic cavitation in the brain as a potential damage mechanism for traumatic brain injury, with studies characterizing cavitation properties in soft biomaterials under impulsive forces. However, experimental investigations of live cell responses under well-characterized acceleration-induced pressure remain severely limited due to technical challenges in maintaining consistent in vitro conditions, temporal cell probing capabilities, and high-throughput analysis of multiplexed cell populations.

Conventional wound healing assays and other in-vitro platforms often overlook critical factors such as thermal effects of electrical pulses and the need for well-controlled impact experiments. The current lack of experimental systems capable of applying precisely controlled mechanical impacts while maintaining optimal cell culture conditions has created a pressing need for improved in-vitro platforms that can reveal fundamental injury mechanisms at the cellular level.

## BRIEF DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a system and method for in vitro characterization of cellular responses to mechanical impact. The invention enables application of biologically relevant mechanical impacts to live cells while maintaining consistent culture conditions and allowing real-time observation. A key innovation involves a drop-tower based system that applies controlled acceleration profiles to cell cultures while integrating with multiplexed live cell imaging capabilities.

This multiplexed approach allows simultaneous monitoring of multiple cell populations before, during, and after impact events, providing statistically significant data on cellular injury mechanisms. The system offers several advantages over conventional methods, including precise control over impact parameters, maintenance of optimal culture conditions, and high-throughput analysis capabilities.

The in vitro platform of the present invention represents a significant advancement in trauma research by enabling well-controlled impact experiments that reveal fundamental injury mechanisms at the cellular level. The system's capabilities include applying acceleration profiles mimicking real-world trauma scenarios while continuously monitoring cell viability, growth patterns, and membrane integrity through integrated imaging technologies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### In Vitro Setup for Blunt Injury Mechanisms

The invention comprises a drop-tower based system specifically designed for applying controlled mechanical impacts to in vitro cell cultures. The system integrates a conventional drop tower impact mechanism with specialized cell culture chambers, high-speed imaging capabilities, and precise sensor arrays for quantifying impact parameters. A weight is vertically lifted to predetermined heights and released to generate impacts that accelerate cell cultures in a manner simulating biological trauma scenarios.

The cell culture setup features a commercially available 35-mm petri dish integrated with aluminum plates and a transparent silicone film, assembled using standard fasteners. This configuration addresses two major technical challenges in impact studies: environmental control and cell heterogeneity. The holder structure is designed to be sufficiently rigid to sustain impacts while preventing direct shock to the cell culture, with two strategically selected foam layers modulating impact characteristics.

The top foam layer (1 mm thick, 4 MPa stiffness) prevents direct solid-solid surface impacts that could generate shock waves, while effectively transferring acceleration to the holder. The bottom foam layer (12 mm thick, 0.4 MPa stiffness) allows controlled vertical movement of the holder. These parameters are optimized to simulate common blunt injury scenarios (>150 g acceleration in <1 ms duration), while remaining adjustable for different injury paradigms.

A critical innovation involves maintaining shallow cell culture media depth (3-5 mm) during regular culture to ensure proper gas exchange, while temporarily increasing media height to ~10 mm during impact experiments to eliminate air pockets that could artifactually damage cells. The system's integration with live cell microscopy enables continuous optical observation of multiple cultures without removing cells from incubator conditions, significantly reducing environmental perturbations.

### Multiplexed In Vitro Cell Cultures

The invention incorporates a multiplexed live cell imaging system that enables preparation and monitoring of multiple cell culture dishes simultaneously. Cells are cultured in standard petri dishes and monitored using automated imaging at regular intervals (e.g., every 30 minutes) until reaching target confluency levels (typically 35-40%). This approach allows characterization of growth rates and detection of passage-dependent variations in cell behavior that could influence injury responses.

A key feature involves the preparation of control cultures that undergo identical handling procedures except for impact application, enabling quantification of handling-related effects. Experimental data demonstrate that the system maintains cell cultures with minimal perturbations for extended periods (up to one week), as evidenced by comparable growth curves between control and handled samples.

The multiplexed imaging capability provides both average confluency measurements across entire dishes and local confluency data from specific regions of interest. This dual-scale analysis reveals significant spatial heterogeneity in cell populations that could mask injury mechanisms if only bulk measurements were considered. The system currently supports parallel monitoring of up to 12 separate cultures, providing sufficient throughput for statistically robust studies of cellular injury responses.

### Quantifying the Blunt Injury

The invention enables precise quantification of critical blunt injury parameters through integrated sensor arrays and high-speed imaging. Accelerometers measure impact profiles while synchronized high-speed cameras (operating at 50,000 fps) capture cavitation dynamics and cellular responses. Experimental results demonstrate a critical drop height threshold (>30 cm, corresponding to ~1000 g acceleration) beyond which significant cell damage occurs.

Analysis of local confluency curves reveals that damage above this threshold manifests as sudden, localized cell population loss rather than gradual global effects. High-speed imaging correlates these damage zones with cavitation bubble formation at the culture dish bottom, suggesting cavitation as the primary damage mechanism rather than acceleration-induced pressure alone.

The system incorporates propidium iodide (PI) assays to assess membrane integrity post-impact. Data show that while some cells exhibit transient membrane compromise after severe impacts, many demonstrate repair capacity over 24-48 hours. This repair phenomenon appears localized to regions near cavitation-induced damage zones, supporting the hypothesis of localized injury mechanisms.

Pressure measurement experiments using modified transparent tubes demonstrate that cavitation-induced pressure changes are significantly more rapid (6,642 kPa/ms) and intense than acceleration-induced pressure (169 kPa/ms). High-speed imaging synchronized with pressure measurements directly visualizes cavitation bubble dynamics and correlates violent bubble collapse with observed cellular damage patterns.

The invention thus provides comprehensive quantitative data on acceleration thresholds, pressure profiles, and cellular responses that collectively establish cavitation as the dominant injury mechanism in this in vitro trauma model. This platform enables systematic investigation of how biological system size, impact parameters, and cellular characteristics influence trauma outcomes at the molecular and cellular levels.
