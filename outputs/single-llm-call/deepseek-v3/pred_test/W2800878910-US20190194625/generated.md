# DESCRIPTION  

## BACKGROUND  

The blood-brain barrier (BBB) is a highly selective semipermeable border that prevents the passage of circulating toxins and pathogens into the central nervous system while allowing essential nutrients to cross. This barrier is formed by specialized endothelial cells that line cerebral microvessels, supported by surrounding pericytes and astrocytes. Current in vitro models of the BBB suffer from significant limitations in accurately recapitulating the complex cellular interactions and physiological properties of the human neurovascular unit. Traditional two-dimensional cell culture systems fail to capture the three-dimensional architecture and cell-cell signaling present in vivo. While some three-dimensional models incorporating endothelial cells, pericytes and astrocytes have been developed, these lack critical neuronal and glial components that influence BBB function. There remains an unmet need for improved in vitro BBB systems that more faithfully mimic human physiology to enable better prediction of drug penetration and neurotoxicity.  

## SUMMARY  

The present invention provides an advanced in vitro blood-brain barrier model that overcomes limitations of prior systems. The model comprises an endothelial cell layer forming a barrier interface and a brain tissue layer containing neuronal cells, with an optional porous membrane separating the layers. In preferred embodiments, the model incorporates all six major cell types found in the human neurovascular unit: brain microvascular endothelial cells, pericytes, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes and neurons. The invention further provides methods for making the model by depositing cells on porous membranes or adding endothelial cells to pre-formed neural organoids. The model may be formed in various configurations including tubular shapes mimicking blood vessels or spheroid structures. The invention also encompasses microfluidic devices incorporating the blood-brain barrier model for drug screening applications.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS  

### Terminology and Definitions  

As used herein, "blood-brain barrier model" refers to an in vitro construct that mimics key structural and functional properties of the neurovascular unit. "Endothelial cell layer" denotes a continuous or semi-continuous layer of brain microvascular endothelial cells exhibiting tight junction formation. "Neuronal cell layer" refers to a population of neurons that may be electrically active and form synaptic connections. "Porous membrane" indicates a semi-permeable substrate allowing molecular transport while providing structural support for cell layers. "Organoid" describes a three-dimensional cellular aggregate exhibiting self-organization resembling native tissue architecture.  

### In Vitro Blood Brain Barrier Model with Endothelial Cell Layer  

The invention provides blood-brain barrier models comprising an endothelial cell layer derived from human brain microvascular endothelial cells (HBMECs). These endothelial cells form tight junctions expressing characteristic proteins including ZO-1, claudin-5 and occludin. The endothelial layer demonstrates functional barrier properties as evidenced by selective permeability to test compounds. In various embodiments, the endothelial layer completely or partially surrounds the brain tissue component.  

### Model with Neuronal Cell Layer and Porous Membrane  

The blood-brain barrier models of the invention incorporate neuronal cells that may be electrically active and form functional neural networks. The neuronal layer may be separated from the endothelial layer by a porous membrane coated with extracellular matrix proteins such as collagen or laminin. The porous membrane allows molecular transport while maintaining distinct cellular compartments. The membrane pore size may range from 0.4 to 8 micrometers to permit diffusion of nutrients and test compounds while preventing cell migration.  

### Making the Model by Depositing Cells on a Porous Membrane  

One method for constructing the blood-brain barrier model involves sequentially depositing cell layers on a porous membrane. Endothelial cells are first seeded on one surface of the membrane, followed by pericytes and astrocytes. The opposite membrane surface receives neuronal cells along with supporting glial cells (microglia and oligodendrocytes). The assembled construct is cultured under conditions promoting tight junction formation and neural network development.  

### Making the Model by Adding Cells to Organoids  

An alternative fabrication method involves forming neural organoids containing neurons, astrocytes, microglia and oligodendrocytes, then adding endothelial cells and pericytes to the organoid surface. This staged assembly approach allows pre-formation of neural networks before establishing the vascular interface. The endothelial cells migrate to coat the organoid surface, forming a functional barrier layer.  

### Model in a Tubular Shape to Mimic a Blood Vessel  

The blood-brain barrier model may be configured in tubular geometry to better mimic cerebral vasculature. In this embodiment, endothelial cells line the inner lumen while neural cells occupy the surrounding matrix. Perfusion through the lumen simulates blood flow and enables dynamic testing of barrier function. The tubular configuration may be achieved using sacrificial hydrogels or three-dimensional bioprinting techniques.  

### Cells May Be Obtained from Donors or Stem Cells  

The cellular components of the blood-brain barrier model may be derived from various sources. Primary cells can be obtained from human donors or surgical specimens. Alternatively, induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) may be differentiated into the required cell types, enabling patient-specific or disease-modeling applications. The use of stem cell-derived cells facilitates scaling and standardization of model production.  

## 1. Blood Brain Barrier Models and Methods of Making the Same  

### Provide In Vitro Blood Brain Barrier Model  

The invention provides comprehensive in vitro blood-brain barrier models incorporating all major cell types of the human neurovascular unit. These models exhibit physiological barrier properties including selective permeability, efflux transporter activity, and appropriate responses to barrier-modulating agents. The models maintain viability for extended periods (at least 21 days) allowing long-term toxicity studies.  

### Model Has Endothelial Cell Layer and Neuronal Cell Layer  

Key components of the model include an endothelial cell layer forming the barrier interface and a neuronal cell layer representing brain parenchyma. The endothelial layer expresses characteristic tight junction proteins (ZO-1, claudin-5) and transport proteins (P-glycoprotein, GLUT1). The neuronal layer contains functional neurons that may exhibit spontaneous electrical activity and form synaptic connections.  

### Model Has Porous Membrane Between Layers  

In certain embodiments, a porous membrane separates the endothelial and neuronal layers. The membrane provides structural support while permitting molecular transport between compartments. Membrane materials may include polyester, polycarbonate or other biocompatible polymers. The membrane may be coated with extracellular matrix proteins to enhance cell attachment and differentiation.  

### Endothelial Cells, Astrocytes, and Pericytes in the Model  

The endothelial layer is supported by pericytes embedded in the basement membrane and astrocytes extending endfeet processes. This cellular triad replicates critical interactions maintaining barrier integrity in vivo. Pericytes regulate endothelial proliferation and barrier function, while astrocytes contribute to tight junction formation and transporter expression.  

### Neuronal Cells Are Electrically Active  

Neurons in the model demonstrate functional properties including action potential generation and synaptic transmission. Electrical activity may be confirmed by calcium imaging or microelectrode array recording. The presence of functional neuronal networks enables assessment of neurotoxic effects that may not be apparent in non-electrically active cells.  

### Making the Model by Depositing Cells on a Porous Membrane  

One fabrication method involves sequential cell deposition on porous membranes. Endothelial cells are first seeded on the membrane upper surface, followed by pericytes and astrocytes. Neurons with supporting glial cells are then plated on the opposite membrane surface. The construct is cultured under conditions promoting barrier formation and neural network development.  

### Making the Model by Adding Cells to Organoids  

An alternative fabrication approach employs staged assembly. Neural organoids containing neurons, astrocytes, microglia and oligodendrocytes are first formed via self-assembly. Endothelial cells and pericytes are subsequently added to coat the organoid surface, forming a functional barrier layer. This method allows pre-establishment of neural networks before vascular interface formation.  

### Model in a Tubular Shape to Mimic a Blood Vessel  

The model may be configured in tubular geometry using sacrificial hydrogels or 3D bioprinting. A hollow lumen lined with endothelial cells is surrounded by concentric layers of pericytes, astrocytes and neural tissue. Tubular models enable perfusion studies and better replicate the hemodynamic forces present in cerebral vasculature.  

### Endothelial Cells May Be Perfused into the Lumen  

In tubular configurations, endothelial cells may be introduced via luminal perfusion. Cell suspensions are circulated through the lumen under conditions promoting endothelial adhesion and monolayer formation. Perfusion parameters (flow rate, duration) may be optimized to achieve complete endothelial coverage.  

### Sacrificial Hydrogel for Endothelial Cells  

Sacrificial hydrogels can template tubular structures for endothelial lining. A hydrogel cylinder is first formed within a culture chamber. Endothelial cells are seeded on the hydrogel surface, followed by addition of pericyte and astrocyte layers. The hydrogel core is then removed (via enzymatic digestion or temperature change) leaving a patent endothelial-lined lumen.  

### Cells May Be Obtained from Donors or Stem Cells  

Model cellular components may be derived from primary human tissue or stem cell sources. Primary cells offer mature phenotypes but have limited availability. Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) provide an alternative source that enables patient-specific models and genetic disease modeling. Differentiation protocols yield functional endothelial cells, pericytes, astrocytes and neurons.  

### Cells May Be Bioprinted in a Carrier  

Three-dimensional bioprinting enables precise spatial arrangement of multiple cell types. Cells are suspended in bioinks (e.g., fibrin, gelatin methacrylate) and deposited layer-by-layer to build the neurovascular unit. Printing parameters (pressure, speed, temperature) are optimized to maintain cell viability while achieving desired structural resolution.  

### Hydrogel Carrier for Cells  

Hydrogels serve as temporary scaffolds supporting cell viability during model assembly. Natural hydrogels (collagen, fibrin) or synthetic variants (PEG-based) may be used. Hydrogel stiffness and composition are tailored to promote specific cellular behaviors - softer gels for neural network formation, stiffer substrates for endothelial barrier function.  

### Sacrificial Hydrogel for Forming a Hollow Space  

Sacrificial hydrogels can create hollow channels for subsequent endothelialization. A printed hydrogel structure contains temporary internal filaments that are later removed (via dissolution or melting), leaving patent lumens. Endothelial cells are then introduced to line these pre-formed channels.  

### Porous Membrane May Be Coated with Collagen or Laminin  

Membrane surfaces may be modified with extracellular matrix proteins to enhance cell attachment and differentiation. Collagen type IV and laminin are particularly suitable for promoting endothelial barrier formation and neuronal network development. Coating methods include adsorption, covalent conjugation, or layer-by-layer deposition.  

### Microfluidic Device Comprising the Model  

The blood-brain barrier model may be incorporated into microfluidic devices enabling controlled perfusion and real-time monitoring. These devices feature separate vascular and parenchymal compartments connected by microchannels. Integrated sensors can measure barrier integrity (transendothelial electrical resistance), metabolic activity, or compound flux.  

## 2. Methods of Use  

### Use the Model for Compound or Treatment Screening  

The blood-brain barrier models are useful for screening pharmaceutical compounds and therapeutic treatments. Test agents are applied to the vascular compartment and their effects monitored in the neural compartment. Screening may assess drug efficacy, toxicity, or ability to cross the blood-brain barrier.  

### Testing for Efficacy, Toxicity, or Other Metabolic Activity  

Models enable simultaneous evaluation of multiple endpoints. Efficacy testing measures desired pharmacological effects on neural targets. Toxicity assessment monitors cell viability, barrier integrity and neural function. Metabolic activity can be tracked via glucose consumption, oxygen utilization or ATP production.  

### Detecting Penetration of a Compound Through the Endothelial Layer  

Barrier penetration is quantified by measuring compound accumulation in the neural compartment. Analytical techniques include mass spectrometry, fluorescence detection or radiolabel tracing. Penetration kinetics (rate, extent) provide insight into a compound's brain accessibility.  

### Determining Immunological Activity  

The models can assess immune-modulating effects mediated by microglia and astrocytes. Parameters include cytokine secretion, phagocytic activity, and antigen presentation. These readouts are relevant for neuroinflammatory conditions and immunomodulatory therapies.  

### Testing Methods of Crossing the Blood Brain Barrier  

The models facilitate evaluation of strategies to enhance drug delivery across the BBB. These include chemical modifications (prodrugs), carrier systems (nanoparticles), or barrier modulation (tight junction openers). Effectiveness is measured by increased parenchymal drug levels with minimal barrier disruption.  

### Personalized Testing with Cells from a Subject  

Patient-derived iPSCs enable personalized medicine applications. Models generated from a subject's cells can predict individual responses to therapies or assess disease-specific barrier properties. This approach is particularly valuable for neurological disorders with genetic components.  

### Model with Cells Having a Known Genetic Mutation  

Incorporating cells with defined genetic mutations creates disease-specific models. Examples include Alzheimer's (APP, PSEN mutations), Parkinson's (LRRK2, SNCA mutations), or Huntington's disease (HTT expansion). These models help elucidate mutation effects on barrier function and test targeted therapies.  

## EXAMPLES  

### Example 1  

#### Bioprint Vessel Model of Blood Brain Barrier  

A tubular blood-brain barrier model was created by 3D bioprinting. First, a fibrin-based bioink containing human astrocytes, pericytes and neurons was extruded to form a hollow cylindrical structure. After crosslinking, human brain microvascular endothelial cells were perfused into the lumen and allowed to adhere. The construct was cultured under flow conditions promoting endothelial barrier formation. Immunostaining confirmed expression of tight junction proteins (ZO-1, claudin-5) and neuronal markers (MAP2, β-III tubulin). Electrophysiological recordings demonstrated spontaneous neuronal activity.  

#### Print Electrically Active Neuronal Cells  

The neuronal component was printed using a bioink optimized for neural network formation. The ink contained fibrinogen, hyaluronic acid and laminin blended with human iPSC-derived neurons at 20×10^6 cells/mL. Printed neurons extended processes and formed synaptic connections within 7 days, as evidenced by synaptophysin staining and calcium imaging showing coordinated activity bursts.  

#### Culture and Differentiate Printed Constructs  

Printed constructs were maintained in neural differentiation medium supplemented with BDNF, GDNF and cAMP for 4 weeks to promote maturation. Medium in the vascular compartment contained VEGF and bFGF to support endothelial barrier function. The model demonstrated selective permeability to test compounds (sucrose, dextran) and appropriate efflux transporter activity.  

### Example 2. Human Cortex Model with Spheroid Culture System  

#### Create 3D Spheroid Model of Blood Brain Barrier  

A spheroid blood-brain barrier model was created using primary human cells. Cells were combined in physiological ratios (30% endothelial, 15% pericytes, 15% astrocytes, 5% microglia, 15% oligodendrocytes, 20% neurons) and cultured in hanging drops to promote self-assembly. After 48 hours, formed spheroids were transferred to low-adhesion plates for long-term culture.  

#### Source and Expand Primary Human Cells  

Primary human brain microvascular endothelial cells, pericytes and astrocytes were obtained from commercial sources and expanded in recommended media. Microglia and oligodendrocytes were differentiated from iPSCs using established protocols. Neurons were derived from fetal cortical tissue and maintained in neurobasal medium with B27 supplement.  

#### Manufacture Spheroids Using Hanging Drop Method  

Cell suspensions were pipetted into hanging drop plates (30 μL drops containing ~2000 cells). Plates were inverted and incubated for 48-72 hours to allow spheroid formation. Larger spheroids were produced by feeding drops with additional medium after initial aggregation.  

#### Characterize Spheroid Viability and Structure  

Spheroid viability exceeded 90% through 21 days in culture, as assessed by calcein-AM/ethidium homodimer staining. Confocal microscopy of sectioned spheroids revealed concentric organization with endothelial cells and pericytes at the periphery, astrocytes forming an intermediate layer, and neurons/glial cells in the core.  

#### Perform Immunohistochemistry for Cell Specific Markers  

Immunofluorescence confirmed expression of cell-type specific proteins: CD31 (endothelial), PDGFR-β (pericytes), GFAP (astrocytes), Iba1 (microglia), O4/MBP (oligodendrocytes), and MAP2 (neurons). Tight junction proteins (ZO-1, claudin-5) localized to endothelial cell borders, while transporters (P-gp, GLUT1) showed appropriate membrane distribution.  

#### Analyze Spheroid Results and Significance  

The spheroid model replicated key features of human neurovascular unit including barrier selectivity, transporter expression and neural-glial interactions. The 3D architecture and cellular diversity enabled more physiologically relevant screening compared to monolayer cultures.  

#### Discuss Applications in Drug Discovery and Neurotoxicity Testing  

This model is particularly suited for neuropharmacology applications. It can simultaneously assess a compound's ability to cross the BBB and its effects on neural function. The inclusion of microglia and oligodendrocytes allows evaluation of immunomodulatory and myelination effects not possible in simpler systems.  

### Example 3: Bioprint Functional Cortical Tissue  

#### Bioprint Cortical Tissue with Neurons  

A cortical tissue construct was bioprinted using human iPSC-derived neurons suspended in fibrin hydrogel. The bioink contained neurons at 25×10^6 cells/mL in fibrinogen (10 mg/mL) supplemented with laminin (5 μg/mL). A pneumatic extrusion printer deposited the bioink in layered patterns mimicking cortical laminae.  

#### Suspend Neurons in Fibrin Hydrogel  

Neurons were gently mixed with fibrinogen solution and thrombin (0.5 U/mL) immediately before printing to initiate gelation. The fibrin matrix provided temporary support while allowing neurite outgrowth and network formation.  

#### Print and Crosslink Structures  

Structures were printed onto glass coverslips coated with poly-D-lysine. After printing, constructs were incubated in neurobasal medium for 30 minutes to complete fibrin crosslinking before transferring to culture dishes.  

#### Culture and Differentiate Printed Constructs  

Printed constructs were maintained in neural differentiation medium (neurobasal/B27 with BDNF, NT-3 and ascorbic acid) for 4 weeks. Medium changes were performed every 3 days. By week 2, extensive neurite outgrowth was visible, forming dense interconnected networks.  

#### Perform Viability Assays  

Cell viability remained above 85% throughout culture, as measured by calcein-AM staining. Metabolic activity (MTT assay) increased steadily during the first two weeks as neural networks established, then stabilized.  

#### Analyze Beta III Tubulin Expression  

Immunostaining for β-III tubulin revealed extensive neurite extension and network formation by day 14. Neuronal processes showed complex branching patterns and frequent crossings, indicative of functional synapse formation.  

#### Bioprint Microvessel with All Cell Types  

A neurovascular unit was created by co-printing endothelial cells, pericytes and astrocytes around the neuronal core. Endothelial cells formed continuous tubes when printed in fibrin/gelatin methacrylate composite bioink. Pericytes and astrocytes were printed in surrounding positions to mimic native vascular relationships.  

#### Design and Print Human Neurovascular Unit  

The complete neurovascular unit was printed as a cylindrical construct with an endothelial-lined central channel surrounded by concentric layers of pericytes, astrocytes and neurons. This configuration allowed perfusion studies and replicated the anatomical organization of cerebral microvessels.  

#### Discuss Applications in Drug Discovery and Neurotoxicity Testing  

The bioprinted neurovascular unit enables sophisticated drug screening by combining BBB permeability assessment with neural functional readouts. Its modular design permits customization for specific applications (e.g., adding disease-specific cell types). The perfusable vascular channel allows real-time monitoring of barrier function during compound exposure.  

### Example 4. Three-Dimensional Bioprinting of the Human Neurovascular Unit  

#### Design and Bioprint NVU Constructs  

A bioprinted neurovascular unit was designed with three compartments: vascular (endothelial/pericytes), glial (astrocytes/microglia), and neuronal (neurons/oligodendrocytes). Each compartment was printed using cell-specific bioinks formulated to promote appropriate cellular behaviors.  

#### Utilize Primary Human Cells or iPSC-Derived Cells  

Constructs were fabricated using either primary human cells or iPSC-derived equivalents. Primary cells offered mature phenotypes while iPSCs enabled genetic modification and patient-specific applications. Both cell sources produced functional models with comparable barrier properties.  

#### Expand Cells in 2D Culture  

All cell types were expanded in monolayer culture before printing. Endothelial cells and pericytes were cultured in endothelial growth medium, astrocytes and microglia in glial medium, and neurons in neurobasal/B27 medium. Cells were harvested at 80-90% confluence for bioink preparation.  

#### Bioprint Cortical Tissue with Mature Neurons  

The neuronal compartment was printed using a fibrin-based bioink containing neurons at 20×10^6 cells/mL. The bioink included laminin (10 μg/mL) and chondroitin sulfate (1 μg/mL) to promote neurite outgrowth. Printed neurons exhibited mature morphology (extensive branching, synaptic puncta) by 3 weeks in culture.  

#### Bioprint Capillary NVU and Micro-Arteriole NVU  

Two vascular configurations were printed: capillary-scale (single endothelial layer, diameter <10 μm) and micro-arteriole (multilayered, diameter 20-50 μm). The capillary model showed higher barrier selectivity (lower dextran permeability) while the arteriole model permitted higher flow rates for perfusion studies.  

#### Perform Immunohistochemistry for Cell Specific Markers  

Immunofluorescence confirmed appropriate marker expression in all compartments: CD31/VE-cadherin (endothelial), PDGFR-β/NG2 (pericytes), GFAP/S100β (astrocytes), Iba1/CD11b (microglia), O4/MBP (oligodendrocytes), and MAP2/NeuN (neurons). Tight junction proteins localized to endothelial cell-cell contacts.  

#### Analyze Viability and Structure of Printed Constructs  

Construct viability exceeded 85% at all timepoints (1-28 days). Confocal imaging revealed maintained 3D architecture with distinct but interacting compartments. Endothelial tubes remained patent with continuous lumens, while neuronal regions showed dense network formation.  

#### Discuss Applications in Drug Discovery and Neurotoxicity Testing  

The bioprinted NVU enables comprehensive preclinical testing by combining BBB permeability assessment with neural functional readouts. Its human cell composition and 3D architecture provide superior predictivity compared to animal models or monolayer cultures. The system is particularly valuable for evaluating neuroprotective therapies and CNS drug delivery strategies.
