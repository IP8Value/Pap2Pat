# DESCRIPTION  

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION  

Phase sensing devices are critical tools in scientific imaging and measurement applications. Commercial optical sensors currently available are limited to detecting only intensity variations of light waves, despite the fact that phase front variations carry substantial additional information about imaged objects. The phase of light waves is particularly important in imaging applications involving transparent biological specimens, which primarily modulate the phase front rather than the intensity of transmitted light.  

Existing phase microscopes, including differential interference contrast (DIC) microscopes, phase contrast microscopes, and Hoffman phase microscopes, have been widely used for several decades. However, these conventional systems suffer from significant limitations. The phase information they provide is inherently entangled with amplitude information, leading to ambiguities in image interpretation and preventing straightforward quantitative phase analysis. Furthermore, these microscopes require specialized optical components that must be mechanically adjusted during operation, increasing complexity and cost. DIC microscopes in particular produce artifacts when imaging birefringent samples due to their reliance on polarized light in the phase-imaging strategy.  

Recent developments in phase microscopy techniques, including phase shifting interferometry, digital holography, Hilbert phase microscopy, swept-source phase microscopy, polarization quadrature microscopy, and harmonically matched grating-based phase microscopy, have attempted to address these limitations. However, these approaches typically require laser sources and sophisticated optical arrangements, limiting their widespread adoption as practical alternatives to conventional DIC microscopes. Alternative methods for calculating optical phase by collecting multiple images at different focal planes are intrinsically limited in speed due to the need for physical actuation of the camera.  

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention discloses a wavefront imaging sensor (WIS) capable of simultaneously measuring both amplitude and phase variations of incident light. The WIS comprises an aperture layer containing one or more apertures, a light detector, and a transparent layer separating the aperture layer from the light detector. This configuration enables direct measurement of phase gradients and amplitude of light waves without requiring complex optical components or polarized light.  

In operation, light transmitted through each aperture forms a projection spot on the light detector. The lateral shift of this projection spot is directly related to the phase gradient of the incident light wave, while the total intensity of the projection provides amplitude information. The WIS operates in a high Fresnel number regime, where light transmitted through an aperture naturally focuses before diffracting, resulting in tightly confined projection spots that enable high sensitivity measurements.  

The invention further includes methods for numerically generating images from the measured phase gradient and amplitude data. These methods allow reconstruction of quantitative phase images without the ambiguities and artifacts present in conventional phase microscopy techniques. The WIS design provides significant advantages including simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and improved image quality compared to existing phase sensing devices.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION  

The wavefront imaging sensor (WIS) of the present invention represents a significant advancement in optical sensing technology. The WIS comprises three primary components: an aperture layer containing precisely defined apertures, a light detector such as a CMOS or CCD sensor, and a transparent spacer layer separating the aperture layer from the light detector. This configuration enables the device to operate in a high Fresnel number regime, where unique self-focusing effects provide superior performance characteristics.  

The aperture layer may consist of a single aperture or an array of apertures arranged in one-dimensional or two-dimensional patterns. Each aperture comprises a light-transmissive region, which may be a physical hole or an area filled with transparent material. The size and shape of the apertures are carefully designed to optimize performance, with typical diameters in the range of several micrometers. When arranged in arrays, the spacing between adjacent apertures is minimized to provide densely populated light data while preventing overlap between projection spots.  

The transparent spacer layer, positioned between the aperture layer and the light detector, may consist of one or more sublayers of transparent or semi-transparent material. The thickness and refractive index of this layer are critical parameters that determine the device's operating characteristics. In preferred embodiments, the spacer layer thickness is selected to place the light detector at the self-focusing plane where light projections from the apertures achieve minimum spot size.  

The light detector comprises an array of light-sensitive elements configured to detect the position and intensity of light projections from the apertures. Each aperture is typically assigned a dedicated group of detector elements to capture its projection spot with sufficient resolution. The detector generates signals containing information about the amplitude, intensity, and phase gradient of the incident light wave.  

When illuminated, light passing through each aperture forms a projection spot on the light detector. The lateral position of this spot relative to a reference position provides a direct measurement of the phase gradient of the incident light wave at that aperture. The mathematical relationship between the lateral shift (Δs) and the normalized phase gradient (θ) is given by Δs = Hθ, where H is the effective distance between the aperture and the light-sensitive surface, accounting for the refractive index of the spacer layer.  

The WIS achieves particularly high sensitivity through its high Fresnel number design. The Fresnel number, defined as F = a²/λH where a is the aperture radius, λ is the wavelength, and H is the spacer thickness, determines the diffraction characteristics of the system. In the high Fresnel number regime (F > 0.5), light transmitted through an aperture undergoes intrinsic focusing before diverging, resulting in narrowed projection spots that enable precise measurement of small phase gradients.  

The invention includes several methods for processing the raw detector data to extract phase and amplitude information. One preferred method involves a cyclic algorithm that analyzes the light projection distribution using complex weights to determine the center position with sub-pixel accuracy. For discrete data from a two-dimensional detector array, integrals are replaced with summations over the relevant pixels while maintaining the essential mathematical relationships.  

Calibration methods are provided to establish the linearity and sensitivity of the phase gradient response. These involve illuminating the WIS with light at known incident angles to establish a calibration curve relating measured spot shifts to actual phase gradients. The calibration process also allows determination of the effective spacer thickness and identification of any systematic biases in the measurements.  

The WIS finds application in numerous imaging systems, including microscopes, cameras, and specialized optical devices. In microscope implementations, the WIS replaces conventional cameras to provide simultaneous intensity and quantitative phase gradient imaging. Compared to DIC microscopy, the WIS provides several advantages: it requires no polarized light, eliminates birefringence artifacts, separates phase and amplitude information, and provides quantitative phase data.  

Additional embodiments include color-sensitive WIS devices using stratified color sensing technologies or Bayer pattern arrangements with advanced processing algorithms. The WIS can also be incorporated into adaptive optics systems, machine vision applications, texture assessment devices, and medical imaging equipment such as retinal imagers and LASIK surgery systems.  

The fabrication of WIS devices follows semiconductor manufacturing processes, enabling cost-effective mass production. The aperture layer can be created using photolithography or focused ion beam milling on metal-coated substrates. Transparent spacer layers may be formed from various dielectric materials deposited or bonded to the sensor surface. Post-processing steps include calibration and testing to verify performance specifications.  

The WIS system architecture typically includes a host computer or processor for data acquisition and image processing. Software implementations of the various algorithms may be stored on computer-readable media and executed by the processing system. The complete system provides a user interface for controlling acquisition parameters and displaying the resulting intensity and phase images.  

Modifications and variations of the disclosed embodiments remain within the scope of the invention. These include changes to aperture arrangements, spacer materials, detector configurations, and processing algorithms. The essential characteristics of the WIS - its ability to simultaneously measure amplitude and phase gradients using a compact, high-sensitivity design - remain central to all embodiments.
