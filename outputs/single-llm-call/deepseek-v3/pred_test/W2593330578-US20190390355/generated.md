# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD  

The present invention relates to carbon anodes used in electrolysis cells for aluminum production. More specifically, the invention concerns an improved manufacturing process for carbonaceous anodes that exhibit reduced air and CO2 reactivities through boron impregnation of coke particles prior to anode formation. The disclosed method provides enhanced protection against premature anode consumption while maintaining suitable electrical conductivity for industrial electrolysis applications.

## BACKGROUND  

Carbonaceous anodes demonstrate significant reactivity with both air and CO2 during aluminum production via the Hall-Héroult process. The conventional electrolytic reduction of alumina to aluminum metal occurs through the overall reaction: 2Al2O3 (dissolved) + 3C (solid anode) → 4Al (liquid metal) + 3CO2 (gas). However, parasitic reactions between the carbon anode and atmospheric oxygen occur simultaneously at operating temperatures between 400-600°C, leading to undesirable carbon consumption through both complete combustion (C + O2 → CO2) and partial oxidation (C + ½O2 → CO). These side reactions contribute substantially to anode overconsumption and dusting phenomena in industrial operations.  

The typical anode composition consists of calcined petroleum coke (65%), recycled anode butts (22%), and coal tar pitch binder (13%). Research has established that the binder matrix (pitch combined with fine coke particles) exhibits higher burning rates than larger coke grains, leading to premature binder degradation and subsequent coke particle detachment from the anode surface. This differential reactivity pattern exacerbates dust generation during electrolysis.  

Current protection strategies focus on either creating impermeable physical barriers on the anode surface or modifying the intrinsic carbon reactivity. Physical barrier approaches include alumina powder coatings and solidified bath layers, which provide limited protection through oxygen diffusion barriers. However, these porous coatings cannot completely prevent anode oxidation. Alternative approaches involve doping carbon with oxidation inhibitors such as boron or phosphorus, though phosphorus contamination of the aluminum product precludes its industrial use.  

Boron doping inhibits graphite oxidation through three proposed mechanisms: (1) electron density redistribution reducing intrinsic carbon reactivity, (2) enhanced graphitization, and (3) formation of protective boron oxide films blocking active sites. However, conventional boron doping methods face significant limitations. High boron concentrations (>1000 ppm) required for mechanism (1) would contaminate the aluminum product, while mechanism (2) requires graphitization temperatures exceeding standard anode baking conditions. The boron oxide film formation (mechanism 3) presents the most viable protection strategy, though prior implementations through direct anode impregnation face practical challenges in achieving uniform protection throughout industrial-scale anodes.  

Previous attempts at boron oxide coatings through aqueous impregnation demonstrated laboratory-scale success but encountered difficulties in industrial deployment. These methods typically involved post-production anode treatment, where boron compounds were applied to finished anodes. While effective for small test samples, scaling these approaches to full-size anodes proved problematic due to insufficient penetration depth and non-uniform distribution of the protective boron species.  

## SUMMARY  

The present invention provides a novel process for manufacturing carbonaceous anodes with improved oxidation resistance through boron impregnation of coke particles prior to anode formation. The method comprises contacting petroleum coke particles with a boron-containing aqueous solution, followed by drying to produce boron-impregnated coke particles. These treated particles are then mixed with coal tar pitch binder to form an anode paste, which is compacted into green anodes and baked to produce the final carbonaceous anode product.  

In one embodiment, the process involves separating the coke particles into first and second fractions based on particle size. The first fraction (typically finer particles) undergoes boron impregnation while the second fraction (coarser particles) remains untreated. The boron-impregnated first fraction is then combined with the untreated second fraction and coal tar pitch to form the anode paste. This selective treatment approach optimizes boron distribution while minimizing total boron usage.  

Another embodiment includes grinding and sieving the boron-impregnated coke particles to achieve desired size distributions before mixing with pitch. The boron-containing solution preferably comprises boric acid or boron oxide dissolved in water at concentrations between 0.1-20% by weight. The contacting process may involve immersion, spraying, or other suitable application methods at temperatures ranging from ambient to boiling point.  

The resulting carbonaceous anode exhibits substantially reduced air and CO2 reactivities compared to conventional anodes, with measured air reactivity rates decreasing from approximately 4%/h to 0.2%/h. This improved performance stems from uniform boron oxide distribution throughout the anode microstructure, particularly in the more reactive binder matrix regions. The invention provides effective oxidation protection while maintaining required electrical conductivity and mechanical properties for industrial aluminum production.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

The following detailed description illustrates specific embodiments of the invention but does not limit its scope, which is defined by the appended claims. The term "about" as used herein indicates ±10% of the stated value unless otherwise specified.  

The invention provides a process for manufacturing carbonaceous anodes with enhanced oxidation resistance through pre-formation boron treatment of coke particles. Unlike conventional approaches that apply protective coatings to finished anodes, the present method integrates boron compounds into the anode structure during its formation, ensuring more uniform protection throughout the entire anode volume.  

The process begins with preparation of boron-impregnated coke particles by contacting petroleum coke with a boron-containing aqueous solution. The solution preferably contains boric acid (H3BO3) or boron oxide (B2O3) at concentrations between 0.1-20% by weight, more preferably 1-5% by weight. The contacting process may involve immersion, spraying, or other suitable application methods, with treatment durations ranging from 1 minute to several hours depending on desired boron loading. The impregnation may be performed at temperatures between 20-100°C, with elevated temperatures (50-100°C) enhancing solution penetration into coke pores.  

Following impregnation, the treated coke particles are dried at temperatures between 80-150°C to remove water and convert boron compounds to their oxide forms. The drying process may be conducted in air or under controlled atmosphere conditions. The resulting boron-impregnated coke particles contain boron oxide distributed throughout their pore networks, providing oxidation protection at the fundamental particle level.  

In preferred embodiments, the coke particles are divided into size fractions before treatment. Typically, particles smaller than 1 mm (first fraction) undergo boron impregnation while larger particles (second fraction) remain untreated. This selective treatment approach concentrates boron in the more reactive fine fraction that forms the binder matrix, optimizing protection while minimizing total boron usage. The size separation may be achieved through sieving or other particle classification methods known in the art.  

The boron-impregnated coke particles are then mixed with coal tar pitch binder at temperatures between 150-200°C to form a homogeneous anode paste. The mixing ratio typically comprises 65-75% coke particles (including both treated and untreated fractions) and 25-35% pitch by weight. The paste may optionally include recycled anode butts (10-30% by weight) as additional aggregate material.  

The anode paste is compacted using vibration or pressing techniques to form green anodes with desired shapes and dimensions. The green anodes undergo baking at temperatures between 1000-1200°C in reducing atmosphere to carbonize the pitch binder and develop final anode properties. The baking process typically requires 2-4 weeks depending on anode size, with careful temperature control to prevent cracking or distortion.  

The resulting carbonaceous anode exhibits unique structural characteristics due to the boron pretreatment. Microscopic analysis reveals boron oxide deposits lining internal pore surfaces throughout the anode structure, particularly in the fine particle/binder matrix regions. This distributed protection mechanism differs fundamentally from conventional surface coatings, providing oxidation resistance even as the anode surface erodes during use.  

The boron content in the final anode preferably ranges between 0.01-1.0% by weight, more preferably 0.05-0.5% by weight. This relatively low boron concentration effectively reduces reactivities without compromising electrical conductivity or introducing excessive impurities into the aluminum product.  

## EXAMPLES  

The following examples illustrate specific embodiments of the invention and demonstrate its advantages over conventional anode technology.  

### Example 1: Anode Preparation  

Anodes were prepared according to the invention using calcined petroleum coke separated into fine (<1 mm) and coarse (1-5 mm) fractions. The fine fraction was impregnated in a 2% boric acid solution at 50°C for 15 minutes, then dried at 140°C for 4 hours. The treated fine fraction (65 parts) was mixed with untreated coarse fraction (22 parts) and coal tar pitch (13 parts) at 180°C to form anode paste. The paste was compacted into cylindrical green anodes (50 mm diameter × 30 mm height) and baked at 1200°C for 24 hours. Control anodes were prepared identically but without boron treatment.  

### Example 2: Anode Characterization  

Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) confirmed uniform boron distribution throughout the treated anodes, with higher concentrations in fine particle regions. X-ray fluorescence (XRF) analysis showed total boron content of 0.12% by weight. X-ray diffraction (XRD) indicated no significant changes in crystallite height (Lc) due to boron treatment, confirming that the baking temperature was insufficient for boron-induced graphitization.  

### Example 3: Air Reactivity Measurements  

Air reactivity tests were conducted at 500°C with 4 L/min airflow. Untreated control anodes exhibited rapid mass loss (4%/h), completely disintegrating within 40 hours. Boron-treated anodes showed substantially lower reaction rates (0.2%/h), retaining structural integrity after 160 hours with only 60% mass loss. Visual inspection revealed minimal dust generation from treated anodes compared to complete powdering of controls.  

### Example 4: CO2 Reactivity Measurements  

CO2 reactivity tests at 960°C showed similar improvements, with treated anodes exhibiting 70% lower reaction rates than controls. The boron-treated anodes maintained smooth reaction surfaces without pitting or preferential binder consumption observed in untreated samples.  

### Example 5: Electrical Resistivity Measurements  

Four-point probe measurements showed no significant difference in electrical resistivity between treated (55 μΩm) and untreated (52 μΩm) anodes, confirming that boron treatment did not adversely affect current conduction.  

The examples demonstrate that the invention provides carbonaceous anodes with dramatically improved oxidation resistance while maintaining all required operational properties for aluminum electrolysis. The pre-formation boron impregnation approach enables uniform protection throughout the anode structure, addressing fundamental limitations of conventional surface treatment methods.
