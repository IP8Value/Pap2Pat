# DESCRIPTION

Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by recurrent, unprovoked seizures resulting from abnormal neuronal activity in the brain. The condition affects approximately 50 million people worldwide and represents a significant public health burden due to its chronic nature and associated comorbidities. Epileptic seizures manifest as transient occurrences of signs and/or symptoms due to abnormal excessive or synchronous neuronal activity in the brain. These seizures vary widely in their clinical presentation, ranging from brief lapses of awareness to convulsive movements affecting the entire body.

Seizures exhibit distinct characteristics based on their origin and propagation patterns through the brain's structural connections. The epileptic activity typically originates in a specific brain region known as the epileptogenic zone (EZ) and spreads to other regions through the individual's unique brain connectome. The clinical manifestations depend on which brain regions become recruited during seizure propagation, leading to diverse ictal symptoms. Seizures may be classified as focal (originating in one hemisphere), generalized (involving both hemispheres simultaneously), or focal to bilateral tonic-clonic, with each type presenting distinct clinical features and underlying network dynamics.

Current treatment options for epilepsy primarily include pharmacological interventions with anti-epileptic drugs (AEDs) and surgical procedures for drug-resistant cases. While approximately 70% of patients achieve adequate seizure control with medication, about 30% develop pharmacoresistant epilepsy requiring alternative therapeutic approaches. Surgical interventions for epilepsy fall into two main categories: resection procedures that remove the epileptogenic tissue and disconnection techniques that sever critical propagation pathways. Resective surgeries such as temporal lobectomy or lesionectomy demonstrate success rates between 30-70% depending on accurate localization of the EZ. Disconnection procedures including corpus callosotomy or multiple subpial transection aim to limit seizure spread rather than eliminate seizure generation.

Despite advances in epilepsy surgery, significant limitations persist in current treatment modalities. Approximately 90% of drug-resistant patients are not considered surgical candidates due to multifocal or eloquently located EZs where resection would cause unacceptable neurological deficits. Even among surgical candidates, outcomes remain variable with risks of postoperative complications including cognitive impairment, motor deficits, or language dysfunction. Existing alternative techniques like multiple subpial transection show inconsistent efficacy, highlighting the need for more precise and personalized surgical approaches. An optimal surgical intervention should effectively reduce seizures, provide flexible options for inoperable EZs, and minimize impact on normal brain function.

The development of novel surgical options requires a comprehensive understanding of epilepsy's network characteristics. Recent advances in network neuroscience have revealed that the epileptic brain exhibits distinct segregation features compared to healthy brains. Graph theoretical analyses demonstrate that epileptic networks show increased local connectivity but decreased global integration, forming more segregated modular structures. These network properties suggest that targeted disruption of critical connections between modules could potentially control seizure propagation without requiring complete removal of the EZ.

Personalized brain network models based on individual connectomes enable simulation of seizure propagation patterns and prediction of surgical outcomes. These computational approaches integrate structural connectivity data from diffusion MRI with functional information to create patient-specific models. By applying graph theoretical analyses such as modularity decomposition to these models, potential target zones for surgical intervention can be identified outside the EZ. The models can then simulate the effects of removing or disconnecting these targets on both seizure propagation and normal brain function.

The proposed invention addresses the critical need for new surgical options by developing a computerized platform for personalized epilepsy surgery planning. This platform combines modularity analysis of structural brain networks with large-scale neural simulations to identify optimal target zones for surgical intervention. The method specifically focuses on cases where the EZ cannot be safely removed, deriving alternative targets that disrupt seizure propagation while preserving normal function. The approach operationalizes safety through quantitative assessment of signal transmission properties in the brain network before and after virtual interventions.

Reconstructing structural brain network details the neuroimaging processing pipeline. Obtaining neuroimaging data describes the acquisition protocols. The platform requires high-quality diffusion MRI (typically 60+ diffusion directions, b-value=1000 s/mm²) and T1-weighted anatomical scans. These data undergo quality control and preprocessing including motion correction, eddy current compensation, and susceptibility distortion correction before tractography analysis.

Dividing brain into 84 regions specifies the anatomical parcellation scheme. The platform uses a modified Desikan-Killiany atlas including 68 cortical regions and 16 subcortical structures. This parcellation provides sufficient spatial resolution for surgical planning while maintaining computational feasibility for whole-brain network simulations.

Defining connection strengths explains the tractography-based quantification. The platform estimates connectivity between regions based on the number of streamlines intersecting both regions, normalized by geometric factors to account for region size and distance. These connection weights form the adjacency matrix representing the brain's structural connectivity network.

Determining signal transmission delays accounts for axonal conduction times. The platform calculates delays between regions based on tract lengths divided by estimated conduction velocity (typically 10 m/s for myelinated fibers). These delays are incorporated into the dynamical models to reproduce realistic temporal patterns of neural activity propagation.

Performing modularity analysis describes the network decomposition algorithm. The platform employs Newman's spectral method to identify modules that maximize within-module connections while minimizing between-module links. The analysis uses a quality function comparing actual connection patterns to a random null model, with higher values indicating stronger modular organization.

Using Matlab toolbox specifies the implementation details. The platform employs established neuroinformatics tools for modularity analysis, adapted to incorporate clinical constraints. The toolbox computes the leading eigenvector of the modularity matrix to identify optimal network partitions at multiple resolution scales.

Computing leading eigen vector explains the mathematical procedure. The platform calculates the principal eigenvector of the modularity matrix, which represents the optimal division of the network into two modules. The sign of each element in the eigenvector determines module assignment for the corresponding brain region, providing the first level of network decomposition.

Dividing network nodes into modules describes the recursive partitioning process. After initial bipartition, the platform recursively applies modularity analysis to each resulting module until no further meaningful divisions are found. This hierarchical decomposition reveals the network's organization at multiple spatial scales relevant for surgical targeting.

Fine-tuning division by node moving method optimizes modular structure. The platform iteratively reassigns nodes between modules to maximize the modularity quality function. This local optimization improves upon the initial spectral partitioning, ensuring the identified modules best represent the network's intrinsic organization.
