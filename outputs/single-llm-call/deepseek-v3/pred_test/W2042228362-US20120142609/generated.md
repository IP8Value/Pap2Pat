# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a non-human animal model useful for studying diseases associated with increased retinal vascular permeability, particularly diabetic retinopathy. More specifically, the invention provides a Dp71-deficient animal model that exhibits characteristic features of retinal vascular dysfunction including impaired potassium and water homeostasis in Müller glial cells and breakdown of the blood-retinal barrier. This animal model serves as a valuable tool for screening potential therapeutic agents targeting retinal edema formation and for investigating the pathophysiology of retinal vascular diseases.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Diabetic retinopathy represents a major cause of vision impairment and blindness among working-age adults worldwide. This microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus leads to progressive damage of retinal blood vessels, resulting in vascular leakage, retinal edema, and ultimately retinal ischemia. The consequences of diabetic retinopathy include impaired visual function, macular edema, retinal detachment, and in advanced stages, proliferative retinopathy that can lead to complete vision loss.

Glial cells, particularly Müller cells, play a crucial role in maintaining retinal function and integrity. As the principal glial cells of the vertebrate retina, Müller cells span the entire retinal thickness and provide essential functions through intimate interactions with neurons and their synapses. Similar to astrocytes in the brain, Müller cells are indispensable for retinal tissue homeostasis, participating in neurotransmitter deactivation and recycling while maintaining the ionic balance of the extracellular fluid. These specialized radial glial cells also contribute to the induction, maintenance, and proper functioning of the blood-retinal barrier (BRB).

In the neural retina, potassium buffering and water drainage through Müller cells are mediated by the coordinated action of inwardly rectifying potassium channels, particularly Kir4.1, with the selective water transport protein Aquaporin-4 (AQP4). Under normal physiological conditions, Kir4.1 and AQP4 proteins exhibit strong expression in Müller cells, with particular enrichment in their vitread endfeet and processes adjacent to intraretinal blood vessels. The polarized expression pattern of these channels in Müller cell plasma membranes is essential for their proper functioning.

The clustering and precise membrane localization of Kir4.1 and AQP4 depend on the dystrophin gene product Dp71. In healthy retina, Dp71 protein displays an expression pattern identical to Kir4.1 and AQP4, participating in the targeting of these channels to specific membrane microdomains through a macromolecular protein complex composed of Dp71 and dystrophin-associated proteins (DAPs). Genetic inactivation of Dp71, as observed in Dp71-null mice, disrupts the normal distribution of Kir4.1 and AQP4 in Müller glial cells. This mislocalization increases retinal neuronal vulnerability to transient ischemia, often resulting in neuronal cell death.

The Dp71-associated protein complex (DAPC) plays a critical role in maintaining retinal homeostasis. The DAPC consists of several components including dystroglycans, sarcoglycans, syntrophins, and dystrobrevins, which collectively form a structural link between the extracellular matrix and the cytoskeleton. In skeletal muscle, this complex provides mechanical stability during contraction, while in the retina it serves primarily to maintain the polarized distribution of ion and water channels in Müller cells.

Disruption of Dp71 or DAPC components leads to significant alterations in retinal physiology. The expression and cytotopographical distribution of Kir4.1 and AQP4 become altered in various retinal injuries including transient ischemia/reperfusion, diabetic retinopathy, retinal vein occlusion, and retinal detachment. Early mislocation of Kir4.1 accompanies dramatically decreased potassium conductance and glial cell membrane depolarization, which subsequently impairs the function of electrogenic uptake carriers for glutamate and GABA. These changes disrupt neurotransmitter recycling and other glia-neuron interactions, potentially explaining the complex neuronal and glial functional changes that precede detectable vascular anomalies in diseases like diabetic retinopathy.

Glial cell dysfunction represents a common feature in various retinal diseases. In diabetic retinopathy, Müller cells undergo characteristic changes including cellular hypertrophy, altered membrane channel expression, and impaired volume regulation. These pathological alterations contribute to the breakdown of the blood-retinal barrier and the formation of retinal edema, which represent hallmarks of diabetic retinopathy progression. Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying these glial cell dysfunctions provides crucial insights for developing targeted therapeutic interventions.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel non-human animal model exhibiting increased retinal vascular permeability, particularly useful for studying diabetic retinopathy and related retinal vascular disorders. The animal model is characterized by deficiency or inhibition of Dp71 expression, leading to functional and morphological alterations in Müller glial cells and consequent breakdown of the blood-retinal barrier. This model recapitulates key features of human retinal vascular diseases, including impaired potassium and water homeostasis, abnormal Müller cell volume regulation, and increased vascular permeability.

The invention encompasses genetically modified animals with targeted disruption of the Dp71 gene, as well as animals treated with inhibitors of Dp71 expression or function. The animal model finds particular utility in screening potential therapeutic agents for retinal vascular diseases, studying disease pathophysiology, and identifying diagnostic or prognostic markers. The invention further provides methods for using the animal model in drug discovery and development processes targeting retinal edema and vascular leakage.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention is based on experimental studies involving retinal detachment models in both control (C57BL/6) and Dp71-null mice. These studies demonstrated that experimental retinal detachment induces reactive Müller cell gliosis accompanied by decreased expression of Dp71 protein and mRNA. The Dp71 downregulation correlates with mislocation of Kir4.1 potassium channels and reduced expression of AQP4 water channels, leading to disturbed cellular volume regulation. Importantly, Dp71-null mice exhibit similar functional deficits in Müller cells even without retinal detachment, along with dramatically increased blood-retinal barrier permeability compared to controls.

The results of these experiments established that Dp71 plays a key role in maintaining retinal potassium and water homeostasis, cellular volume regulation, and blood-retinal barrier integrity. The Dp71-deficient animal model therefore provides a valuable tool for studying retinal vascular diseases and developing therapeutic interventions.

### Non Human Model According to the Invention

The non-human animal model according to the invention is characterized by increased retinal vascular permeability resulting from deficiency or inhibition of Dp71 expression or function. The term "increased retinal vascular permeability" refers to a pathological condition wherein the blood-retinal barrier exhibits enhanced permeability to plasma components, particularly proteins such as albumin, leading to vascular leakage and retinal edema formation. This condition can be quantified using established methods such as the Evans blue dye technique, which measures albumin extravasation from retinal blood vessels into the retinal tissue.

The term "Dp71" refers to the 71 kDa dystrophin gene product, a cytoskeletal protein belonging to the dystrophin-glycoprotein complex. Dp71 represents the major dystrophin product expressed in the central nervous system and retina, where it plays crucial roles in maintaining membrane stability and organizing specialized membrane domains. The Dp71 protein structure includes an N-terminal domain that binds to actin filaments, a central rod domain containing spectrin-like repeats, and a C-terminal domain that interacts with dystroglycans and other components of the dystrophin-associated protein complex (DAPC).

Dp71 exists as multiple transcripts and isoforms generated through alternative splicing and promoter usage. The predominant Dp71 isoform in retina contains all constitutive exons and shows specific expression in Müller glial cells. Alternative splicing generates variants with different C-terminal domains that may influence protein-protein interactions within the DAPC.

The term "DAP" refers to dystrophin-associated proteins that form the DAP complex (DAPC) with Dp71. The DAPC structure comprises transmembrane proteins (α- and β-dystroglycans, sarcoglycans), cytoplasmic proteins (syntrophins, dystrobrevins), and extracellular proteins (laminin). In skeletal muscle, the DAPC provides mechanical stability by linking the extracellular matrix to the intracellular cytoskeleton. In retina, the DAPC primarily functions to maintain the polarized distribution of Kir4.1 and AQP4 channels in Müller cells.

The DAPC components include α-dystroglycan, which binds extracellular matrix proteins; β-dystroglycan, which interacts with dystrophin; the sarcoglycan subcomplex (α-, β-, γ-, δ-sarcoglycans); syntrophins, which bind to ion channels; and dystrobrevins, which interact with syntrophins and dystrophin. These components collectively form a scaffold that anchors membrane proteins to the cytoskeleton and organizes signaling complexes.

In the context of the invention, an "inhibitor of expression" refers to any agent that reduces or prevents the expression of Dp71 or DAP genes at the transcriptional or translational level. Such inhibitors include small organic molecules that interfere with Dp71 or DAP gene transcription or mRNA translation. These molecules may target transcription factors, promoter elements, or translation initiation factors involved in Dp71 or DAP expression.

Anti-sense oligonucleotide constructs represent another class of expression inhibitors. These are single-stranded DNA or RNA molecules designed to hybridize with complementary sequences in Dp71 or DAP mRNA, thereby blocking translation or promoting mRNA degradation. The oligonucleotides typically range from 15-25 nucleotides in length and may include chemical modifications to enhance stability and binding affinity.

siRNA inhibitors of Dp71 or DAP genes comprise double-stranded RNA molecules approximately 21-23 nucleotides long that induce sequence-specific mRNA degradation through the RNA interference pathway. These siRNAs are designed to target specific regions of Dp71 or DAP mRNA and are typically introduced into cells using transfection reagents or viral vectors.

shRNA inhibitors represent DNA constructs that encode short hairpin RNAs, which are processed intracellularly into functional siRNAs. These constructs are typically delivered using viral vectors or plasmids and allow for sustained inhibition of target gene expression. The shRNA sequences are designed to form stem-loop structures that are processed by Dicer into active siRNA duplexes.

Ribozyme inhibitors are RNA molecules with catalytic activity that can cleave specific Dp71 or DAP mRNA targets. These ribozymes recognize their target mRNAs through complementary base pairing and cleave the phosphodiester backbone at specific sites, leading to mRNA degradation. Ribozymes can be delivered as synthetic molecules or expressed from DNA vectors.

Modifications to oligonucleotides can be introduced to increase their stability and efficacy. These modifications include phosphorothioate backbone substitutions, 2'-O-methyl or 2'-fluoro sugar modifications, locked nucleic acids (LNAs), and peptide nucleic acids (PNAs). Such modifications enhance resistance to nucleases and improve target binding affinity while maintaining biological activity.

Vectors for delivering inhibitors of expression include various viral and non-viral systems. Viral vectors provide efficient gene transfer and sustained expression in target cells. Retroviral vectors integrate into the host genome, enabling long-term expression of inhibitory RNAs. These vectors are particularly useful for creating stable cell lines or transgenic animals with reduced Dp71 expression.

Adenoviral vectors offer high transduction efficiency for both dividing and non-dividing cells, making them suitable for retinal gene delivery. These vectors remain episomal and provide transient but high-level expression of inhibitory RNAs. Adeno-associated viral (AAV) vectors combine efficient transduction with long-term expression and low immunogenicity, making them particularly suitable for retinal applications.

Plasmid vectors represent non-viral alternatives for delivering inhibitory constructs. These circular DNA molecules can be engineered to express shRNAs or other inhibitory RNAs under the control of appropriate promoters. Delivery methods for plasmid vectors include electroporation, lipofection, and nanoparticle-mediated transfer, which can be optimized for retinal tissue targeting.

Heterologous regulatory regions can be incorporated into expression vectors to achieve cell-specific or inducible expression of inhibitory RNAs. Specific promoters for Müller glial cells include those derived from the glutamine synthetase, cellular retinaldehyde-binding protein (CRALBP), or vimentin genes. These promoters drive selective expression in Müller cells while minimizing effects on other retinal cell types.

Deficient animals for Dp71 or DAP genes can be produced using various genetic engineering techniques. Methods for producing deficient animals include conventional knockout strategies, where the target gene is disrupted in all cells throughout development. Embryonic stem cell technology allows for precise targeting of specific genomic loci through homologous recombination, followed by generation of germline-transmitting chimeric animals.

Conditional knock-out of targeted genes enables tissue-specific or temporally controlled gene inactivation. The Cre-loxP system represents a widely used approach, where loxP sites flank the target gene and Cre recombinase expression is controlled by cell-specific or inducible promoters. This system allows for precise control over the timing and location of gene inactivation.

An example of a deficient animal for Dp71 is the Dp71-null mouse, which was generated by replacing most of the first exon and part of the first intron of the Dp71 gene with a β-gal-neomycin resistance cassette. This modification abolishes Dp71 expression without affecting other dystrophin gene products. These mice exhibit characteristic retinal vascular abnormalities including mislocalization of Kir4.1 and AQP4, impaired Müller cell function, and increased blood-retinal barrier permeability.

Factors to consider include species characteristics, genetic background, disease relevance, and practical considerations such as lifespan and breeding characteristics. ## EXAMPLE

The following example illustrates the generation and characterization of the Dp71-null mouse model according to the invention:

Dp71-null mice were obtained by replacing most of the first exon and part of the first intron of the Dp71 gene with a β-gal-neomycin resistance (β-geo) cassette through homologous recombination. Control mice (C57BL/6J strain) were obtained from commercial suppliers. All animal procedures followed institutional guidelines for animal care and use, with appropriate anesthesia and aseptic techniques employed for survival surgeries.

Primary antibodies used for characterization included monoclonal anti-β-Actin and anti-GFAP antibodies, monoclonal anti-β-dystroglycan antibodies, and polyclonal antibodies against dystrophins (H4), utrophin (K7), Kir4.1, and AQP4. Secondary antibodies conjugated to fluorophores or horseradish peroxidase enabled detection through immunohistochemistry or immunoblotting.

Retinal detachment was induced in adult mice through subretinal injection of sodium hyaluronate under ketamine/xylazine anesthesia. Pupils were dilated with tropicamide before surgery. Control eyes received sham injections without hyaluronate. Animals with surgical complications were excluded from the study.

Immunohistochemistry procedures involved fixation of enucleated eyes in paraformaldehyde, cryoprotection in sucrose, and sectioning at 10 μm thickness. Sections underwent permeabilization, blocking, and incubation with primary antibodies followed by fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies. Fluorescence microscopy visualized protein localization patterns.

Western blot analysis utilized retinal protein extracts resolved on gradient gels and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Chemiluminescence detection revealed protein expression levels, with band densities quantified relative to β-Actin controls.

Quantitative RT-PCR analysis extracted total retinal RNA using Trizol reagent, followed by reverse transcription and real-time PCR amplification with SYBR Green. Primer pairs specific for Dp71 and β-Actin enabled relative quantification of mRNA levels using the ΔΔCt method.

Glial cell isolation involved retinal digestion with papain and DNase I, followed by mechanical dissociation to obtain single-cell suspensions. Cells were maintained in serum-free medium for electrophysiological recordings within three hours of isolation.

Electrophysiological recordings employed the whole-cell patch-clamp technique to measure membrane currents, potentials, and capacitances. Voltage-clamp protocols assessed ion channel activity, while current-clamp measurements determined resting membrane potentials. Data analysis compared parameters between experimental groups.

Müller cell swelling experiments utilized acutely isolated retinal slices loaded with Mitotracker Orange dye. Hypotonic challenge induced cellular swelling measured by confocal microscopy. Pharmacological agents tested for swelling inhibition included adenosine, phospholipase A2 inhibitors, and cyclooxygenase inhibitors.

Blood-retinal barrier permeability was quantified using the Evans blue method. Mice received intravenous Evans blue injections followed by perfusion and retinal extraction. Formamide extracted the dye from dried retinas, with spectrophotometric quantification relative to plasma concentrations.

Results demonstrated that experimental retinal detachment in control mice induced reactive Müller cell gliosis evidenced by GFAP upregulation. Detachment caused significant reductions in Dp71 protein and mRNA levels, accompanied by Kir4.1 mislocation and AQP4 downregulation. Dp71-null mice exhibited similar abnormalities without detachment, including increased vascular permeability, osmotic Müller cell swelling, and altered potassium currents. These findings establish the Dp71-null mouse as a valid model for studying retinal vascular permeability disorders.
