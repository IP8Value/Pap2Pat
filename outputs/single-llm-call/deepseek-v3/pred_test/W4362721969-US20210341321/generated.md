# DESCRIPTION  

## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention relates generally to microfluidic flow rate sensors and more specifically to a nanomembrane-based flow sensor incorporating a microwave resonator for precise flow rate measurement in microfluidic applications. The invention provides an integrated on-chip solution for monitoring fluid flow rates with high sensitivity and fast response times, suitable for both liquid and gas flow measurements. The disclosed flow sensor operates by detecting deformations of a thin nanomembrane caused by fluid flow, where such deformations are transduced into measurable electrical signals through an integrated microwave resonator structure.  

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION (PRIOR ART)  

Microfluidic systems require precise control and measurement of flow rates for applications ranging from biomedical diagnostics to chemical synthesis. Conventional flow measurement techniques rely on syringe pumps or peristaltic pumps, which suffer from inherent delays and fluctuations that compromise measurement accuracy. While commercial flow sensors exist, they are typically not integrated at the chip scale, limiting their utility for localized flow sensing in microfluidic channels.  

Thermal flow sensors represent one class of on-chip flow measurement devices that operate without moving parts. However, their performance depends critically on the thermal properties of the fluid being measured, making them unreliable when fluid composition varies. Coriolis flowmeters offer improved dynamic range but require complex oscillating channel structures that increase fabrication costs and complexity.  

Capacitive flow sensors have been developed that measure flow-induced deflections of microstructures. For example, perforated cantilever structures have been used where fluid flow causes measurable deflection. While sensitive, these designs require fluid to pass through the sensing structure itself, potentially disrupting flow patterns. Optical flow sensors provide non-contact measurement but require complex optical setups that are difficult to integrate.  

Recent developments include electrical flow sensors operating at radio frequencies. One approach uses polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) membrane deflection measured by nearby microwave sensors. While achieving reasonable sensitivity, the elastomeric nature of PDMS results in slow recovery times on the order of minutes, limiting measurement frequency. There remains a need for robust, sensitive, and fast-responding flow sensors that can be fully integrated into microfluidic systems without disrupting fluid flow patterns.  

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention provides a nanomembrane-based flow rate sensor that overcomes limitations of prior art devices. The sensor comprises a thin silicon nitride membrane integrated with a microwave resonator structure, where fluid flow induces measurable deformations of the membrane. The microwave resonator detects these deformations through changes in its resonant frequency, providing a sensitive and rapid measurement of flow rate.  

Key aspects of the invention include the use of a crystalline silicon nitride membrane that provides mechanical robustness while maintaining sufficient flexibility for flow sensing. The microwave resonator is fabricated directly on the membrane surface, creating an integrated sensing structure. Flow rate measurement is achieved through three distinct mechanisms: (1) quasi-static membrane deformation causing proportional frequency shifts, (2) periodic membrane pulsations at constant flow with frequency dependent on flow rate, and (3) transient frequency shifts during flow changes.  

The invention offers several advantages over prior art, including compatibility with both liquid and gas flows, fast response times due to the crystalline membrane material, and high sensitivity enabled by the microwave resonator transduction. The sensor can be fabricated using standard microfluidic manufacturing techniques and integrated directly into microfluidic channels without disrupting flow patterns. Multiple sensing mechanisms provide flexibility in measurement approaches suitable for different flow regimes and applications.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION  

The flow rate sensor of the present invention comprises a thin silicon nitride membrane integrated with a microwave resonator structure. The membrane typically has a thickness of approximately 220 nm and lateral dimensions ranging from 300 μm to 1.5 mm, depending on the desired flow measurement range. The microwave resonator is fabricated as a coplanar waveguide (CPW) structure with signal and ground electrodes patterned directly on the membrane surface.  

Fluid flow through an adjacent microchannel induces deformations of the membrane, which in turn alter the capacitance between the CPW electrodes. This capacitance change modifies the resonant frequency of the microwave structure, providing a sensitive measure of flow rate. The CPW resonator is designed with a characteristic impedance of 50 Ω to match standard measurement electronics. The active sensing region is defined by a narrowing of the electrode gap to approximately 20 μm over the membrane area, enhancing sensitivity to membrane deformations.  

Three distinct mechanisms enable flow rate measurement. First, quasi-static membrane deformation under constant flow causes a proportional shift in resonator frequency. Second, at certain flow rates, the membrane exhibits periodic pulsations with frequency dependent on flow rate. Third, abrupt flow changes induce transient frequency shifts that can be correlated with flow rate changes. These mechanisms provide complementary approaches suitable for different flow regimes and measurement requirements.  

The silicon nitride membrane provides several advantages over elastomeric materials like PDMS. Its crystalline structure enables rapid mechanical response, with recovery times on the order of seconds compared to minutes for PDMS-based sensors. The material's robustness allows operation with both liquids and gases, including at pressures up to 4 bar. Membrane dimensions can be optimized for specific flow ranges, with larger membranes providing higher sensitivity at low flow rates and smaller membranes suitable for higher flow regimes.  

Fabrication of the flow sensor begins with a silicon wafer having a layered structure comprising a 500 μm silicon substrate, 2 μm silicon dioxide layer, and 220 nm silicon nitride top layer. The membrane is formed by photolithographic patterning of an etch window on the wafer backside, followed by inductively coupled plasma etching and potassium hydroxide wet etching to remove silicon and silicon dioxide layers, leaving the silicon nitride membrane.  

The microwave resonator is fabricated by patterning gold electrodes on the membrane surface using photolithography and metal deposition. The CPW structure includes signal and ground lines with a 400 μm gap that narrows to 20 μm over the membrane area. A 100 nm gold layer provides sufficient conductivity while maintaining mechanical flexibility. The microfluidic channel is fabricated separately using SU-8 photoresist molds and polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) casting, then aligned and bonded to the membrane chip using plasma activation.  

Operation of the flow sensor involves monitoring changes in the microwave resonator's resonant frequency caused by membrane deformations. A phase-locked loop circuit maintains the resonator at its resonant frequency, with any deviations caused by membrane deformation appearing as error signals. This approach provides high sensitivity, with demonstrated resolution of 0.5 μL/min for liquid flows. The sensor's response time is on the order of seconds, limited primarily by the mechanical response of the membrane rather than the electronic measurement.  

Experimental verification of the flow sensor demonstrates its operation across a wide range of flow rates. For liquid flows, the sensor shows a linear relationship between flow rate and frequency shift in the quasi-static deformation regime. At constant flows, periodic membrane pulsations occur with frequency proportional to flow rate. Transient response to flow changes provides additional measurement capability. Gas flow measurements show similar behavior, with frequency shifts proportional to applied pressure up to 4 bar.  

The sensor's performance was characterized using an experimental setup comprising a microfluidic flow controller and electronic measurement system. A pressure-driven pump provided controlled fluid flow through the microchannel, with a commercial thermal flow sensor serving as a reference. Optical microscopy allowed direct observation of membrane deformations simultaneous with electronic measurements. Data acquisition captured both resonator frequency and reference flow rate at 20 Hz sampling frequency.  

In liquid flow experiments, the sensor demonstrated three distinct response regimes. At low flow rates (<0.4 μL/min), no measurable membrane deformation occurred. Intermediate flow rates (0.4-3.5 μL/min) induced periodic pulsations with frequency proportional to flow rate. Higher flow rates (>5 μL/min) caused quasi-static membrane deformations with magnitude proportional to flow rate. The transition between regimes depends on membrane dimensions and fluid properties.  

Air flow experiments showed similar behavior, with frequency shifts proportional to applied pressure. A linear response was observed at higher pressures (>20 mbar), with slight nonlinearity at lower pressures. The sensor demonstrated robustness to high pressure operation, withstanding pressures up to 4 bar without damage. Membrane dimensions were varied to optimize for different flow ranges, with larger membranes providing higher sensitivity at low flow rates.  

The flow sensor's performance advantages include its integrated design, allowing direct incorporation into microfluidic systems without disrupting flow patterns. The crystalline silicon nitride membrane provides fast response times and durability compared to elastomeric materials. Multiple sensing mechanisms offer flexibility in measurement approaches suitable for different applications. The microwave transduction provides high sensitivity without requiring optical access or complex measurement setups.  

Potential applications of the invention include organ-on-a-chip systems requiring precise nutrient flow control, drug screening platforms needing accurate dosage measurements, and microfluidic reactors requiring real-time flow monitoring. The sensor's compatibility with both liquids and gases expands its utility to pneumatic microfluidic systems as well. Fabrication using standard microfluidic processes enables cost-effective production and integration with existing microfluidic platforms.
