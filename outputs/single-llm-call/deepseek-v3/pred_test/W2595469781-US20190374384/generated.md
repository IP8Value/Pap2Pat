# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Glaucoma represents a group of ocular disorders characterized by progressive optic nerve damage that constitutes the leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide. The fundamental pathophysiology involves impaired aqueous humor drainage leading to elevated intraocular pressure (IOP), which remains the primary modifiable risk factor for disease progression. Current therapeutic strategies aim to reduce IOP through pharmacological interventions, laser therapies, and surgical procedures when conservative measures prove insufficient.

The surgical armamentarium for glaucoma management primarily consists of trabeculectomy, glaucoma drainage implants (GDIs), and minimally invasive glaucoma surgeries (MIGS). Trabeculectomy, considered the gold standard surgical intervention for primary open-angle glaucoma, creates an artificial drainage pathway by removing a portion of the trabecular meshwork and sclera. However, this procedure demonstrates limited efficacy in pediatric populations and certain secondary glaucomas. Furthermore, trabeculectomy carries significant risks including hypotony, bleb leaks, and endophthalmitis.

Glaucoma drainage implants were developed to address the limitations of trabeculectomy, particularly for refractory cases and secondary glaucomas. These devices typically consist of a tube inserted into the anterior chamber connected to an episcleral plate that facilitates aqueous drainage. Despite their widespread use, conventional GDIs suffer from high failure rates primarily due to fibrotic encapsulation of the episcleral plate, which obstructs aqueous outflow over time. Histopathological studies demonstrate that fibrous capsule formation around the implant plate represents the principal mechanism of late-term device failure.

Minimally invasive glaucoma surgeries have emerged as a safer alternative with reduced complication profiles compared to traditional procedures. MIGS devices target specific portions of the aqueous outflow pathway and are generally indicated for mild to moderate glaucoma cases. While exhibiting improved safety characteristics, current MIGS technologies demonstrate limited efficacy in advanced glaucoma and often serve primarily to reduce medication burden rather than replace conventional surgeries.

The common failure mode across all current glaucoma surgical modalities involves the biological response to implanted materials. The human body naturally attempts to isolate foreign objects through fibrotic encapsulation, ultimately compromising device functionality. This fundamental limitation underscores the critical need for innovative implant designs that can maintain aqueous drainage while mitigating fibrotic responses.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention discloses a novel glaucoma drainage implant featuring a microfluidic meshwork structure that fundamentally addresses the limitations of existing devices. The implant comprises a network of interconnected microchannels arranged in a flexible lattice configuration that promotes aqueous humor drainage while minimizing fibrotic tissue responses.

The microfluidic meshwork incorporates longitudinal microchannels oriented parallel to the primary drainage direction and lateral microchannels intersecting at predetermined angles to create a continuous fluid network. These microchannels feature porous walls that permit controlled fluid exchange with surrounding tissues while maintaining structural integrity. The microchannel geometry includes diameters between 5-50 μm and wall thicknesses of 1-10 μm, optimized for both fluid conductance and mechanical flexibility.

Multiple embodiments of the invention include two-dimensional planar mesh configurations and three-dimensional volumetric constructions. The two-dimensional embodiment provides advantages in surgical handling and implantation ease, while three-dimensional variants offer increased surface area and enhanced fluid distribution capabilities. A hybrid three-dimensional configuration combines dense microchannel networks in inner regions with more open architectures at peripheral zones.

The implant interfaces with conventional drainage tube components through a specialized transition region that maintains fluid communication while accommodating mechanical stresses. An integrated pressure-sensitive valve mechanism regulates flow rates in response to intraocular pressure fluctuations, providing protection against hypotony. The microchannel walls incorporate permeable sections with precisely controlled porosity to modulate drainage characteristics.

The microfluidic meshwork demonstrates exceptional flexibility with elastic moduli matching ocular tissues, typically in the range of 0.5-10 kPa. This mechanical compatibility minimizes interfacial stresses that typically provoke fibrotic responses. The lattice structure branches into multiple outlets that distribute aqueous humor over an expanded surface area, further reducing localized fluid accumulation that could stimulate fibrosis.

Methods of managing intraocular pressure using the invention involve implanting the microfluidic meshwork in the subconjunctival or subscleral space, where it establishes communication between the anterior chamber and episcleral venous system. The device maintains physiological pressure gradients while adapting to ocular movements through its compliant structure.

### Glaucoma Drainage Meshwork

The invention provides a revolutionary approach to glaucoma drainage through microfluidic meshwork technology. The fundamental structure comprises mesh 10, a flexible lattice incorporating longitudinal microchannels 12 and lateral microchannels 14 arranged in a precisely patterned network (Figure 1). These microchannels form continuous fluid pathways with cross-sectional dimensions optimized for aqueous humor transport while maintaining structural stability.

Microchannel geometry features circular, oval, or polygonal cross-sections with inner diameters between 8-20 μm and outer diameters of 15-30 μm. Channel walls 16 maintain thicknesses of 2-5 μm, providing sufficient strength without compromising flexibility. The microchannel network demonstrates porosity values of 30-70%, achieving optimal balance between fluid conductance and tissue integration.

The mesh 10 exhibits exceptional mechanical compliance with elastic modulus values matching episcleral tissue (1-5 kPa). This compatibility minimizes mechanical mismatch at the implant-tissue interface, a primary driver of fibrotic encapsulation. The lattice structure branches into multiple outlets 18 that distribute aqueous humor over an expanded area, reducing localized fluid accumulation that could stimulate inflammation.

Fluid outlets 20 incorporate adjustable flow characteristics through variable porosity membranes or pressure-sensitive valves. These regulate drainage rates in response to intraocular pressure fluctuations between 5-30 mmHg. The microchannel density ranges from 50-200 channels per millimeter, with periodic spacing of 50-150 μm between channel centers.

Two-dimensional mesh constructions provide advantages in surgical handling and implantation simplicity. These planar configurations facilitate placement under conjunctival flaps while maintaining sufficient surface area for effective drainage. Three-dimensional embodiments stack multiple mesh layers or incorporate out-of-plane channel architectures that enhance fluid distribution capabilities.

The hybrid three-dimensional construction combines dense microchannel networks in inner regions 22 with more open peripheral architectures 24. This configuration directs initial fluid flow through central high-capacity channels while permitting gradual distribution through peripheral zones. The transition between regions occurs gradually over 0.5-2 mm to prevent flow discontinuities.

The glaucoma drainage implant device 30 integrates the microfluidic meshwork with conventional tube components (Figure 2). The interface region 32 connects the drainage tube 34 to the distributing section 36 of the meshwork. This transition zone accommodates mechanical stresses while maintaining uninterrupted fluid communication. The pressure-regulating mechanism 38 incorporates responsive elements that modulate flow based on intraocular pressure measurements.

For minimally invasive applications, the MIGS stent device 40 provides a compact version of the technology (Figure 3). This embodiment features a reduced profile suitable for ab interno implantation through corneal incisions. The MIGS construction maintains the essential microchannel architecture while optimizing for delivery through small-gauge injector systems.

### Methods of Making

Fabrication of the glaucoma drainage meshes employs advanced micromanufacturing techniques adapted from semiconductor processing. The photolithography method begins with preparation of a silicon wafer substrate coated with a nickel-releasing layer. Photoresist patterning defines the microchannel walls through sequential exposure and development steps.

Sacrificial material deposition fills the channel spaces using specialized polymers that maintain structural integrity during subsequent processing. Mesh material deposition applies biocompatible polymers such as silicone or polyimide through spin-coating or vapor deposition techniques. The sacrificial layer undergoes selective dissolution after mesh formation, leaving the interconnected microchannel network.

Alternative fabrication approaches employ sacrificial template methods where drawn fiber networks serve as channel templates. Coating these templates with polymer solutions followed by template removal produces the microfluidic architecture. Surface treatments including plasma activation and chemical grafting enhance mesh biocompatibility and tissue integration.

Protein and peptide coatings applied through absorption or covalent bonding further modulate the healing response. These bioactive surfaces may incorporate anti-inflammatory agents, extracellular matrix components, or growth factors that promote favorable tissue integration while suppressing fibrosis.

### Methods of Use

Implantation procedures for the GDI device involve standard surgical approaches modified to accommodate the microfluidic meshwork. The surgical method includes creating a conjunctival peritomy followed by dissection of a sub-Tenon's pocket. The meshwork component positions within this space without suturing due to its self-adhering properties. The drainage tube inserts into the anterior chamber through a separate sclerotomy.

For MIGS applications, the ab interno approach utilizes a clear corneal incision for device delivery. The compact mesh structure deploys into the supraciliary or suprachoroidal space using specialized injector systems. This minimally invasive technique preserves conjunctival integrity and reduces postoperative recovery time.

Target implantation sites include the subconjunctival space for conventional GDIs and the supraciliary space for MIGS devices. The microfluidic meshwork adapts to these anatomical locations through its compliant structure, maintaining effective drainage across various ocular geometries.

## EXPERIMENTAL EXAMPLES

### Example 1: A Novel Flexible Microfluidic Meshwork to Reduce Fibrosis in Glaucoma Surgery

This example presents the development and testing of a modified glaucoma drainage implant incorporating a microfluidic meshwork design. The study aimed to evaluate the hypothesis that an ultra-flexible, conformable mesh structure could reduce fibrotic tissue formation compared to conventional implants.

The meshwork design featured interconnected microchannels with outer diameters of 20 μm and inner diameters of 8 μm, arranged in a grid pattern with 100 μm periodicity. Finite element simulations confirmed this configuration could provide aqueous outflow of 2 μL/min at 10 mmHg pressure differential. Fabrication employed photolithography techniques on silicon wafers with nickel-releasing layers, producing meshworks with 4 μm thickness and 7 mm × 7 mm overall dimensions.

Animal studies utilized three New Zealand white rabbits receiving bilateral implants - the microfluidic meshwork in one eye and a conventional AGV PF7 plate in the contralateral eye as control. Surgical implantation followed standard protocols without tube connection to isolate tissue responses. Postoperative evaluations at 3 months revealed striking differences in fibrotic responses.

Histological analysis demonstrated average fibrotic capsule thickness of 89 ± 6.6 μm around conventional implants compared to only 1.3 ± 0.6 μm surrounding the microfluidic meshworks (p = 0.002). These results strongly support the design hypothesis that cellular-scale, flexible microchannel networks can substantially suppress fibrotic encapsulation.

The study further identified that meshwork folding during implantation created localized areas of increased inflammation where flexibility was compromised. This observation confirmed the importance of maintaining the ultra-flexible characteristics of the unimpeded mesh structure. The rabbit model provided appropriate correlation with known human responses to glaucoma implants, supporting clinical applicability of the findings.

These experimental results demonstrate the significant advantages of the microfluidic meshwork approach over conventional glaucoma drainage devices. The dramatic reduction in fibrotic encapsulation suggests substantial improvements in long-term device functionality and surgical outcomes.
