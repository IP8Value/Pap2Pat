# DESCRIPTION  

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION  

Malaria represents a significant global health burden, causing substantial morbidity and mortality worldwide. The disease is caused by Plasmodium parasites transmitted through the bites of infected Anopheles mosquitoes. Malaria manifests clinically through cyclical fevers, chills, and flu-like symptoms, with severe cases progressing to organ failure, coma, and death. The World Health Organization estimates hundreds of millions of clinical cases annually, with the majority of fatalities occurring in children under five years of age in sub-Saharan Africa.  

The impact of malaria extends beyond public health concerns, imposing substantial economic burdens on affected regions and posing operational challenges for military personnel deployed in endemic areas. Current prevention strategies rely primarily on vector control measures such as insecticide-treated bed nets and indoor residual spraying, combined with chemoprophylaxis for travelers and military personnel. However, these approaches exhibit limitations including insecticide resistance, poor compliance with drug regimens, and lack of protection against drug-resistant parasite strains.  

Vaccine development has been pursued as a more sustainable solution for malaria control. The most advanced candidate, RTS,S, demonstrates partial efficacy through antibody-mediated mechanisms targeting the pre-erythrocytic stage. However, its limited protection duration and inability to induce robust cell-mediated immunity highlight the need for improved vaccine strategies. Phase 1/2 trials with whole parasite approaches, including radiation-attenuated sporozoites, have demonstrated higher efficacy but face manufacturing and delivery challenges.  

Recent advances in vaccine technology have explored immune-stimulatory conjugates and gene-based delivery systems. DNA vaccines offer advantages in stability and production but typically induce weak immune responses. Viral vectors, particularly adenoviruses, demonstrate superior immunogenicity for inducing cell-mediated immunity but face challenges from pre-existing immunity and rapid vector clearance. The present invention addresses these limitations through novel adenoviral vector designs and heterologous prime-boost regimens that synergistically enhance protective immune responses against malaria.  

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention provides a method for inducing an immune response against Plasmodium parasites through administration of a multivalent vaccine composition. The method comprises priming the immune system with DNA plasmids encoding malaria antigens followed by boosting with recombinant adenoviral vectors expressing the same antigens. The vaccine composition includes codon-optimized genes for circumsporozoite protein (CSP) and apical membrane antigen-1 (AMA1) from Plasmodium falciparum, delivered via engineered viral vectors designed to enhance immunogenicity while minimizing anti-vector immunity.  

The adenoviral vectors feature strategic deletions of viral genes essential for replication, rendering them replication-deficient while maintaining high-level transgene expression. Additional modifications include coat protein alterations to evade neutralizing antibodies and enhance targeting to antigen-presenting cells. The invention further provides methods for constructing the vaccine components, pharmaceutical formulations for administration, and regimens for optimal immune stimulation. Clinical data demonstrate the vaccine's ability to induce sterile protection against malaria challenge in human subjects, representing a significant advance over existing vaccine technologies.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION  

The invention provides a multivalent malaria vaccine with distinct advantages over current methodologies. The vaccine employs a heterologous prime-boost strategy combining DNA plasmid priming with adenoviral vector boosting to generate synergistic immune responses against multiple Plasmodium life cycle stages. This approach overcomes limitations of single-modality vaccines by leveraging the unique immunological properties of each platform.  

The adenoviral vector composition forms a critical component of the vaccine system. The vectors derive from human adenovirus serotype 5 (Ad5) with specific genomic modifications to enhance safety and immunogenicity. Essential modifications include deletion of the E1 region to prevent replication and partial deletion of the E3 region to accommodate larger transgene inserts. The vectors may further incorporate deletions in the E4 region to reduce host cell gene modulation. These replication-deficient vectors propagate in complementing cell lines engineered to provide the missing viral gene functions in trans.  

The adenoviral genome architecture incorporates several innovative features to optimize antigen expression and immune recognition. The expression cassette includes a strong viral promoter (human cytomegalovirus immediate early promoter) driving transcription of codon-optimized malaria antigens. The antigens incorporate mammalian signal sequences to ensure proper cellular processing and presentation. The vector design further includes optimized polyadenylation signals and spacer sequences to stabilize mRNA transcripts.  

A key innovation involves modification of adenoviral coat proteins to evade pre-existing immunity and enhance targeting to immune cells. The invention teaches manipulation of fiber proteins through insertion of RGD motifs that bind integrins on antigen-presenting cells. Additional modifications include shortening of the fiber shaft and incorporation of heterologous targeting sequences specific for dendritic cell surface markers. These alterations reduce hepatic sequestration while promoting direct antigen presentation to professional immune cells.  

The invention further provides methods for generating diverse coat protein variants through directed evolution. This process involves creating libraries of mutated coat protein genes, incorporating them into adenoviral vectors, and selecting variants with improved immune cell targeting under competitive conditions. Selected variants demonstrate reduced neutralization by pre-existing antibodies while maintaining efficient transduction of antigen-presenting cells.  

The malaria antigens expressed by the vaccine include Plasmodium falciparum CSP and AMA1, selected for their expression across multiple parasite life cycle stages. CSP represents the dominant surface protein on sporozoites and is expressed during early liver stage development. AMA1 participates in hepatocyte invasion and is additionally expressed during blood stage infection. The antigens are engineered with mammalian signal sequences and codon-optimized for high expression in human cells.  

The expression system allows for flexible configuration of transcriptional control elements. The invention encompasses use of constitutive, inducible, and tissue-specific promoters to regulate antigen expression. Chimeric promoters combining elements from viral and cellular sources provide additional control over expression levels and kinetics. The system further incorporates multiple polyadenylation signals to ensure proper transcript processing.  

Administration of the vaccine follows an optimized prime-boost regimen. The priming phase comprises three intramuscular injections of DNA plasmids at monthly intervals. The boosting phase follows with a single administration of adenoviral vectors approximately four months after the final prime. Alternative regimens may incorporate different intervals between prime and boost or multiple boost administrations. The vaccine may be delivered via various routes including intramuscular, intradermal, or subcutaneous injection, with formulations designed for either immediate or controlled release.  

The vaccine components are formulated as sterile pharmaceutical compositions suitable for human administration. The adenoviral vectors are prepared in buffered solutions containing stabilizing excipients to maintain vector viability during storage. The invention provides lyophilized formulations for extended shelf life and ready reconstitution prior to use. Dose ranges are established to balance immunogenicity with acceptable reactogenicity profiles.  

Clinical evaluation demonstrates the vaccine's ability to induce protective immunity against malaria challenge. In human trials, the regimen induced sterile protection in 27% of vaccinees, representing the highest efficacy reported for a subunit malaria vaccine. Protection correlated strongly with cell-mediated immune responses, particularly CD8+ T cell activity against AMA1. The vaccine exhibited a favorable safety profile with primarily mild local and systemic reactions.  

### Example 1  

Adenoviral vectors expressing P. falciparum CSP and AMA1 were generated using standard molecular biology techniques. The vectors were derived from Ad5 with deletions in E1 and partial E3 regions. The expression cassettes incorporated a CMV promoter driving transcription of codon-optimized antigen genes. BALB/c mice were immunized with separate or combined vectors via intramuscular injection. Sera and splenocytes were collected four weeks post-immunization for immune analysis.  

ELISA measurements demonstrated antigen-specific antibody responses in immunized animals. ELISpot assays revealed robust IFN-γ production by splenocytes stimulated with CSP or AMA1 peptides. Flow cytometry confirmed antigen-specific CD8+ T cell responses in vaccinated mice. Comparison of separate versus combined vector administration showed equivalent immunogenicity, supporting the use of mixed vector preparations.  

### Example 2  

New Zealand White rabbits received intramuscular injections of the adenoviral vaccine formulation at doses ranging from 1x10^9 to 1x10^11 viral particles. Animals were monitored for local and systemic reactions over 28 days. No severe adverse events were observed, with only mild transient reactions at the injection site.  

Serum samples collected at multiple time points demonstrated dose-dependent antibody responses to both CSP and AMA1. ELISpot analysis of peripheral blood mononuclear cells showed antigen-specific T cell activation. Necropsy and histopathology revealed no vaccine-related abnormalities, confirming the formulation's safety in this animal model.  

### Example 3  

A Phase 1/2a clinical trial evaluated the vaccine in malaria-naïve adults. Subjects received three priming doses of DNA plasmids followed by one adenoviral vector boost. The regimen was well tolerated with primarily mild injection site reactions.  

Controlled human malaria infection challenge demonstrated sterile protection in 27% of vaccinees. Protected subjects exhibited strong CD8+ T cell responses to AMA1 by ELISpot and intracellular cytokine staining. No correlation was found between antibody responses and protection, confirming the cell-mediated mechanism of immunity. The trial established proof-of-concept for adenoviral vector-based malaria vaccination in humans.
