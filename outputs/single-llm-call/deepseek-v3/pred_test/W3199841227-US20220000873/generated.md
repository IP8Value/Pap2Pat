## DESCRIPTION

### BACKGROUND

The intestinal barrier serves as a critical interface between the external environment and internal physiological systems, maintaining selective permeability to nutrients while preventing passage of harmful luminal contents. This barrier function is primarily mediated by the intestinal epithelium, which forms tight junctions between adjacent cells to regulate paracellular transport. The intestinal barrier also includes a mucous layer produced by goblet cells and an underlying network of immune cells that provide additional protection against pathogens and toxins. Proper functioning of this multilayered barrier is essential for maintaining homeostasis and preventing systemic inflammation.

Loss of intestinal barrier function has been implicated in numerous pathological conditions. When the integrity of the epithelial lining is compromised, increased intestinal permeability allows translocation of bacteria, bacterial products, and dietary antigens into the systemic circulation. This phenomenon, often referred to as "leaky gut," has been associated with inflammatory bowel diseases, celiac disease, irritable bowel syndrome, metabolic disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and aging-related pathologies. The breakdown of intestinal barrier function appears to be both a cause and consequence of various disease processes, creating a vicious cycle that exacerbates underlying conditions.

The relationship between intestinal barrier dysfunction and disease progression has become increasingly recognized in medical research. Inflammatory bowel diseases, for example, are characterized by alterations in tight junction proteins and increased epithelial permeability. Similarly, celiac disease patients demonstrate enhanced intestinal permeability even before the development of villous atrophy. Emerging evidence suggests that intestinal barrier defects may precede and contribute to the pathogenesis of conditions ranging from autoimmune disorders to neurodegenerative diseases, highlighting the importance of maintaining intestinal barrier integrity for overall health.

### SUMMARY

The present invention relates to the use of S3QEL compounds (Suppressors of site IIIQo Electron Leak) for preserving intestinal barrier function and treating conditions associated with increased intestinal permeability. S3QELs are small-molecule compounds that specifically suppress superoxide generation at the outer ubiquinone-binding site (site IIIQo) of mitochondrial complex III without inhibiting normal electron transport or oxidative phosphorylation. These compounds have been found to effectively reduce intestinal permeability in both Drosophila and mammalian models.

When administered to Drosophila melanogaster fed high-nutrient diets, S3QELs significantly decreased intestinal permeability, reduced enterocyte apoptosis, and extended median lifespan. Similar protective effects were observed in mice fed high-fat diets, where S3QEL treatment prevented diet-induced increases in intestinal permeability and preserved expression of tight junction and mucin genes. The compounds appear to work by specifically inhibiting superoxide production from mitochondrial complex III in enterocytes, thereby preventing oxidative stress-induced damage to the intestinal epithelium.

The invention encompasses multiple embodiments of methods for treating or preventing intestinal barrier dysfunction. In one embodiment, the method comprises administering to a subject an effective amount of an agent that partially suppresses superoxide generation from mitochondrial complex III. Another embodiment involves administering a composition containing one or more S3QEL compounds. Further embodiments specify particular structural classes of S3QELs, including sulfanyloxoquinazoline derivatives (embodiment 4) and pyrazolopyrimidine derivatives (embodiment 6). Additional embodiments describe methods using specific S3QEL compounds, including but not limited to S3QEL1.2, S3QEL2.2, and S3QEL3 (embodiments 5, 7-22).

The invention also includes embodiments involving various delivery methods for S3QEL compounds. One embodiment describes a method incorporating a delivery vehicle to enhance bioavailability (embodiment 23). Another embodiment utilizes lipid or liposome formulations to improve compound stability and absorption (embodiment 24). These various embodiments provide flexible approaches for addressing intestinal barrier dysfunction in different clinical contexts and patient populations.

### DETAILED DESCRIPTION

Experimental evidence demonstrates that S3QEL compounds effectively protect against intestinal permeability and reduce apoptotic cell death in enterocytes. In Drosophila models, administration of S3QELs to flies fed high-nutrient diets significantly decreased intestinal permeability as measured by the smurf assay. The compounds also reduced the number of apoptotic cells in the intestinal epithelium while having no significant effect on proliferating cell numbers. These findings suggest that S3QELs specifically protect against oxidative stress-induced enterocyte damage without altering normal epithelial turnover.

In mammalian studies, S3QEL administration to mice fed high-fat diets prevented diet-induced increases in intestinal permeability as measured by FITC-dextran absorption and fecal albumin levels. The compounds preserved normal expression of tight junction proteins and mucins in both the colon and distal small intestine. Notably, S3QEL treatment also prevented the downregulation of Klf4, a transcription factor critical for goblet cell differentiation and mucin production. These protective effects occurred without significant changes in body weight, adiposity, or glucose tolerance, indicating that S3QELs specifically target intestinal barrier function rather than systemic metabolic parameters.

Observations in enterocytes reveal that superoxide production from complex III of the mitochondrial electron transport chain plays a key role in intestinal barrier dysfunction. Genetic knockdown of superoxide dismutases (Sod1 and Sod2) in Drosophila enterocytes increased intestinal permeability and apoptosis while decreasing lifespan, effects that were ameliorated by S3QEL treatment. This demonstrates that superoxide generated in both the cytosol and mitochondrial matrix contributes to epithelial damage and that S3QELs can protect against oxidative stress in both compartments.

The invention provides methods for treatment or prophylaxis of age-related and pathology-associated increases in intestinal barrier permeability. One embodiment involves treating diet-induced intestinal permeability by administering S3QEL compounds to subjects consuming high-fat or high-nutrient diets. Another embodiment addresses age-related increases in intestinal permeability through periodic or continuous S3QEL administration to elderly subjects. A further embodiment targets pathology-associated increases in intestinal barrier permeability by combining S3QEL treatment with standard therapies for underlying conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease or celiac disease.

Therapeutic treatment with S3QEL compounds can ameliorate one or more symptoms of pathologies associated with intestinal permeability. In various embodiments, such treatment may reduce systemic inflammation, decrease bacterial translocation, improve nutrient absorption, or slow disease progression. The compounds may be administered as monotherapies or in combination with other agents targeting specific disease processes.

#### Celiac Disease and Ulcerative Colitis (UC)

Intestinal barrier dysfunction plays a central role in the pathogenesis of celiac disease and ulcerative colitis. In celiac disease, gluten exposure triggers an immune response that damages the intestinal epithelium, leading to increased permeability and further antigen exposure. This creates a self-perpetuating cycle of inflammation and barrier disruption. The invention provides methods to break this cycle by preserving epithelial integrity during gluten exposure, potentially allowing for gradual immune tolerance development.

Ulcerative colitis and other inflammatory bowel diseases are characterized by profound alterations in epithelial tight junctions and increased intestinal permeability. Proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IFN-γ disrupt tight junction proteins, while increased epithelial apoptosis creates gaps in the mucosal barrier. The invention addresses these mechanisms by reducing oxidative stress in enterocytes, thereby preserving tight junction integrity and decreasing apoptotic cell death.

Prion protein dislocation has been observed in inflammatory bowel disease, potentially contributing to disease pathogenesis through mechanisms involving oxidative stress. The invention's ability to reduce mitochondrial superoxide production may help maintain proper protein folding and localization in the intestinal epithelium. Additionally, the increased incidence of apoptotic events in IBD may be mitigated by S3QEL-mediated reduction of oxidative damage to enterocytes.

The role of proinflammatory cytokines in IBD pathogenesis is well-established, with TNF-α being a particularly important mediator of barrier dysfunction. The invention's effects on intestinal permeability may help reduce cytokine production by limiting bacterial translocation and subsequent immune activation. This creates a potential positive feedback loop where improved barrier function reduces inflammation, which in turn further improves barrier integrity.

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) has also been associated with intestinal barrier dysfunction, though the mechanisms differ from those in IBD. Structural changes in epithelial cells, including alterations in microvilli and tight junctions, have been observed in IBS patients. The invention provides methods to strengthen the intestinal barrier in IBS, potentially reducing visceral hypersensitivity and abnormal motility associated with increased permeability.

#### Parkinson's Disease and Alzheimer's Disease.

Emerging research highlights the importance of brain-gut axis interactions in neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson's and Alzheimer's disease. Gut dysbiosis and increased intestinal permeability have been observed in both conditions, potentially contributing to neuroinflammation and disease progression. The invention provides methods to preserve intestinal barrier function in neurodegenerative disorders, possibly slowing disease progression by reducing systemic inflammation.

The innate immune system plays a significant role in Parkinson's disease pathogenesis, with microglial activation being a hallmark of the condition. Increased intestinal permeability may contribute to this activation through systemic dissemination of gut-derived inflammatory mediators. By maintaining intestinal barrier integrity, the invention may help modulate neuroimmune responses in Parkinson's disease.

Disturbances in the adaptive immune system have also been documented in Parkinson's disease, including alterations in T cell populations and autoantibody production. These changes may be secondary to increased exposure to gut-derived antigens through a permeable intestinal barrier. The invention's ability to preserve epithelial integrity could help normalize adaptive immune function in neurodegenerative conditions.

Gut microbiota changes have been extensively documented in both Parkinson's and Alzheimer's disease, with specific alterations correlating with disease severity. The invention's effects on intestinal permeability may help maintain a healthier gut microbiome by preventing pathological bacterial translocation and subsequent dysbiosis.

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from gram-negative bacteria has been implicated in Alzheimer's disease pathology, with elevated levels found in AD brains. The invention may reduce systemic LPS exposure by preserving intestinal barrier function, potentially slowing neuroinflammatory processes in Alzheimer's disease. This approach could complement existing therapies targeting amyloid and tau pathology.

#### Active Agents.

The invention involves administration of S3QEL compounds that specifically target site IIIQo electron leak in mitochondrial complex III. This site has the largest capacity for superoxide production among mitochondrial sites and delivers superoxide to both the mitochondrial matrix and cytosol. By selectively suppressing superoxide generation at this site without affecting normal electron transport, S3QELs provide a targeted approach to reducing oxidative stress in enterocytes.

High-throughput chemical screening has identified multiple S3QEL compounds with varying chemical structures but shared mechanism of action. These include compounds with sulfanyloxoquinazoline and pyrazolopyrimidine scaffolds, among others. The structural diversity of S3QELs allows for optimization of pharmacokinetic properties while maintaining target specificity.

Several S3QEL compounds are available from commercial suppliers or can be synthesized using published methods. Screening protocols to identify new S3QEL candidates typically involve measuring oxygen consumption and hydrogen peroxide production in isolated mitochondria under conditions that promote site IIIQo electron leak. Secondary assays assess compound effects on normal electron transport and oxidative phosphorylation to confirm specificity.

Desirable properties of S3QEL compounds include high specificity for site IIIQo, minimal effects on normal mitochondrial function, good bioavailability, and stability in the gastrointestinal environment. Structure-activity relationship studies have identified key molecular features that confer these properties, enabling rational design of improved S3QEL derivatives.

#### Permeability Assays

Various permeability assays can be used to assess the effectiveness of S3QEL treatment in clinical and research settings. These typically involve administration of non-digestible markers that are normally excluded by an intact intestinal barrier but appear in circulation or urine when permeability is increased. The choice of marker depends on the specific clinical or research context.

Fluorescent-labeled dextrans, particularly FITC-dextran, are commonly used in animal studies to quantify intestinal permeability. After oral administration, serum concentrations of FITC-dextran are measured by fluorescence spectroscopy, with higher levels indicating greater permeability. This assay provides a sensitive and quantitative measure of barrier function that correlates well with histological and molecular markers.

Non-metabolizable oligosaccharides such as lactulose and mannitol are frequently used in human studies. The lactulose/mannitol test involves oral administration of these sugars followed by urine collection and chromatographic analysis. The lactulose/mannitol ratio reflects paracellular permeability, as lactulose crosses primarily through tight junctions while mannitol passes via transcellular routes.

The triple sugar test extends this principle by adding sucralose to assess whole-gut permeability over longer time periods. Multi-sugar tests incorporating various molecular weight markers provide even more detailed information about pore and leak pathways in the intestinal barrier. These assays have demonstrated increased permeability in numerous diseases, including IBD, celiac disease, and IBS.

Polyethylene glycols (PEGs) of varying molecular weights serve as useful permeability markers due to their chemical stability and lack of metabolism. PEG absorption patterns can distinguish between different types of barrier defects based on molecular size exclusion characteristics. This approach has been particularly valuable in research settings.

Fecal albumin measurement provides a non-invasive indicator of intestinal permeability in clinical practice. As albumin is normally retained in circulation, its appearance in feces suggests increased epithelial leakage. This assay is easily performed using standard laboratory techniques and correlates with disease activity in IBD.

Various intestinal biomarkers complement direct permeability measurements in assessing barrier function. Zonulin, a modulator of tight junctions, has emerged as a particularly useful marker of intestinal permeability regulation. Elevated fecal or serum zonulin levels indicate increased intestinal permeability in multiple conditions.

Calprotectin, a neutrophil-derived protein, serves as a marker of intestinal inflammation that often accompanies barrier dysfunction. While not a direct measure of permeability, elevated fecal calprotectin correlates with disease activity in IBD and may predict response to therapy.

Alpha-1-Antitrypsin (A1AT) clearance provides another indirect measure of intestinal permeability, particularly for protein loss. Increased fecal A1AT occurs in conditions with substantial barrier defects and can help monitor disease progression and treatment response.

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and circulating Endotoxin Core Antibodies (EndoCAb) reflect bacterial translocation secondary to increased intestinal permeability. Elevated plasma LPS or altered EndoCAb profiles indicate barrier dysfunction and systemic immune activation in various diseases.

Plasma D-lactate levels have been proposed as a marker of intestinal permeability, particularly in critical illness. As D-lactate is produced by intestinal bacteria but not human cells, its appearance in circulation suggests bacterial translocation across a compromised barrier.

### EXAMPLES

#### Example 1

The intestinal epithelial barrier serves as a critical interface between the external environment and internal physiology. Its proper function is essential for nutrient absorption while preventing harmful substance penetration. Oxidative stress has been identified as a major driver of barrier dysfunction, though the specific sources and mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

Drosophila melanogaster and murine models provide valuable systems for studying metabolic disease and aging-related intestinal permeability. In Drosophila, dietary manipulation significantly impacts lifespan and intestinal barrier function. Increasing dietary yeast extract (YE) content from 0.5% to 5% decreased median lifespan while increasing intestinal permeability as measured by the smurf assay.

The balance between enterocyte proliferation and apoptosis is critical for maintaining intestinal homeostasis. High-YE diets increased both apoptotic cell numbers and proliferating (PH3-positive) cells in Drosophila intestines. These changes correlated negatively with lifespan and positively with intestinal permeability, suggesting a causal relationship between epithelial turnover and longevity.

Gene expression analysis confirmed diet-induced barrier dysfunction, showing increased expression of antimicrobial peptides (Dpt, Drs, Def) and the cytokine upd3. Tight junction gene expression also increased, likely representing a compensatory response to epithelial damage. Elevated expression of antioxidant genes (Sod1, Sod2, Cat) indicated oxidative stress as a probable mediator of diet-induced permeability.

Mitochondrial complex III emerged as a key source of oxidative stress in enterocytes. Site IIIQo of complex III has particularly high superoxide production capacity, delivering reactive oxygen species to both mitochondrial matrix and cytosol. S3QEL compounds specifically suppress this electron leak without affecting normal respiration.

Feeding S3QELs (S3QEL1.2, S3QEL2.2, S3QEL3) to Drosophila on high-YE diets significantly reduced intestinal permeability and apoptotic cell numbers while extending median lifespan. These effects were dose-dependent and consistent across structurally distinct S3QELs, supporting an on-target mechanism. The compounds did not affect food consumption, ruling out caloric restriction as the protective mechanism.

Genetic evidence further supported the role of complex III-derived superoxide in barrier dysfunction. Enterocyte-specific knockdown of Sod1 or Sod2 increased intestinal permeability and apoptosis while decreasing lifespan - effects reversed by S3QEL treatment. This demonstrated that superoxide generated in both cytosolic and mitochondrial compartments contributes to epithelial damage.

In murine models, high-fat feeding induced intestinal permeability measured by FITC-dextran absorption and fecal albumin levels. This was accompanied by decreased tight junction and mucin gene expression, glucose intolerance, and increased body weight and adiposity. S3QEL treatment prevented the permeability increases and preserved epithelial gene expression without affecting metabolic parameters.

The experimental design involved standardized fly husbandry with controlled dietary conditions. Lifespan assays tracked survival under various treatments, while smurf assays quantified intestinal permeability. Cell turnover was assessed using Acridine Orange/Ethidium Bromide staining for apoptosis and Phosphohistone H3 staining for proliferation.

Mouse experiments employed established protocols for high-fat diet induction and metabolic phenotyping. Intestinal permeability was measured using FITC-dextran assays and fecal albumin quantification. Glucose tolerance tests and body composition analysis provided complementary metabolic data. Molecular analyses included qPCR of intestinal gene expression patterns.

Statistical analysis confirmed significant effects of diet and S3QEL treatment on all measured parameters. While the assays have certain limitations, particularly in extrapolating from model organisms to humans, the consistent findings across species strengthen the conclusions. The results support therapeutic applications of S3QELs for intestinal barrier disorders.
