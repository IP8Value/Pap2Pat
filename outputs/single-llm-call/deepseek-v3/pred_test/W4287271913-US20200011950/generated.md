## BACKGROUND  

Tumor heterogeneity presents significant challenges in cancer diagnosis and treatment. Malignant tumors exhibit complex growth patterns that extend beyond visible surgical margins, creating variations in cellular composition, genetic mutations, and phenotypic characteristics across different tumor regions. This heterogeneity contributes to differential responses to therapy and variable clinical outcomes. In glioblastoma (GBM), the most aggressive primary brain tumor in adults, this heterogeneity manifests not only within the tumor core but also in the surrounding brain parenchyma through the tumor field effect. The field effect describes pathological changes in histologically normal tissue adjacent to tumors, resulting from tumor-induced mechanical stresses, molecular alterations, and microenvironmental modifications. Current diagnostic approaches often fail to adequately capture this spatial heterogeneity, leading to suboptimal treatment planning and prognostic inaccuracy. There exists an unmet need for quantitative methods that can comprehensively characterize tumor heterogeneity and its field effects to enable more precise prognosis and personalized treatment strategies.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

Cancer represents a systemic disease characterized by uncontrolled cellular proliferation and invasive growth patterns. The tumor field effect describes the phenomenon whereby malignant tumors influence surrounding histologically normal tissue through mechanical forces, molecular signaling, and microenvironmental alterations. In glioblastoma (GBM), this field effect manifests as structural deformations in the brain around tumor (BAT) regions, which correlate with disease aggressiveness and patient outcomes.  

Existing approaches for predicting overall survival in GBM suffer from several limitations. Current methods primarily rely on clinical parameters such as age, performance status, and molecular markers including IDH mutation status and MGMT promoter methylation. While these factors provide some prognostic value, they fail to capture the spatial heterogeneity of tumors and their mechanical impact on surrounding tissues. Radiographic assessment typically focuses on the enhancing tumor volume and peritumoral edema, overlooking subtle but prognostically significant changes in the BAT regions. Histopathological evaluation, while informative, provides only localized sampling and cannot comprehensively assess the tumor's spatial heterogeneity or its field effects.  

Radiomic features derived from medical imaging offer a promising approach for capturing intra-tumoral heterogeneity non-invasively. Radiomics involves the high-throughput extraction of quantitative features from medical images that reflect underlying pathological processes. These features can characterize tumor morphology, texture, and functional properties across three-dimensional space. However, current radiomic approaches have primarily focused on features extracted from within the tumor and immediate peritumoral regions, neglecting the biomechanical deformations in surrounding parenchyma that may provide complementary prognostic information.  

The extraction of radiomic features from peritumoral regions enables quantification of the tumor field effect. These features can capture subtle structural deformations in the BAT resulting from tumor-induced mechanical stresses. As aggressive tumors grow, they exert pressure on surrounding tissues, causing displacement, herniation, and microstructural alterations. These deformations manifest as changes in tissue density, orientation, and architecture that can be quantified through advanced image analysis techniques. The magnitude and spatial distribution of these deformations correlate with tumor aggressiveness and may serve as independent prognostic markers.  

The tumor field effect in GBM produces measurable radiographic changes in surrounding brain parenchyma. These changes include midline shift, ventricular compression, and sulcal effacement resulting from increased intracranial pressure. More subtle alterations include microstructural deformations visible only through quantitative image analysis. The spatial extent of these changes varies with tumor biology, with more aggressive tumors inducing more widespread parenchymal alterations. These field effects persist beyond standard margins used in radiation therapy planning, suggesting that current treatment volumes may inadequately address the full extent of tumor infiltration.
