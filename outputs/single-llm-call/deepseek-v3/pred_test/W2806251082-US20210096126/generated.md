## DESCRIPTION  

### BACKGROUND  

Radio frequency identification (RFID) technology has become integral to modern applications requiring personal identification, traceability, and environmental monitoring. The technology operates wirelessly, wherein RFID tags collect energy from nearby reader interrogations, enabling data transmission without direct physical contact. RFID tags exhibit remarkable durability, withstanding extreme temperatures, high or low pressure, and exposure to various solvents, making them superior to traditional barcode systems. These attributes have led to widespread adoption in transportation, retail, and security sectors.  

Despite these advantages, RFID applications in biological and medical contexts remain limited. Conventional tissue culture systems lack efficient methods for tracking individual cell populations or organoids, particularly in high-throughput screening environments. Current approaches rely on manual handling, which is labor-intensive and prone to errors, especially when dealing with large-scale organoid libraries derived from multiple donors. The inability to uniquely identify and trace organoids in pooled cultures hinders progress in precision medicine, drug discovery, and disease modeling.  

### BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention provides a novel system and method for integrating RFID technology with three-dimensional organoid cultures. By embedding ultra-compact RFID chips (referred to as "O-Chips") within organoids during their self-assembly process, the invention enables digital tracking and identification of individual organoids without disrupting their native structure or function. The resulting RFID-integrated organoids (RiOs) retain full biological functionality, including secretory, metabolic, and structural properties comparable to conventional organoids.  

The invention further encompasses methods for generating RiOs from various precursor cell types, including induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) and definitive endoderm cells. The disclosed compositions and methods facilitate high-throughput phenotypic screening, cryopreservation, and in vivo tracking of organoids, offering significant advantages for precision medicine, drug development, and disease modeling.  

### DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

#### Definitions  

As used herein, the following terms shall have the meanings ascribed below:  

- **"About" or "approximately"** refers to a numerical value that may vary by ±10% without altering the function or outcome of the invention.  
- **"Individual," "host," "subject," and "patient"** refer to a human or animal from whom biological samples are derived for organoid generation.  
- **"Precursor cell"** denotes a stem or progenitor cell capable of differentiating into organ-specific tissues, including but not limited to iPSCs, embryonic stem cells, and adult stem cells.  
- **"RFID technology"** encompasses wireless communication systems comprising a microchip and antenna for data transmission and reception.  
- **"Organoid"** refers to a three-dimensional, self-organized cellular structure that mimics the architecture and function of a native organ.  
- **"Digitized organoid"** denotes an organoid containing an embedded RFID chip for identification and tracking.  
- **"Pooled organoid composition"** refers to a mixture of organoids derived from multiple donors, wherein each organoid is uniquely identifiable via RFID.  

#### RFID Technology and Applications  

RFID systems consist of a tag (microchip and antenna) and a reader that wirelessly interrogates the tag. Passive RFID tags, as used in the present invention, derive power from the reader's radiofrequency signals, eliminating the need for an internal power source. Applications of RFID in healthcare include patient identification, medication adherence monitoring, and surgical instrument tracking. The invention extends these applications to organoid cultures, enabling real-time monitoring and data collection in vitro and in vivo.  

#### Human Organoids and Their Features  

Organoids are derived from stem cells that self-organize into three-dimensional structures resembling native organs. These structures exhibit polarized epithelia, functional secretory cells, and lumen formation, making them ideal for disease modeling and drug screening. The invention leverages the self-assembly properties of organoids to incorporate RFID chips without disrupting their biological integrity.  

#### Digitized Organoids (RiOs)  

The invention provides RiOs comprising an O-Chip embedded within the organoid lumen during aggregation. The O-Chip, measuring approximately 460 × 480 μm, integrates seamlessly into the organoid structure without impairing cellular function. RiOs exhibit preserved morphology, gene expression, and metabolic activity comparable to non-chipped organoids.  

#### Method of Making RFID-Integrated Organoids  

The method involves:  
1. Differentiating precursor cells (e.g., iPSCs) into definitive endoderm.  
2. Dissociating and re-aggregating cells in the presence of an O-Chip.  
3. Culturing the aggregates in a laminin-rich matrix to promote polarization and lumen formation.  
4. Validating organoid functionality through gene expression, protein secretion, and metabolic assays.  

#### Organoid Types  

The invention encompasses RiOs derived from various organ systems, including:  
- **Esophageal organoids**  
- **Stomach organoids**  
- **Small intestinal and colon organoids**  
- **Hepatic and bile duct organoids**  
- **Pancreatic organoids**  
- **Lung and kidney organoids**  

Each organoid type is generated using lineage-specific differentiation protocols involving growth factors (e.g., FGF, Wnt, BMP activators) and small molecule inhibitors (e.g., GSK3 inhibitors).  

#### Phenotypic Screening and Forward Cellomics  

The invention enables high-throughput phenotypic screening by pooling RiOs from multiple donors and identifying specific phenotypes via RFID detection. For example, lipid accumulation in steatohepatitis models can be quantified using fluorescent dyes, with individual RiOs traced back to their donor origin. This "forward cellomics" approach allows scalable analysis of genetic-phenotypic correlations.  

#### Examples  

1. **O-Chip Integration into Liver Organoids**  
   - iPSCs were differentiated into hepatic lineages and co-cultured with O-Chips.  
   - RiOs exhibited normal albumin secretion, bile transport, and lipid metabolism.  

2. **Cryopreservation and Durability Testing**  
   - RiOs retained functionality after freezing at −196°C and exposure to solvents.  
   - O-Chips remained detectable post-thawing and after paraffin embedding.  

3. **Multi-Donor Phenotypic Screening**  
   - RiOs from Wolman disease patients showed elevated lipid accumulation, identifiable via RFID.  

4. **Simultaneous Fluorescence and RFID Detection**  
   - A custom device enabled real-time phenotyping and RFID tracking in flow-based assays.
