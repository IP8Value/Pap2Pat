## TECHNICAL FIELD  

The present invention relates to the field of microbial analysis and metagenomics, specifically to methods for immobilizing and preparing living microorganisms from environmental samples such as soil and sediment using electrochemical techniques. More particularly, the invention provides novel methods for selectively attaching microorganisms to electrode surfaces through application of constant electrical potentials and subsequently detaching them using high-frequency wave potentials, enabling efficient separation and analysis of viable microorganisms from complex environmental matrices.  

## BACKGROUND ART  

Metagenomic analysis of environmental samples has become increasingly important for studying microbial communities and discovering novel biomolecules. However, current methods face significant limitations in effectively separating living microorganisms from soil and sediment samples while maintaining viability. Traditional DNA extraction methods often co-extract inhibitory substances like humic acids, while indirect extraction methods typically recover only 25-35% of total microorganisms present.  

Existing microbial isolation techniques including dilution-to-extinction, micromanipulation, flow cytometry, microfluidics, and compartmentalization have proven inadequate for processing soil and sediment samples due to the strong adhesion of microorganisms to soil aggregates. These methods either fail to maintain cell viability or introduce significant bias in microbial community representation. There remains an unmet need for techniques that can efficiently separate viable microorganisms from environmental samples while preserving the native microbial community structure.  

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION  

### Object to be Achieved by the Invention  

The primary object of the present invention is to provide methods for efficiently immobilizing and preparing living microorganisms from environmental samples. A further object is to enable selective attachment and detachment of microorganisms while maintaining high viability rates. Another object is to facilitate metagenomic analysis by providing representative samples of microbial communities from complex environmental matrices.  

### Means for Achieving the Objects  

The invention achieves these objects through several key discoveries: First, the inventors discovered that applying a constant negative potential to an electrode surface in non-nutritive media induces selective attachment of living microorganisms while leaving dead cells and soil particles unattached. Second, they developed a method for detaching the attached microorganisms using high-frequency triangular wave potentials while maintaining cell viability. Third, they established optimal parameters for both attachment and detachment processes across various microorganism types and environmental conditions.  

The method comprises several steps: (1) preparing a suspension containing microorganisms from soil or sediment samples; (2) disposing the suspension on an electrode surface; (3) applying a constant negative potential to induce microorganism attachment; (4) washing to remove unattached material; (5) applying a high-frequency wave potential to detach viable microorganisms; and (6) collecting the detached microorganisms for analysis.  

Key parameters include constant potential ranges of -0.2V to -0.5V vs. Ag/AgCl for attachment, triangular wave potentials of ±4mV to ±10mV at 9MHz for detachment, and specific electrolyte compositions optimized for different sample types. The methods maintain microorganism viability rates above 90% during attachment and up to 88% during detachment.  

### Effect of the Invention  

The invention provides significant advantages over prior art methods. It enables efficient separation of living microorganisms from complex environmental samples with minimal bias in community representation. The electrical methods are gentle, maintaining high cell viability rates while avoiding chemical or mechanical stress. The techniques are broadly applicable across diverse microorganism types and environmental conditions.  

Potential applications include metagenomic studies, cultivation of previously unculturable microbes, microbial battery development, and environmental monitoring. The methods facilitate discovery of novel biomolecules by providing access to a more complete representation of environmental microbial communities.  

## MODES FOR CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION  

### <Method for Immobilizing Living Microorganisms>  

The method for immobilizing living microorganisms comprises disposing a suspension containing microorganisms on an electrode surface and applying a constant potential to attach the microorganisms. The constant potential is typically between -0.2V and -0.5V vs. Ag/AgCl, with optimal attachment occurring at -0.4V in most buffers and -0.3V in seawater conditions.  

The electrolyte composition is critical for successful attachment. Suitable electrolytes include phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), MOPS buffer, tricine buffer, and artificial seawater, all at neutral pH (6.8-7.2). The salt concentration should be between 100mM and 300mM to maintain proper ionic strength without compromising cell viability.  

The electrode material is preferably indium tin oxide (ITO) due to its biocompatibility and conductivity. The electrode surface may be patterned with arrayed regions to facilitate spatial organization of attached microorganisms. Optimal electrode layer dimensions range from 5μm diameter spots for single-cell studies to large plane electrodes up to 125×85mm for bulk sample processing.  

The application period for constant potential typically ranges from 2 to 24 hours, depending on sample type and desired attachment density. Microorganism density in the electrolyte should be adjusted to 1×10^6 cells/mL for cultured microorganisms or 50μg/mL for environmental samples.  

The method maintains microorganism viability rates above 90% during attachment. It is effective for diverse microorganism types including Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, actinomycetes, and deep-sea microorganisms. For seawater samples, artificial seawater electrolyte with -0.3V applied potential provides optimal attachment conditions while maintaining microorganism survival rates comparable to natural conditions.  

### <Method for Preparing Living Microorganisms>  

The method for preparing living microorganisms builds upon the immobilization method by adding steps for detaching and collecting viable microorganisms. After attachment, a high-frequency triangular wave potential is applied to detach microorganisms while maintaining viability.  

The detachment potential is typically a ±10mV vs. Ag/AgCl 9MHz triangular wave applied for 30-60 minutes. The frequency range may be adjusted between 3-9MHz depending on microorganism type, with optimal detachment occurring at higher frequencies. The potential amplitude may range from ±4mV to ±10mV, with higher potentials providing faster detachment but potentially reducing viability.  

The electrolyte composition for detachment matches that used for attachment (PBS, artificial seawater, etc.), but with fresh solution to remove detached cells. Salt concentrations should be maintained as during attachment to prevent osmotic shock.  

Microorganism survival rates during detachment typically exceed 80% for most bacterial types when using optimal parameters. The method enables collection of microorganisms at distances up to several millimeters from the electrode surface, facilitated by fluid flow during the detachment process.  

The electrode shape and disposition may be optimized for specific applications. Patterned arrays facilitate single-cell studies, while large plane electrodes enable bulk sample processing. The method supports iterative attachment and detachment cycles at the same positions on the electrode surface, enabling repeated analysis of specific microbial populations.  

Microorganism retrieval is accomplished by collecting the electrolyte solution after detachment, optionally followed by centrifugation or filtration to concentrate cells. The method maintains culturability of detached microorganisms, with viable colony counts typically exceeding 80% of input for many bacterial species.  

## EXAMPLES  

The following examples illustrate specific embodiments of the invention but are not intended to limit its scope.  

### Example 1  

A garden soil sample was prepared by suspending 0.1g soil in 5mL PBS(-) with vortexing. Microorganisms were attached to an ITO electrode by applying -0.4V vs. Ag/AgCl for 24 hours at room temperature. After washing, microorganisms were detached by applying ±10mV 9MHz triangular wave potential for 60 minutes. Detached microorganisms were stained with live/dead viability dyes and counted, showing 99.8% detachment rate and 91% viability. Approximately 3.0×10^9 cells/g were collected from the original soil sample.  

### Example 2  

Bacillus subtilis and Escherichia coli cultures were grown overnight, centrifuged, and resuspended in PBS(-). Cells were attached to electrodes at -0.4V for 24 hours, then detached with ±10mV 9MHz potential. Viability was 83% for E. coli and 47% for B. subtilis. Colony counts showed 88% viable recovery for E. coli and 48% for B. subtilis.  

### Example 3  

A garden soil sample was prepared as in Example 1 but with varied attachment potentials from -0.1V to -0.6V. Maximum attachment occurred at -0.4V, with attachment efficiency decreasing symmetrically around this potential. No significant attachment occurred at positive potentials.  

### Example 4  

A soil sample was attached at -0.4V as in Example 1, then detached with varied potentials (±4mV to ±10mV). Detachment rates increased with potential amplitude, but viability decreased below ±6mV. Optimal balance was ±10mV with 99.8% detachment and 91% viability.  

### Example 5  

Soil samples were prepared with different electrolytes: PBS(-), MOPS buffer, tricine buffer, and LB medium. Attachment was successful in all non-nutritive buffers but minimal in LB medium, despite 92% cell viability, suggesting nutrient availability competes with electrical attachment.  

#### (1) Various Buffers  

Comparison of PBS(-), MOPS, and tricine buffers showed similar attachment efficiencies (±10% variation) and viability (>90% in all cases), indicating broad applicability across neutral pH buffers.  

#### (2) Artificial Seawater  

Deep-sea sediment samples in artificial seawater showed optimal attachment at -0.3V rather than -0.4V, with 87% viability. This shift is attributed to ionic composition differences affecting the electrode double layer.  

### Example 6  

Deep-sea sediment from Sagami Bay (1,176m depth) was processed using the electrical retrieval method. Microorganisms were attached at -0.3V in artificial seawater for 2 hours at 4°C, detached with ±10mV 9MHz potential, then reprocessed for purity. From 5g sediment, 1.3×10^8 cells were collected. DNA was extracted, 16S rRNA genes amplified by PCR, and phylogenetic analysis performed, revealing 19 phyla and 23 classes of microorganisms with 95% phylum-level overlap with direct DNA extraction methods.  

### Example 7  

Phylogenetic analysis of Example 6 samples showed the electrically retrieved microbial community closely matched the native community structure. Comparative examples using conventional attachment methods showed either poor representation (25-35% recovery) or low viability (<50%), demonstrating the superiority of the electrical methods.
