**PATENT APPLICATION**  
**METHOD FOR EXTRACTING METALS FROM METAL-CONTAMINATED LIGNOCELLULOSIC BIOMASS USING IONIC LIQUIDS**  

### **DESCRIPTION**  

**Introduce metal pollutants in wood**  
Metal pollutants such as copper, arsenic, and chromium are commonly introduced into wood through preservative treatments. These metals enhance the durability of wood products by preventing decay and insect infestation. However, the presence of these metals poses significant environmental and health risks when wood is disposed of improperly.  

**Describe wood treatment with metal-based preservatives**  
Wood is frequently treated with metal-based preservatives to extend its lifespan. These preservatives include chromated copper arsenate (CCA), alkaline copper quaternary (ACQ), and copper azole. While effective, these treatments lead to contamination of wood waste, complicating disposal and recycling efforts.  

**Mention toxicity of copper, arsenic, and chromium**  
Copper, arsenic, and chromium are highly toxic, particularly in their leachable forms. Arsenic is a known carcinogen, while chromium (VI) is mutagenic and corrosive. Copper, though less toxic, can accumulate in ecosystems, disrupting aquatic life. Safe disposal of treated wood is critical to prevent environmental contamination.  

**Describe limitations of wood disposal**  
Current disposal methods for treated wood, such as landfilling or incineration, are problematic. Landfilling risks groundwater contamination through metal leaching, while incineration releases toxic particulates. Regulatory restrictions further limit disposal options, necessitating alternative methods for metal recovery.  

**Introduce controlled landfill method**  
A controlled landfill method involves isolating treated wood waste in lined landfills to prevent leaching. While this reduces environmental impact, it does not recover valuable metals and requires long-term monitoring.  

**Describe thermal treatment and disposal of metal-enriched ash**  
Thermal treatment, such as pyrolysis or gasification, decomposes organic matter while concentrating metals in the residual ash. However, this ash must still be disposed of in hazardous waste facilities, and metal recovery remains inefficient.  

**Introduce leaching and disposal of chemically precipitated metal ions**  
Chemical leaching processes, such as acid or alkaline extraction, dissolve metals from wood waste. The dissolved metals can then be precipitated as hydroxides or sulfides for disposal. However, these methods generate secondary waste streams and require extensive processing.  

**Describe acid leaching and metal recovery**  
Acid leaching, typically using sulfuric or hydrochloric acid, effectively extracts metals from treated wood. The leachate is then subjected to precipitation or electrodeposition to recover metals. This method is more efficient than thermal treatment but still involves corrosive chemicals and waste generation.  

**Mention electrodeposition of Cu(II) from aqueous solution**  
Electrodeposition is a promising method for recovering copper from leachates. By applying an electric current, Cu(II) ions are reduced and deposited as metallic copper on cathodes. This process is selective and produces high-purity metal.  

**Discuss techno-economic model for dilute acid-leaching**  
A techno-economic model for dilute acid-leaching evaluates cost efficiency based on acid consumption, metal recovery rates, and waste treatment expenses. Optimizing acid concentration and leaching time can reduce operational costs while maximizing metal extraction.  

**Estimate operating cost of metal extraction**  
The operating cost of metal extraction via acid leaching and electrodeposition is estimated at $X per ton of treated wood. Key cost drivers include chemical consumption, energy use, and labor. Recovery of high-value metals like copper can offset expenses.  

**Compare cost of virgin wood feedstock for biofuel production**  
Virgin wood feedstock for biofuel production is significantly cheaper than metal-contaminated wood due to lower processing requirements. However, utilizing contaminated wood as a biorefinery feedstock could provide dual benefits of metal recovery and biofuel generation.  

**Introduce use of contaminated waste wood as feedstock for biorefinery**  
Contaminated waste wood can serve as a feedstock for biorefineries by integrating metal extraction with biomass conversion. This approach maximizes resource utilization while mitigating disposal challenges.  

**Describe electrochemical recovery of metals**  
Electrochemical recovery involves applying an electric potential to leachates to selectively deposit metals on electrodes. This method is efficient for copper recovery and can be integrated with biorefinery processes.  

**Introduce lignocellulosic biomass composition**  
Lignocellulosic biomass consists of cellulose (40-50%), hemicellulose (20-30%), and lignin (15-25%). These components provide structural integrity but also contribute to recalcitrance during processing.  

**Describe cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin components**  
Cellulose is a linear glucose polymer, hemicellulose is a branched heteropolymer of sugars, and lignin is a complex aromatic polymer. These components must be separated for efficient biorefining.  

**Explain cellulosic biofuel production process**  
Cellulosic biofuel production involves pretreatment to break down lignin and hemicellulose, enzymatic hydrolysis to release sugars, and fermentation to produce ethanol. Metal contaminants interfere with enzymatic and microbial activity, necessitating pretreatment.  

**Mention limitations of using preservative-treated biomass**  
Preservative-treated biomass contains metals that inhibit enzymes and microorganisms in biofuel production. Additionally, metal leaching can corrode equipment and contaminate products.  

**Introduce ionic liquid fractionation of cellulose and lignin**  
Ionic liquids (ILs) are effective solvents for fractionating lignocellulosic biomass. They selectively dissolve lignin and hemicellulose while leaving cellulose as a solid residue. This facilitates metal extraction and biofuel production.  

**Describe process of extracting metals from treated cellulosic biomass**  
The process involves:  
1. Contacting biomass with an ionic liquid to dissolve lignin and hemicellulose.  
2. Separating the cellulose-rich solid.  
3. Recovering metals from the ionic liquid phase via electrodeposition or precipitation.  

**Define "extraction" and "cellulosic" or "lignocellulosic biomass"**  
"Extraction" refers to the removal of metals or organic components from biomass. "Cellulosic biomass" refers to plant-derived material rich in cellulose, while "lignocellulosic biomass" includes lignin and hemicellulose.  

**Describe metal pollutant removal efficiency**  
The efficiency of metal removal depends on ionic liquid composition, temperature, and contact time. Optimal conditions achieve >90% extraction of copper and arsenic.  

**Introduce treated cellulosic biomass and metal-based preservatives**  
Treated cellulosic biomass refers to wood impregnated with metal preservatives. The disclosed method enables simultaneous metal recovery and biomass valorization.  

**Define "metal pollutant" and its effects on downstream processes**  
A "metal pollutant" is a toxic metal introduced into biomass. These metals inhibit enzymatic hydrolysis, fermentation, and catalyst performance in biorefineries.  

**Describe preferred metal pollutants and their sources**  
Preferred metal pollutants include copper, arsenic, and chromium, primarily from wood preservatives such as CCA and ACQ.  

**Introduce ionic liquid composition and properties**  
Ionic liquids consist of organic cations (e.g., imidazolium, ammonium) and anions (e.g., chloride, hydrogen sulfate). They exhibit low volatility, high thermal stability, and tunable solvation properties.  

**Describe preferred anions and cations for ionic liquid**  
Preferred cations include alkylammonium and imidazolium derivatives. Preferred anions include hydrogen sulfate ([HSO4]⁻) and acetate ([OAc]⁻) for lignin dissolution.  

**Introduce protic cation ion and ammonium derivative**  
Protic ionic liquids, such as triethylammonium hydrogen sulfate ([TEA][HSO4]), are cost-effective and efficient for biomass fractionation.  

**Describe aliphatic, alkyl, alkenyl, and carbocycle groups**  
Cations may include aliphatic (e.g., ethyl), alkyl (e.g., methyl), alkenyl (e.g., vinyl), or carbocyclic (e.g., phenyl) groups to tailor solvent properties.  

**Introduce lignin solubility in ionic liquid**  
Lignin is highly soluble in hydrogen sulfate-based ionic liquids due to disruption of hydrogen bonds and π-π interactions.  

**Describe solid residue or pulp production**  
After lignin dissolution, the remaining cellulose-rich pulp is separated by filtration or centrifugation. This pulp is suitable for enzymatic saccharification.  

**Mention hemicellulose solubility in ionic liquid**  
Hemicellulose dissolves in ionic liquids, particularly those with hydrogen sulfate anions, and can be recovered as sugars or furfural.  

**Describe metal pollutant retention in ionic liquid**  
Metals remain in the ionic liquid phase after biomass dissolution and are subsequently recovered via electrodeposition.  

**Introduce heating and stirring conditions for reaction**  
Optimal conditions include heating to 120-170°C with stirring for 2-24 hours, depending on biomass type and metal content.  

**Describe reaction time and temperature ranges**  
Reaction times range from 2 to 24 hours, with temperatures between 120°C and 170°C for efficient fractionation.

A heteroaryl group is an aromatic ring containing heteroatoms such as nitrogen or oxygen (e.g., pyridine, furan).  

**Define aryl or carbocycle group**  
An aryl group is a hydrocarbon ring (e.g., phenyl), while a carbocycle is any ring structure composed solely of carbon atoms.  

**Define halogen atom**  
A halogen atom includes fluorine, chlorine, bromine, or iodine, which may be present in ionic liquid anions.  

**Describe alkylammonium cations**  
Alkylammonium cations, such as triethylammonium ([TEA]⁺), are derived from amines and exhibit high solubility for lignin.  

**Describe trialkylammoniums**  
Trialkylammonium cations (e.g., triethylammonium) are preferred for their low cost and high pretreatment efficiency.  

**Describe dialkylammoniums**  
Dialkylammonium cations (e.g., diethylammonium) are less common but still effective for biomass fractionation.  

**Describe monoalkylammoniums**  
Monoalkylammonium cations (e.g., ethylammonium) are less stable but can be used in specific applications.  

**Describe preferred alkylammonium cations**  
Triethylammonium ([TEA]⁺) is preferred due to its low cost ($1.24/kg) and high delignification efficiency.  

**Describe nitrogen-containing heterocyclic moiety**  
Nitrogen-containing heterocycles, such as imidazolium, enhance lignin solubility due to their hydrogen-bonding capacity.  

**Describe cation ring**  
Cation rings, such as imidazolium or pyridinium, improve thermal stability and solvation properties.  

**Describe C2-6 alkoxy**  
C2-6 alkoxy groups (e.g., ethoxy, propoxy) can modify ionic liquid properties for specific applications.  

**Describe C2-6 alkoxyalkyl group**  
Alkoxyalkyl groups (e.g., methoxyethyl) enhance hydrophilicity and biomass solubility.  

**Describe preferred cations**  
Preferred cations include triethylammonium, 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium, and 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium for balanced cost and performance.  

**Describe ionic liquid composition**  
The ionic liquid comprises 70-90% [TEA][HSO4] and 10-30% water for optimal fractionation.  

**Describe preparation of ionic liquids**  
Ionic liquids are prepared by mixing a stoichiometric acid (e.g., sulfuric acid) with a base (e.g., triethylamine) followed by drying.  

**Describe excess base in ionic liquid**  
Excess base increases pH, reducing lignin condensation but slowing delignification.  

**Describe water in ionic liquid composition**  
Water (10-30%) enhances fractionation by promoting hydrolysis of lignin-carbohydrate bonds.  

**Describe excess acid in ionic liquid composition**  
Excess acid (e.g., 9 mol% H2SO4) accelerates pretreatment but risks excessive lignin condensation.  

**Describe contacting biomass with ionic liquid**  
Biomass is mixed with ionic liquid at a 1:10 (w/w) ratio and heated with stirring for 2-24 hours.  

**Describe lubrication properties of ionic liquid**  
Ionic liquids reduce friction during biomass processing, minimizing mechanical wear.  

**Describe metal extraction and lignin solubilization**  
Metals are extracted simultaneously with lignin dissolution, enabling integrated recovery.  

**Describe electrodeposition of metal pollutant**  
Electrodeposition recovers metals from the ionic liquid phase at >90% efficiency.  

**Describe recovery of metal pollutant**  
Metals are recovered as pure deposits or salts, suitable for recycling.  

**Describe recycling of ionic liquid**  
The ionic liquid is recycled by evaporating water and reusing it for subsequent pretreatments with >99% recovery.  

**Describe separation of ionic liquid from cellulose**  
Cellulose is separated by filtration, washed with ethanol, and dried for enzymatic hydrolysis.  

**Describe washing of cellulose containing solid residue**  
The cellulose pulp is washed with ethanol to remove residual ionic liquid and lignin.  

**Describe precipitation of lignin**  
Lignin is precipitated by diluting the ionic liquid solution with water and filtering.  

**Describe removal of anti-solvent**  
Water or ethanol is removed by evaporation to regenerate the ionic liquid.  

**Describe drying of ionic liquid**  
The ionic liquid is dried under vacuum at 40-70°C to remove residual water.  

**Describe saccharification of cellulose containing solid residue**  
The cellulose pulp is enzymatically hydrolyzed to glucose using cellulases.  

**Describe enzymatic hydrolysis**  
Enzymatic hydrolysis yields >70% glucose from the cellulose pulp.  

**Describe glucose obtained by hydrolysis**  
The glucose is fermented to ethanol or other biofuels.  

**Summarize process of the invention**  
The invention provides a method for extracting metals from contaminated biomass using ionic liquids, enabling simultaneous metal recovery and biofuel production.  

### **EXAMPLE 1. DECONSTRUCTION OF BIOMASS AND EXTRACTION OF METALS IN VARIOUS IONIC LIQUIDS**  

**Introduce deconstruction and extraction process**  
Miscanthus biomass (10 g) was mixed with 100 g of [TEA][HSO4] (80% IL, 20% water) and heated at 120°C for 8 hours.  

**Prepare ionic liquid/water mixture**  
The ionic liquid was prepared by mixing triethylamine with sulfuric acid and adjusting water content to 20%.  

**Add biomass to ionic liquid mixture**  
The biomass was added at a 1:10 (w/w) ratio and stirred continuously.  

**Incubate mixture at 170° C.**  
The mixture was heated to 170°C for 4 hours to enhance delignification.  

**Transfer mixture to centrifuge tube**  
After incubation, the mixture was centrifuged to separate the cellulose pulp.  

**Wash and centrifuge mixture**  
The pulp was washed with ethanol and centrifuged to remove residual lignin.  

**Soxhlet extract pulp with ethanol**  
The pulp was Soxhlet-extracted with ethanol to ensure complete lignin removal.  

**Evaporate ethanol and recover ionic liquid/lignin mixture**  
Ethanol was evaporated, and the ionic liquid was recovered for reuse.  

**Precipitate lignin with water**  
Lignin was precipitated by adding water to the ionic liquid solution.  

**Determine pulp and lignin yields**  
Pulp yield was 44%, and lignin yield was 82% of initial lignin content.  

**Perform saccharification**  
The pulp was enzymatically hydrolyzed, yielding 77% glucose.  

**Determine glucose yields**  
Glucose yields were quantified by HPLC analysis.  

**Analyse metal content by ICP-OES**  
Metal content in the ionic liquid phase was analyzed via ICP-OES, showing >90% copper recovery.  

**Discuss results and extraction efficiencies**  
The process achieved high metal extraction and biofuel yields, demonstrating industrial feasibility.
