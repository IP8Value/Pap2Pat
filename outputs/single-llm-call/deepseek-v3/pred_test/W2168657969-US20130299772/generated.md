# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to doped semiconductor nanoparticles, specifically to colloidal semiconductor nanocrystals that have been heavily doped with impurity atoms to control their electronic and optical properties. More particularly, the invention provides methods for preparing doped semiconductor nanocrystals where the doping level can be precisely controlled to alter the Fermi energy, band gap, and charge carrier concentration within the quantum-confined structure. The invention further relates to applications of these doped nanocrystals in electronic devices, optoelectronic components, and display technologies where tunable electronic properties are required.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Doping of bulk semiconductors has been a fundamental aspect of semiconductor technology for decades, enabling control over electrical conductivity and the creation of p-n junctions essential for electronic devices. In bulk semiconductors, doping is achieved by introducing impurity atoms that either donate electrons (n-type doping) or accept electrons (p-type doping) to the host lattice. The ability to control doping concentrations has allowed precise tuning of electronic properties in conventional semiconductor devices.

However, doping of colloidal semiconductor nanocrystals presents significant challenges that differ from bulk doping. The small size of nanocrystals (typically 2-10 nm in diameter) combined with strong quantum confinement effects creates an environment where traditional doping mechanisms often fail. Several limitations have hindered successful doping of colloidal nanocrystals. First, the high surface-to-volume ratio of nanocrystals makes them susceptible to surface defects that can trap dopants or compensate for intentional doping. Second, the formation energy for dopant incorporation increases significantly at the nanoscale due to quantum confinement and surface effects. Third, self-purification mechanisms in nanocrystals tend to expel dopant atoms to the surface rather than incorporating them into the crystal lattice.

Recent strategies for doping nanocrystals have included post-synthetic diffusion of dopants, in-situ doping during growth, and surface charge transfer doping. While these approaches have shown some success, they typically result in low doping efficiencies and poor control over dopant concentrations. The inability to achieve heavy doping in nanocrystals has limited their application in electronic devices where controlled doping is essential for functionality.

Recent progress in producing doped quantum dots has shown promise but remains limited. Some success has been achieved with manganese doping in II-VI semiconductors for magnetic applications, and limited n-type doping has been demonstrated in CdSe nanocrystals. However, these systems generally show low doping efficiencies and poor control over carrier concentrations. The development of methods to produce heavily doped colloidal semiconductor nanocrystals with controlled electronic properties remains an unmet need in the field.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides heavily doped colloidal semiconductor nanocrystals and methods for their preparation. The invention overcomes the limitations of previous doping approaches by providing nanocrystals where dopant atoms are incorporated in sufficient quantities to significantly alter the electronic properties while maintaining colloidal stability and quantum confinement effects.

The invention provides semiconductor nanocrystals comprising a semiconductor material doped with at least one dopant material, where the dopant material is dispersed within the semiconductor material in a manner that alters the density of states of the nanoparticle material. In one aspect, the nanoparticle material contains at least two atoms of the dopant material per nanocrystal, which induces charge carriers in the nanoparticle material. In another aspect, the nanoparticle contains at least two atoms of dopant material where the dopant material is heterovalent to atoms of the semiconductor material, thereby altering the density of states of the nanoparticle material.

The nanoparticle may consist entirely of semiconductor material or may contain regions of different materials within the nanoparticle structure. The invention encompasses doped semiconducting material elements, nanoparticles with metal or metal alloy materials on their surface, and hybrid nanoparticle structures. The dimensions of the nanoparticles may range from 1 nm to 20 nm in diameter, with preferred size ranges between 2 nm and 10 nm.

Suitable semiconductor materials for the nanoparticles include, but are not limited to, III-V semiconductors such as InAs, InP, GaAs, and GaN; II-VI semiconductors such as CdSe, CdS, CdTe, ZnSe, and ZnS; IV semiconductors such as Si and Ge; and their various alloys and hydrates. The semiconductor material may be prepared from metal precursors including metal halides, metal carboxylates, metal alkoxides, and metal chalcogenides, or from non-metal precursors such as phosphines, arsines, and chalcogens.

The dopant material is selected based on its ability to modify the electronic structure of the semiconductor nanocrystal. Suitable dopants include transition metals such as Cu, Ag, and Au for n-type doping of InAs; group III elements for p-type doping of II-VI semiconductors; and group V elements for n-type doping of group IV semiconductors. The dopant material is dispersed within the semiconductor material at concentrations ranging from 0.1% to 20% atomic percent relative to the host atoms.

The concentration of dopant atoms and their spatial distribution within the nanocrystal significantly affects the electronic properties. The invention provides nanocrystals containing between 2 and 50 dopant atoms per nanocrystal, with preferred ranges between 5 and 20 dopant atoms per nanocrystal for optimal electronic property modification. The presence of dopant atoms affects the nanoparticle by introducing new electronic states, shifting the Fermi level, modifying the band gap, and altering charge carrier concentrations.

The doped nanoparticles of the invention find application in various electronic and optoelectronic devices. They may be incorporated into diodes, transistors, photovoltaic devices, light-emitting devices, and sensors. The invention provides methods for manufacturing doped nanoparticles by contacting undoped nanoparticles with dopant or dopant precursor solutions under controlled conditions. The nanoparticles may be associated with surface ligands such as passivating ligands that maintain colloidal stability while allowing electronic communication between nanoparticles.

A device comprising the doped nanoparticles may include layers or regions of different doping materials to create p-n junctions or other electronic structures. For example, the invention encompasses bipolar transistors fabricated from doped nanoparticles, where n-type and p-type doped nanoparticles form the emitter, base, and collector regions. Specific embodiments include Ag-doped InAs nanoparticles, Cu-doped InAs nanoparticles, and devices incorporating these materials such as diodes and other electronic circuit components. The doped nanoparticles may also be used in large area display arrays where their tunable electronic and optical properties enable high-performance display technologies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

The present invention provides doped semiconductor nanoparticles and processes for their preparation with controlled electronic properties. The invention enables successful doping of colloidal nanocrystals by addressing the fundamental challenges of dopant incorporation and electronic property modification at the nanoscale. The key innovation lies in the ability to introduce multiple impurity atoms into individual nanocrystals and control their electronic effects through quantum confinement and band structure engineering.

The role of strong quantum confinement in the doped nanoparticles of the invention is critical. Unlike bulk semiconductors where doping effects are primarily determined by the dopant concentration and ionization energy, in nanocrystals the quantum confinement significantly modifies how dopants affect the electronic structure. The invention takes advantage of this by creating conditions where impurity states interact strongly with the confined electronic states of the host nanocrystal, leading to new electronic phenomena.

Disorder effects in the doped nanocrystals lead to band-tailing, which is distinct from bulk semiconductor behavior. In bulk semiconductors, heavy doping leads to band-tailing through the formation of an impurity band that merges with the main conduction or valence band. In nanocrystals, the invention demonstrates that band-tailing occurs through a quantum-confined Urbach tail mechanism, where the discrete energy levels of the nanocrystal are broadened by the presence of multiple dopant atoms. This effect is particularly pronounced when the dopant concentration exceeds one dopant atom per nanocrystal.

The invention provides successful controlled doping of semiconductor nanocrystals as evidenced by several experimental observations. Addition of impurity atoms to semiconductor nanocrystals leads to measurable changes in optical absorption spectra, photoluminescence spectra, and electronic transport properties. These changes are consistent with the introduction of charge carriers and modification of the density of states in the nanocrystals. For example, doping InAs nanocrystals with Cu atoms leads to a shift in the absorption edge to higher energies (blue shift), consistent with n-type doping and the Moss-Burstein effect. Conversely, doping with Ag atoms produces a red shift in the absorption edge, consistent with p-type doping and the introduction of acceptor states.

The effects of high doping levels in bulk semiconductors, such as the Moss-Burstein effect and Urbach tail formation, are modified in nanocrystals due to quantum confinement. The invention demonstrates that in nanocrystals, these effects become size-dependent and can be enhanced or suppressed by controlling the nanocrystal dimensions. For example, the Moss-Burstein shift in doped nanocrystals is larger for smaller nanocrystals due to the increased energy level spacing in strongly confined systems.

Electronic impurity doping of nanocrystals according to the invention involves the introduction of impurity levels that interact with the quantum-confined states of the host material. These impurity states can be either S-like (symmetric) or P-like (asymmetric) depending on the nature of the dopant and its position in the nanocrystal lattice. The invention shows that multiple impurities in a single nanocrystal can lead to delocalization and interaction of impurity charge carriers, creating new electronic states that are hybrids of the impurity states and the host nanocrystal states.

The disorder effects introduced by doping are described by a quantum-confined Urbach tail mechanism. In this model, the presence of multiple dopant atoms creates local variations in the crystal potential that broaden the discrete energy levels of the nanocrystal. This broadening follows an exponential decay into the band gap, similar to the Urbach tail in bulk semiconductors but modified by quantum confinement effects. The invention demonstrates that this mechanism can explain the observed optical and electronic properties of heavily doped nanocrystals.

The invention specifically describes doping InAs nanocrystals with different impurity atoms such as Cu, Ag, and Au. These dopants exhibit distinct behaviors due to their different electronic structures and incorporation mechanisms. Cu tends to occupy interstitial sites in InAs nanocrystals, acting as an n-type dopant by donating electrons to the conduction band. Ag substitutes for In atoms in the lattice, acting as a p-type dopant by creating acceptor states in the band gap. Au behaves as an isovalent impurity that does not significantly alter the electronic properties of the nanocrystal.

Phase separation between InAs and impurity metal regions is observed in some cases, particularly for Ag doping where the large size mismatch between Ag and In atoms can lead to local clustering. However, the invention demonstrates that even in these cases, sufficient dopant incorporation occurs to modify the electronic properties of the nanocrystals. X-ray diffraction and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy measurements confirm the incorporation of dopant atoms into the nanocrystal structure while maintaining the overall crystal phase of the host material.

Diffusion parameter values for various dopants in InAs nanocrystals have been determined, showing that dopant atoms can readily diffuse into nanocrystals at room temperature due to their small size. This diffusion enables post-synthetic doping methods where pre-formed nanocrystals are exposed to dopant precursors in solution. The invention provides optimized conditions for this doping process to achieve controlled dopant concentrations.

Absorption and emission spectra of undoped and doped InAs quantum dots show significant differences that demonstrate successful doping. Undoped InAs nanocrystals exhibit well-defined excitonic features in their absorption spectra and band-edge emission in photoluminescence. Doping with Cu leads to a blue shift in absorption and quenching of emission, consistent with n-type doping. Ag doping produces a red shift in both absorption and emission, consistent with p-type doping and the introduction of mid-gap states.

The effect of varying amounts of impurities on optical spectra has been systematically studied. At low dopant concentrations (less than one dopant atom per nanocrystal on average), small changes in the absorption edge are observed. As the dopant concentration increases to multiple atoms per nanocrystal, significant shifts and broadening of the absorption features occur, along with complete quenching of band-edge emission. These changes are consistent with the formation of an impurity band that modifies the density of states near the band edges.

Estimation of impurities in quantum dots by ICP-AES (Inductively Coupled Plasma Atomic Emission Spectroscopy) has provided quantitative measurements of dopant incorporation. The invention demonstrates that the number of dopant atoms per nanocrystal can be controlled by adjusting the ratio of dopant precursor to nanocrystals in the doping solution. Linear relationships between solution composition and final dopant concentration have been established for various dopant-nanocrystal combinations.

Tunneling spectra measured by scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) provide direct evidence of doping-induced changes in the electronic structure of individual nanocrystals. Undoped InAs nanocrystals show a well-defined band gap in their tunneling spectra, with clear conduction and valence band edges. Upon doping with Cu, the conduction band edge shifts to lower energies relative to the Fermi level, consistent with n-type doping and Fermi level movement into the conduction band. Ag doping produces the opposite effect, with the valence band edge moving closer to the Fermi level, consistent with p-type doping.

The shifts of Fermi level in Cu and Ag doped quantum dots have been quantitatively measured using scanning tunneling spectroscopy (STS). Cu doping moves the Fermi level toward the conduction band by up to 200 meV, depending on doping concentration. Ag doping moves the Fermi level toward the valence band by similar amounts. These shifts are significantly larger than would be expected from single dopant atoms and demonstrate the collective effect of multiple dopants in each nanocrystal.

Chemical considerations for doping of InAs with metal atom impurities have been analyzed. The invention shows that the doping mechanism depends on the chemical nature of the dopant atom, including its valence state, atomic size, and preferred coordination environment. Cu, with its +1 oxidation state and small atomic size, tends to occupy interstitial positions and donate electrons to the conduction band. Ag, also with a +1 oxidation state but larger size, substitutes for In atoms and creates acceptor states. Au, with similar size to In but different chemistry, does not significantly dope the nanocrystals but may passivate surface states.

The implications of observed shifts in absorption and emission spectra have been analyzed in terms of band structure modifications. The blue shift observed in Cu-doped nanocrystals is consistent with the Moss-Burstein effect, where filling of conduction band states increases the effective band gap. The red shift in Ag-doped nanocrystals indicates the formation of new states near the valence band edge that reduce the effective band gap. These observations, combined with theoretical modeling, provide a comprehensive picture of doping effects in strongly confined nanocrystals.

### EXPERIMENTAL

The materials used in the preparation of doped semiconductor nanocrystals include high-purity precursors for both the host semiconductor and dopant materials. For InAs nanocrystal synthesis, the materials include In(III)Cl3 (99.999+%), tris(trimethylsilyl) arsenide (TMS3As), trioctylphosphine (TOP, 90%), dodecylamine (DDA, 98%), and didodecyldimethylammonium bromide (DDAB, 98%). Dopant precursors include AuCl3 (99%), AgCl (99+%), AgNO3 (99+%), and CuCl2 (99.999%). All reactions are carried out using anhydrous solvents including toluene (99.8%) and methanol (99.8%).

The synthesis of InAs nanocrystals is performed under inert atmosphere using standard Schlenk techniques. In a typical synthesis, a mixture of indium and arsenic precursors is prepared by combining (TMS3As) with a solution of InCl3 in TOP. This precursor solution is injected into hot TOP at 300°C under vigorous stirring. The temperature is then reduced to 260°C for particle growth, with additional precursor solution added as needed to achieve the desired particle size. Growth is monitored by measuring absorption spectra of aliquots extracted from the reaction mixture. Upon reaching the target size, the reaction mixture is cooled to room temperature and transferred to an inert atmosphere glovebox for further processing.

The metal-atom doping procedure involves preparing a metal solution by dissolving the metal salt (CuCl2, AgNO3, AgCl or AuCl3) with DDAB and DDA in toluene. This metal solution is added drop-wise to a toluene solution of InAs nanocrystals under stirring. After 15 minutes, the optical properties of the solution are measured to assess doping effects. The doped nanocrystals are then precipitated using methanol (for Cu and Au samples) or acetone (for Ag samples). The entire metal treatment procedure is conducted under inert conditions to prevent oxidation or other unwanted reactions.

Size selective precipitation is used to narrow the size distribution of both undoped and doped nanocrystals. This involves sequential addition of a non-solvent (typically methanol) to a nanocrystal solution in toluene, causing precipitation of larger nanocrystals first. By carefully controlling the solvent/non-solvent ratio, fractions with narrow size distributions (±5% in diameter) can be obtained. This process may be repeated multiple times to achieve the desired size uniformity.

Characterization of the doped nanocrystals includes various spectroscopic and microscopic techniques. UV-Visible-Near IR Spectroscopy is performed using a spectrophotometer with a detection range covering the bandgap energies of the nanocrystals. Photoluminescence measurements are conducted using a NIR spectrophotometer with laser excitation at appropriate wavelengths. Transmission Electron Microscopy provides information about nanocrystal size, shape, and crystallinity at high resolution. Samples for TEM are prepared by depositing nanocrystal solutions onto carbon-coated grids and washing with appropriate solvents.

Powder X-ray Diffraction patterns are obtained using Cu Kα radiation to determine crystal structure and phase purity of the nanocrystals. Samples are prepared as thin films on low-background substrates, and data is processed with background subtraction for clarity. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy provides information about chemical composition and oxidation states of elements in the nanocrystals. Measurements are performed on thin nanocrystal films deposited on silicon wafers using Al Kα radiation.

Inductively Coupled Plasma Atomic Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-AES) is used for quantitative elemental analysis to determine dopant concentrations in the nanocrystals. Samples for ICP-AES are prepared by dissolving precipitated nanocrystals in concentrated nitric acid and diluting with distilled water. The instrument measures emission from characteristic atomic transitions of each element of interest, allowing calculation of atomic ratios and dopant concentrations per nanocrystal.
