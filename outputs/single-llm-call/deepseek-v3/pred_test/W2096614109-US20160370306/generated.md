# DESCRIPTION  

## BACKGROUND  

Serial femtosecond crystallography (SFX) represents a groundbreaking advancement in structural biology that enables determination of protein structures from microcrystals at room temperature. This technique involves delivering protein crystals into the path of an X-ray free-electron laser (XFEL) beam, where femtosecond pulses capture diffraction patterns before radiation damage occurs. The conventional approach utilizes a gas dynamic virtual nozzle (GDVN) liquid jet system that propels crystals in their mother liquor at high velocities. However, this method suffers from significant limitations including excessive protein consumption, as only a minute fraction of delivered crystals actually interact with the X-ray pulses.  

The SFX technique has proven particularly valuable for membrane protein structure determination, despite facing unique challenges. Membrane proteins constitute over 60% of pharmaceutical drug targets yet remain notoriously difficult to crystallize due to their hydrophobic nature. Current approaches involve crystallization either in detergent micelles or within lipidic cubic phase (LCP) matrices. While LCP crystallization has enabled structure determination of numerous membrane proteins, it presents substantial difficulties for large, multi-protein complexes due to restricted diffusion and bilayer curvature constraints.  

Existing crystal delivery systems for SFX exhibit several critical shortcomings. The GDVN liquid jet consumes impractical quantities of precious membrane protein samples. LCP injection systems, while more efficient, are limited to crystals grown specifically in LCP and cannot accommodate detergent-grown crystals. Alternative viscous media such as mineral oil-based greases and petroleum jellies have been explored but produce undesirable background scattering patterns and have not demonstrated suitability for membrane proteins. These limitations highlight the pressing need for an improved crystal delivery medium that combines low sample consumption, broad compatibility with diverse crystal types, and minimal background interference.  

The development of an effective crystal delivery medium must address multiple technical challenges. The medium must maintain crystal integrity during delivery while forming a stable, continuous stream at appropriate flow rates. It should exhibit minimal X-ray scattering background to avoid obscuring diffraction patterns. Furthermore, the medium must be compatible with various crystallization conditions and capable of operating across a practical temperature range. Current alternatives fail to meet all these requirements simultaneously, creating a significant technological gap in the field of serial femtosecond crystallography.  

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention provides a novel method for preparing an inert crystal delivery medium for serial femtosecond crystallography. The method involves creating an agarose-based medium that addresses the limitations of existing delivery systems. The inventive process begins with dissolving agarose in an appropriate buffer solution at elevated temperatures, followed by controlled cooling to form a gel matrix. The method further includes mixing protein crystals with the agarose medium using a specialized syringe system that ensures homogeneous crystal distribution while maintaining sample integrity.  

The invention discloses a crystal delivery medium composition comprising agarose at concentrations between 5-10% (w/v), combined with crystallization buffer and optional cryoprotectants such as glycerol or sucrose. The medium exhibits unique physical properties including tunable viscosity, temperature stability from 4-30°C, and compatibility with diverse crystallization conditions. The agarose matrix provides mechanical stability for crystal delivery while contributing minimal background scattering during X-ray diffraction experiments.  

A system for preparing the crystal delivery medium forms another aspect of the invention. The system includes temperature-controlled mixing apparatus, specialized syringes for medium preparation, and coupling devices for homogeneous crystal distribution. The complete solution enables efficient embedding of pre-formed crystals into the agarose medium while preserving their diffraction quality. The invention represents a significant advance over existing technologies by providing a versatile, low-consumption delivery method suitable for both soluble and membrane protein crystals in SFX applications.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION  

The invention provides an agarose-based crystal delivery medium specifically designed for serial femtosecond crystallography applications. The medium composition comprises ultralow-gelling-temperature agarose dissolved in crystallization buffer at concentrations ranging from 5-10% (w/v), with preferred embodiments utilizing approximately 5.6% agarose. The crystallization buffer component matches the specific mother liquor conditions appropriate for each protein crystal type, ensuring compatibility and stability during delivery.  

The medium accommodates protein crystals ranging from nanocrystals to microcrystals, typically between 1-10 μm in size. The crystallization buffer may contain various precipitants including salts (e.g., ammonium sulfate, sodium chloride), polyethylene glycols (PEGs) of different molecular weights, or organic solvents such as 2-methyl-2,4-pentanediol (MPD). The invention demonstrates particular effectiveness with high-molecular-weight PEGs (above 2000 Da) which contribute to stream stability.  

An essential component of the medium is the cryoprotectant, which prevents crystal damage during delivery, particularly in vacuum conditions. The invention employs cryoprotectants selected from glycerol, sucrose, or similar compounds, with glycerol concentrations around 30% proving particularly effective. The cryoprotectant serves dual functions of preventing freezing while increasing medium viscosity for improved stream formation.  

The agarose component provides critical physical properties to the medium. Agarose forms a three-dimensional helical network upon cooling, creating a gel matrix that embeds and stabilizes protein crystals. The concentration of agarose can be adjusted between 5-10% to achieve optimal viscosity for different injection systems, with 5.6% representing a preferred embodiment that balances stream stability with ease of extrusion.  

Preparation of the agarose solution involves dissolving the agarose powder in heated crystallization buffer (above 85°C) containing the selected cryoprotectant. The solution is maintained at elevated temperature for sufficient time (approximately 30 minutes) to ensure complete dissolution before cooling to form the gel. This process creates a homogeneous medium with consistent viscosity properties suitable for crystal delivery.  

The viscosity of the medium is carefully tuned to meet injector requirements, typically achieving flow rates around 160 nl/min. This represents a two-order-of-magnitude reduction in sample consumption compared to conventional liquid jet systems. The viscosity can be adjusted by modifying agarose concentration or cryoprotectant content to suit specific experimental conditions or protein crystal types.  

Mixing of the crystal solution with agarose solution employs a specialized syringe coupling system that ensures homogeneous distribution while minimizing crystal damage. The process involves drawing crystal suspension into one syringe and prepared agarose solution into another, followed by connection via a coupler device. Alternate plunger movement achieves thorough mixing through 40 or more exchange cycles, resulting in uniform crystal distribution within the agarose matrix.  

The agarose medium exhibits exceptional inertness and produces minimal background X-ray scattering. Comparative studies demonstrate significantly lower background interference than lipidic cubic phase or grease-based delivery systems, particularly in the low-resolution range (>30 Å). This characteristic makes the medium particularly suitable for large unit cell crystals where low-angle scattering could obscure important diffraction features.  

The invention includes a method for preparing the crystal delivery medium comprising several key steps. First, the crystal solution is drawn into a first syringe, typically containing highly concentrated crystal suspension in mother liquor. Simultaneously, agarose solution is prepared in a second syringe by drawing heated solution and allowing it to cool to gelation temperature. The syringes are then coupled and the contents mixed through repeated reciprocal transfer until homogeneous crystal distribution is achieved.  

The preparation method allows precise tuning of solution volumes and ratios to achieve optimal crystal density and medium viscosity. The initial agarose concentration is adjusted to compensate for dilution during mixing, with 7% agarose solution typically yielding 5.6% final concentration after combining with crystal suspension. This tuning capability ensures consistent performance across different protein crystal types and experimental conditions.  

A system for preparing the crystal delivery medium forms another embodiment of the invention. The system includes temperature-controlled heating elements for agarose solution preparation, specialized syringes designed for viscous media handling, and coupling devices that facilitate efficient mixing. The components are compatible with operation across a wide temperature range (4-30°C), accommodating diverse crystallization conditions.  

Use of the agarose crystal delivery medium in injection systems represents a further aspect of the invention. The medium is compatible with standard LCP injector hardware, utilizing a high-velocity inert-gas sheath (typically nitrogen) to center and stabilize the extrusion stream. The system operates effectively in both vacuum and ambient-pressure environments, with vacuum operation preferred when cryoprotectants are used to prevent freezing.  

The delivery system provides several advantages including reduced sample consumption, improved stream stability, and compatibility with diverse crystal types. Ambient-pressure operation is possible using helium environments, particularly for crystals that cannot tolerate cryoprotectants. The invention demonstrates particular utility for large, complex membrane protein assemblies that are challenging to crystallize in large quantities.  

The scope of the invention encompasses various equivalents, alternatives, and modifications to the described embodiments. These include variations in agarose concentration, alternative cryoprotectants, different mixing methodologies, and adaptations for specific experimental setups. The invention represents a significant advance in serial femtosecond crystallography by providing a versatile, efficient crystal delivery solution that addresses longstanding limitations in the field.  

### EXAMPLE 1  

The invention is demonstrated through application to phycocyanin (PC), a model photosynthetic antenna protein complex. PC purification begins with cell disruption using high-pressure homogenization followed by ultracentrifugation to remove cellular debris. The supernatant undergoes concentration using 100 kDa cutoff filters to isolate the PC complex while removing smaller contaminants.  

PC crystallization employs free interface diffusion techniques with precipitant solution containing 1.0 M ammonium sulfate and 40 mM MES buffer at pH 6.4. Crystals form within 24 hours, reaching sizes of 1-5 μm, and are verified using second-order nonlinear imaging of chiral crystals. Prior to embedding, crystals undergo filtration through 10 μm stainless steel filters to ensure uniform size distribution.  

For photosystem I (PSI) and photosystem II (PSII) membrane protein complexes, purification follows established protocols involving multiple chromatography steps. PSI crystallization occurs at low ionic strength in buffer containing 5 mM MES pH 6.4 and 0.02% β-dodecylmaltoside detergent. PSII crystallization utilizes batch methods with decreasing precipitant concentrations in buffer containing PIPES, calcium chloride, and PEG 2000.  

Preparation of the agarose solution involves dissolving 7% (w/v) ultralow-gelling-temperature agarose in appropriate crystallization buffer containing 30% glycerol. The mixture undergoes heating in a boiling water bath for 30 minutes to ensure complete dissolution. For PC, the agarose solution incorporates 15% PEG 2000, 30 mM magnesium chloride, and 75 mM HEPES buffer at pH 7.0.  

Crystal embedding employs a two-syringe mixing system where 20 μL agarose solution is drawn into one syringe and 5 μL crystal suspension into another. After agarose gelation, the syringes are coupled and the contents mixed through 40 reciprocal transfers to achieve homogeneous distribution. The final crystal density reaches approximately 2×10^10 crystals per milliliter, with agarose concentration stabilizing at 5.6%.  

Data collection utilizes the Coherent X-ray Imaging (CXI) instrument at the Linac Coherent Light Source. The agarose stream extrudes through a 50 μm capillary at 160 nL/min flow rate, stabilized by nitrogen gas sheath. Diffraction patterns are collected at 120 Hz repetition rate, with detector readouts processed using Cheetah software to identify hits containing Bragg reflections.  

Data processing involves indexing patterns using CrystFEL software with hexagonal lattice parameters (a = b = 153.4 Å, c = 39.6 Å). The merohedral space group (P63) ambiguity is resolved using ambigator algorithms. Data merging occurs at 2.5 Å resolution cutoff based on multiplicity and CC* values, yielding a complete data set with 14,143 indexed patterns.  

Structure solution proceeds via molecular replacement using known PC structures as search models. Rigid-body refinement of individual subunits precedes iterative cycles of positional and B-factor refinement. Water molecules are added during final refinement stages, yielding a structure with Rwork = 18.7% and Rfree = 25.5%.  

Comparative studies evaluate different viscous media for crystal delivery. Glycerol proves optimal as cryoprotectant, preventing freezing while increasing viscosity. Agarose concentration optimization identifies 5.6% as ideal for stable stream formation. Syringe-based embedding methods demonstrate superior crystal distribution compared to manual mixing techniques.  

The agarose stream exhibits excellent stability in vacuum operation, maintained by nitrogen gas sheath flow. Ambient-pressure setups using helium environment show slightly reduced stability but remain viable for crystals incompatible with cryoprotectants. Comparative background scattering measurements demonstrate significantly lower interference from agarose versus LCP or grease-based media.  

Data collection statistics for PC reveal high multiplicity (250.6 overall, 12.5 in highest resolution shell), enabling accurate structure factor determination. Electron density maps show clear definition of protein loops, side chains, and chromophore cofactors. The results confirm agarose medium suitability for SFX of large protein complexes while consuming minimal sample quantities.  

The example concludes that agarose represents an ideal delivery medium for serial femtosecond crystallography, combining low background scattering, broad compatibility with diverse crystal types, and dramatically reduced sample consumption compared to conventional methods. The technique extends SFX applicability to challenging membrane protein complexes and scarce biological samples.
