# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) therapeutics. More specifically, the invention provides compositions and methods for treating IBD through the selective sequestration of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) protein using DNA-conjugated beads. The invention encompasses therapeutic beads comprising DNA molecules immobilized on solid supports, methods of making such beads, and their use in reducing HMGB1 levels in the gastrointestinal tract to ameliorate inflammation and tissue damage associated with IBD. The invention further provides specific embodiments of DNA sequences, bead compositions, coating methods, administration protocols, and treatment regimens effective for managing IBD symptoms and progression.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Inflammatory bowel diseases, including ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease, represent significant healthcare burdens with annual costs exceeding $1.7 billion in the United States alone. These chronic inflammatory conditions of the gastrointestinal tract substantially reduce patients' quality of life and are associated with considerable morbidity. Despite advances in medical management, current therapeutic strategies targeting excessive cytokine production or employing immune-suppressive regimens have demonstrated limited success, and no curative treatments currently exist.

High mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) has emerged as a critical mediator in the pathogenesis of IBD. This ubiquitous nuclear protein, when released into the extracellular space during infection or injury, functions as a potent pro-inflammatory mediator. In both IBD patients and animal models of colitis, HMGB1 is secreted by inflamed intestinal tissues and accumulates at high levels in feces. The elevated HMGB1 concentrations in the gastrointestinal tract contribute to inflammation and gastrointestinal barrier dysfunction. Neutralization of HMGB1 activity through administration of anti-HMGB1 antibodies or ethyl pyruvate has been shown to attenuate colon injury, reduce weight loss, and improve colon scores in animal models of colitis, suggesting HMGB1 as a promising therapeutic target in IBD.

The biological activity of extracellular HMGB1 is determined by its redox state, particularly involving three cysteine residues (C23, C45 and C106). Different redox forms of HMGB1 exhibit distinct biological activities: cytokine-stimulating disulfide HMGB1 contains C23 and C45 in a disulfide linkage with C106 in its reduced form; fully reduced HMGB1 acts as a chemotactic mediator; while terminally oxidized sulfonyl HMGB1 lacks biological activity. Additionally, post-translational modifications such as acetylation and phosphorylation regulate HMGB1 release from cells.

HMGB1 exhibits strong binding affinity to various DNA structures including linear, bent, bulged, and four-way junction DNA. This DNA-binding property has been utilized to neutralize HMGB1 cytokine activity and reduce immune responses in animal models of inflammation associated with elevated HMGB1 levels. However, direct DNA injection approaches have limitations including rapid degradation, potential immunogenicity, and systemic distribution issues. There remains an unmet need for targeted HMGB1 sequestration strategies that can effectively reduce local HMGB1 levels in the gastrointestinal tract while minimizing systemic effects and toxicity.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides novel therapeutic beads for the treatment of inflammatory bowel diseases through selective HMGB1 sequestration. The invention comprises DNA molecules immobilized on solid support beads, wherein the DNA specifically binds HMGB1 with high affinity. These DNA-conjugated beads effectively capture HMGB1 from biological fluids including intestinal luminal contents, feces, and inflamed tissues.

The therapeutic beads of the invention comprise several key components: a solid support matrix, typically sepharose beads with diameters ranging from 45-165 μm; DNA molecules covalently attached to the beads through appropriate conjugation chemistry; and optional enteric coatings to protect the DNA from degradation in the acidic gastric environment. The DNA sequences are selected for high HMGB1 binding affinity and may include linear DNA constructs, four-way junction DNA, or kinked duplex DNA structures.

Methods of treatment according to the invention involve oral administration of the DNA-conjugated beads to subjects with IBD. The beads traverse the gastrointestinal tract, binding and sequestering HMGB1 present in the intestinal lumen and inflamed tissues. This localized HMGB1 reduction attenuates inflammation, preserves epithelial barrier function, and ameliorates disease symptoms without systemic immunosuppression. The invention provides specific treatment protocols, dosing regimens, and monitoring methods to optimize therapeutic outcomes in both acute and chronic forms of IBD.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention provides a method of treating inflammatory bowel disease by reducing levels of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) in the gastrointestinal tract. The method comprises administering to a subject in need thereof a therapeutically effective amount of DNA-conjugated beads capable of binding HMGB1. The reduction of HMGB1 levels occurs through specific binding and sequestration of the protein by the DNA molecules immobilized on the beads, thereby decreasing the available pool of pro-inflammatory HMGB1 in the intestinal environment.

The method of reducing HMGB1 levels involves several mechanisms. The DNA-conjugated beads bind both disulfide and fully reduced forms of HMGB1, as well as various acetylated forms present in the inflamed intestinal milieu. The beads effectively capture HMGB1 released by activated immune cells and damaged epithelial cells, interrupting the cycle of inflammation and tissue injury. The binding occurs with high affinity, typically in the nanomolar range, ensuring efficient sequestration even at low HMGB1 concentrations.

The therapeutic beads of the invention comprise several essential components. The solid support matrix consists of sepharose beads, preferably CNBr-activated sepharose 4 fast flow resin, with an average diameter of approximately 90 μm. The beads are non-absorbable and designed to transit through the entire gastrointestinal tract. The DNA component includes oligos selected from sequences with high HMGB1 binding affinity, such as linear DNA (S1 and S2), four-way junction DNA (S3), or kinked duplex DNA (S4). These oligos are typically 20-30 base pairs in length and may contain phosphorothioate or phosphodiester backbones for nuclease resistance.

The bead coating agents include covalent linkers such as C6 amino group linkers [NH2(CH2)6O-P(O)2] conjugated to the 5′ end of the DNA oligos for attachment to the solid support. The conjugation chemistry involves standard CNBr activation protocols, with coupling efficiencies typically exceeding 90% as measured by pre- and post-coupling optical density at 260 nm. Approximately 8 nmol of linear DNA or 4 nmol of structured DNA are bound per ml of drained beads.

Administration methods for the DNA-conjugated beads include oral delivery, typically as a 50% slurry in physiologically compatible buffers. The beads may be administered once daily or every other day, depending on disease severity and response. For acute colitis models, administration may occur on days 0, 1, 2, 4 and 6 following disease induction, while chronic colitis may require extended treatment periods of six weeks or more. The beads are formulated to withstand the acidic gastric environment and release their DNA binding sites in the intestinal lumen where HMGB1 concentrations are elevated.

The bead materials are selected for biocompatibility and stability in the gastrointestinal tract. Sepharose provides an ideal matrix due to its chemical modifiability, mechanical stability, and lack of toxicity. The beads may include enteric coatings to protect the DNA from gastric acid degradation while allowing release in the neutral pH environment of the intestines. The coating materials may include pH-sensitive polymers that dissolve at intestinal pH while remaining intact in the stomach.

The reduction of HMGB1 levels by the DNA-conjugated beads has been demonstrated both in vitro and in vivo. The beads bind HMGB1 with molar ratios of approximately 6:1 for linear DNA beads (B1 and B2) and 12:1 for four-way junction DNA beads (B3). Binding occurs rapidly, reaching saturation within 15-30 minutes at room temperature. The beads effectively reduce HMGB1 levels in biological samples including activated macrophage supernatants, septic serum, and fecal extracts from colitis models.

The DNA-conjugated beads specifically bind HMGB1 through interaction with the DNA-binding domains of the protein. The binding agents include DNA sequences selected for high HMGB1 affinity, typically with dissociation constants in the low nanomolar range. The beads demonstrate specificity for HMGB1, showing minimal binding to other proteins such as TNF or histone H3 under similar conditions.

The nucleic acid sequences used in the invention include several options optimized for HMGB1 binding. Linear DNA constructs (S1 and S2) exhibit Kd values of approximately 5 nM, while four-way junction DNA (S3) shows even higher affinity with Kd = 1 nM. Kinked duplex DNA (S4) provides an alternative structure with Kd = 22 nM. These sequences may be modified with phosphorothioate backbones to enhance nuclease resistance without compromising HMGB1 binding affinity.

The nucleic acid backbone options include both phosphorothioate and phosphodiester configurations. Linear DNA constructs typically employ phosphorothioate backbones throughout the sequence to prevent DNase degradation, while structured DNA may use phosphodiester backbones where the three-dimensional configuration provides inherent protection. The choice of backbone depends on the specific application and desired balance between stability and binding kinetics.

Agent attachment methods involve covalent conjugation through amino linkers. A C6 amino group linker [NH2(CH2)6O-P(O)2] is conjugated to the 5′ end of the oligos, which then reacts with CNBr-activated sepharose beads. The conjugation protocol involves incubation in coupling buffer (typically 0.1 M NaHCO3, pH 8.3 containing 0.5 M NaCl) for 2-4 hours at room temperature or overnight at 4°C, followed by blocking of remaining active groups with ethanolamine.

### EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS

The invention includes DNA molecules conjugated to solid support beads for HMGB1 sequestration. The binding of HMGB1 to DNA beads was characterized through extensive experimentation. Recombinant HMGB1 protein showed concentration-dependent binding to DNA beads B1, B2 and B3, with maximum binding observed at 2 μg HMGB1 per 50 μl bead slurry. Control beads lacking DNA and B4 beads with kinked duplex DNA demonstrated negligible HMGB1 binding under identical conditions.

The materials used in these studies included CNBr-activated sepharose 4 fast flow resin, recombinant human HMGB1, and custom DNA oligos synthesized with C6 amino linkers. Cell culture experiments utilized RAW 264.7 macrophages, HeLa cells, and Caco-2 intestinal epithelial cells maintained in DMEM with appropriate serum supplements. Animal studies employed IL-10 knockout mice and dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis models in BALB/c mice.

Cell culture methods followed standard protocols with cells used at 90% confluence. Treatment experiments were conducted in serum-free Opti-MEM I medium. Primary human monocytes were purified from healthy donor blood using Ficoll density gradient centrifugation. All cell cultures were maintained under sterile conditions at 37°C with 5% CO2.

DNA-bead generation involved conjugation of amino-modified oligos to CNBr-activated sepharose beads. The coupling efficiency was calculated by measuring optical density at 260 nm before and after conjugation. Approximately 8 nmol of linear DNA (48 μg) or 4 nmol of structured DNA (180 μg) were bound per ml of drained beads, corresponding to final concentrations of approximately 8 μM for linear DNA beads and 4 μM for structured DNA beads.

Preparation of DNA-beads included washing steps to remove unconjugated DNA and blocking of remaining reactive groups. The beads were stored as 50% slurries in phosphate-buffered saline with 0.02% sodium azide at 4°C. Prior to use, beads were washed extensively with PBS to remove storage buffer and resuspended in appropriate media for experiments.

The binding ratio of HMGB1 to DNA beads was calculated based on maximum binding capacity. For B1 and B2 beads (linear DNA), the ratio was approximately 2:1 (76 pmoles HMGB1 per 40 pmoles DNA), while for B3 beads (four-way junction DNA) the ratio was 4:1 (76 pmoles HMGB1 per 20 pmoles DNA). These ratios reflect the higher binding capacity of structured DNA constructs for HMGB1.

FAM-labeled B2 DNA-beads were prepared using carboxyl terminal fluorescein modifications. These fluorescent beads were used to assess stability in biological fluids by measuring fluorescence intensity in supernatants after incubation with fecal extracts. No significant release of fluorescent DNA was observed after two hours at 37°C, confirming the stability of the DNA-bead conjugation.

Ethidium bromide staining of DNA beads confirmed the presence of accessible DNA binding sites after conjugation. The stained beads showed uniform fluorescence under microscopy, indicating even distribution of DNA across the bead surface. Control beads without DNA showed minimal background staining under identical conditions.

Concentration-dependent binding of HMGB1 and DNA beads was characterized using western blot analysis. Fixed amounts of DNA beads (5 μl) were incubated with HMGB1 concentrations ranging from 0.01 to 3 μg in 50 μl total volume. Bound HMGB1 was eluted and quantified by densitometry, revealing saturable binding with EC50 values in the 0.1-0.2 μg range.

HMGB1 protein preparation involved expression in E. coli and purification to homogeneity. Endotoxin was removed using triton X-114 extraction, resulting in preparations with less than 10 pg LPS per mg protein as measured by Limulus Amebocyte Lysate assay. Redox-modified HMGB1 proteins were generated by treatment with hydrogen peroxide or dithiothreitol as needed.

Removal of LPS from protein preparations was critical for avoiding confounding inflammatory responses. The triton X-114 phase separation method effectively reduced endotoxin levels while maintaining protein structure and function. Final preparations were verified by both LAL assay and lack of TNF induction in macrophage stimulation assays.

Cytokine measurements included TNF, IL-6 and HMGB1 quantification by ELISA. Cell culture supernatants were analyzed using commercial kits according to manufacturer protocols. HMGB1 levels were additionally assessed by western blot for isoform characterization and binding studies.

HMGB1 measurement techniques included both ELISA and western blot analysis. The ELISA provided quantitative assessment of total HMGB1 levels, while western blotting allowed differentiation between bound and free HMGB1 in bead binding experiments. Blots were developed using chemiluminescence and quantified by densitometry.

Surface plasmon resonance analysis provided detailed binding kinetics data. HMGB1 was immobilized on CM5 dextran sensor chips, and various DNA constructs were used as analytes at concentrations ranging from 0-10 μM. Association and dissociation curves were recorded and analyzed using BIAeval software to determine kinetic parameters.

Binding of DNA-beads with HMGB1 in vitro was characterized under various conditions. Beads (20 μl) were incubated with 500 ng HMGB1 in PBS for time periods ranging from 0-960 minutes at room temperature. Bound HMGB1 was eluted and quantified by western blot, showing rapid association kinetics with saturation reached by 30-60 minutes.

Depletion and saturation studies established the binding capacity of DNA beads. Fixed HMGB1 concentrations (2 μg) were incubated with increasing bead volumes (0-15 μl) for two hours. The resulting binding curves showed linear increases in bound HMGB1 up to approximately 5 μl beads, approaching saturation at higher volumes.

The time course of DNA-beads and HMGB1 binding revealed rapid association kinetics. Significant binding occurred within 15 minutes for all bead types, with B3 beads reaching saturation fastest. These kinetics support the practical utility of the beads for HMGB1 sequestration in biological systems where contact times may be limited.

Stability studies assessed DNA bead performance under physiological conditions. Beads were incubated in PBS (pH 7.0) or acidic buffers (pH 1-2) for one hour at room temperature prior to HMGB1 binding assays. B1 and B2 beads maintained binding capacity across the pH range, while B3 beads showed reduced binding at low pH.

Cytotoxicity of DNA beads was evaluated in epithelial cell lines. HeLa and Caco-2 cells were incubated with bead concentrations up to 100 μl/ml for 24 hours, with cell viability assessed by LDH release. No significant cytotoxicity was observed compared to untreated controls, supporting the safety profile of the beads.

Removal of HMGB1 by DNA-beads in vitro was demonstrated using activated macrophage supernatants. RAW 264.7 cells stimulated with LPS released HMGB1 into the medium, which was concentrated 10-fold and incubated with various DNA beads. B1 and B2 beads removed >90% of HMGB1, while control beads had no effect.

Animal experiments validated the therapeutic potential of DNA-conjugated beads. IL-10 knockout mice with spontaneous colitis and DSS-induced colitis models were treated with oral bead administrations. Body weight, clinical scores, and histological parameters were monitored throughout the treatment periods.

Ex vivo removal of HMGB1 from septic mice demonstrated the broad applicability of DNA beads. Serum from cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) mice, containing high HMGB1 levels, was incubated with B2 beads. The beads effectively captured HMGB1 from the serum as shown by western blot and ELISA analysis.

Removal of HMGB1 from intestinal tissue and stool was specifically evaluated in DSS-induced colitis models. Fecal extracts and colon tissue homogenates from colitis mice were incubated with DNA beads, showing significant reduction in detectable HMGB1 compared to control bead treatments.

Treatment with anti-HMGB1 antibodies in DSS-induced colitis provided positive controls for therapeutic effects. Antibody administration improved colon scores and reduced inflammation markers, validating HMGB1 targeting as an effective strategy for colitis management.

Treatment with DNA beads in colitis mice demonstrated significant therapeutic benefits. Oral administration of B2 beads improved body weight, reduced colon swelling, and decreased histological injury scores in both DSS-induced and IL-10 knockout colitis models compared to control bead treatments.

Monitoring of body weight provided a sensitive measure of therapeutic response. Mice treated with B2 beads showed significantly less weight loss than controls during active colitis phases, with weight stabilization or gain during recovery periods in acute models.

Harvesting of blood, feces, and colon tissues enabled comprehensive analysis of treatment effects. Samples were collected at sacrifice for HMGB1 measurement, cytokine analysis, and histological evaluation. Systematic sampling protocols ensured consistent data collection across experimental groups.

Analysis of HMGB1 levels in various compartments revealed the systemic effects of DNA bead treatment. Serum, fecal, and tissue HMGB1 concentrations were significantly reduced in bead-treated animals, correlating with improved clinical outcomes and histological scores.

The therapeutic potential of DNA-conjugated beads was conclusively demonstrated through these integrated experiments. The beads effectively sequestered HMGB1 in vitro and in vivo, reducing inflammatory markers and improving disease parameters in multiple colitis models without observable toxicity.

Histology protocols included standard hematoxylin and eosin staining of paraffin-embedded colon sections. Five-micron sections were evaluated by blinded observers using standardized scoring systems to quantify inflammation, crypt damage, and tissue involvement.

The colitis scoring system provided quantitative assessment of disease severity. Scores incorporated inflammation severity (0-3), extent (0-3), crypt damage (0-4), and percentage involvement (0-4), yielding a composite score ranging from 0-14. Ten high-power fields were evaluated per sample and averaged.

Quantitative PCR analysis measured cytokine gene expression in colon tissues. RNA was extracted from proximal and distal colon segments, reverse transcribed, and analyzed for IL-6 and IL-1β mRNA levels using SYBR green chemistry. Expression was normalized to the housekeeping gene HPRT.

Statistical analysis employed appropriate parametric and non-parametric tests depending on data distribution. Group comparisons used Student's t-test or one-way ANOVA with post-hoc testing. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, with p<0.05 considered statistically significant.
