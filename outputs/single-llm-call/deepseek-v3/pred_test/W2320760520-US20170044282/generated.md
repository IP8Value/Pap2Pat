# DESCRIPTION  

## BACKGROUND TO THE INVENTION  

The global demand for sustainable materials has intensified the need for renewable polymers derived from non-petrochemical feedstocks. Conventional synthetic polymers rely heavily on fossil fuel resources, contributing significantly to carbon emissions and energy consumption. Current renewable polymers occupy less than 5% of the market due to perceived cost inefficiencies and performance limitations compared to petroleum-based counterparts. A critical gap exists in developing commercially viable bio-derived polymers that match or exceed the properties of synthetic alternatives while maintaining cost competitiveness.  

Terpenes and terpenoids represent a promising class of biorenewable feedstocks due to their natural abundance and structural diversity. These compounds, derived from citrus and wood waste, are available on multi-tonne scales and possess alkene functionalities that suggest potential for polymerization. However, prior attempts to polymerize terpenes directly have encountered substantial limitations, including low molecular weights, poor incorporation rates, and the need for extreme reaction conditions (e.g., -80°C for cationic polymerization). Existing methods often require complex catalysts or are restricted to terpenes with pre-existing functional groups (e.g., -COOH or -OH), excluding the majority of commercially available terpenes.  

A persistent challenge in the field is the lack of control over molecular weight and polymer architecture when using terpene-based monomers. Previous approaches have failed to provide a scalable, economically feasible route to high-performance bio-derived polymers. There remains an unmet need for a versatile polymerization methodology that can transform abundant terpenes and terpenoids into functionalized monomers with tunable physical properties suitable for industrial applications.  

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention discloses a novel method for producing bio-derived polymers from terpenes and terpenoids through controlled functionalization and polymerization. The invention provides a systematic approach to modifying terpene structures with acrylate or methacrylate groups, enabling facile polymerization via free radical or controlled radical mechanisms. Key advantages include precise control over molecular weight, polymer architecture, and glass transition temperatures (Tg), allowing customization of material properties for specific end uses.  

In a first aspect, the invention describes a method for producing functionalized monomers comprising: (a) modifying terpenes or terpenoids to introduce hydroxyl groups via hydroboration-oxidation or carbonyl reduction, and (b) esterifying the hydroxylated intermediates with acryloyl or methacryloyl chloride. An alternative first aspect utilizes a greener route employing carboxylic acids (acrylic or methacrylic acid) with coupling agents like propyl phosphonic anhydride (T3P®) to form esters.  

A second aspect introduces a catalytic method for directly introducing vinyl groups at allylic positions using palladium acetate and benzoquinone, enabling one-pot monomer synthesis. The third aspect encompasses the functionalized monomers themselves, characterized by terpene backbones with pendant acrylate/methacrylate groups. A fourth aspect details polymerization methods using these monomers, including controlled radical techniques with terpene-based chain transfer agents. The fifth aspect covers the resulting bio-derived polymers exhibiting Tg values ranging from 12°C to 142°C, while the sixth aspect describes articles manufactured from these polymers, such as high-temperature resistant components and powder coatings.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION  

Terpenes and terpenoids serve as the foundational starting materials, defined as C10H16 compounds with open or closed ring structures. Representative examples include α-pinene, β-pinene, limonene, and carvone, each containing at least one reactive alkene moiety. Hydroxylation proceeds via hydroboration-oxidation using borane complexes in tetrahydrofuran at 0-25°C, followed by oxidation with hydrogen peroxide and sodium hydroxide. Alternatively, carbonyl-containing terpenoids like carvone undergo reduction to alcohols using lithium aluminium hydride in diethyl ether at -78°C to 25°C.  

Esterification with acryloyl/methacryloyl chloride occurs in dichloromethane with triethylamine at 0-25°C, while the greener route employs T3P® (50% in ethyl acetate) with acrylic/methacrylic acid in toluene at 80-110°C. The catalytic vinylation method utilizes 2-10 mol% palladium acetate, 1-2 equivalents benzoquinone, and methacrylic acid as both solvent and reactant at 50-80°C under air, yielding regioisomeric monomers in ratios up to 10:1.  

Polymerization techniques include conventional free radical polymerization using azobisisobutyronitrile (AIBN) at 60-110°C, with molecular weight control achieved through dodecanethiol (0.5-5 wt%) or terpene-based chain transfer agents. The resulting polymers demonstrate structure-property relationships where methacrylate derivatives exhibit higher Tg values (85-142°C) than acrylates (12-71°C) due to increased backbone sterics. Notably, poly(β-pinene methacrylate) achieves a Tg of 142°C, rivaling engineering plastics like polycarbonates.  

Crosslinkable systems derive from monomers retaining additional alkene functionalities (e.g., carvone methacrylate), enabling UV-curable coatings with pencil hardness of 2B and König hardness of 185 seconds. The polymers find application in powder coatings, reusable surgical instruments, and high-temperature containers, formed via extrusion, injection molding, or solvent casting.  

### Terpinolene and Gamma-Terpinene as Chain Transfer Agents  

Terpenes such as terpinolene and γ-terpinene function effectively as chain transfer agents (CTAs) in controlled polymerizations. Experimental data demonstrate molecular weight reduction proportional to CTA concentration, with terpinolene showing superior control due to its higher chain transfer constant. Optimal conditions employ 5-10 wt% CTA relative to monomer at 70°C in toluene, yielding polymers with dispersities (Đ) below 1.5.  

## EXAMPLES  

### Example 1  

**General Method 1a (Hydroboration-Oxidation):** A solution of β-pinene (10 g, 73.5 mmol) in THF (50 mL) was treated with borane-THF complex (1M, 80 mL) at 0°C under nitrogen. After stirring for 4 h at 25°C, NaOH (4M, 40 mL) and H2O2 (30%, 40 mL) were added dropwise. The mixture was stirred for 12 h, extracted with ethyl acetate, and purified by silica gel chromatography to yield (1R,2S,5R)-2-hydroxy-6,6-dimethylbicyclo[3.1.1]heptane (8.2 g, 72%).  

**General Method 2 (Esterification):** The above alcohol (5 g, 32.8 mmol) was dissolved in CH2Cl2 (50 mL) with triethylamine (4.6 mL, 33 mmol). Methacryloyl chloride (3.4 mL, 35 mmol) was added dropwise at 0°C. After 12 h at 25°C, the solution was washed with brine, dried (MgSO4), and concentrated to give (1R,2S,5R)-2-methacryloyloxy-6,6-dimethylbicyclo[3.1.1]heptane (6.1 g, 85%).  

**One-Pot Catalytic Method:** β-Pinene (10 g), methacrylic acid (8.6 g), Pd(OAc)2 (0.22 g, 2 mol%), and benzoquinone (4.3 g, 2 eq) were stirred in air at 50°C for 24 h. The mixture was filtered through silica, yielding regioisomeric methacrylates (12.1 g, 82% combined yield).  

### Example 2  

**Polymerization:** β-Pinene methacrylate (5 g, 22.8 mmol), AIBN (0.037 g, 1 mol%), and terpinolene (0.5 g, 10 wt%) in toluene (10 mL) were heated at 70°C under nitrogen for 6 h. The polymer was precipitated into methanol, yielding poly(β-pinene methacrylate) (3.8 g, 76%) with Mn = 12,400 g/mol and Tg = 138°C by DSC.  

## CONCLUSIONS  

The invention provides a comprehensive platform for converting terpenes into high-performance polymers through modular functionalization and controlled polymerization. The methods enable tunable thermal and mechanical properties, bridging the gap between renewable feedstocks and industrial material requirements.
