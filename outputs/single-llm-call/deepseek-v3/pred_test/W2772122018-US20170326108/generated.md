# DESCRIPTION  

## BACKGROUND  

Prion diseases, also known as transmissible spongiform encephalopathies (TSEs), represent a group of fatal neurodegenerative disorders affecting both humans and animals. These diseases are characterized by the conformational conversion of the normal cellular prion protein (PrPC) into an abnormal, pathogenic isoform (PrPSc). The accumulation of PrPSc in the central nervous system leads to progressive neuronal loss, spongiform degeneration, and ultimately death.  

Human prion diseases include Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD), Gerstmann-Sträussler-Scheinker syndrome (GSS), and fatal familial insomnia (FFI). Animal prion diseases encompass scrapie in sheep and goats, bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE) in cattle, and chronic wasting disease (CWD) in cervids. The infectious nature of prions, coupled with their remarkable resistance to conventional sterilization methods, poses significant challenges for disease management and prevention.  

Prion propagation occurs through a template-directed process whereby PrPSc induces the conformational conversion of PrPC into additional PrPSc molecules. This self-perpetuating mechanism underlies the progressive nature of prion diseases and their transmissibility. Current understanding suggests that prion diseases can manifest sporadically, genetically, or through infectious transmission, with sporadic CJD being the most common form in humans.  

The transmission of human prion diseases has been documented through various routes, including iatrogenic transmission via contaminated surgical instruments, growth hormone preparations, and dura mater grafts. Additionally, variant CJD (vCJD) has been linked to consumption of BSE-contaminated meat products, highlighting the potential for cross-species transmission. These transmission pathways underscore the urgent need for effective therapeutic interventions.  

Experimental anti-prion drugs developed to date have shown limited efficacy in clinical settings. Compounds such as pentosan polysulfate, quinacrine, and doxycycline have demonstrated anti-prion activity in vitro and in animal models, but their translation to human therapy has been disappointing. The blood-brain barrier presents a significant obstacle to drug delivery, and many compounds fail to achieve therapeutic concentrations in the central nervous system without causing unacceptable toxicity.  

Cell culture models have proven invaluable for screening potential anti-prion compounds. Persistently prion-infected cell lines, such as ScN2a (murine neuroblastoma cells infected with 22L prions), ScCAD5 (murine catecholaminergic neuronal cells), and ScMEF (prion-infected mouse embryonic fibroblasts), provide robust systems for evaluating drug efficacy. These models allow for rapid assessment of PrPSc clearance and can help identify compounds with potential therapeutic value.  

Autophagy, the cellular process of degrading and recycling cytoplasmic components, has emerged as a promising anti-prion strategy. Induction of autophagy has been shown to enhance the clearance of PrPSc through lysosomal degradation. Various autophagy inducers, including rapamycin, lithium, and trehalose, have demonstrated anti-prion effects in experimental models. However, the development of clinically viable autophagy-inducing drugs remains an unmet need in prion disease therapeutics.  

AR-12 (OSU-03012) is a celecoxib derivative that lacks cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) inhibitory activity. Originally developed as an anti-cancer agent, AR-12 has shown activity against various infectious agents, including bacteria, fungi, and viruses. The compound is known to cross the blood-brain barrier effectively and has completed Phase I clinical trials for cancer treatment. AR-12's mechanism of action involves downregulation of host cell chaperones and induction of autophagy through upregulation of Atg13 and PERK. These properties make AR-12 a compelling candidate for prion disease therapy.  

## SUMMARY  

The present invention provides methods for reducing prion levels through administration of AR-12 or its analog AR-14. These compounds demonstrate potent anti-prion activity across multiple prion-infected cell models, including neuronal and non-neuronal cell lines. Treatment with AR-12 or AR-14 results in significant reduction of PrPSc levels, as measured by immunoblot analysis and real-time quaking-induced conversion (RT-QuIC) assays.  

The methods involve administering AR-12 at concentrations ranging from 0.5 μM to 5 μM, with an effective concentration (EC50) of approximately 1.5 μM in ScN2a cells, 4 μM in ScCAD5 cells, and 2 μM in ScMEF cells. AR-12 treatment induces autophagy, as evidenced by increased LC3-II levels, and this autophagy induction contributes to the observed anti-prion effects. Remarkably, prolonged treatment with AR-12 (3 μM) for 20 days completely clears prion infection from ScN2a cells, with no reappearance of prion seeding activity even after discontinuation of treatment.  

Similarly, AR-14 demonstrates potent anti-prion activity at concentrations ranging from 0.1 μM to 2 μM, with an EC50 of approximately 1.5 μM in ScN2a cells. Like AR-12, AR-14 induces autophagy and shows reduced efficacy in autophagy-deficient cells. Long-term treatment with AR-14 (2 μM) completely eradicates prion infection in ScMEF cells, with sustained clearance observed after treatment cessation.  

The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions containing AR-12 or AR-14, methods of administration (including oral, parenteral, and intrathecal routes), and combination therapies with other anti-prion agents. The compounds' ability to cross the blood-brain barrier, coupled with their established safety profile in human trials, positions them as promising therapeutic candidates for prion diseases and related protein misfolding disorders.
