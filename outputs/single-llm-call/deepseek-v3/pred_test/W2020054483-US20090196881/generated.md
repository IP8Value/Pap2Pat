## INTRODUCTION  

Spring viremia of carp virus (SVCV) is a rhabdoviral pathogen that causes significant mortality in common carp (Cyprinus carpio carpio) populations throughout Europe. The virus has been reported in multiple European countries including Russia, Romania, Netherlands, Moldavia, Georgia, Germany, France, United Kingdom, and Denmark. SVCV outbreaks typically occur during spring months when water temperatures begin rising after cold winter periods, with highest mortality rates observed between 11-17°C.  

Infected fish exhibit severe pathological symptoms including inflammation, hemorrhage, and swelling of the spleen, kidney, intestines, and air bladder. Disease progression leads to necrosis of internal organs and eventual death. While common carp are the principal host species, natural SVCV infections have been documented in numerous other cyprinid fish including koi (Cyprinus carpio koi), goldfish (Carassius auratus), crucian carp (Carassius carassius), silver carp (Hypopthalmichthys molitirix), bighead carp (Aristichthys nobilis), grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella), orfe (Leucisucus idus), and tench (Tinca tinca). Experimental infections have also been achieved in additional fish species from various families.  

Due to its highly infectious nature and potential impact on global fish populations, SVCV detection requires mandatory notification to the Office International des Epizooties (OIE) within 48 hours. SVCV is one of only nine piscine viruses recognized by OIE as a notifiable animal disease. The first North American outbreaks occurred in 2002 at a major koi production facility, resulting in 15,000 deaths and euthanasia of 135,000 additional fish. Subsequent outbreaks were reported in Wisconsin, Illinois, Washington State, Missouri, and Canada, prompting USDA import restrictions on susceptible fish species.  

Current SVCV control measures rely on eradication of infected fish and hygiene protocols, as no commercially available vaccine exists. Inactivated SVCV vaccines using European strains have demonstrated limited protection but face multiple limitations including incomplete virus inactivation risks, regulatory hurdles, high production costs, and insufficient protection quantification.  

DNA vaccines represent an attractive alternative to traditional vaccine approaches due to their straightforward design, heat stability, low production costs, and long-term storage capability. Additional advantages include no risk of pathogen reversion and minimal chemical impurities. While regulatory approval, promoter selection, and delivery technologies remain challenges, research on fish DNA vaccines has expanded significantly in recent years.  

Previous SVCV DNA vaccines targeting European isolates have shown lower efficacy compared to DNA vaccines against related fish rhabdoviruses - infectious hematopoietic necrosis virus (IHNV) and viral hemorrhagic septicemia virus (VHSV). These three viruses belong to the rhabdovirus family but are classified into different genera. Phylogenetic analysis of SVCV isolates has identified four genogroups, with all North American isolates clustering in the Ia genogroup suggesting Asian origin.  

The glycoprotein (G) gene encodes the primary antigen triggering host immune responses and serves as the target for DNA vaccine development. While novirhabdovirus DNA vaccines (IHNV and VHSV) have demonstrated high protection levels in salmonids, previous attempts to develop an equally effective SVCV DNA vaccine have been unsuccessful. Early reports of European genogroup SVCV DNA vaccines showed limited efficacy, with relative percent survival (RPS) values ranging from -11% to 48% in single trials.  

The emergence of SVCV in North America has created an urgent need for effective prevention strategies. The present invention addresses this need through development of a novel SVCV DNA vaccine utilizing the G-gene from a North American isolate (SVCVnc).  

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention provides a novel DNA vaccine construct (pSGnc) for protection against Spring viremia of carp virus. The vaccine comprises nucleic acid encoding the G-gene of SVCV North Carolina isolate (SVCVnc) cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector. The invention further includes methods for protecting susceptible fish species against SVCV infection through administration of the DNA vaccine.  

A reliable challenge model was developed to evaluate vaccine efficacy, comparing susceptibility of goldfish and koi to SVCVnc through injection and immersion exposure. Koi demonstrated significantly higher susceptibility, with 100% mortality following injection challenge and 75% mortality after immersion exposure, establishing them as an appropriate model species.  

Four independent trial studies demonstrated the pSGnc vaccine consistently provided significant protection against homologous SVCVnc challenge in immunized koi. Relative percent survival values ranged from 50-88% across trials conducted under varying conditions of fish age, virus dose, and seasonal timing. In all trials, pSGnc-vaccinated fish showed significantly lower mortality compared to both non-vaccinated controls and fish receiving the mock luciferase vaccine (pLuc).  

Notably, the majority of survivors from pSGnc-vaccinated groups showed complete virus clearance, while all survivors from control groups remained virus-positive. This represents the first successful demonstration of a SVCV DNA vaccine in koi and the most efficacious SVCV DNA vaccine reported to date.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

The following terms are defined for purposes of the present invention:  

An "epitope" refers to the antigenic determinant recognized by the immune system, typically a specific portion of a macromolecule that binds to an antibody or T-cell receptor. Epitopes may be "linear epitopes" comprising continuous amino acid sequences or "conformational epitopes" formed by three-dimensional folding of discontinuous sequences.  

A "solid phase" refers to any solid support material used in immunoassays, including but not limited to microtiter plates, membranes, beads, and dipsticks. "Biological samples" encompass any material derived from living organisms, while "biological fluids" specifically refer to liquid biological materials such as blood, serum, plasma, mucus, or coelomic fluid.  

"Immunologically reactive" describes the specific binding interaction between an antigen and its corresponding antibody. An "immune complex" forms when antibodies bind to their target antigens. "Purified protein" refers to protein preparations with reduced contaminants compared to their natural state.  

"Isolated G protein" indicates SVCV glycoprotein separated from viral components. "Essentially purified proteins" are preparations where the desired protein constitutes at least 90% of total protein content. "Recombinantly expressed" proteins are produced through genetic engineering techniques rather than natural viral replication.  

Host cells for recombinant expression include "lower eukaryotes" such as yeast, "prokaryotes" including various bacterial species, and "higher eukaryotes" such as mammalian cell lines. A "polypeptide" is any chain of amino acids, while "recombinant polynucleotide or nucleic acid" refers to artificially constructed DNA or RNA molecules.  

"Recombinant host cells" are cells containing exogenous genetic material. A "replicon" is a genetic element capable of independent replication, while a "vector" is a DNA molecule used to transport genetic material into host cells. "Control sequences" regulate gene expression and include promoters, enhancers, and terminators.  

A "promoter" is a DNA sequence that initiates transcription of a downstream gene. "Operably linked" describes the functional connection between a promoter and coding sequence enabling proper expression. An "open reading frame" is a nucleotide sequence that could potentially encode a protein, while a "coding sequence" specifically refers to the protein-coding portion of a gene.  

"Immunogenic" describes the ability to provoke an immune response, and "therapeutic" refers to treatment of existing conditions. An "effective amount" is the quantity required to achieve a desired biological effect.  

The DNA vaccine of the present invention comprises isolated DNA encoding the SVCV G protein antigen, operably linked to appropriate expression control sequences in a suitable vector. Various delivery methods may be employed including intramuscular, intraperitoneal, or immersion administration. Vector selection may include plasmid DNA, viral vectors, or other nucleic acid delivery systems.  

Host cells transformed with the vaccine construct may be used for protein production or as live vector vaccines. Transformation methods include electroporation, chemical transformation, viral transduction, or particle bombardment. Expression control sequences may include viral, bacterial, or eukaryotic promoters selected for optimal expression in the target species.  

Vaccine formulations may incorporate pharmacologically acceptable carriers such as saline solutions, buffers, or stabilizers. Adjuvants may be included to enhance immune responses. Administration methods include injection (intramuscular, intraperitoneal), immersion, or oral delivery. Effective immunizing amounts typically range from 0.1-10 μg per dose, with vaccination schedules including single or multiple administrations.  

The invention further provides compositions for in vitro SVCV detection, comprising the recombinant G protein or immunogenic fragments thereof. Diagnostic kits may include solid supports coated with G protein, detection antibodies, and necessary reagents for immunoassay performance.  

Immunoassay formats may include heterogeneous assays requiring separation steps (e.g., ELISA) or homogeneous assays performed in solution. Standard formats directly measure antigen-antibody binding, while competitive formats measure inhibition of known interactions. Complex detection techniques include immunoprecipitation, Western blotting, or lateral flow assays.  

For therapeutic and prophylactic applications, the invention provides methods for active immunization through vaccine administration. Vaccine preparation involves cloning the SVCV G gene into an appropriate expression vector, transforming host cells for production, and purifying the DNA vaccine construct.  

The materials and methods for vaccine development include maintenance of specific pathogen-free fish stocks, virus propagation in permissive cell lines, and establishment of reliable challenge models. Susceptibility testing compares different fish species and challenge methods, while cold stress protocols evaluate temperature effects on infection outcomes.  

Vaccine construction involves sequencing the SVCVnc G gene, designing specific cloning primers, and directional cloning into expression vectors. Vaccine trials assess protection levels through controlled challenges, mortality monitoring, and virus detection in survivors. Statistical analysis includes calculation of cumulative percent mortality, mean day to death, and relative percent survival.  

### Susceptibility Testing of Goldfish and Koi  

Comparative susceptibility testing revealed significant differences between goldfish and koi responses to SVCVnc challenge. Goldfish demonstrated remarkable resistance, with 96% survival across both injection and immersion challenge experiments. In contrast, koi showed 100% mortality following injection challenge and 75% mortality after immersion exposure, establishing their suitability as a model species for vaccine development.  

### Example 2  

Cold stress challenge experiments compared two temperature acclimation protocols in koi prior to SVCVnc exposure. The rapid cold stressor, involving immediate transfer from 16-18°C to 10-12°C, produced equivalent mortality outcomes (75% CPM immersion, 100% CPM injection) to the slow 2-week acclimation protocol. This validated the more time-efficient rapid cold stressor for incorporation into the challenge model.  

### Example 3  

Four independent vaccine trials demonstrated consistent protection by the pSGnc DNA vaccine. Mock-challenged controls showed 0-10% mortality, while non-vaccinated fish suffered 70-100% mortality across trials. The pLuc mock vaccine provided no protection (85-100% mortality), whereas pSGnc-vaccinated groups exhibited significantly lower mortality (10-50%).  

Relative percent survival values for pSGnc ranged from 50-88%, with virus clearance observed in most survivors. In contrast, all survivors from control groups remained virus-positive. These results represent the first successful demonstration of a protective SVCV DNA vaccine in koi and the highest efficacy reported for any SVCV DNA construct to date.  

The pSGnc vaccine's performance compares favorably with European SVCV DNA constructs and approaches the efficacy levels seen with novirhabdovirus DNA vaccines. The consistent protection across multiple trials under varying conditions demonstrates the vaccine's reliability and potential for practical application in SVCV control programs.
