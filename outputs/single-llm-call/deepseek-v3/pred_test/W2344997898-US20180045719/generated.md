## DESCRIPTION  

### TECHNICAL FIELD  

The present invention relates generally to the field of virology and protein biochemistry, with specific applications in the detection and analysis of protein-protein interactions. More particularly, the invention concerns virus-like particles (VLPs) and their use as tools for trapping and identifying molecular interactions within biological systems. The disclosed methods and compositions provide novel approaches for studying protein complexes under native conditions without requiring cell lysis or homogenization.  

### BACKGROUND  

Molecular interactions form the basis of nearly all biological processes, yet their comprehensive detection and characterization remains challenging. Protein-protein interactions (PPIs) govern cellular signaling pathways, metabolic regulation, and disease mechanisms, making their study crucial for both basic research and therapeutic development.  

Traditional methods for detecting PPIs suffer from significant limitations. The yeast two-hybrid system, while widely used, is restricted to yeast cells and often produces false positives due to artificial nuclear localization of bait and prey proteins. Phage display techniques are limited to extracellular or secreted proteins and cannot capture interactions occurring in their native cellular context.  

Genetic interaction traps such as the MAPPIT (Mammalian Protein-Protein Interaction Trap) system provide mammalian cell-based alternatives but rely on artificial signaling pathways that may not reflect natural binding affinities or stoichiometries. These systems also require extensive genetic engineering of both bait and prey proteins, limiting their applicability to certain classes of interactions.  

Biochemical strategies for studying protein complexes, particularly affinity purification coupled with mass spectrometry (AP-MS), have become standard tools in proteomics. However, these methods require cell lysis which disrupts native cellular environments and often leads to loss of weakly associated complex members. The lysis conditions themselves - including detergent type, salt concentration, and pH - can dramatically affect which interactions are preserved. Co-purification strategies face similar challenges, as the harsh conditions needed to solubilize membrane proteins may disrupt their interaction networks.  

Classical approaches to identify target proteins typically involve tagging a bait protein, expressing it in cells, lysing the cells under optimized conditions, and purifying associated proteins. This process requires extensive optimization for each bait protein and often fails to capture transient or weak interactions that are critical for understanding biological systems. The limitations of current technologies highlight the need for new methods that can preserve native protein complexes without requiring cell disruption.  

### BRIEF SUMMARY  

The present invention addresses these limitations by providing a novel method derived from Virotrap technology for trapping protein complexes within virus-like particles (VLPs). This approach eliminates the need for cell lysis by packaging protein complexes into secreted VLPs that protect the interactions during purification. The method involves expressing a fusion protein comprising a VLP-forming polypeptide linked to a bait protein of interest. As the VLPs assemble and bud from the cell membrane, they incorporate not only the bait protein but also any interacting prey proteins, effectively trapping the native complex within the protective VLP structure.  

Key advantages of this invention include preservation of protein complexes under native conditions, protection of interactions during purification, and compatibility with both cytoplasmic and membrane-associated proteins. The method can detect weak interactions that would be lost during traditional purification procedures and can be adapted for studying dynamic interactions in response to cellular stimuli.  

The invention further provides artificial VLPs engineered for specific applications, including small molecule target identification. By incorporating small molecule binding domains into the VLP-forming scaffold, the system can trap proteins that interact with drugs or other bioactive compounds, facilitating identification of both intended targets and off-target effects. These artificial VLPs represent a significant advance in chemical proteomics and drug discovery.

#### Definitions  

For purposes of interpreting this patent application, the following terms shall have the meanings specified unless context clearly indicates otherwise. The examples and drawings provided herein are intended to be non-limiting illustrations of the invention's embodiments.  

The term "comprising" is to be interpreted as open-ended, meaning that the recited elements or steps do not exclude additional, unrecited elements or steps. The articles "a" and "an" are used herein to refer to one or to more than one (i.e., to at least one) of the grammatical object of the article.  

Ordinal terms such as "first," "second," etc. are used solely to distinguish similar elements and do not imply any particular order or ranking unless explicitly stated.  

As used herein, a "virus-like particle" or "VLP" refers to a non-infectious, self-assembling structure that mimics the organization of a native virus but lacks viral genetic material. VLPs typically comprise one or more viral structural proteins capable of spontaneous assembly into particulate structures.  

A "VLP-forming polypeptide" refers to any polypeptide capable of directing the formation of VLPs when expressed in appropriate host cells. In preferred embodiments, the VLP-forming polypeptide is derived from viral GAG proteins, particularly HIV-1 GAG.  

A "fusion protein" refers to a chimeric polypeptide created by joining two or more protein coding sequences, typically via a peptide linker. In the context of this invention, fusion proteins generally comprise a VLP-forming polypeptide linked to a bait protein or recruiting element.  

The term "polypeptide" encompasses proteins, protein fragments, and synthetic peptide sequences of any length, including modified or derivatized forms.  

A "fusion construct" refers to a nucleic acid molecule encoding a fusion protein as described above, typically contained within an expression vector suitable for transfection into host cells.  

A "recruiting element" refers to any molecule capable of specifically binding to a target protein or small molecule. Recruiting elements may include protein domains, antibodies, aptamers, or synthetic binding scaffolds.  

The term "bait" refers to the protein or molecule of interest that serves as the starting point for interaction studies. In various embodiments, the bait may be a full-length protein, protein domain, peptide, or small molecule.  

A "small molecule" refers to organic compounds typically under 1000 Daltons in molecular weight, including drugs, metabolites, and other bioactive compounds.  

The phrase "interacts with" indicates any specific molecular association between two molecules, including but not limited to covalent bonding, non-covalent binding, and transient associations.  

"Recruited to" refers to the process by which a prey protein becomes associated with a VLP through interaction with the bait protein or recruiting element.  

VLPs according to the invention may be derived from various viral sources, with HIV-1 GAG being particularly preferred due to its well-characterized assembly properties. The VLP-forming polypeptide may be modified to optimize particle formation, bait presentation, or interaction trapping efficiency. Such modifications include but are not limited to truncations, point mutations, and insertions of flexible linkers.  

Fusion protein embodiments may include various configurations of VLP-forming polypeptides linked to bait proteins or recruiting elements. The orientation (N-terminal or C-terminal fusion) and linker sequences may be varied to optimize both VLP formation and bait functionality.  

VLP-forming polypeptide embodiments encompass not only wild-type viral structural proteins but also engineered variants with altered assembly properties, modified surface characteristics, or enhanced stability.  

Small molecule embodiments include drugs, drug candidates, metabolites, and other bioactive compounds that may be linked to recruiting elements for target identification studies.  

Recruiting element embodiments include various protein domains known to bind small molecules (e.g., dihydrofolate reductase for methotrexate), antibody fragments, and engineered binding scaffolds.  

Bait embodiments cover a wide range of proteins and protein domains involved in signaling pathways, metabolic processes, and disease mechanisms. The system is particularly suited for studying difficult-to-express membrane proteins and large multi-protein complexes.  

VLP composition may vary depending on the specific application but typically includes the VLP-forming polypeptide, bait protein, any recruited prey proteins, and associated cellular components trapped during particle formation.  

Fusion construct composition generally includes regulatory elements for expression in mammalian cells (e.g., promoters, enhancers), sequences encoding the fusion protein, and selection markers.  

VLP-forming polypeptide interactions with cellular components drive particle assembly and budding while simultaneously presenting the bait protein in its native conformation for interaction studies.  

Recruiting element interactions with small molecules or other targets provide the specificity needed for trapping particular protein complexes of interest.  

Bait interactions with prey proteins occur under native cellular conditions before being trapped within VLPs, preserving interaction stoichiometries and post-translational modifications.  

The VLP entrapped complex represents a snapshot of the protein interaction network surrounding the bait protein at the time of particle formation, protected from degradation or dissociation during subsequent purification steps.  

VLP formation proceeds through normal viral assembly pathways, with the bait protein and its interaction partners becoming incorporated into budding particles. This process occurs continuously, allowing capture of dynamic interactions over time.  

VLP composition may be varied by altering the ratio of VLP-forming polypeptide to bait protein, co-expressing wild-type VLP-forming polypeptide to reduce bait density, or including additional viral envelope proteins to modify particle properties.  

Prey protein embodiments include any cellular proteins that interact with the bait protein, whether directly or as part of larger complexes. The system is capable of trapping both strong and weak interactions that might be missed by traditional methods.  

VLP-forming polypeptide interaction variations allow tuning of the system for different applications, such as emphasizing capture of weak interactions through increased bait presentation or favoring specific subcomplexes through controlled expression levels.  

VLP entrapped complex variations reflect the diversity of protein interaction networks that can be captured, from simple binary interactions to large multi-protein assemblies.  

VLP detection methods include standard techniques such as western blotting, mass spectrometry, and immunoassays, facilitated by the particulate nature of VLPs which simplifies purification and concentration.  

VLP analysis methods encompass both targeted approaches (e.g., probing for specific known interactors) and discovery approaches (e.g., proteomic analysis of all trapped proteins).  

VLP purification methods benefit from the particulate nature of the complexes, allowing efficient recovery by techniques such as ultracentrifugation, affinity capture, or size-exclusion chromatography.  

VLP enrichment methods may employ affinity tags on either the VLP-forming polypeptide or bait protein to facilitate specific isolation from complex biological mixtures.  

VLP isolation methods can be adapted to various scales, from small-scale experiments suitable for validation studies to large-scale preparations for comprehensive proteomic analyses.  

VLP analysis variations include different strategies for identifying and quantifying trapped proteins, from antibody-based detection to advanced mass spectrometry techniques.  

#### EXAMPLES  

##### Plasmid Generation  

Expression plasmids for the Virotrap system were constructed using standard molecular biology techniques. The p55 GAG coding sequence was amplified from the pCMV-dR8.74 packaging construct and inserted into a mammalian expression vector containing a strong promoter (SRalpha). Gateway recombination sites were incorporated to allow efficient transfer of bait protein coding sequences. Various epitope tags (MYC, FLAG, VSV) were included to facilitate detection and purification. Control constructs expressing untagged GAG or fluorescent protein fusions were generated for system validation.  

##### VLP Production  

HEK293T cells were transfected with the GAG-bait fusion constructs along with supporting plasmids encoding viral envelope proteins (VSV-G). Following transfection, cells were maintained in standard culture conditions allowing continuous production and secretion of VLPs. Supernatants were harvested at defined time points, cleared of cellular debris by low-speed centrifugation, and filtered through 0.45 μm membranes to remove residual particulates.  

##### VLP Analysis  

VLPs were concentrated and purified using either ultracentrifugation or affinity capture methods. For affinity purification, VLPs displaying VSV-G were captured using anti-FLAG antibody-coated magnetic beads. Captured particles were eluted under mild conditions to preserve protein complexes and analyzed by western blotting or mass spectrometry.  

##### Mass Spectrometry Analysis  

Trapped protein complexes were digested with trypsin and analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) using a high-resolution Q Exactive instrument. Data were searched against human protein databases using MASCOT algorithms, with false discovery rates controlled at <1%. Spectral counting was used to quantify relative protein abundances across samples.  

##### VLP Trapping of Simvastatin Binders  

To demonstrate small molecule target identification, the system was adapted to trap proteins binding to simvastatin. A bivalent molecule was created by linking methotrexate (MTX) to simvastatin via a polyethylene glycol spacer. Cells expressing GAG fused to Escherichia coli dihydrofolate reductase (eDHFR) were treated with this compound, allowing MTX to bind eDHFR while presenting simvastatin to cellular targets. VLPs produced under these conditions were analyzed by mass spectrometry, successfully identifying HMG-CoA reductase as the known simvastatin target along with potential novel interactors.  

##### Results of VLP Trapping Experiment  

The simvastatin trapping experiment specifically enriched HMG-CoA reductase (HMGCR) in the VLPs, with spectral counts significantly higher than control samples. Additional proteins involved in cholesterol biosynthesis were also detected, suggesting capture of functional protein complexes rather than isolated targets. Control experiments using DMSO alone showed minimal background binding, demonstrating the specificity of the trapping system.  

#### Example 1  

##### VLP Trapping of Simvastatin Binders  

This example details the application of Virotrap technology to identify protein targets of the cholesterol-lowering drug simvastatin. HEK293T cells were transfected with a plasmid encoding HIV-1 GAG fused to eDHFR along with plasmids for VSV-G expression. Cells were treated with a bivalent MTX-simvastatin conjugate (1 μM final concentration) for 24 hours post-transfection. VLPs were harvested from conditioned media by affinity capture using anti-FLAG magnetic beads.  

##### Results of VLP Trapping Experiment  

Mass spectrometry analysis of trapped proteins identified HMGCR as the predominant simvastatin-binding protein, with 78 unique peptides detected across replicate experiments. The known simvastatin target showed >50-fold enrichment compared to DMSO-treated controls. Secondary hits included other enzymes in the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway, suggesting the system can capture not only primary drug targets but also associated metabolic complexes.  

##### Protein Identification Methods  

Proteins were identified by LC-MS/MS analysis following tryptic digestion of VLP contents. Database searches were performed against the human proteome supplemented with common contaminant proteins. Identification criteria required at least two unique peptides per protein at 99% confidence. Spectral counts were normalized to total protein content and compared across experimental conditions.  

##### Results of Protein Identification  

The experiment identified 42 proteins significantly enriched in simvastatin-treated samples compared to controls (p<0.01, Fisher's exact test). Besides HMGCR, notable hits included squalene epoxidase (SQLE) and several proteins involved in lipid metabolism. Control experiments using unrelated small molecules showed distinct protein profiles, confirming the specificity of the trapping system.  

#### Example 2  

A viral trapping experiment was performed using TNFα-stimulated cells expressing GAG-A20 fusion protein. HEK293T cells were co-transfected with GAG-A20, VSV-G, and TNF receptor constructs, then treated with 300 IU/ml TNFα for 4 hours prior to VLP collection. Affinity-purified VLPs were analyzed by mass spectrometry, revealing enrichment of known TNF signaling components including RIPK1, TRADD, and TNFRSF1A along with TNF itself. This experiment demonstrated the system's ability to capture dynamic protein complexes formed in response to pathway activation.  

#### Example 3  

Another viral trapping experiment was conducted using cells expressing GAG-RNF41 fusion protein. Unstimulated HEK293T cells were transfected and VLPs collected after 24 hours. Mass spectrometry analysis identified multiple transmembrane proteins as RNF41 interactors, including insulin receptor (INSR) and insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF1R). These interactions were confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation, validating the system's ability to detect novel interactions involving membrane proteins that are challenging to study by traditional methods.  

#### Example 4  

##### MASPIT Assay Protocol  

To confirm interactions identified by Virotrap, selected hits were validated using the Mammalian Small Molecule Protein Interaction Trap (MASPIT) assay. HEK293T cells were co-transfected with: 1) a bait plasmid encoding eDHFR fused to a signaling domain, 2) a prey plasmid expressing the candidate interacting protein fused to a cytokine receptor fragment, and 3) a luciferase reporter plasmid responsive to receptor activation. Cells were treated with MTX-conjugated small molecules (1 μM) or DMSO control for 24 hours before measuring luciferase activity.  

##### Confirmation of Interaction Between Tamoxifen and HSD17B4  

The MASPIT assay confirmed a novel interaction between tamoxifen and hydroxysteroid (17-beta) dehydrogenase 4 (HSD17B4) that was initially detected by Virotrap. Cells expressing eDHFR bait and HSD17B4 prey showed significant luciferase induction (8-fold over background, p<0.001) when treated with MTX-tamoxifen but not with DMSO or unconjugated MTX. This orthogonal validation demonstrated the reliability of Virotrap for identifying small molecule-protein interactions and highlighted HSD17B4 as a potential secondary target of tamoxifen.
