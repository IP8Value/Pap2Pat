# DESCRIPTION

The present invention introduces a novel microfluidic device and system for precise manipulation of microdroplets within microchannels. The invention provides a robust platform for performing complex liquid handling operations without requiring precise external control of flow parameters.

Operations on microdroplets in channels are achieved through carefully designed geometric features that exploit interfacial tension effects. The system takes advantage of the large imbalance between interfacial tension forces and shear stresses characteristic of microfluidic environments. This allows precise control over droplet behavior based solely on channel geometry rather than active flow regulation.

Microdroplet-based microsystems offer significant advantages over conventional microfluidic approaches. The present invention enables passive control over droplet volumes, positions, and interactions without requiring external valves, pumps, or precise timing systems. This makes the technology particularly suitable for point-of-care diagnostics and field applications where simplicity and robustness are essential.

The use of microfluidic systems in chemistry has grown substantially due to their ability to minimize reagent volumes, increase reaction speeds, and enable high-throughput screening. However, most current systems require precise control over flow rates and timing, limiting their application in settings where such control is impractical. The present invention addresses this limitation by encoding liquid handling protocols into the device geometry itself.

Existing robotic stations for chemical reactions typically rely on complex mechanical systems to pipette and mix liquids. The present microfluidic approach replaces these bulky systems with compact chips that can perform equivalent operations autonomously. This is achieved through the generation of microdroplets on demand using passive geometric features rather than active valving systems.

Valve-based microdroplet generation has been demonstrated previously but requires external control systems. The present invention instead utilizes carefully designed channel geometries to control droplet formation and manipulation. Key to this approach is the understanding of channel geometry and Laplace pressure effects on droplet behavior. The relationship between channel dimensions and interfacial forces determines droplet movement and deformation within the system.

The operation of the metering trap begins with droplet entry into the main channel. As the droplet approaches the obstruction, the increasing Laplace pressure slows its movement.
