# DESCRIPTION  

## BACKGROUND  

### Technical Field  

The present invention relates generally to the field of metallurgy and materials science, specifically to improved low-carbon steel compositions and manufacturing methods. More particularly, the invention concerns novel steel materials exhibiting ultra-high surface hardness while maintaining desirable bulk properties through controlled microstructure formation during a single-step production process.  

### Description of the Related Art  

Low-carbon steels are widely utilized in industrial applications due to their favorable combination of ductility, toughness, and weldability. However, these materials typically exhibit insufficient hardness and strength for many demanding applications, particularly at the surface where wear resistance is crucial. Conventional approaches to enhance surface hardness include secondary processing techniques such as laser hardening, induction hardening, carburizing, and nitriding. These methods invariably increase production costs and complexity while potentially compromising other material properties.  

Existing low-carbon steels suffer from inherent limitations regarding their hardness capabilities due to their low carbon content and resulting microstructure characteristics. Traditional manufacturing methods typically produce steels with uniform properties throughout the cross-section, lacking the desired gradient in mechanical properties where a hard surface is needed over a tough core. There exists a significant unmet need in the art for low-carbon steel materials that achieve ultra-high surface hardness without requiring additional processing steps or compromising the beneficial bulk properties of low-carbon steels.  

## SUMMARY  

The present invention provides novel low-carbon steel materials and manufacturing methods that overcome the limitations of conventional approaches. The invention encompasses steel compositions and processing techniques that produce materials with exceptionally high surface hardness while maintaining desirable bulk properties. A key embodiment involves the controlled formation of specific microstructures through precise regulation of free oxygen content and cooling rates during production.  

The invention provides steel materials characterized by a unique property gradient, featuring an ultra-hard surface region transitioning to a tougher interior. This is achieved through a single-step Direct-Cast Hardening (DiCH) method that eliminates the need for subsequent heat treatment or surface modification processes. The resulting materials exhibit surface hardness values comparable to high-carbon steels while maintaining the beneficial characteristics of low-carbon steels in their bulk composition.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

The present invention introduces improved low-carbon steels and innovative production methods that enable the formation of optimized microstructures. A critical aspect involves the controlled formation of oxide inclusions within the steel matrix, where the size, distribution, and composition of these inclusions significantly influence the resulting material properties. The invention demonstrates that precise control of free oxygen content and cooling rate during solidification enables the formation of fine oxide inclusions that promote advantageous microstructure development.  

The effect of free oxygen content and cooling rate on oxide inclusion formation has been carefully characterized. Higher free oxygen content combined with rapid cooling promotes the formation of finer oxide inclusions, which serve as effective nucleation sites for acicular ferrite formation during subsequent phase transformations. The benefits of these fine oxide inclusions include enhanced microstructure refinement and improved mechanical properties throughout the material cross-section.  

For clarity in description, the following abbreviations are used to denote specific ferrite and martensite morphologies: AF for acicular ferrite, PF for polygonal ferrite, Q-PF for quasi-polygonal ferrite, and M for martensite. The role of fine inclusions in promoting acicular ferrite formation is particularly significant, as this microstructure provides superior mechanical properties compared to conventional polygonal ferrite structures.  

Variations in oxide inclusion composition have been shown to significantly influence microstructure development. Titanium-containing inclusions are particularly effective in promoting acicular ferrite nucleation due to their favorable lattice matching characteristics with the ferrite phase. The invention identifies a critical size range for heterogeneous nuclei of acicular ferrite, where inclusions between 0.2-0.5 microns demonstrate optimal nucleation efficiency.  

The invention encompasses several techniques for producing low-carbon steel with enhanced properties. A preferred embodiment involves the single-step Direct-Cast Hardening (DiCH) method, which integrates composition control and thermal management to achieve desired microstructures throughout the material cross-section. The benefits of controlling free oxygen content and cooling rate in this process include the formation of a property-gradient material with ultra-hard surface characteristics.  

The resulting ultra-hard, low-carbon steel exhibits significant differences between surface hardness and interior hardness, with surface values reaching up to 420 HV while maintaining conventional low-carbon steel properties in the bulk material. The technique involves precise heating and melting of precursor materials with controlled additions of alloying elements and oxygen-controlling agents.  

The steelmaking process involves carefully controlled solidification and cooling of molten steel to achieve the desired microstructure gradient. The invention includes specific techniques for making the steel structure, including the addition of FeO and other components to molten steel to regulate oxygen activity and inclusion formation. These process controls result in a steel structure featuring an ultra-hard surface region with gradually changing properties towards the interior.  

### EXAMPLES  

The invention has been demonstrated through production of ultra-hard low-carbon steel samples and comparison with ordinary hardness steel materials. Experimental work involved preparing samples with three different free oxygen levels while adding controlled amounts of manganese, silicon, and titanium to the molten steel. Two critical parameters - free oxygen content and cooling rate - were systematically varied to investigate their effects on microstructure and properties.  

The single-step DiCH method successfully produced property-gradient steel with excellent microstructure refinement throughout the cross-section. Analysis of oxide inclusions revealed their critical role in microstructure development, with higher cooling rates promoting the formation of submicron oxide inclusions that contributed to ultra-high hardness achievement.  

Four representative samples were prepared with varying process parameters to demonstrate the invention's principles. Cooling rates were carefully calculated for each sample, with average values ranging from 400-1000 K/s at different depths from the surface. Stress tests including Vickers hardness measurements were performed to characterize the mechanical properties of each sample.  

Detailed analysis of particle density variation (PDV) revealed the effects of free oxygen content and cooling rate on inclusion distribution. Samples S1 and S3 exhibited particular PDV characteristics, while S2 and S4 showed different oxide distribution patterns that influenced their final microstructures. Examination of the low-carbon steel samples' microstructures revealed the presence of Ti-containing oxide inclusions and their relationship to phase formation.  

Microstructural examination along sample cross-sections using SEM imaging revealed distinct zones at the surface, ¼ depth, and ½ depth positions. Various ferrite morphologies were identified including Acicular Ferrite (AF), Martensite-like ferrite (M), Polygonal Ferrite (PF), and Quasi-Polygonal Ferrite (Q-PF), with their distribution varying according to sample processing conditions.  

The coupling effect of initial free oxygen content and cooling rate was clearly demonstrated through systematic variations in microstructure. Analysis of nucleation behavior for PF, Q-PF, and AF revealed the critical role of processing parameters in phase formation. Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) analysis of four samples provided detailed information about inclusion morphology and chemical composition.  

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging and selected area diffraction pattern (SADP) analysis provided atomic-scale characterization of inclusions, particularly in sample S4. Investigation of inclusion size distribution revealed important differences between samples, with S4 showing particularly favorable inclusion characteristics. Comparative analysis of inclusion density between samples S3 and S4 demonstrated the processing parameter effects on microstructure development.  

Vickers hardness and nanoindentation hardness measurements provided quantitative assessment of mechanical property variations. The significance of hardness differences was explained through detailed microstructure analysis, confirming the relationship between processing parameters, microstructure, and final material properties.
