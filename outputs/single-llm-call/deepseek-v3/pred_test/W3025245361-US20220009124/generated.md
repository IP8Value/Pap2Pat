## BACKGROUND

Advanced manufacturing technologies have revolutionized material processing across multiple industries. Additive manufacturing, in particular, has enabled the fabrication of complex geometries that were previously impossible to achieve through conventional manufacturing methods. However, significant limitations remain in the additive manufacturing of ceramic materials, particularly for protonic ceramic energy devices (PCEDs). Traditional ceramic manufacturing techniques such as dry pressing, screen printing, tape casting, and paste extrusion face substantial challenges in producing PCEDs with large active areas, high surface-area-to-volume ratios, and customized geometries. These methods often result in delamination, cracking, and limited design flexibility. Furthermore, conventional sintering processes for ceramics require lengthy processing times and high temperatures, which can lead to undesirable microstructural evolution and phase separation. The development of integrated laser processing and additive manufacturing systems capable of overcoming these limitations represents a significant unmet need in the field of advanced ceramic manufacturing.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel Laser 3D Printing (L3DP) method that integrates digital microextrusion-based 3D printing with rapid and precise laser processing for manufacturing protonic ceramic components. The method enables the formation of silica films through a sol-gel deposition process followed by CO2 laser sintering, where the laser scanning parameters including power density between 5-50 W/mm² and scanning speeds between 0.1-10 mm/s are precisely controlled to achieve optimal densification. The invention further discloses the fabrication of Er-doped silica films for scintillation applications, where the laser sintering process produces films with controlled thickness between 10-500 μm and minimal cracking through optimized thermal stress management.

For ceramic component fabrication, the invention describes a method comprising: (a) preparing a ceramic paste comprising ceramic precursor powders, binders, and solvents with optimized rheological properties; (b) depositing the paste through microextrusion-based 3D printing; (c) subjecting the deposited paste to CO2 laser scanning at speeds between 1-20 mm/s and powers between 10-100 W to achieve controlled melting and densification; and (d) implementing multiple scanning paths with overlap percentages between 10-50% to ensure complete coverage and uniform microstructure development.

The ceramic paste materials disclosed in the invention comprise mixtures of oxides and carbonates including but not limited to BaCO3, CeO2, Y2O3, ZrO2, Yb2O3, and NiO, with particle sizes between 0.1-10 μm and binder contents between 1-5 wt%. The invention further enables 3D printing of fused silica glass components through CO2 laser melting of deposited glass precursor layers, where the layer deposition thickness is controlled between 100-500 μm and the laser melting depth is precisely matched to the deposited layer thickness.

For optical fiber sensor fabrication, the invention discloses a method comprising: (a) laser machining microchannels with widths between 50-500 μm into ceramic substrates using picosecond lasers; (b) filling the microchannels with ceramic slurry having solids loading between 40-60 vol%; and (c) sintering the filled slurry using CO2 laser irradiation at power densities between 10-100 W/mm² to achieve dense encapsulation of the optical fiber sensors. The invention also provides methods for fabricating fiber-optic pressure sensors through 3D printing of housing structures followed by paste extrusion and laser melting to form hermetic seals.

The invention further discloses a protonic ceramic energy device fabrication method based on rapid laser reactive sintering (RLRS), where precursor layers are converted to dense, phase-pure ceramic components through localized laser heating at temperatures between 1000-1600°C for durations between 1-100 seconds. The RLRS process achieves complete solid-state reactions and densification while minimizing thermal stress accumulation through controlled heating and cooling rates between 10-100°C/s.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides an integrated laser processing and additive manufacturing system specifically designed for processing ceramic, glass, and carbide materials. The system comprises several key components including a CO2 laser heating system operating at 10.6 μm wavelength with adjustable power output between 10-200 W, a picosecond laser microfabrication system providing pulse durations between 1-100 ps at repetition rates up to 1 MHz, a free-forming extrusion system capable of depositing pastes with viscosities between 100-10,000 Pa·s through nozzles with diameters between 100-1000 μm, and an ultrasonic spray coating system for uniform thin film deposition with thickness control between 1-100 μm.

The CO2 laser processing parameters are precisely controlled through a Galvo scanner system providing scanning speeds up to 5 m/s with positional accuracy of ±10 μm. The ps laser parameters include pulse energies between 10-500 μJ, spot sizes between 10-100 μm, and machining depths per pass between 10-200 μm. Optimal printing parameters for the system include layer thicknesses between 100-500 μm, extrusion rates between 0.1-1 mL/min, and stage speeds between 5-50 mm/s, with all parameters being dynamically adjustable during the manufacturing process.

For silicon carbide applications, the system enables the fabrication of SiC-based composites with tailored thermal and mechanical properties through controlled incorporation of boron nitride (BN) as a compliant interface material and zirconium diboride (ZrB2) as a thermal conductivity modifier. The invention demonstrates particular utility in protonic ceramic fuel cell applications, where the integrated system allows for simultaneous fabrication of dense electrolyte layers (10-50 μm thick) and porous electrode layers (100-1000 μm thick) with precisely controlled interfacial characteristics.

Glass object fabrication is achieved through direct laser melting of deposited glass precursor layers, with optimal laser processing parameters for glass including power densities between 10-50 W/mm², interaction times between 1-100 ms, and controlled cooling rates between 10-100°C/s to prevent cracking and achieve optical quality surfaces. The system's in-situ material processing capability enables real-time monitoring and adjustment of processing parameters based on thermal imaging and optical monitoring of the manufacturing process.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1—Integrated Multi-Laser 3D Manufacturing System

The integrated multi-laser 3D manufacturing system comprises three primary subsystems: a CO2 laser heating system, a picosecond laser microfabrication system, and a free-forming extrusion system. The CO2 laser heating system includes a 100 W continuous wave CO2 laser (λ = 10.6 μm) coupled to a high-precision translation stage with 100 nm positioning resolution and maximum speeds of 5 m/s. The laser beam is focused through a ZnSe lens system providing spot sizes adjustable between 0.1-5 mm through defocusing distances of 0-50 mm. The picosecond laser microfabrication system incorporates a 10 W ps laser (λ = 1064 nm) with pulse durations of 10 ps and repetition rates adjustable between 1 kHz-1 MHz, coupled to a 5× objective lens (NA = 0.13) producing focused spot sizes of 18 μm. Beam positioning is achieved through a Galvano scanner system with angular resolution of 5 μrad and maximum scan speeds of 2 m/s.

The free-forming extrusion system utilizes a pneumatically actuated microextruder with nozzle diameters between 100-1000 μm and extrusion pressure control between 0.1-1 MPa. Paste flow rates are monitored in real-time through an integrated flow sensor with 0.01 mL/min resolution. A centralized control system synchronizes all subsystems through custom software that generates toolpaths from 3D CAD models, with particular attention to timing synchronization between extrusion deposition and subsequent laser processing steps. The subsystems are arranged in a modular configuration allowing for sequential or simultaneous processing operations, with all components mounted on a vibration-isolated optical table to maintain micron-level alignment stability during operation.

### Example 2—CO2 Laser Sintering of Sol-Gel Deposition

The conventional sol-gel process for silica film formation faces limitations including lengthy processing times, uncontrolled cracking, and difficulty achieving full densification at low temperatures. The present invention overcomes these limitations through CO2 laser sintering of sol-gel derived silica films, where a sol-gel precursor solution is prepared by mixing tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), ethanol, water, and hydrochloric acid in molar ratios of 1:4:4:0.01 and aging for 24 hours. The precursor is deposited onto fused silica substrates through dip-coating at withdrawal speeds between 1-10 mm/s, producing as-deposited film thicknesses between 0.5-5 μm.

Laser sintering is performed using a 50 W CO2 laser focused to a 1 mm spot size, with scanning speeds between 1-10 mm/s and power densities between 10-50 W/mm². The laser power density is calculated based on measured beam profiles and calibrated power meters, ensuring precise energy delivery. Sample preparation for characterization includes sectioning using a diamond saw and polishing to 1 μm surface finish. Characterization methods comprise scanning electron microscopy (SEM) for microstructural analysis, atomic force microscopy (AFM) for surface roughness measurement, and optical microscopy for crack density assessment.

Shrinkage is calculated as the percentage reduction in film thickness after sintering, while relative density is determined through ellipsometry measurements of refractive index compared to theoretical fully dense silica. Porosity is derived from the difference between measured and theoretical densities. A finite element method (FEM) model is developed to simulate the thermal processes during laser sintering, incorporating temperature-dependent thermal properties of the fused silica substrate including thermal conductivity (1.4 W/m·K), specific heat (740 J/kg·K), and density (2200 kg/m³). The model solves heat transfer equations accounting for conduction, radiation (emissivity = 0.85), and convection (heat transfer coefficient = 10 W/m²·K) under the laser beam scanning conditions.

SEM images reveal that laser-sintered silica thin films exhibit dense, crack-free microstructures with surface roughness below 10 nm RMS, compared to furnace-treated films which show extensive cracking and roughness exceeding 100 nm. AFM results confirm the superior surface quality achieved through laser sintering, with measured shrinkage of 20-30% and porosity below 5%, representing significant improvements over conventional furnace sintering. Optical images of thick silica films (10-20 μm) demonstrate successful cracking control through optimized laser parameters, with thickness reduction after laser sintering limited to 25±2%.

The FEM model predictions show excellent agreement with analytical solutions for 1D heat conduction, validating the modeling approach. Temperature profiles on the silica substrate indicate peak temperatures of 1200-1500°C at the center of the laser beam, with rapid cooling rates exceeding 100°C/s at the sides of sintered tracks. Thermal conductivity modifications due to densification are accounted for in the model, showing good correlation with measured thickness profiles across laser-sintered tracks. Implementation of a Galvo scanner enables fabrication of silica thin films with designed patterns through controlled variation of scanning speed (1-20 mm/s) and spacing (0.1-1 mm), demonstrating the flexibility of the laser sintering technique for producing functional ceramic coatings with tailored properties.

### Example 3—CO2 Laser Melting of Ceramic Paste

Ceramic pastes for additive manufacturing are formulated to achieve optimal printability through rheology control, with compositions comprising 40-60 vol% ceramic powder (Al2O3 or SiO2, particle size 0.1-1 μm), 1-5 wt% binder (hydroxypropyl methylcellulose), and water. The pastes exhibit shear-thinning behavior with viscosities between 100-1000 Pa·s at shear rates of 10 s⁻¹, enabling smooth extrusion while maintaining shape retention after deposition. Green body density is improved through optimization of particle size distribution and binder content, achieving relative densities of 55-60% in as-printed structures.

The invention overcomes limitations of indirect selective laser sintering (SLS) processes, which require post-processing to achieve full densification, by implementing a direct laser melting approach. Multi-component ceramic systems are employed where the addition of 5-10 wt% SiO2 to Al2O3 forms a liquid phase during laser melting, promoting densification at reduced temperatures (1600-1800°C compared to >1900°C for pure Al2O3). Preheating of the substrate to 200-300°C prevents cracking during laser processing by reducing thermal gradients.

Experimental studies of CO2 laser melting of alumina and fused silica pastes are conducted using a 100 W CO2 laser with spot sizes of 0.5-2 mm and scanning speeds of 1-20 mm/s. SEM images of laser-processed alumina tracks reveal distinct melting behavior depending on scanning parameters, with complete melting achieved at power densities >30 W/mm² and interaction times >50 ms. Cross-section analysis shows permeation of melted alumina into the porous compact following the Lucas-Washburn equation, where the capillary pressure (ΔP = 2γcosθ/r) drives liquid flow into pores with radii (r) of 0.1-1 μm.

Optimization of line spacing (0.1-0.5 mm) produces uniform surface layers with roughness <10 μm, though micro-cracks are observed in pure alumina systems due to thermal stress. Heterogeneous dense-porous dual-layered structures are achieved through controlled laser parameters, with dense surface layers (porosity <5%) and porous substrates (porosity 30-40%). Periodic thickness variations are minimized through beam profile shaping and overlap optimization. Fused silica paste demonstrates superior controllability during laser melting due to its lower thermal expansion coefficient (0.5×10⁻⁶/°C) compared to alumina (8×10⁻⁶/°C), resulting in crack-free melted layers with optical transparency.

### Example 4—Thick Er-Doped Silica Films Sintered by CO2 Laser for Scintillation Applications

Er-doped silica films are prepared by mixing TEOS-based sol with Er(NO3)3·5H2O to achieve Er³⁺ concentrations of 0.1-1 mol%. Poloxamer 407 (10-20 wt%) is added to control viscosity between 10-100 cP for dip-coating deposition. Films with thicknesses of 10-50 μm are achieved through multiple coating cycles with intermediate laser drying steps. CO2 laser sintering is performed at power densities of 20-40 W/mm² and scanning speeds of 2-5 mm/s, producing crack-free films through controlled thermal stress management.

Photoluminescence and radioluminescence spectra show characteristic Er³⁺ emission at 1540 nm with intensity 2-3 times higher than furnace-sintered counterparts due to reduced hydroxyl quenching in laser-processed films. SEM micrographs reveal dense microstructures with grain sizes of 50-100 nm, while AFM measurements show surface roughness <5 nm RMS. Shrinkage ratios of 15-20% are measured, significantly lower than the 30-35% observed in furnace sintering due to constrained sintering in the laser process.

Finite element modeling of thermal stress distribution indicates peak tensile stresses of 50-100 MPa at the film-substrate interface, below the fracture strength of silica (∼150 MPa). Stress relaxation occurs through viscous flow in the silica matrix at temperatures above 1000°C, with crack spacing predicted by the model matching experimental observations of 0.5-1 mm between cracks in partially sintered regions. The combination of controlled laser parameters and optimized film composition produces Er-doped silica films with superior scintillation properties for radiation detection applications.

### Example 5—High-Resolution Laser 3D Printing of Transparent Fused Silica Glass

Fused silica glass components are fabricated through direct laser melting of deposited glass precursor layers. A paste comprising 60 vol% SiO2 powder (particle size 0.5-5 μm) and 40 vol% binder solution is extruded through a 200 μm nozzle to form layers with thicknesses of 200-300 μm. CO2 laser melting is performed immediately after deposition using power densities of 30-50 W/mm² and scanning speeds of 5-10 mm/s, with melting depths precisely controlled to match layer thickness.

Curved thin wall structures with wall thicknesses of 300-500 μm are demonstrated, with optical transparency exceeding 90% in the visible spectrum after laser polishing. SEM images reveal fully dense microstructures with minimal bubbles (<0.1 vol%) and surface roughness <10 nm after laser polishing. Transmission spectra show equivalent optical performance to commercial fused silica substrates, with absorption coefficients <0.1 cm⁻¹ at 1550 nm. The process enables fabrication of complex glass components with optical quality surfaces without requiring post-processing.

### Example 6—Laser-Assisted Embedding of all-Glass Optical Fiber Sensors into Bulk Ceramics for High Temperature Applications

Ceramic components for high-temperature applications require embedded sensors for structural health monitoring, but traditional attachment methods fail above 500°C. The invention discloses a laser-assisted process for embedding all-glass optical fiber sensors into alumina substrates. A two-step microchannel design is implemented, where a 200 μm wide channel is machined using a 10 ps laser (100 μJ/pulse, 10 kHz) to 500 μm depth, followed by a 100 μm wide channel extending to 1 mm depth.

Al2O3 slurry (55 vol% solids) is dispensed into the microchannel and sintered using a 50 W CO2 laser at 5 mm/s scan speed, achieving >95% density while maintaining sensor functionality. In-situ monitoring of the sensor spectrum during laser sintering confirms temperature stability up to 1200°C, with spectral shifts <0.1 nm during processing. SEM analysis shows complete encapsulation of the fiber with interfacial gaps <1 μm, while transmission spectra demonstrate <1 dB insertion loss after embedding.

High-temperature testing reveals linear spectral shifts of 0.01 nm/°C up to 800°C, with long-term stability exceeding 100 hours at 700°C. Residual stress effects are mitigated through co-embedding of a dummy fiber, reducing measurement deviations from ±5°C to ±1°C. The laser-assisted embedding process enables robust integration of optical sensors into ceramic components for extreme environment applications.

### Example 7—SiC Applications

The integrated additive and subtractive manufacturing (IASM) platform enables fabrication of SiC-based composites with tailored microstructures. A lattice-like microstructure is created through freeform extrusion of SiC paste containing 5-10 vol% ZrB2 particles for thermal conductivity adjustment, followed by ps laser micromachining to define micro-domains with 50-200 μm features. Boron nitride (BN) precursor ink is deposited via inkjet printing between domains, with solvent evaporation controlled through CO2 laser tracking at 10 W power.

Pyrolysis and consolidation at 1600°C produces SiC/BN composites with interfacial shear strengths exceeding 50 MPa, representing a 5× improvement over conventional layered composites. Microstructural characterization reveals uniform BN layers of 1-5 μm thickness between SiC domains, with minimal cracking due to matched shrinkage behavior during heat treatment (3.5% for SiC vs 3.8% for BN). The IASM platform demonstrates capability to fabricate SiCf/SiC ceramic matrix composites with complex architectures unattainable through conventional manufacturing.

### Example 8—3D Printing of all-Glass Fiber-Optic Pressure Sensor for High-Temperature Applications

An all-glass extrinsic Fabry-Perot interferometric (EFPI) pressure sensor is fabricated through extrusion-based 3D printing of fused silica housing structures followed by CO2 laser melting. The sensor comprises a 3D printed cone-shaped housing (wall thickness 300 μm) with integrated diaphragm (thickness 50-100 μm), formed through controlled laser irradiation at 20 W power and 2 mm/s scan speed. Gold coating (50 nm thickness) is applied via sputtering to enhance optical reflection (reflectivity >90% at 1550 nm).

Finite element simulation predicts linear diaphragm deflection up to 1 μm at 1 MPa pressure, with calculated sensitivity of 5 nm/kPa matching experimental measurements within 5%. Temperature testing shows minimal sensitivity variation (<1%) between 25-700°C due to all-glass construction. The 3D printing process enables customized sensor geometries with diaphragm diameters adjustable from 0.5-5 mm and cavity lengths controllable from 50-500 μm through precise laser processing parameters.

### Example 9—Laser-Assisted Embedding of all-Glass Optical Fiber Sensors into Bulk Ceramics for High-Temperature Applications

The laser-assisted embedding process is extended to intrinsic Fabry-Perot interferometric (IFPI) sensors fabricated by fs laser machining of glass fibers. A two-step microchannel design enables precise sensor positioning, with primary channel dimensions of 300 μm width × 800 μm depth and secondary channel of 150 μm width × 200 μm depth. Al2O3 slurry filling is optimized to achieve 98% density after CO2 laser sintering at 40 W power and 2 mm/s speed, with in-situ spectral monitoring confirming sensor survival temperatures up to 1400°C.

Finesse factors of 8-10 are measured for embedded IFPI sensors, indicating high-quality cavities formed during the embedding process. High-temperature testing reveals linear spectral shifts of 15 pm/°C from 25-800°C, with long-term stability exceeding 200 hours at 700°C. Co-embedding of a dummy fiber compensates for thermal expansion mismatch effects, reducing measurement uncertainty from ±3°C to ±0.5°C. SEM analysis shows excellent interfacial bonding between fiber and ceramic matrix, with no detectable reaction zones after extended high-temperature exposure.

### Example 10—Rapid Laser Reactive Sintering of Protonic Ceramic Fuel Cells

Protonic ceramic fuel cell (PCFC) single cells are fabricated through rapid laser reactive sintering (RLRS) of BCZYYb electrolyte films on NiO-BCZYYb anode substrates. The RLRS process converts precursor films to dense electrolytes in 30-60 seconds at laser power densities of 50-100 W/mm², compared to 10-20 hours for conventional sintering. Electrochemical testing reveals peak power densities of 300 mW/cm² at 600°C, comparable to furnace-sintered cells but with 100× faster processing.

XRD analysis confirms phase-pure BCZYYb formation through RLRS, with grain sizes of 2-5 μm versus 5-10 μm for furnace-sintered samples. SEM characterization shows 98% dense electrolytes with thicknesses of 15-20 μm, well-bonded to porous anodes (30-40% porosity). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy indicates area-specific resistances of 0.3 Ω·cm² at 600°C, dominated by electrode polarization resistance. The RLRS method enables rapid prototyping of PCFCs with performance matching conventionally processed cells.

### Example 11—Rapid Laser Reactive Sintering of Protonic Ceramic Components

The RLRS technique is applied to nine protonic ceramic compositions including BCZYYb, BZY20, and LSC, demonstrating universal applicability. Green pastes are prepared from oxide/carbonate precursors with 1-3 wt% binder, 3D printed to 200-500 μm thickness, and laser sintered at 50-150 W power with 0.1-5 mm/s scan speeds. XRD confirms phase purity in all compositions, with relative densities exceeding 95% for electrolytes and 60-70% for porous electrodes.

Proton conductivity measurements show Arrhenius behavior with activation energies of 0.4-0.6 eV, matching furnace-sintered samples. Cost analysis reveals 90% reduction in energy consumption compared to conventional sintering, with total processing time reduced from 20 hours to 30 minutes. The RLRS method enables rapid manufacturing of protonic ceramic components with tailored microstructures for energy conversion and storage applications.

### Example 12—RLRS of La0.8Sr0.2CrO3-δ Interconnects

La0.8Sr0.2CrO3-δ (LSC) interconnect films are fabricated on MgO substrates through RLRS of terpineol-based green slurries. Laser power (50-100 W) and scan speed (0.5-2 mm/s) are optimized to achieve >95% density while preventing Cr sublimation (verified through EDS analysis). The RLRS-derived LSC films exhibit electrical conductivities of 80-100 S/cm at 800°C, with area-specific resistances of 0.05 Ω·cm² for 50 μm thick films - comparable to furnace-sintered samples but with 100× faster processing.

XPS analysis confirms Sr surface segregation is reduced in RLRS samples versus furnace-sintered counterparts, explaining the improved conductivity. Microstructural analysis reveals a unique micrograin-agglomerated structure with 0.5-1 μm grains forming 5-10 μm agglomerates, providing both high density and tortuous gas permeation paths. The RLRS process enables rapid fabrication of high-performance interconnects for solid oxide fuel cells and electrolyzers.

### Example 13—Integrated Additive Manufacturing and Laser Processing for Protonic Ceramic Energy Devices (PCEDs)

An integrated additive manufacturing and laser processing (I-AMLP) system is developed for complete PCED fabrication. The system combines microextrusion 3D printing, CO2 laser drying (10-20 W, 5-20 mm/s), ps laser machining (100 μJ, 10 kHz), and CO2 laser sintering (50-100 W, 0.1-5 mm/s) in a single platform. Paste viscosity is optimized between 500-1000 Pa·s for printing thin walls (100-300 μm) while maintaining interlayer bonding.

Multilayer PCED stacks are fabricated through alternating printing of porous electrodes (40 wt% BCZYYb + 60 wt% NiO) and dense electrolytes (BCZYYb + 1 wt% NiO), with laser sintering after each layer. The I-AMLP process achieves 100 μm resolution in all three dimensions, with total fabrication time reduced from 48 hours to 4 hours for complete cells. Electrochemical testing shows performance equivalent to conventionally fabricated cells, validating the integrated manufacturing approach.

### Example 14—L3DP of Microchannel Membrane Reactors

Microchannel membrane reactors are fabricated through L3DP of BCFZY0.1 paste followed by ps laser machining of 300 μm wide channels at 200 μm pitch. CO2 laser sintering at 60 W power and 1 mm/s speed produces fully dense membranes (thickness 500 μm) with open microchannels. The reactors demonstrate hydrogen production rates of 5 mL/min·cm² at 600°C, representing a 3× improvement over conventional tubular designs due to increased surface-area-to-volume ratio.

### Example 15—Direct Inkjet Printing of Mullite Nano-Ribbons

Mullite nano-ribbons are fabricated through inkjet printing of sol-gel precursors (Al/Si = 2/1 molar ratio) with 5 wt% PVP additive. Substrate temperature is maintained at 50-80°C during printing to control line stability, with post-printing heat treatment at 1400°C producing crack-free ribbons. The printed mullite exhibits flexural strength of 300 MPa, comparable to single crystal mullite, demonstrating the potential for bio-inspired ceramic fabrication through additive manufacturing.
