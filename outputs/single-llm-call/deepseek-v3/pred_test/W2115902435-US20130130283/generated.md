# DESCRIPTION  

The present invention relates to compositions and methods for targeting bacterial type III secretion systems (T3SS), which are utilized by numerous pathogenic bacteria to deliver effector proteins into host cells. More specifically, the invention provides novel assays and screening methods for identifying inhibitors of T3SS assembly and function.  

### Introduce type III secretion system (T3SS)  
The type III secretion system is a specialized protein transport machinery employed by Gram-negative bacterial pathogens to inject virulence factors directly into eukaryotic host cells. This needle-like apparatus spans the bacterial inner and outer membranes, forming a continuous channel through which effector proteins are translocated. The T3SS is essential for the pathogenesis of many medically important bacteria including Salmonella, Shigella, Yersinia, enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC), enterohemorrhagic E. coli (EHEC), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa.  

### Describe problems with antibiotics  
Conventional antibiotic therapies face significant challenges in treating infections caused by T3SS-dependent pathogens. Many antibiotics fail to specifically target virulence mechanisms while exerting selective pressure that drives antibiotic resistance. Furthermore, certain antibiotics may inadvertently enhance toxin release from dying bacteria. There exists an urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies that directly disrupt bacterial virulence without promoting resistance.  

### Motivate T3SS as therapeutic target  
The T3SS represents an attractive therapeutic target because it is: (1) essential for virulence in multiple pathogens, (2) absent in commensal bacteria and mammalian cells, (3) structurally conserved among diverse pathogens, and (4) unlikely to develop resistance when targeted by inhibitors due to its complex multi-component nature. Disabling the T3SS could attenuate bacterial virulence while preserving commensal flora and minimizing selective pressure for resistance.  

### Describe type III secretion systems  
The T3SS is a syringe-like nanomachine composed of approximately 20-25 proteins that assemble into three major substructures: (1) a multi-ring base embedded in the bacterial membranes, (2) an inner rod connecting the base to (3) an external needle filament. The base contains concentric rings formed by secretin family proteins in the outer membrane and additional ring-forming proteins in the inner membrane. The needle filament protrudes from the bacterial surface and serves as a conduit for effector protein translocation.  

### List bacterial pathogens using T3SS  
Pathogens utilizing T3SS include: Salmonella enterica (typhoid fever, gastroenteritis); Shigella spp. (bacillary dysentery); Yersinia pestis (plague); Yersinia pseudotuberculosis and Yersinia enterocolitica (gastroenteritis); EPEC and EHEC (diarrheal diseases); Pseudomonas aeruginosa (nosocomial infections); and Chlamydia spp. (sexually transmitted diseases, trachoma).  

### Describe secreted effectors  
T3SS effector proteins manipulate host cell processes to promote bacterial invasion, intracellular survival, immune evasion, and dissemination. Examples include: Salmonella SipB (induces macrophage apoptosis); Yersinia YopH (tyrosine phosphatase disrupting phagocytosis); Shigella IpaB (induces pore formation in host membranes); EPEC Tir (reorganizes host cytoskeleton).  

### Describe needle complex  
The needle complex is the core structural component of the T3SS, comprising: (1) an outer membrane secretin ring (e.g., InvG in Salmonella); (2) periplasmic neck region connecting to (3) inner membrane rings (PrgH/PrgK in Salmonella); (4) an inner rod (PrgJ); and (5) needle filament (PrgI). Cryo-EM reveals the Salmonella needle complex as a 25nm wide base with 30nm length, connected to a 20-50nm needle.  

### Describe assembly of needle complex  
Assembly occurs sequentially: (1) base formation allows initial secretion of rod/needle components; (2) inner rod assembly enables needle polymerization; (3) completed needle complex undergoes conformational changes to switch substrate specificity from structural to effector proteins. Proper assembly requires specific interactions between component domains.  

### Introduce chaperones  
T3SS chaperones are cytoplasmic proteins that prevent premature aggregation or degradation of effector proteins and some structural components. They typically bind to N-terminal secretion signals or specific domains of their cognate substrates.  

### Describe class I chaperones  
Class I chaperones (e.g., SicP for SptP in Salmonella) specifically bind effector proteins. They contain conserved tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) domains that recognize amphipathic helices in effector N-termini. Chaperone binding maintains effectors in secretion-competent states.  

### Describe removal of chaperones  
Chaperones dissociate from effectors upon contact with the T3SS ATPase (InvC in Salmonella), which unfolds substrates for translocation. The ATPase recognizes conserved chaperone-effector complexes rather than free effectors, ensuring proper substrate engagement.  

### Summarize prior art methods  
Previous T3SS inhibition strategies include: (1) small molecules targeting secretion ATPases; (2) antibodies against needle components; (3) peptide inhibitors of translocon pore formation; (4) expression of dominant-negative chaperone variants. However, these approaches lack broad-spectrum efficacy or suitable pharmacological properties.  

### Describe WO2009/145829  
This application discloses peptide inhibitors derived from translocon proteins (e.g., SipB from Salmonella) that block pore formation in host membranes. While effective in vitro, these peptides show poor stability in vivo.  

### Describe WO2009/137133  
This patent describes small molecules that inhibit the T3SS ATPase (InvC homologs) by competing with ATP binding. Although reducing secretion, these compounds often exhibit cytotoxicity due to off-target effects on host ATPases.  

### Describe WO2009/061491  
This application covers antibodies targeting conserved epitopes in needle filament proteins (e.g., PrgI in Salmonella). While neutralizing secretion, antibody-based therapies face challenges with bacterial access and immune evasion.  

### Describe Gauthier et al. (2005)  
Gauthier identified aurodox, a natural product inhibitor of EPEC T3SS that blocks effector secretion without affecting bacterial growth. However, aurodox shows narrow spectrum activity limited to certain EPEC strains.  

### State object of present invention  
The present invention provides novel methods for identifying broad-spectrum T3SS inhibitors that: (1) disrupt assembly of functional needle complexes; (2) block effector-chaperone interactions; or (3) interfere with substrate engagement by the secretion apparatus. These methods enable high-throughput screening of compound libraries using specific T3SS component interactions as molecular targets.  

### Describe novel method  
The invention comprises parallel assay platforms that monitor: (1) effector-chaperone binding (Secretion ELISA); (2) needle complex assembly (Structure ELISA); and (3) translocon formation (Membrane Insertion Assay). These complementary approaches allow comprehensive screening for inhibitors targeting different T3SS functional stages.  

### Describe first step of method  
The first step involves preparing recombinant T3SS components, including: (1) effector proteins (e.g., SptP) with cognate chaperones (e.g., SicP); (2) base proteins (e.g., PrgH, PrgK) and needle components (e.g., PrgI); (3) translocon proteins (e.g., SipB, SipC). Components are expressed in E. coli and purified by affinity chromatography.  

### Describe detection methods  
Interaction between components is detected using ELISA-based formats with modifications for specific applications. Detection employs colorimetric, fluorescent, or luminescent signals proportional to target complex formation.  

### Describe ELISA  
Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) provides quantitative measurement of protein-protein interactions. The standard format involves: (1) immobilizing capture protein; (2) blocking nonspecific sites; (3) adding test sample; (4) washing; (5) detecting bound analyte.  

### Describe conventional ELISA protocol  
Conventional ELISA procedures include: (1) coating plates with capture antibody (1-10 μg/ml in PBS, 4°C overnight); (2) blocking with 1-5% BSA or nonfat milk (1 hr, RT); (3) adding sample (1-2 hr incubation); (4) washing with PBS-Tween; (5) adding detection antibody (1 hr); (6) adding enzyme substrate; (7) measuring absorbance.  

### Describe immobilization of capture reagent  
For effector-chaperone assays, chaperones (e.g., GST-SicP) are immobilized via glutathione-coated plates or anti-tag antibodies. For structure assays, his-tagged base proteins (e.g., PrgH-His6) are captured on Ni-NTA plates. Optimal coating concentrations (0.5-5 μg/ml) are determined by checkerboard titration.  

### Describe blocking agent  
Blocking agents prevent nonspecific binding while preserving target interactions. Suitable blockers include: (1) proteins (BSA, casein, milk); (2) polymers (PVP, PEG); (3) surfactants (Tween-20, Triton X-100). Blocking conditions (1-5% concentration, 30-120 min incubation) are optimized for each assay.  

### Describe incubation of sample  
Test samples (culture supernatants, purified proteins, compound libraries) are incubated under conditions maintaining native interactions (e.g., 25-37°C, physiological pH and ionic strength). Incubation times (30 min-2 hr) balance signal intensity with throughput requirements.  

### Describe washing and detection  
Unbound material is removed by washing (3-5x) with buffer containing mild detergent (0.05% Tween-20). Detection employs: (1) primary antibodies against target proteins; (2) enzyme-conjugated secondary antibodies; (3) chromogenic (TMB, ABTS), fluorescent (FITC, R-phycoerythrin), or chemiluminescent (luminol) substrates.  

### Describe labels and detection methods  
Labels include: (1) enzymes (HRP, AP) with colorimetric substrates; (2) fluorophores (FITC, Alexa dyes) detected by plate readers; (3) lanthanides (Eu3+) for time-resolved fluorescence; (4) quantum dots for multiplex detection. Signal is quantified by absorbance (450nm for TMB), fluorescence (ex/em appropriate for dye), or luminescence (RLU).  

### Describe conjugation of labels to antibodies  
Antibodies are conjugated to detection moieties via: (1) NHS-ester chemistry for amines; (2) maleimide-thiol coupling; (3) hydrazide-aldehyde reactions. Conjugates are purified by size exclusion chromatography and characterized by SDS-PAGE and activity assays.  

### Describe measurement of bound antibody  
Bound antibody is quantified by: (1) absorbance (spectrophotometer); (2) fluorescence (plate reader); (3) luminescence (photomultiplier). Data is analyzed by comparison to standard curves generated with known analyte concentrations.  

### Describe second step of method  
The second step involves validating hits from primary screens in secondary assays: (1) secretion inhibition (immunoblot of culture supernatants); (2) needle complex assembly (native PAGE); (3) bacterial invasion (gentamicin protection assay); (4) cytotoxicity (LDH release).  

### Describe preferred embodiment of first step  
The preferred primary screen is a modified ELISA detecting effector-chaperone complexes (Secretion ELISA). This assay specifically monitors early T3SS substrate engagement by measuring the interaction between effectors (e.g., SptP) and their cognate chaperones (e.g., SicP).  

### Describe detection of effector bound to chaperone  
Effector binding to immobilized chaperone is detected using: (1) anti-effector antibodies; (2) tagged effectors (e.g., SptP-FLAG); (3) competitive displacement with labeled effectors. Signal reduction indicates compounds disrupting the chaperone-effector interaction.  

### Describe ELISA-type assay  
The secretion ELISA comprises: (1) coating plates with chaperone (GST-SicP); (2) blocking; (3) adding effector (SptP) ± test compounds; (4) detecting bound effector with anti-SptP antibody. Controls include chaperone alone (background) and known inhibitors (validation).  

### Describe immobilization of chaperone  
Chaperones are immobilized via: (1) GST tag on glutathione plates; (2) His tag on Ni-NTA plates; (3) direct adsorption to high-binding plates. Immobilization preserves chaperone's effector-binding capacity as verified by pull-down assays.  

### Describe detection of effector  
Effectors are detected by: (1) direct labeling (biotin, fluorophores); (2) sandwich ELISA (capture chaperone, detect effector); (3) competitive ELISA (labeled vs unlabeled effector). Sensitivity reaches 1-10 nM effector concentration.  

### Describe secretion ELISA  
The secretion ELISA specifically measures T3SS substrate recognition by quantifying effector-chaperone complexes. This interaction is essential for proper effector delivery, making it an ideal target for inhibitors. The assay is adaptable to high-throughput screening (384-well format).  

### Describe advantages of secretion ELISA  
Advantages include: (1) specificity for T3SS substrates; (2) sensitivity to small molecule inhibitors; (3) scalability for HTS; (4) quantitative output; (5) adaptability to multiple pathogen systems.  

### Describe applicability to other bacteria  
The method applies to any T3SS with identified effector-chaperone pairs, including: (1) Yersinia (YopE-SycE); (2) Pseudomonas (ExoS-Orf1); (3) Shigella (IpaC-IpgC); (4) EPEC (Tir-CesT). Conserved chaperone domains enable broad-spectrum inhibitor identification.  

### List examples of effector proteins  
Exemplary effectors include: Salmonella SptP, SopE, SipB; Yersinia YopE, YopH, YopT; Shigella IpaB, IpaC, IpaD; EPEC Tir, EspB, EspF; Pseudomonas ExoS, ExoT, ExoU.  

### Describe type III protein secretion system  
The T3SS is a multi-protein apparatus injecting effectors directly into host cells. Its assembly requires coordinated expression of: (1) membrane-embedded base; (2) extracellular needle; (3) cytoplasmic sorting platform; (4) translocon pore. Proper assembly is verified by blue native PAGE and electron microscopy.  

### Introduce effector and chaperone proteins  
Effectors contain N-terminal secretion signals recognized by specific chaperones. Chaperone binding prevents effector aggregation/degradation and targets effectors to the T3SS. Examples: SicP binds SptP N-terminus; SycE binds YopE N-terminus; IpgC binds IpaB N-terminus.  

### Provide examples of effector and chaperone pairs  
Exemplary pairs: Salmonella (SptP-SicP, SopE-SopE2); Yersinia (YopE-SycE, YopH-SycH); Shigella (IpaB-IpgC, IpaC-IpgC); EPEC (Tir-CesT, EspB-CesT); Pseudomonas (ExoS-Orf1, ExoT-Orf2).  

### Describe method for determining chaperone existence  
Chaperone presence is confirmed by: (1) co-purification with effectors; (2) pull-down assays; (3) yeast two-hybrid; (4) bioinformatic analysis of T3SS gene clusters. Essential chaperones show synthetic lethality when deleted.  

### Explain importance of activating type III protein secretion system  
T3SS activation is required for: (1) needle complex assembly; (2) effector secretion; (3) translocon insertion; (4) virulence in vivo. Activation signals include: low calcium, host cell contact, temperature shift.  

### Describe methods for activating type III protein secretion system  
Activation methods include: (1) low-calcium media (<100 μM); (2) temperature shift to 37°C; (3) host cell co-culture; (4) overexpression of transcriptional regulators (e.g., HilA in Salmonella). Activation is monitored by effector secretion (immunoblot).  

### Introduce high-throughput screening method  
The invention enables HTS by: (1) 384-well plate format; (2) automated liquid handling; (3) multiplexed detection; (4) Z' factor >0.5. Libraries screened include: FDA-approved drugs, natural products, synthetic small molecules.  

### Describe Alpha technology  
AlphaScreen (Amplified Luminescent Proximity Homogeneous Assay) detects molecular interactions using donor/acceptor beads emitting light when brought within 200nm. Applied to T3SS, it measures: (1) effector-chaperone binding; (2) needle component interactions.  

### Explain principle of AlphaScreen  
The principle involves: (1) biotinylated bait on streptavidin donor beads; (2) His-tagged prey on nickel-chelate acceptor beads; (3) laser excitation at 680nm; (4) singlet oxygen transfer; (5) chemiluminescence at 520-620nm. Signal requires proximity (<200nm) between interacting proteins.  

### Describe sandwich format assay  
The sandwich format comprises: (1) immobilized chaperone; (2) test compound; (3) biotinylated effector; (4) streptavidin donor beads; (5) anti-chaperone antibody; (6) protein A acceptor beads. Inhibitors reduce luminescence by disrupting effector-chaperone interaction.  

### Describe direct and indirect detection methods  
Direct detection uses labeled proteins (e.g., GFP-SptP, His-SicP). Indirect detection employs antibodies against unlabeled components. Both methods achieve comparable sensitivity (nM range) but differ in throughput and interference potential.  

### Introduce alternative assay types (e.g. DELFIA, FRET)  
Alternative assays include: (1) DELFIA (time-resolved fluorescence); (2) FRET (fluorescence resonance energy transfer); (3) SPR (surface plasmon resonance); (4) ITC (isothermal titration calorimetry). Each offers unique advantages for specific applications.  

### Describe DELFIA assay  
DELFIA (Dissociation-Enhanced Lanthanide Fluoroimmunoassay) uses: (1) europium-labeled antibody; (2) enhancement solution releasing lanthanides; (3) time-delayed fluorescence measurement. Advantages include: low background, large Stokes shift, multiplexing capability.  

### Introduce method for monitoring assembly of structural components  
The Structure ELISA monitors needle complex assembly by detecting interactions between base (PrgH/PrgK) and needle (PrgI) components. This identifies compounds disrupting structural integrity of the secretion apparatus.  

### Describe structure assay  
The structure assay involves: (1) immobilizing base proteins (PrgH-His6); (2) adding needle components (PrgI); (3) detecting complex formation with anti-PrgI antibody. Signal reduction indicates impaired base-needle interaction.  

### Explain principle of structure ELISA  
The principle relies on: (1) his-tagged base capture; (2) native needle binding; (3) antibody detection. Proper assembly requires specific interactions between PrgH C-terminus and PrgI N-terminus, providing a precise readout of structural integrity.  

### Describe example of structure ELISA (PrgH and PrgI)  
An exemplary assay: (1) coats Ni-NTA plates with PrgH-His6; (2) blocks with BSA; (3) adds PrgI ± compounds; (4) detects bound PrgI with anti-PrgI-HRP. Controls include PrgH alone (background) and EDTA (competes nickel, negative control).  

### Introduce alternative tags for structural components  
Alternative tags include: (1) FLAG for anti-FLAG detection; (2) GST for glutathione capture; (3) biotin for streptavidin binding; (4) SNAP/CLIP tags for covalent labeling. Tag position (N- vs C-terminus) is optimized to avoid interference with interactions.  

### Describe analysis of test compound's effect on ATPase  
Test compounds are evaluated for ATPase inhibition by: (1) malachite green phosphate assay; (2) NADH-coupled assay; (3) radioactive ATP hydrolysis. ATPase activity correlates with T3SS function, providing orthogonal validation of hits.  

### Explain applicability of structure assay to other bacteria  
The assay adapts to other pathogens by substituting cognate components: Yersinia (YscD-YscF); Shigella (MxiD-MxiH); EPEC (EscD-EscF). Conserved interaction interfaces enable identification of broad-spectrum inhibitors.  

### Describe identification of inhibitors of T3SS  
Inhibitors are identified by: (1) primary screening (Secretion/Structure ELISA); (2) secondary validation (secretion immunoblot, ATPase assay); (3) tertiary testing (bacterial invasion, cytotoxicity). Hits are prioritized by potency (IC50), selectivity (counter-screens), and drug-like properties.  

### Introduce method for testing inhibitors against other bacteria  
Cross-species testing involves: (1) expressing heterologous T3SS in E. coli; (2) adapting assays to cognate components; (3) comparing inhibitor sensitivity. Broad-spectrum compounds show activity against multiple T3SS types.  

### Describe modeling of test compound into structural components  
Computational modeling predicts binding sites by: (1) docking to crystal structures (PrgH, SicP); (2) molecular dynamics simulations; (3) free energy calculations. Models guide structure-activity relationship (SAR) optimization.  

### Introduce de novo design of inhibitors  
De novo design employs: (1) pharmacophore modeling of interaction sites; (2) fragment-based lead discovery; (3) scaffold hopping from known inhibitors. Designed compounds are synthesized and tested iteratively.  

### Describe computer-based design of inhibitors  
Computer-aided design uses: (1) homology modeling of target proteins; (2) virtual screening of compound libraries; (3) QSAR analysis of active compounds. Tools include Schrödinger Suite, MOE, AutoDock.  

### Introduce X-ray crystallography  
X-ray crystallography determines atomic structures of: (1) target proteins (chaperones, needle components); (2) protein-inhibitor complexes. Structures guide rational design of improved inhibitors.  

### Describe computer programs for designing inhibitors  
Programs include: (1) Rosetta for protein design; (2) GOLD for docking; (3) AMBER for dynamics; (4) PyMOL for visualization. These tools predict binding modes and optimize compound properties.  

### Explain crystallization methods  
Crystallization employs: (1) vapor diffusion (hanging/sitting drop); (2) microbatch under oil; (3) lipidic cubic phase for membrane proteins. Conditions are screened by robotic systems (Mosquito, Gryphon).  

### Describe evaluation of candidate compounds  
Candidates are evaluated by: (1) biochemical potency (IC50); (2) selectivity (counter-screens); (3) solubility/logP; (4) metabolic stability; (5) cytotoxicity. Lead compounds exhibit IC50 <10 μM, >10-fold selectivity, and suitable drug-like properties.

Lead compounds undergo: (1) acute toxicity testing (MTD); (2) pharmacokinetics (Cmax, T1/2); (3) efficacy in infection models. Successful candidates show reduced bacterial burden without host toxicity.

PK/toxicity studies include: (1) plasma exposure (IV, PO dosing); (2) tissue distribution; (3) liver microsome stability; (4) hERG liability; (5) genotoxicity (Ames test). Optimal compounds exhibit oral bioavailability >20% and clean safety profiles.

Relevant models include: (1) Salmonella murine typhoid; (2) Yersinia bubonic plague; (3) Pseudomonas lung infection; (4) EPEC infant rabbit diarrhea. Models recapitulate human disease and assess T3SS inhibition in vivo.

EPEC/EHEC testing uses: (1) infant rabbit diarrhea model; (2) oral infection with 10^8 CFU; (3) compound administration (oral/IV); (4) fecal shedding quantification; (5) histopathology scoring. Effective compounds reduce bacterial colonization and disease severity.

Salmonella testing employs: (1) murine gastroenteritis model; (2) oral infection with 10^5 CFU; (3) compound treatment; (4) CFU counts in Peyer's patches/spleen; (5) cytokine profiling. Successful inhibitors attenuate systemic spread.

The murine typhoid model involves: (1) IV infection with 10^3 S. typhimurium; (2) daily compound treatment; (3) survival monitoring; (4) organ CFU at endpoint. Compounds prolong survival and reduce liver/spleen colonization.

In the typhoid model: (1) mice receive test compound (PO/IP) starting 24h post-infection; (2) efficacy is assessed by: (a) survival curve (Kaplan-Meier); (b) bacterial load (CFU/organ); (c) histopathology; (d) cytokine levels. Positive controls include ciprofloxacin.

Organ colonization reflects: (1) bacterial dissemination; (2) T3SS-dependent virulence; (3) compound efficacy. Reduced liver/spleen CFU indicates successful T3SS inhibition and correlates with survival benefit.  

### Describe potential of compounds as antibacterial therapeutics  
T3SS inhibitors offer: (1) virulence attenuation without killing; (2) reduced resistance pressure; (3) synergy with antibiotics; (4) broad-spectrum potential. Clinical applications include: sepsis prophylaxis, adjunctive therapy, immunocompromised hosts.

In summary, the invention provides comprehensive methods for identifying T3SS inhibitors through: (1) effector-chaperone interaction assays; (2) needle complex assembly screens; (3) in vivo validation. These approaches enable discovery of novel anti-virulence therapeutics.  

## EXAMPLE 1  

### Secretion ELISA for Detecting the Effector Protein SptP when Bound to its Cognate Chaperone SicP  

The secretion ELISA was performed as follows: Glutathione-S-transferase (GST)-tagged SicP was expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) and purified by glutathione affinity chromatography. Purified GST-SicP (1 μg/ml in PBS) was immobilized on glutathione-coated 96-well plates overnight at 4°C. Plates were blocked with 3% BSA in PBS for 1 hour at room temperature. Serial dilutions of Salmonella culture supernatant containing SptP were added to wells and incubated for 1 hour at 37°C. After washing (3x PBS-Tween), bound SptP was detected with rabbit anti-SptP primary antibody (1:1000, 1 hour), followed by HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:5000, 1 hour). Color development used TMB substrate (15 min), stopped with 1M H2SO4, and measured at 450nm.
