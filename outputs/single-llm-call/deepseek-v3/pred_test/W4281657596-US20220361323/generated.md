### FIELD  

The present invention relates generally to the field of neural interfaces and more specifically to three-dimensional multielectrode arrays fabricated using integrated 3D printing and photolithography techniques for neural recording and stimulation applications.  

### BACKGROUND  

Traditional microfabricated electrode arrays for neural interfaces have been limited to planar geometries due to constraints of thin-film lithography processes. These planar arrays suffer from several limitations when interfacing with three-dimensional neural tissue structures. The two-dimensional nature of planar electrodes results in suboptimal contact with target neural tissue, leading to higher impedance interfaces and reduced signal quality. Furthermore, planar electrode designs often induce significant scar tissue formation when implanted chronically, which further degrades recording and stimulation performance over time.  

Existing approaches to create three-dimensional electrode structures have relied on manual assembly of individual wire electrodes or complex silicon micromachining processes. These methods lack the precision, scalability, and design flexibility needed for advanced neural interface applications. There remains an unmet need for high-resolution, customizable three-dimensional electrode arrays that can be efficiently manufactured at scale while providing optimal electrical and mechanical interfaces with neural tissue.  

### SUMMARY  

The present invention provides novel three-dimensional multielectrode arrays fabricated through an integrated process combining high-resolution 3D printing with conventional photolithography techniques. The 3D electrodes feature raised structures with precisely controlled geometries that extend from a substrate surface, providing enhanced surface area and improved tissue interface characteristics compared to planar electrodes.  

Key aspects of the invention include 3D electrodes formed from photopolymer materials that are subsequently coated with conductive metal layers. The electrodes exhibit conical or pyramidal shapes with aspect ratios (height to base width) ranging from 5:1 to 20:1, providing optimal mechanical properties for tissue penetration while maintaining structural integrity. The tip portions of the electrodes are selectively exposed to create electrical contact surfaces, while the shaft portions remain insulated to prevent signal cross-talk.  

The fabrication process involves first preparing a substrate with patterned electrical contacts and traces using standard photolithography techniques. A sacrificial metal layer is then applied and etched to define electrode sites. Three-dimensional structures are printed onto the substrate using a high-resolution two-photon direct laser writing system capable of micron-scale feature resolution. The printed structures are then metallized via sputter deposition, and the sacrificial layer is lifted off to define the final conductive traces and electrodes.  

The resulting 3D multielectrode arrays provide numerous advantages over conventional planar arrays. The three-dimensional geometry increases the effective surface area for neural recording and stimulation while maintaining a small footprint. The customizable electrode shapes can be optimized for specific applications, including biomimetic designs that reduce insertion forces and improve chronic stability. The integrated fabrication process enables wafer-scale production of high-density arrays with precisely controlled electrode geometries.  

### DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

The present invention provides advanced three-dimensional multielectrode arrays and methods for their fabrication. These devices address critical limitations of traditional planar electrode arrays by providing three-dimensional electrode structures with precisely controlled geometries that optimize the interface with neural tissue.  

Multielectrode arrays find application in various neural recording and stimulation contexts, including brain-machine interfaces, neuroprosthetics, and basic neuroscience research. Conventional arrays manufactured using thin-film lithography processes are inherently limited to two-dimensional planar geometries. These planar designs suffer from poor surface-area-to-footprint ratios and suboptimal mechanical interactions with three-dimensional neural tissue structures.  

The limitations of planar multielectrode arrays become particularly apparent in tissue applications. When implanted, planar arrays often induce significant scar tissue formation that encapsulates the electrodes and increases interface impedance over time. The two-dimensional nature of these devices also limits their ability to make intimate contact with target neural populations distributed in three-dimensional space. For stimulation applications, planar electrodes require higher power consumption to achieve effective neural activation due to their inefficient current distribution patterns.  

The present invention overcomes these limitations through three-dimensional electrode structures fabricated using an innovative combination of high-resolution 3D printing and conventional photolithography. The 3D printing process enables creation of electrode structures with precisely controlled geometries that extend perpendicularly from the substrate surface. These raised structures provide significantly increased surface area for electrical contact while maintaining a small footprint.  

A key innovation of the invention is the integration of 3D printing with standard photolithography processes to create fully functional electronic devices. The fabrication begins with preparation of a substrate containing patterned electrical contacts and traces using conventional photolithography techniques. Windows are opened through an insulating layer to expose contact areas where 3D electrode structures will be formed.  

The 3D printing process utilizes two-photon direct laser writing lithography to create high-resolution polymer structures. A raster-scanning direct laser writing (rDLW) printer provides exceptional precision, with build volumes up to several cubic millimeters at micron-scale resolution. The printer incorporates an immersion objective lens that focuses femtosecond laser pulses into a liquid photopolymer resin, inducing localized polymerization at the focal point. By precisely controlling the position of the focal point in three dimensions, complex electrode shapes can be fabricated with sub-micron precision.  

Following 3D printing, the structures undergo selective metallization through a photolithography process. A metal layer is deposited over the entire substrate surface, coating both the pre-patterned traces and the 3D printed structures. The sacrificial metal layer is then removed through a lift-off process, leaving behind metallized 3D electrodes connected to the substrate traces.  

The resulting 3D electronic devices exhibit several advantageous characteristics. The surface-area-to-footprint ratio is significantly increased compared to planar electrodes, improving both recording sensitivity and stimulation efficiency. The three-dimensional geometry allows for closer integration with neural tissue structures, reducing interface impedance. Customizable electrode shapes can be designed to optimize specific performance characteristics such as insertion force, chronic stability, or current density distribution.  

Various embodiments of the 3D electrode structures are possible within the scope of the invention. Conical or pyramidal shapes provide optimal mechanical properties for tissue penetration, with aspect ratios (height to base width) typically ranging from 5:1 to 20:1. The tip portions of the electrodes are selectively exposed to create electrical contact surfaces, while the shaft portions remain insulated to prevent signal cross-talk.  

Alternative embodiments incorporate porous or mesh-like structures that further increase surface area while allowing tissue integration. These designs may include internal channels or voids that promote vascularization or neural process ingrowth, enhancing long-term stability. Some embodiments feature specialized tip geometries inspired by natural structures, such as mosquito proboscis designs that reduce insertion forces.  

The fabrication process enables creation of both rigid and flexible 3D electrode arrays. Flexible embodiments utilize polymer substrates such as polyimide, allowing the arrays to conform to curved tissue surfaces. The integrated nature of the fabrication process allows 3D electrode arrays to be combined with existing device platforms, including complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) electronics for signal processing.  

Methods for manufacturing the 3D electronic components involve several key steps. First, a substrate is prepared with patterned electrical contacts and traces using standard photolithography techniques. Windows are opened through an insulating layer to expose contact areas. A sacrificial metal layer is then applied and etched to define electrode sites.  

Three-dimensional structures are printed onto the substrate using the high-resolution two-photon direct laser writing system. The printer utilizes a resonant raster scanner combined with galvanometer mirrors to achieve high-speed, precise patterning. An imaging system provides real-time feedback on print quality and substrate localization.  

Following printing, the structures undergo selective metallization. A positive photoresist is applied and patterned to expose only the desired metalization areas. Metal layers such as titanium and platinum are deposited via sputtering, coating both the pre-patterned traces and the 3D printed structures. The sacrificial metal layer is then removed through a lift-off process.  

Final device preparation involves applying an insulating layer over the entire structure, followed by selective removal of insulation from the electrode tips. This can be accomplished through precise laser ablation or other micromachining techniques. The completed devices may undergo additional surface treatments such as plasma etching or iridium oxide electrodeposition to further optimize interface characteristics.  

The invention encompasses various embodiments of 3D electrode structures tailored for specific applications. Neural recording electrodes typically feature solid conical or pyramidal shapes with exposed tips optimized for signal acquisition. Stimulation electrodes may incorporate porous or high-surface-area designs to maximize charge injection capacity. Chronic implant designs often include anchoring features such as barbs or hooks to improve mechanical stability.  

The 3D printing process enables creation of specialized electrode geometries that would be difficult or impossible to achieve with conventional fabrication methods. Examples include:  

- High-aspect-ratio spike electrodes with tapered geometries that minimize tissue damage during insertion  
- Porous electrode structures with interconnected voids that promote tissue integration  
- Biomimetic designs featuring mosquito proboscis-inspired tips for reduced insertion force  
- Scaffold-like structures with internal channels for delivery of neurotrophic factors  
- Arrays with varying electrode heights to match anatomical curvature  

The resulting 3D multielectrode arrays provide significant advantages over conventional devices. The increased surface-area-to-footprint ratio improves both recording sensitivity and stimulation efficiency. The three-dimensional geometry allows for closer integration with neural tissue structures. Customizable electrode shapes can be optimized for specific applications and anatomical targets. The integrated fabrication process enables wafer-scale production of high-density arrays with precisely controlled electrode geometries.  

These advanced 3D multielectrode arrays enable new capabilities in neural interface technology. Potential applications include high-density cortical recording arrays for brain-machine interfaces, precision stimulation electrodes for neuroprosthetics, and customizable research tools for systems neuroscience. The invention represents a significant advancement over existing planar electrode technologies, offering improved performance and expanded functionality through innovative three-dimensional designs.
