## BACKGROUND  

Existing methods for interrogating biological systems provide valuable insights but suffer from significant limitations. Genome analysis, for instance, enables the study of DNA sequences but does not directly reveal protein structures or interactions. Exome analysis focuses on coding regions but similarly lacks the resolution to assess dynamic conformational changes in proteins. Transcriptome analysis measures RNA expression levels but cannot determine solvent accessibility or tertiary protein structures. Proteome analysis offers a broader perspective on protein composition but often fails to capture transient structural modifications or binding-induced conformational shifts.  

Current techniques for protein footprinting, such as Hydrogen Deuterium Exchange (HDX) and hydroxyl radical footprinting (HRF), are constrained by technical challenges. HDX requires rapid processing at low temperatures to prevent back-exchange and relies on acidic conditions that limit protease specificity. HRF methods, including fast photochemical oxidation of proteins (FPOP) and synchrotron radiolysis, face barriers such as the need for hydrogen peroxide (FPOP) or access to specialized radiation facilities (synchrotron). These limitations hinder widespread adoption and restrict the ability to study proteins in their native states under diverse conditions.  

## SUMMARY  

The present invention introduces a plasma-induced oxidation method for modifying biological molecules in a sample. The method involves generating plasma within a fluid or sample to convert marker radical precursors into reactive marker radicals. These radicals diffuse into the sample and interact with biological molecules, inducing modifications that reveal structural and solvent accessibility information. The plasma can be generated as a single pulse or a sequence of pulses, with adjustable voltage, pulse width, and frequency to control radical concentration and exposure duration.  

The invention further encompasses systems for implementing the method, including a sample chamber with electrodes, a power supply, and a control system. A plasma jet configuration enables precise delivery of plasma to the sample, while cooling mechanisms prevent denaturation. The system may include automated loading, temperature sensors, and feedback controls to optimize performance.  

Compositions of matter are also disclosed, comprising biological molecules mixed with marker radical precursors in a fluid medium. Synthetic biological molecules with predictable oxidation responses are included, as are kits containing reference samples and instructions for system configuration.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

The invention pertains to methods, systems, and compositions for plasma-induced oxidation of biological molecules. The scope includes modifications to proteins, nucleic acids, and other biomolecules to assess solvent accessibility, structural changes, and interactions.  

### Methods  

The method of modifying a biological molecule begins by generating plasma in a fluid or sample. A time-varying high-voltage signal is applied to create a dielectric-barrier discharge, producing microsecond bursts of hydroxyl radicals. The plasma is sustained for a predetermined duration, allowing radicals to diffuse into the sample and react with accessible regions of the biological molecule.  

Key parameters include plasma voltage (1–31 kV), pulse frequency (0–15 kHz), and exposure time (e.g., 5–90 seconds). The system elevates the sample temperature but limits it to prevent denaturation. The concentration of marker radical precursors, such as water or gaseous feedstocks, is adjustable to control oxidation levels.  

The method supports high-throughput analysis by processing multiple samples or multiplexing biological molecules in a single sample. Buffer solutions maintain pH stability, while cleavage factors (e.g., proteases) expose buried regions for subsequent oxidation. Oxidation patterns are analyzed via mass spectrometry, gel electrophoresis, or sequencing to determine solvent accessibility and structural features.  

### Systems  

A system for modifying biological molecules includes a sample chamber with a ground electrode and a plasma electrode connected to a high-voltage power supply. The chamber is constructed from dielectric materials and may feature translation devices to position the sample or plasma source. A control system optimizes operational parameters, such as pulse width and frequency, via a user interface.  

In a plasma jet configuration, the system directs plasma through a nozzle into the sample, enabling localized treatment. Cooling devices maintain sample integrity, while gas manifolds regulate feedgas composition. Analytical devices, such as mass spectrometers, are integrated for real-time monitoring.  

### Compositions of Matter  

The invention includes compositions comprising a biological molecule, a fluid medium, and a marker radical precursor. Synthetic biological molecules with known oxidation profiles are disclosed, as are mixtures of such molecules for calibration purposes.  

### Kits  

Kits for plasma-induced oxidation contain reference samples, marker radical precursors, and instructional materials for system setup. Optional components include buffer solutions, cleavage factors, and analytical standards.  

## EXAMPLE 1  

### Labeling Cytochrome C  

A plasma generation system was configured with a 25 kV power supply and 10 kHz pulse frequency. Cytochrome C in phosphate-buffered saline was treated for 5–60 seconds, and oxidation was quantified via mass spectrometry. Dose-dependent modification was observed, with solvent-accessible residues showing higher reactivity.  

## EXAMPLE 2  

### Labeling Bovine Serum Albumin  

Native and predigested BSA samples were exposed to plasma for 15–60 seconds. Digested BSA exhibited increased oxidation in previously buried regions, confirming the method’s ability to probe solvent accessibility without denaturing intact proteins.  

## EXAMPLE 3  

### Breakdown of DNA in Size-Dependent and Exposure-Dose-Dependent Fashion  

Plasma treatment of DNA samples induced strand breaks proportional to exposure time. Electrophoresis revealed size-dependent fragmentation patterns, demonstrating the method’s applicability to nucleic acids.  

## EXAMPLE 4  

### Protein Labeling in Intact/Live Cells  

Live *E. coli* cells were treated with plasma, and intracellular proteins were subsequently analyzed. Oxidation was detected in solvent-accessible regions, validating the method’s compatibility with cellular systems.  

## EXAMPLE 5  

### Comparison of Oxidation of Intact and Denatured Cytochrome C  

Intact cytochrome C showed limited oxidation in buried regions, whereas denatured samples exhibited uniform modification. This contrast confirmed the method’s sensitivity to conformational states.  

## EXAMPLE 6  

### Comparison of Oxidation of Native and Digested Bovine Serum Albumin  

Digestion increased oxidation in hydrophobic domains of BSA, enabling mapping of solvent-inaccessible regions.  

## EXAMPLE 7  

### Study of Solvent Accessibility of Protein Bound and Unbound by a Ligand  

EGFR was treated with and without EGF. Oxidation decreased in dimerization interface residues upon ligand binding, aligning with crystallographic data.
