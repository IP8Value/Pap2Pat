# DESCRIPTION  

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention relates to novel antibiotic derivatives and their synergistic combinations for use in treating bacterial infections. More particularly, the invention provides improved lankacidin C (LC) and lankamycin (LM) derivatives that bind simultaneously to adjacent sites in the bacterial ribosome, resulting in enhanced antibacterial activity through synergistic mechanisms. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions containing these antibiotic combinations, methods for their production, and therapeutic applications for preventing and treating bacterial infections.  

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION  

Ribosomal interfering antibiotics represent a critical class of antimicrobial agents that inhibit protein synthesis by binding to functional regions of the bacterial ribosome. Extensive crystallographic studies over the past decade have elucidated the precise binding sites of numerous antibiotics within the ribosomal structure. The large ribosomal subunit contains two primary antibiotic binding sites: the peptidyl transferase center (PTC) and the nascent peptide exit tunnel (NPET). The PTC serves as the binding site for phenicols, lincosamides, pleuromutilins, and oxazolidinones, while the NPET accommodates macrolides and streptogramin B compounds. These antibiotics inhibit successive steps in protein synthesis - peptide bond formation at the PTC and nascent chain progression through the NPET.  

The simultaneous inhibition of consecutive steps in protein synthesis often results in synergistic antibiotic effects. Nature has exploited this principle through the evolution of synergistic antibiotic combinations such as streptogramins, which consist of two structurally distinct compounds produced by Streptomyces species. Streptogramin A (SA) components bind the PTC, while streptogramin B (SB) components target the NPET. Although individually weak antibiotics, streptogramin combinations exhibit potent synergistic activity, as demonstrated by the clinically successful formulation Synercid (dalfopristin/quinupristin) for treating Gram-positive infections.  

Lankacidin C (LC) and lankamycin (LM) represent another pair of structurally distinct antibiotics produced by Streptomyces rochei 7434AN4. LC is a 17-membered macrocyclic compound with antibiotic and antitumor activities, while LM is a 14-membered macrolide structurally similar to erythromycin but with weaker antibacterial activity. Although these compounds are co-produced and their biosynthesis appears co-regulated, prior to this invention their ribosomal binding sites and potential functional interactions remained unknown. Previous studies indicated LC competes with chloramphenicol for ribosome binding, suggesting PTC targeting, while LM's structural similarity to erythromycin implied NPET binding.  

The present invention resolves these uncertainties through detailed crystallographic and biochemical analyses, revealing that LC and LM bind simultaneously to adjacent sites in the ribosome and exhibit synergistic antibacterial effects. These findings provide the foundation for developing improved antibiotic derivatives and combinations with enhanced therapeutic properties.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention is based on crystallographic and biochemical studies elucidating the ribosomal binding sites and mechanisms of action of lankacidin C (LC) and lankamycin (LM). These studies revealed that LC and LM bind simultaneously to adjacent sites in the large ribosomal subunit - LC to the peptidyl transferase center (PTC) and LM to the nascent peptide exit tunnel (NPET) - resulting in synergistic inhibition of bacterial protein synthesis.  

LC binds the PTC in a shallow depression near the A-site, forming van der Waals interactions with nucleotides U2504, G2505, and U2506. The 2-methyl group of LC's lactone ring inserts into a hydrophobic crevice formed by A2451 and C2452, overlapping with the aminoacyl-tRNA binding site. LC binding induces structural rearrangements in several ribosomal RNA residues, creating a unique network of interactions that stabilizes the antibiotic's position. These interactions include hydrogen bonding between LC's carbonyl oxygen and G2061, and stacking interactions involving G2576, G2505, C2610, and G2581.  

LM binds the NPET in an orientation similar to erythromycin but with distinct interactions. While both antibiotics protect nucleotides A2058 and A2059 from dimethyl sulfate modification, LM exhibits weaker binding affinity (IC50 = 275 μM) compared to erythromycin. Key differences include LM's C5-linked sugar moiety, which differs from erythromycin's desosamine sugar and contributes to LM's ability to bind simultaneously with LC. RNA probing experiments demonstrate that LC and LM can simultaneously protect distinct ribosomal residues (U2506/U2585 by LC; U2609 by LM), confirming concurrent binding.  

The structural basis for LC/LM synergism resembles that of streptogramins, where PTC-binding SA components and NPET-binding SB components act cooperatively. However, LC and LM exhibit unique binding properties enabling their simultaneous interaction despite their proximity. Evolutionary optimization has apparently selected for LM structural features that permit coexistence with LC in the ribosome, unlike erythromycin which competes with LC for binding.  

These findings enable rational design of improved antibiotic derivatives and combinations. Structural modifications can enhance synergistic effects by:  

1) Strengthening individual antibiotic binding to their respective sites  
2) Optimizing interactions between simultaneously bound antibiotics  
3) Reducing steric clashes that might prevent concurrent binding  
4) Improving pharmacological properties while maintaining synergistic activity  

Specific LM derivatives include deoxy-LM, desoamine-LM, C-15 LM, and 3-O-arcanosyl LM, each designed to enhance NPET binding and synergistic interactions with LC. Similarly, LC derivatives can be optimized for improved PTC binding and complementary interactions with LM or other NPET-targeting antibiotics.  

### Lankacidin Binding Site  

The lankacidin binding pocket in the PTC is characterized by extensive interactions with ribosomal RNA components. The macrocyclic ring of LC fits into a shallow depression in the A-site wall, forming van der Waals contacts with U2504, G2505, and U2506. The 2-methyl group at the lactone edge inserts into a hydrophobic cleft formed by A2451 and C2452, which also accommodates the aminoacyl moiety of A-site tRNA.  

Key hydrogen bonds include:  
- LC carbonyl oxygen O1 with G2061 N2 (2.8 Å)  
- LC O4 with G2505 N2 (3.1 Å)  
- LC O3 with U2506 O4 (2.9 Å)  

LC binding induces significant conformational changes in ribosomal RNA, including:  
- 45° rotation of A2602  
- Stacking of G2576 upon G2505  
- Stacking of C2610 upon G2581  
- Formation of a non-canonical G2505-C2610 base pair  

These structural rearrangements create a stabilized binding environment for LC while potentially interfering with tRNA positioning during protein synthesis. The binding site overlaps with those of chloramphenicol, dalfopristin, and virginiamycin M, explaining LC's competition with these antibiotics. However, LC's unique interactions enable simultaneous binding with LM, unlike other PTC-targeting antibiotics.  

### Lankamycin Binding Site  

Lankamycin binds the NPET in an orientation similar to erythromycin but with distinct interactions contributing to its synergistic relationship with LC. The macrolide ring of LM occupies the upper NPET region near the PTC, with its C5-linked sugar extending toward the tunnel wall.  

Key interactions include:  
- Hydrogen bonds between LM's 3-OH and A2058 N1 (3.0 Å)  
- Hydrophobic contacts between LM's lactone ring and A2058/A2059  
- Stacking of LM's cladinose sugar with G2505  

Notable differences from erythromycin binding include:  
- Absence of strong hydrogen bonding with A2058 via desosamine  
- Different orientation of the C5 sugar moiety  
- Weaker overall binding affinity (IC50 275 μM vs 0.1 μM for erythromycin)  

These differences enable LM to bind simultaneously with LC despite their proximity, whereas erythromycin competes with LC for ribosome binding. RNA probing shows LM protects U2609 from CMCT modification while LC protects U2506/U2585, with both patterns observable during simultaneous binding.  

### LC and LM Act Synergistically Upon Bacterial Cells  

The synergistic antibacterial effect of LC and LM has been demonstrated through both in vitro and in vivo experiments. In checkerboard assays using S. aureus, the combination shows a concave fractional inhibitory concentration (FIC) curve characteristic of synergism, with FIC indices <0.5.  

Mechanistically, the synergy arises from:  
1) Simultaneous inhibition of consecutive protein synthesis steps  
2) Allosteric enhancement of binding affinity when both antibiotics are present  
3) Structural compatibility enabling concurrent ribosome binding  

This synergism mirrors that of streptogramins but with distinct structural advantages. The smaller size of LC/LM compared to streptogramins may permit better tissue penetration while maintaining effective ribosomal inhibition.  

## Materials and Methods  

### Crystallography  

Crystals of D. radiodurans 50S ribosomal subunits were grown and soaked in solutions containing 0.025 mM lankacidin for 20 hours at 20°C. X-ray diffraction data were collected at synchrotron beamlines using cryo-cooled crystals. Data processing utilized HKL2000, with structure refinement performed using CNS and model building in Coot. Ribosome-antibiotic interactions were analyzed with LigPlot and LPC, with figures generated using PyMol.  

### Antibiotic Binding Assays  

Binding affinity was determined by incubating D50S subunits with radiolabeled antibiotics followed by gel filtration separation. Bound radioactivity was measured by scintillation counting. Competition experiments assessed displacement of labeled erythromycin by LC.  

### Protein Synthesis Inhibition  

Cell-free transcription-translation systems (E. coli) measured inhibition of luciferase expression by antibiotics. Peptidyl transferase activity was assayed via puromycin reaction inhibition using S. aureus 70S ribosomes or D50S subunits.  

### RNA Probing  

Chemical modification of 23S rRNA used CMCT and DMS to probe antibiotic-induced protections. Modifications were detected by primer extension analysis. Simultaneous binding was confirmed by observing distinct protection patterns from LC and LM together.
