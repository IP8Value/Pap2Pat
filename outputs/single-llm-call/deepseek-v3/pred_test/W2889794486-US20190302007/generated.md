## BACKGROUND  

Optical extinction and absorption spectroscopy has been a fundamental analytical tool for over 150 years, enabling the quantification of molecular constituents in complex media. Ultraviolet-visible (UV-VIS) spectrophotometry emerged in the 1940s as a standard laboratory technique for chemical analysis across disciplines including materials science, physical chemistry, and molecular biology. In conventional UV-VIS spectrophotometry, the portion of incident light that fails to reach the detector is presumed to be absorbed by the medium. However, this assumption neglects the significant contribution of light scattering, which can substantially distort measured spectra.  

Light scattering arises from refractive index mismatches at internal interfaces within complex media, causing photons to be redirected rather than absorbed. While absorption represents an intrinsic molecular property, scattering depends on extrinsic factors such as medium morphology and structure. For simple samples like pure liquids, absorption dominates spectral measurements. However, for complex samples including biological tissues, blood, nanoparticle suspensions, polymer films, and plasmonic nanostructures, scattering contributions can obscure molecular absorption signatures. This limitation parallels the visual analogy of a coin visible in water but undetectable in milk, despite similar absorption properties between the two liquids.  

The challenge of isolating absorption from scattering in UV-VIS spectra has been recognized for decades, prompting development of various optical techniques to mitigate scattering effects. However, these methods often prove too complex for integration into conventional spectrometers or multispectral devices. The integrating sphere method represents one approach to independently measure absorption and scattering, but requires meticulous calibration across wavelengths and depends heavily on sample geometry and chamber design. Time-resolved techniques analyzing ultra-short laser pulse propagation through media can separate absorption from scattering, but require specialized femtosecond lasers and sophisticated detection systems unsuitable for routine measurements.  

Plasmonic nanoparticles, particularly gold nanoparticles (GNPs), present additional challenges as they exhibit both strong absorption and scattering properties that vary with size, shape, and local environment. While theoretical models can predict absorption and scattering components for ideal nanoparticles, synthesized particles often deviate from ideal characteristics, making theoretical corrections impractical. Photothermal spectroscopy methods have shown promise in measuring pure absorption spectra at single-particle scales, but fail in environments with strong scattering backgrounds.  

## SUMMARY  

The present invention provides a non-contact photoacoustic spectrophotometry system capable of accurately measuring optical absorption spectra independent of scattering contributions. The system comprises a tunable time-modulated light source, a non-contact optical detector, an interferometer, and a processor configured to construct absorption spectra from detected signals.  

In one embodiment, the tunable light source comprises a diode-pumped nanosecond laser rapidly switchable across wavelengths from 700-900 nm. The non-contact detector employs a fiber-optic Sagnac interferometer to measure ultrasound signals generated by photoacoustic effects. The system further includes a sample chamber with XY translation stage for spatial averaging and a reflective coating optimized for 1550 nm probe light.  

The method involves directing modulated laser pulses into a sample, where absorbed energy generates acoustic waves detected at the sample surface. The processor correlates signal amplitudes with absorption coefficients, compensating for scattering effects through narrow beam geometry that minimizes fluence dependence on optical properties. Calibration against standards enables absolute absorption measurements across a broad dynamic range.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

The invention exploits the photoacoustic effect, where time-modulated light absorption generates ultrasonic waves proportional to the absorption coefficient. Unlike conventional photoacoustic spectroscopy requiring direct sample contact, the present system uses optical interferometry for non-contact detection. This innovation overcomes limitations of both UV-VIS spectrophotometry and contact-based photoacoustic methods.  

The system addresses fundamental limitations of the Bouguer-Lambert-Beer law, which fails in scattering media where light attenuation doesn't follow simple exponential decay. Scattering redistributes photons spatially rather than absorbing them, causing conventional spectrophotometers to overestimate absorption. The invention's narrow excitation beam (≤0.5 mm diameter) ensures detected signals primarily reflect absorption by maintaining beam dimensions below the photon transport mean free path.  

Key system components include:  
1) A tunable pulsed laser (700-900 nm) with rapid wavelength switching (<1 ms) and high repetition rates (≥1 kHz)  
2) A sample chamber with quartz windows and reflective coating  
3) A fiber-optic Sagnac interferometer detecting surface displacements from photoacoustic waves  
4) An XY translation stage for spatial averaging  
5) Signal processing electronics and calibration algorithms  

In operation, the system excites photoacoustic signals in a sample's subsurface layer. The interferometer detects surface displacements induced by acoustic waves, with signal amplitude proportional to local absorption. Spatial averaging across multiple measurement points compensates for sample heterogeneity. Calibration against standards like cupric sulfate solutions establishes quantitative absorption coefficients.  

The system demonstrates particular utility for characterizing plasmonic nanoparticles, where it accurately resolves absorption components obscured by scattering in conventional UV-VIS measurements. Applications extend to biological samples, enabling measurement of nanoparticle absorption spectra within cells despite strong cellular scattering backgrounds. This capability supports optimization of nanoparticle applications in sensing, imaging, and therapy by providing true absorption spectra unaffected by scattering environments.  

Potential applications include:  
- Flow cytometry with absorption-based nanoparticle detection  
- Point-of-care pathology using absorption signatures  
- Spatially localized measurements in complex media  
- Integration with conventional UV-VIS spectrophotometers  

The system's non-contact nature facilitates incorporation into existing analytical platforms while maintaining measurement accuracy across diverse sample types. By providing scattering-insensitive absorption spectra, the invention enables new applications in analytical chemistry, biomedical research, and materials characterization.
