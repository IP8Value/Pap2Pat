## FIELD OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention relates generally to the field of bacterial quorum sensing and specifically to novel compounds and methods for activating quorum sensing pathways in Vibrio species to inhibit virulence factor production. More particularly, the invention provides small molecule inhibitors targeting the LuxO response regulator in Vibrio cholerae and related pathogenic bacteria.  

## BACKGROUND  

Quorum sensing (QS) represents a fundamental mechanism of bacterial cell-cell communication that enables coordinated population-wide behaviors in response to changes in cell density. This process relies on the production, detection, and response to extracellular signaling molecules called autoinducers. Through QS, bacterial communities synchronously regulate diverse physiological processes including bioluminescence, sporulation, biofilm formation, and virulence factor production.  

The QS process initiates when bacteria produce and release autoinducer molecules into their environment. As bacterial population density increases, the extracellular concentration of these signaling molecules rises proportionally. When a threshold concentration is reached, the autoinducers bind to specific receptor proteins, triggering intracellular signal transduction cascades that ultimately alter gene expression patterns across the entire bacterial population.  

In pathogenic bacteria, QS frequently controls the production of virulence factors that mediate infection and disease pathogenesis. The relationship between QS and virulence has motivated the development of therapeutic strategies that target QS pathways to attenuate bacterial pathogenicity. Such anti-virulence approaches offer potential advantages over traditional antibiotics by potentially reducing selective pressure for resistance development.  

Vibrio cholerae, the causative agent of cholera, employs a particularly sophisticated QS system involving multiple autoinducer molecules and signaling pathways. The V. cholerae QS circuit regulates virulence in a unique manner compared to other pathogens. While many bacteria activate virulence factor production at high cell density (HCD) through QS, V. cholerae actually represses virulence genes during the HCD phase. This inverse regulation pattern aligns with the organism's disease cycle, where virulence factors expressed at low cell density (LCD) promote intestinal colonization, while their subsequent repression at HCD facilitates environmental dissemination.  

The V. cholerae QS system utilizes two primary autoinducers: CAI-1 ((S)-3-hydroxytridecan-4-one) and AI-2 ((2S,4S)-2-methyl-2,3,3,4-tetrahydroxytetrahydrofuran borate). These molecules are detected by membrane-bound receptors CqsS and LuxPQ, respectively. In the absence of autoinducers (at LCD), these receptors function as kinases, initiating a phosphorylation cascade that activates the response regulator LuxO. Active LuxO~P promotes transcription of small regulatory RNAs (Qrr1-4) that ultimately repress production of the master QS regulator HapR while activating production of AphA.  

At HCD, autoinducer binding switches the receptors from kinases to phosphatases, leading to LuxO dephosphorylation and inactivation. This results in decreased Qrr sRNA production, allowing HapR accumulation and AphA repression. HapR then modulates expression of hundreds of downstream genes, including virulence factors.  

The central role of LuxO in the V. cholerae QS pathway makes it an attractive target for therapeutic intervention. LuxO belongs to the NtrC family of response regulators, which typically contain three functional domains: an N-terminal receiver domain for phosphorylation, a central AAA+ ATPase domain, and a C-terminal DNA-binding domain. LuxO's ATPase activity is essential for its transcriptional activation function at σ54-dependent promoters.  

While several inhibitors targeting two-component system histidine kinases have been reported, developing effective inhibitors of response regulators has proven challenging due to the specificity of their molecular interactions. Moreover, no small molecule inhibitors targeting NtrC-family ATPase domains have been previously described. The present invention addresses this unmet need by providing the first known inhibitors of LuxO ATPase activity that can activate QS and repress virulence in Vibrio species.  

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention provides novel strategies for activating quorum sensing in Vibrio species through targeted inhibition of the LuxO response regulator. The invention encompasses several classes of small molecule compounds that either act as agonists of QS receptors or as direct inhibitors of LuxO ATPase activity.  

Key aspects of the invention include virulence inhibitor molecules that activate QS by targeting LuxO, thereby prematurely inducing the high cell density gene expression program that normally represses virulence factor production. These compounds represent innovative anti-infective agents that operate through anti-virulence mechanisms rather than traditional bactericidal or bacteriostatic actions.  

The invention describes specific compounds capable of activating QS in V. cholerae, including Class 2 molecules featuring a conserved 5-thio-6-azauracil core structure. These LuxO inhibitors function uncompetitively by binding to the LuxO-ATP complex and preventing ATP hydrolysis, thereby blocking LuxO's transcriptional activation function.  

Methods are provided for inhibiting virulence in Vibrio species by administering these QS-activating compounds. The inhibitors can be used prophylactically to prevent initiation of the virulence program or therapeutically to repress ongoing virulence factor production. Additionally, the compounds demonstrate efficacy in inhibiting biofilm formation, another QS-regulated process important for Vibrio pathogenesis.  

The LuxO inhibitors exhibit broad-spectrum activity across multiple Vibrio species due to the high conservation of LuxO in these bacteria. This includes pathogens such as V. parahaemolyticus and V. vulnificus that utilize different autoinducer molecules than V. cholerae. The compounds' ability to target the conserved ATPase domain rather than species-specific signaling components underlies this broad activity.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION  

The invention provides compositions and methods for activating quorum sensing in Vibrio species by inhibiting the LuxO response regulator. Two classes of QS-activating molecules have been identified through high-throughput screening: Class 1 compounds that act as agonists of the CqsS receptor, and Class 2 compounds that directly inhibit LuxO ATPase activity.  

The Class 2 LuxO inhibitors represent a particularly innovative aspect of the invention. These compounds feature a 5-thio-6-azauracil core structure with variable hydrocarbon side chains that influence potency. The most potent inhibitors contain bulky, hydrophobic terminal groups such as tert-butyl carbinol moieties. These molecules activate QS in both wild-type V. cholerae and strains expressing the constitutively active LuxO D47E variant, confirming their direct action on LuxO rather than upstream signaling components.  

Mechanistic studies reveal that the LuxO inhibitors function uncompetitively by binding to the LuxO-ATP complex and preventing ATP hydrolysis. This inhibition is specific to the ATPase activity, as the compounds do not affect LuxO's ability to bind DNA or participate in phosphorylation/dephosphorylation. The inhibitors interact with a region adjacent to the Walker B motif in the AAA+ ATPase domain, as demonstrated by isolation of inhibitor-resistant LuxO mutants with specific amino acid substitutions (I211F, L215F, L242F, and V294L).  

The LuxO inhibitors demonstrate broad efficacy in activating QS across multiple Vibrio species, including V. cholerae, V. harveyi, and V. parahaemolyticus. This cross-species activity stems from the high conservation of LuxO's ATPase domain in these bacteria. The compounds can induce QS-controlled phenotypes such as bioluminescence in V. harveyi and colony opacity changes in V. parahaemolyticus.  

Importantly, the LuxO inhibitors significantly reduce production of key virulence factors in pathogenic vibrios. In V. cholerae, treatment with these compounds decreases production of the toxin co-regulated pilus (TCP) component TcpA while increasing HapR levels. Similarly, in V. parahaemolyticus, the inhibitors reduce production and secretion of the type III secretion system effector VopD. These molecular changes translate to reduced cytotoxicity in cellular infection models, demonstrating the compounds' potential as anti-virulence agents.  

The LuxO inhibitors possess several favorable pharmacological properties including water solubility, stability, and low cytotoxicity to mammalian cells. Their specific mechanism of action targeting the AAA+ ATPase domain suggests potential for development as general inhibitors of NtrC-family response regulators. The compounds' thio-azauracil core structure may serve as a scaffold for designing inhibitors of other AAA+ ATPases across different biological systems.  

### Materials and Methods  

The bacterial strains used in developing this invention include derivatives of wild-type V. cholerae C6706str, V. harveyi BB120, and V. parahaemolyticus strains provided by Dr. Linda McCarter. Escherichia coli strains S17-1 pir, DH5α, and Top10 were utilized for cloning purposes. All strains were cultured under standard conditions appropriate for each species, with antibiotic selection where necessary.  

High-throughput screening for V. cholerae QS-activating molecules employed a library of 90,000 compounds. Screening strains included V. cholerae BH1578 (ΔcqsA ΔluxS pBB1) and BH1651 (luxO D47E pBB1), which were grown in microtiter plates with test compounds. Light production was measured after incubation to identify molecules inducing QS responses.  

Bioluminescence assays for quantifying QS activation used reporter strains grown in appropriate media with serial dilutions of test compounds. Luminescence and optical density measurements were taken following incubation to calculate induction levels.  

Protein purification methods involved cloning the luxO D47E gene into expression vectors, transforming into E. coli BL21 Gold (DE3), and inducing protein production with IPTG. His-tagged LuxO D47E was purified using nickel affinity chromatography followed by concentration and storage at -80°C. Similar protocols were used for purifying other LuxO variants and E. coli NtrC.  

ATPase activity assays employed a coupled-enzyme system measuring ADP production from ATP hydrolysis. Reactions contained LuxO D47E, ATP, and test compounds in buffer with necessary cofactors. Rates of ATP hydrolysis were determined by monitoring NADH oxidation spectrophotometrically.  

DNA binding studies used electrophoretic mobility shift assays and fluorescence anisotropy to assess LuxO's interaction with qrr promoter DNA in the presence and absence of inhibitors. These experiments confirmed that the compounds do not affect LuxO's DNA binding capability.  

Genetic screening for LuxO mutants resistant to inhibition involved random mutagenesis of the luxO D47E gene followed by selection in V. cholerae for clones maintaining dark phenotypes in the presence of inhibitors. Resistant mutants were sequenced to identify specific amino acid changes conferring resistance.  

Western blot analyses measured changes in virulence factor production (TcpA in V. cholerae and VopD in V. parahaemolyticus) following treatment with LuxO inhibitors. Cytotoxicity assays evaluated compound effects on V. parahaemolyticus-mediated lysis of HeLa cells by measuring lactate dehydrogenase release.  

### Chemical Reactions  

The synthesis of representative LuxO inhibitors proceeded through established organic chemistry protocols. General procedures involved condensation reactions to form the 5-thio-6-azauracil core followed by side chain modifications. Key synthetic targets included:  

Neopentyl 2-((3,5-dioxo-2,3,4,5-tetrahydro-1,2,4-triazin-6-yl)thio)acetate was prepared by reacting 6-mercapto-1,2,4-triazine-3,5(2H,4H)-dione with neopentyl bromoacetate in the presence of base. The product was purified by silica gel chromatography to yield white crystals.  

Isobutyl 2-((3,5-dioxo-2,3,4,5-tetrahydro-1,2,4-triazin-6-yl)thio)acetate was synthesized similarly using isobutyl bromoacetate. The reaction proceeded at room temperature in DMF with triethylamine as base, affording the product in good yield after aqueous workup.  

Cyclobutylmethyl 2-((3,5-dioxo-2,3,4,5-tetrahydro-1,2,4-triazin-6-yl)thio)acetate was obtained by alkylation of the triazinedione with cyclobutylmethyl bromoacetate. The product exhibited improved solubility in aqueous solutions compared to more hydrophobic analogs.  

Cyclopropylmethyl 2-((3,5-dioxo-2,3,4,5-tetrahydro-1,2,4-triazin-6-yl)thio)acetate was prepared analogously but required longer reaction times due to the decreased reactivity of cyclopropylmethyl bromide derivatives.  

3-methylbutan-2-yl 2-((3,5-dioxo-2,3,4,5-tetrahydro-1,2,4-triazin-6-yl)thio)acetate represented a key intermediate in structure-activity studies, demonstrating the importance of branched side chains for potency.  

(Trimethylsilyl)methyl 2-((3,5-dioxo-2,3,4,5-tetrahydro-1,2,4-triazin-6-yl)thio)acetate was synthesized to explore sterically bulky side chain effects. This compound showed moderate activity but poor aqueous solubility.  

3,3-dimethylbutan-2-yl 2-((3,5-dioxo-2,3,4,5-tetrahydro-1,2,4-triazin-6-yl)thio)acetate proved to be one of the most potent inhibitors, featuring the optimal tert-butyl carbinol side chain. This compound served as the basis for further analog development.  

2-((3,5-dioxo-2,3,4,5-tetrahydro-1,2,4-triazin-6-yl)thio)acetic acid was prepared as a reference compound for structure-activity comparisons, demonstrating the necessity of ester side chains for effective LuxO inhibition.  

All compounds were characterized by NMR spectroscopy and high-resolution mass spectrometry to confirm structures and purity. Analytical methods included reverse-phase HPLC for purity assessment and UV spectroscopy for concentration determinations.  

## Results  

The invention's key findings include the identification of molecules that activate quorum sensing in V. cholerae by targeting the LuxO response regulator. High-throughput screening yielded two classes of active compounds: Class 1 molecules acting as CqsS receptor agonists and Class 2 molecules inhibiting LuxO ATPase activity.  

Class 2 compounds, featuring a conserved 5-thio-6-azauracil core, demonstrated potent LuxO inhibition. The most active analog (compound 12) exhibited an EC50 of 8.2 µM in bioluminescence assays using the V. cholerae luxO D47E strain. Structure-activity studies revealed that sterically bulky side chains, particularly tert-butyl carbinol moieties, enhanced potency significantly.  

### Investigation of the Targets of the QS Activating Compounds  

Class 2 compounds were shown to act specifically on LuxO rather than other QS components. Experiments using a qrr4-gfp transcriptional fusion demonstrated that these compounds reduce qrr sRNA production by 3-fold without affecting expression of other σ54-dependent genes. This specificity confirmed LuxO as the primary target.  

Testing against various V. cholerae mutants established that Class 2 compounds activate QS in both wild-type LuxO and the constitutively active LuxO D47E variant, indicating their action is independent of LuxO phosphorylation state. The compounds did not affect LuxO's DNA binding affinity, as shown by electrophoretic mobility shift and fluorescence anisotropy assays measuring LuxO-qrr promoter interactions.  

### Structure-Activity-Relationship of Class 2 Compounds  

Systematic modification of the 5-thio-6-azauracil core's side chains revealed critical structure-activity relationships. Increasing steric bulk at the terminal position enhanced potency, with the tert-butyl carbinol derivative (compound 12) showing 3-fold greater activity than the parent compound. Linear alkyl chains or small cyclic groups decreased activity, demonstrating the importance of bulky hydrophobic interactions for effective LuxO inhibition.  

### Class 2 Compounds Inhibit the LuxO ATPase Activity  

Enzymatic assays confirmed that the inhibitors target LuxO's ATP hydrolysis function. Both compounds 11 and 12 decreased the Km and Vmax of the ATPase reaction in dose-dependent manner. Lineweaver-Burk analysis revealed parallel lines for inhibited and uninhibited reactions, characteristic of uncompetitive inhibition where the compounds bind specifically to the LuxO-ATP complex.  

The percent inhibition of LuxO ATPase activity by various analogs correlated strongly with their potency in whole-cell QS activation assays (R2 = 0.91), providing mechanistic validation that ATPase inhibition underlies the compounds' QS-activating effects.  

### A Genetic Screen to Identify LuxO Mutants Resistant to Compound 12  

Random mutagenesis of luxO D47E followed by selection in the presence of compound 12 yielded four inhibitor-resistant mutants carrying single amino acid changes: I211F, L215F, L242F, and V294L. These mutations map to regions adjacent to the Walker B motif in the AAA+ ATPase domain. Purified LuxO D47E/I211F protein showed complete resistance to ATPase inhibition, confirming these residues participate in inhibitor binding.  

### Broad Spectrum Activation of Vibrio QS  

The LuxO inhibitors activated QS in multiple Vibrio species despite differences in their autoinducer systems. In V. harveyi, compound 12 induced 10,000-fold increases in bioluminescence from the luxO D47E mutant. In V. parahaemolyticus, the compounds converted the translucent colony morphology of a luxO* mutant to opaque, mimicking the ΔluxO phenotype. This cross-species activity demonstrates the broad utility of targeting LuxO's conserved ATPase domain.  

### New Chemical Tools for Controlling Virulence in Vibrios  

Treatment with LuxO inhibitors significantly reduced virulence factor production in pathogenic vibrios. Western blots showed decreased TcpA in V. cholerae and reduced VopD secretion in V. parahaemolyticus following compound exposure. These molecular changes translated to functional effects, with compound 12 reducing V. parahaemolyticus cytotoxicity toward HeLa cells by 70% at early time points.  

## Discussion Of Results  

The identification of LuxO inhibitors represents a significant advance in quorum sensing modulation and anti-virulence drug development. These compounds provide both novel chemical probes for studying QS mechanisms and potential leads for therapeutic development against Vibrio infections.  

The Class 2 molecules' uncompetitive inhibition mechanism targeting the LuxO-ATP complex is particularly innovative, representing the first reported small molecules that modulate an NtrC-family response regulator's ATPase activity. The inhibitors' binding site adjacent to the Walker B motif suggests they may induce conformational changes that block ATP hydrolysis without affecting ATP binding itself.  

The LuxO inhibitors' broad activity across Vibrio species stems from their targeting of a highly conserved domain rather than species-specific signaling components. This feature makes them potentially useful against multiple Vibrio pathogens regardless of which autoinducer systems they employ. The compounds' ability to reduce virulence factor production and cytotoxicity supports their potential as anti-infective agents.  

From a drug development perspective, the LuxO inhibitors possess several favorable properties including water solubility, stability, and low mammalian cell cytotoxicity. Their thio-azauracil core structure provides a scaffold for further optimization to improve potency, selectivity, and pharmacological properties.  

The discovery that AAA+ ATPase domains can be selectively inhibited by small molecules has implications beyond quorum sensing. Similar approaches could be applied to other NtrC-family proteins controlling diverse bacterial processes or to eukaryotic AAA+ ATPases involved in various cellular functions.  

Therapeutic use of QS activators represents a novel strategy for acute infections where QS represses virulence. Unlike traditional antibiotics, these compounds could prevent virulence initiation without affecting bacterial growth, potentially reducing resistance development. Prophylactic applications might protect against Vibrio infections by preventing colonization through premature virulence repression.  

In conclusion, the LuxO inhibitors described herein provide powerful new tools for studying bacterial signaling and represent promising leads for anti-virulence drug development. Their unique mechanism of action and broad-spectrum activity against pathogenic vibrios offer significant advantages for combating these important human pathogens.
