## BACKGROUND  

Genetic engineering has revolutionized biotechnology by enabling precise modifications to DNA sequences. Current genome editing methods rely primarily on programmable nucleases that create double-strand breaks, which are then repaired by endogenous cellular mechanisms. However, these methods suffer from limitations including off-target effects, dependence on cellular repair pathways, and inability to function efficiently in non-dividing cells. Site-specific recombinases (SSRs) offer an alternative approach by directly catalyzing DNA strand exchange without requiring double-strand breaks or cellular repair machinery. While SSRs provide advantages such as high efficiency and activity in diverse cell states, their utility has been limited by an inability to readily alter their native substrate specificities. Current methods for evolving recombinase specificity require extensive laboratory effort, typically involving over 100 rounds of evolution to achieve modest changes in target recognition. There remains an unmet need for technologies that can rapidly profile and engineer recombinase specificity to enable their widespread use as genome editing tools.  

## SUMMARY  

The present invention provides methods and compositions for profiling and engineering site-specific recombinase (SSR) activity. The technology, termed Rec-seq, combines in vitro selection with high-throughput sequencing to comprehensively map the DNA sequence preferences of SSRs at single-nucleotide resolution.  

Programmable nucleases such as CRISPR-Cas systems have transformed genome editing but suffer from limitations including dependence on cellular repair pathways and frequent off-target activity. Site-specific recombinases offer distinct advantages as genome editing agents by directly catalyzing DNA strand exchange without creating double-strand breaks. However, current recombinases are limited by their fixed substrate specificities, which are difficult to alter through conventional protein engineering approaches.  

The invention describes phage-assisted continuous evolution (PACE) methods for evolving recombinases to recognize non-native target sequences. PACE enables rapid directed evolution by coupling protein function to phage propagation through continuous culture in lagoons containing host cells. This system allows for the generation of recombinase variants with altered specificity through iterative rounds of mutation and selection.  

The invention further provides methods for evolving recombinases comprising: (a) contacting host cells with phage vectors encoding recombinase variants; (b) incubating the host cells under conditions allowing mutation of the recombinase; (c) isolating replicated phage vectors; and (d) selecting phage vectors encoding recombinases with desired activity on target sequences. The system employs expression constructs in host cells where recombination of target sequences leads to excision of a transcriptional terminator and subsequent expression of genes required for infectious phage particle production.  

Evolved recombinases of the invention recognize non-native target sequences while maintaining high activity. The recombinases comprise amino acid sequences with mutations that alter DNA binding specificity while preserving catalytic function. The target sequences recognized by the evolved recombinases differ from canonical recombinase recognition sites at multiple nucleotide positions while retaining the structural features required for recombination.  

The invention provides methods for engineering nucleic acid molecules using the evolved recombinases. These methods comprise contacting a nucleic acid molecule containing a target sequence with an evolved recombinase under conditions allowing recombination. The methods enable precise genome editing applications including integration of heterologous sequences into safe harbor loci and excision or deletion of specific genomic regions.  

Methods for evaluating recombinase specificity are provided, comprising: (a) providing a recombinase; (b) contacting the recombinase with a library of candidate nucleic acid molecules; (c) identifying recombined molecules; and (d) determining the sequence preferences of the recombinase. These methods employ libraries containing randomized versions of recombinase target sites to comprehensively profile specificity determinants.  

The invention further provides libraries for assessing recombinase target site preferences. These libraries comprise nucleic acid molecules containing partially randomized recombinase target sequences flanked by constant regions. The libraries enable high-throughput characterization of recombinase specificity through deep sequencing of selected substrates.  

The evolved recombinases of the invention provide several advantages over current genome editing technologies. They enable precise DNA modifications without creating double-strand breaks, function in diverse cell types including non-dividing cells, and exhibit high specificity for their target sequences. The recombinases are particularly useful for therapeutic applications requiring precise genome editing with minimal off-target effects.  

Potential applications of the evolved recombinases include: (a) targeted integration of transgenes into safe harbor loci; (b) excision of pathogenic sequences such as integrated viral DNA; (c) deletion of disease-causing mutations; (d) chromosomal rearrangement; and (e) conditional gene regulation through recombinase-mediated excision of regulatory elements. The technology addresses current limitations in genome editing by providing tools for precise DNA modification with reduced cellular toxicity and improved specificity.  

## DEFINITIONS  

Accessory plasmid: A plasmid that provides functions required for viral propagation in phage-assisted continuous evolution systems. The accessory plasmid typically contains a gene required for production of infectious viral particles under control of a conditional promoter that is activated by the desired activity of the evolving protein.  

Cellstat: A continuous culture device that maintains constant cell density through regulated dilution with fresh media. Cellstats are used in phage-assisted continuous evolution to provide a constant flow of fresh host cells to the lagoon.  

Continuous evolution process: A directed evolution method comprising the steps of: (a) providing host cells containing mutagenesis plasmids and accessory plasmids; (b) infecting the host cells with selection phage encoding variants of the protein to be evolved; (c) culturing the infected host cells under conditions allowing mutation and selection; (d) continuously diluting the culture with fresh host cells; and (e) isolating evolved protein variants from replicated phage particles.  

Flow of host cells: The continuous introduction of fresh host cells into an evolution lagoon at a rate sufficient to maintain constant culture density while allowing phage propagation. The flow rate is typically between 1 and 3 lagoon volumes per hour.  

Fresh host cells: Uninfected host cells that are continuously introduced into a phage-assisted continuous evolution system to maintain culture density and provide targets for phage infection.  

Gene of interest: A gene encoding a protein to be evolved, such as a site-specific recombinase. Examples include genes encoding Cre recombinase, Tre recombinase, Brec1 recombinase, Dre recombinase, VCre recombinase, and Bxb1 integrase.  

Helper phage: A viral particle that provides functions required for phage propagation but lacks the gene encoding the protein to be evolved. Helper phage complement selection phage by providing essential viral genes in trans.  

High and low copy number plasmids: Plasmids that are maintained at different copy numbers per cell. High copy number plasmids (e.g., pUC origin) are present at 100-700 copies per cell, while low copy number plasmids (e.g., pSC101 origin) are present at 1-5 copies per cell.  

Host cell: A cell capable of supporting phage propagation and protein evolution. Suitable host cells include prokaryotic cells such as E. coli and eukaryotic cells such as yeast, insect, and mammalian cells.  

Infectious viral particle: A viral particle capable of infecting host cells and initiating viral replication. In phage-assisted continuous evolution, production of infectious viral particles is coupled to the desired activity of the evolving protein.  

Lagoon: The main chamber of a phage-assisted continuous evolution apparatus where host cells, selection phage, and accessory plasmids are maintained in continuous culture under selective conditions.  

Mutagen: An agent that increases mutation frequency, such as a chemical mutagen (e.g., nitrosoguanidine) or a mutagenic plasmid encoding error-prone DNA polymerases.  

Mutagenesis plasmid: A plasmid encoding components of a mutagenic system, such as error-prone DNA polymerases or DNA repair inhibitors, that increases the mutation rate of the gene of interest during evolution.  

Nucleic acid: A polymer of nucleotides including DNA, RNA, and synthetic analogs thereof. Nucleic acids may be single-stranded or double-stranded.  

Phage: A virus that infects bacteria. The invention primarily uses filamentous phage such as M13 for phage-assisted continuous evolution.  

Phage-assisted continuous evolution (PACE): A directed evolution technology that couples protein function to phage propagation through continuous culture in lagoons containing host cells and selection phage.  

Promoter: A DNA sequence that directs transcription of an operably linked gene. Promoters may be constitutive (e.g., lac promoter) or inducible (e.g., arabinose promoter).  

Protein: A polymer of amino acids that performs a biological function. The invention primarily concerns recombinase proteins that catalyze site-specific DNA recombination.  

Replication product: A nucleic acid molecule produced by replication of a template, such as a phage genome or plasmid.  

Selection phage: A viral vector encoding variants of the protein to be evolved. Selection phage propagation is dependent on the desired activity of the encoded protein variant.  

Small molecule: An organic compound with molecular weight less than 1000 daltons. Small molecules may be used to induce or inhibit recombinase activity.  

Turbidostat: A continuous culture device that maintains constant optical density by regulating media flow based on turbidity measurements.  

Vector: A nucleic acid molecule capable of replicating in a host cell and transporting inserted nucleic acids. Vectors include plasmids, viral vectors, and artificial chromosomes.  

Viral life cycle: The process of viral infection, replication, and propagation. The viral life cycle typically includes attachment, entry, replication, assembly, and release stages.  

Viral particle: A complete virus consisting of a nucleic acid genome packaged within a protein capsid.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

The invention provides comprehensive methods and compositions for profiling and engineering site-specific recombinase activity. Recombinase technology offers distinct advantages for genome editing by enabling precise DNA modifications without creating double-strand breaks. However, current recombinases are limited by their fixed substrate specificities, which are difficult to alter through conventional protein engineering approaches.  

Selection strategies for PACE experiments involve coupling recombinase activity to phage propagation through conditional expression of essential phage genes. In one embodiment, successful recombination at a target sequence excises a transcriptional terminator, allowing expression of gene III (pIII) required for infectious phage production. The stringency of selection can be modulated by using different promoters to control pIII expression or by incorporating dominant-negative pIII variants that inhibit phage propagation when undesired recombination events occur.  

Methods for assessing recombinase specificity involve in vitro selection of randomized target site libraries followed by high-throughput sequencing. These methods enable comprehensive profiling of recombinase sequence preferences at single-nucleotide resolution. The specificity profiles reveal critical interactions between recombinase residues and DNA bases, including long-range effects not evident from structural studies alone.  

Directed evolution strategies for recombinases employ continuous culture systems that allow iterative rounds of mutation and selection. Suitable recombinases for evolution include tyrosine recombinases (e.g., Cre, Dre, VCre) and serine integrases (e.g., Bxb1). The evolution process generates variants with altered specificity while maintaining catalytic activity.  

Evolved recombinases find application in diverse genome modification scenarios. They enable precise integration of heterologous nucleic acid sequences into safe harbor loci such as Rosa26 and AAVS1. The recombinases also mediate excision or deletion of specific genomic sequences with high efficiency. Additional applications include chromosomal rearrangement, conditional gene regulation, and excision of pathogenic sequences.  

Safe harbor loci for recombinase-mediated integration have been identified in various species, including mice (Rosa26), humans (AAVS1), and other model organisms. These loci allow stable transgene expression without disrupting endogenous gene function. The invention provides recombinases optimized for targeting these loci with high specificity.  

### Phage-Assisted Continuous Evolution  

Phage-assisted continuous evolution (PACE) provides a powerful platform for evolving recombinase specificity. The PACE system utilizes the M13 bacteriophage, which infects E. coli through the F-pilus. The M13 genome is manipulated to create selection phage that encode recombinase variants and require successful recombination for propagation.  

The PACE process occurs in a lagoon containing host cells, selection phage, and accessory plasmids. Host cells continuously flow into the lagoon while culture supernatant containing phage particles flows out. This continuous culture system enables rapid evolution through iterative rounds of mutation and selection.  

Accessory plasmids provide essential functions for PACE selection. In one embodiment, the accessory plasmid encodes gene III (pIII) under control of a promoter activated by recombinase-mediated excision of a transcriptional terminator. Alternative embodiments employ different selection strategies based on other essential phage genes (e.g., genes I, II, IV, V, VI, VII, VIII, IX, or X).  

Conditional promoter regulation allows tuning of selection stringency. Weak promoters require higher recombinase activity for sufficient pIII expression, while strong promoters allow propagation of phage encoding less active variants. The promoter choice can be adjusted to balance selection pressure with maintenance of sufficient phage diversity.  

Host cell requirements for M13-PACE include E. coli strains containing the F' episome, which enables phage infection. Suitable strains include SS320, XL1-Blue, and ER2738. The host cells may additionally contain mutagenesis plasmids encoding error-prone polymerases to increase mutation rates during evolution.  

### Methods for Evolving Recombinases  

Methods for evolving recombinases comprise contacting host cells with phage vectors encoding recombinase variants and incubating under conditions allowing mutation and selection. The host cells contain expression constructs where recombination at target sequences leads to excision of a transcriptional terminator and expression of genes required for infectious phage production.  

The methods involve isolating replicated phage vectors from culture supernatant and repeating the infection cycle to enrich for variants with desired activity. Negative selection strategies can be implemented by linking undesired recombinase activity (e.g., recognition of off-target sequences) to production of dominant-negative pIII that inhibits phage propagation.  

Mutagenesis plasmids increase the mutation rate of the recombinase gene during evolution. These plasmids may encode components of the E. coli translesion synthesis machinery (e.g., Pol V) or other mutagenic systems. Continuous replenishment of host cells maintains culture density and provides fresh targets for phage infection.  

### Evolved Recombinases  

Evolved recombinases of the invention comprise amino acid sequences with mutations that alter DNA binding specificity while preserving catalytic function. The recombinases recognize target sequences that differ from canonical recognition sites at multiple nucleotide positions while retaining the structural features required for recombination.  

In one embodiment, evolved Cre variants recognize loxLTR or loxBTR sequences that differ from loxP at 50% and 68% of base pairs, respectively. These variants contain mutations at residues that contact DNA (e.g., Arg259, Gln90) as well as distal residues that influence specificity through long-range interactions.  

The target sequences recognized by evolved recombinases are typically 30-50 bp in length and contain inverted repeats flanking an asymmetric core region where strand exchange occurs. The sequences may be derived from endogenous genomic loci (e.g., Rosa26) or designed de novo for specific applications.  

Pharmaceutical compositions comprising evolved recombinases may be administered to cells or organisms for therapeutic genome editing. The compositions may include delivery vehicles such as viral vectors (e.g., AAV, lentivirus) or non-viral delivery systems (e.g., lipid nanoparticles).  

### Methods For Recombinase-Mediated Genetic Engineering  

Methods for engineering nucleic acid molecules using evolved recombinases comprise contacting a target sequence with the recombinase under conditions allowing recombination. The methods enable precise genome modifications including:  

1. Integration of heterologous sequences into specific genomic loci  
2. Excision or deletion of defined DNA regions  
3. Inversion or translocation of chromosomal segments  
4. Replacement of endogenous sequences with modified versions  

The methods are particularly useful for therapeutic applications requiring precise genome editing with minimal off-target effects. For example, evolved recombinases can excise integrated viral DNA from host genomes or correct disease-causing mutations through targeted sequence replacement.  

### Methods for Evaluating the Specificity of Recombinases  

Methods for evaluating recombinase specificity comprise:  

1. Providing a recombinase to be profiled  
2. Contacting the recombinase with a library of candidate nucleic acid molecules containing randomized target sequences  
3. Isolating recombined molecules through selective digestion or other enrichment strategies  
4. Determining the sequence preferences of the recombinase by high-throughput sequencing  

These methods provide comprehensive specificity profiles that reveal preferences at each position in the target site. The profiles enable prediction of off-target activity and identification of critical specificity-determining residues.  

### Libraries for Assessing Recombinase Target Site Preferences  

Libraries for assessing recombinase specificity comprise nucleic acid molecules containing partially randomized target sequences. The libraries typically contain:  

1. A constant hairpin region for primer extension  
2. A randomized recombinase target site (e.g., 13 bp half-sites with 21% non-canonical bases at each position)  
3. A unique molecular identifier (UMI) for quantifying recombination events  

The libraries enable high-throughput characterization of recombinase specificity through deep sequencing of selected substrates. Library complexity typically exceeds 10^11 sequences, allowing comprehensive coverage of possible target site variants.  

### Vectors and Reagents  

The invention provides selection phage with genomes deficient in genes required for infectious phage particles. These selection phage encode the recombinase of interest and require successful recombination for propagation.  

Helper phage complement selection phage by providing essential viral genes in trans. The helper phage genome contains genes required for the phage life cycle but lacks the recombinase gene.  

Mutagenesis plasmids increase mutation rates during evolution. These plasmids may encode error-prone DNA polymerases (e.g., Pol V), deoxyadenosine methylase (Dam), or other mutagenic factors.  

### Expression Constructs  

Expression constructs for evolved recombinases comprise nucleic acids encoding the recombinase operably linked to regulatory elements. The constructs may be plasmids, viral vectors, or linear DNA fragments suitable for delivery to target cells.  

Regulatory elements include promoters (e.g., CMV, EF1α), enhancers, and transcriptional termination sequences. The constructs may additionally encode selectable markers (e.g., antibiotic resistance genes) or reporter genes (e.g., fluorescent proteins).  

### Host Cells  

Host cells for continuous evolution processes include E. coli strains containing the F' episome (e.g., SS320, XL1-Blue). These cells support M13 phage propagation and provide the necessary machinery for recombinase expression and evolution.  

Eukaryotic host cells (e.g., HEK293T, HeLa) may be used for testing evolved recombinase activity in mammalian systems. The cells are typically transfected with recombinase expression constructs and reporter plasmids containing target sequences.  

## EXAMPLES  

### Example 1: Development of PACE Selection for Cre Recombinase  

A PACE selection system was developed to alter the DNA specificity of Cre recombinase. An accessory plasmid (AP) was constructed containing gene III (pIII) under control of a promoter activated by Cre-mediated excision of a transcriptional terminator flanked by loxP sites.  

Host cells (E. coli SS320) containing the AP were infected with selection phage encoding wild-type Cre. Phage propagation was dependent on Cre-mediated excision of the terminator from the AP. After 24 hours of PACE, phage titers increased 100-fold, demonstrating successful coupling of Cre activity to phage propagation.  

### Example 2: Evolution of Cre Variants Recognizing Rosa26 Locus  

Cre variants were evolved to recognize the Rosa26 locus using PACE. The selection phage encoded Cre variants, while the AP contained pIII under control of a promoter activated by recombination at RosaLox sites.  

After 7 days of PACE, phage were harvested and Cre genes sequenced. Evolved variants contained mutations at residues known to contact DNA (Arg259, Gln90) as well as distal residues (Glu262, Lys244). The variants showed 50-fold higher activity on RosaLox sites compared to wild-type Cre while maintaining activity on loxP.  

### Example 3: Specificity Profiling of Evolved Recombinases  

Rec-seq was used to profile the specificity of evolved Cre variants. Libraries containing randomized loxP sites were incubated with recombinase, and recombined products were analyzed by high-throughput sequencing.  

The profiles revealed that evolved variants maintained strong preferences at critical positions (e.g., position 10) while showing relaxed specificity at other sites. The profiles accurately predicted off-target activity in human cells, with variants showing recombination at genomic pseudosites matching the in vitro preferences.  

### Example 4: Therapeutic Application of Evolved Recombinases  

Evolved Cre variants were used to excise integrated HIV provirus from latently infected cells. The variants were delivered via lentiviral vectors and showed efficient provirus excision with minimal off-target effects.  

Excision frequencies exceeded 80% in infected T-cell lines, with no detectable cytotoxicity. The results demonstrate the therapeutic potential of evolved recombinases for treating viral infections and other genetic diseases.
