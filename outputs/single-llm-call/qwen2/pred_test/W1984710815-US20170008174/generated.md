## BACKGROUND

The field of artificial intelligence (AI) has long sought to create machines that can think and behave like humans. One of the key challenges in achieving this goal is the development of robotic systems that can process and interpret sensory data in a manner analogous to human cognition. Traditional AI systems often lack the ability to integrate and correlate data from multiple sensory inputs, leading to a fragmented and incomplete understanding of the environment. The present invention addresses this challenge by providing a Robotic Relational Controller (RRC) that emulates the human brain's ability to relate, correlate, prioritize, and remember sensory input data. This invention is particularly focused on the development of an RRC-Humanoid Robot that can achieve human-like levels of intelligence through the integration of tactile, visual, auditory, olfactory, gustatory, and vestibular sensors.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Section B—the Optical Apparatus Stage (Steps 1 and 2)

The optical apparatus stage of the RRC-Humanoid Robot involves the processing of visual data to form a high-fidelity 3D-photometric image. The process begins with the acquisition of two 2D images from a pair of video cameras mounted on the robotic system. These cameras are designed to converge at a point on the midline line-of-sight (LOS) and are calibrated to form a 3D-photometric image that accurately represents the objects in the field of view (FOV). The FOV-space coordinate frame is defined relative to the tactile-XYZ coordinate frame, ensuring that the visual data is integrated with the tactile data.

The first step in the optical apparatus stage involves defining the FOV-space coordinate frame. This is achieved by mechanically calibrating the FOV-space with the near-space coordinate frame. The cameras are mounted at a specific tilt angle to ensure that the FOV intersects a large segment of the self-location and identification coordinate frame. The second step involves processing the two 2D images to form a 3D-photometric image. This is achieved by measuring the binocular dispersion and image size visual cues, which provide depth information for the pixels in the image.

### Section C—the Interface Circuit Stage (Steps 3, 4, 5, 6)

The interface circuit stage of the RRC-Humanoid Robot involves the processing of raw data from the various sensors to form a format that is compatible with the RRC. This stage is critical for ensuring that the RRC can effectively process and interpret the sensory data. The interface circuit performs several functions, including the processing of auditory raw data, the tuning of collective modalities to specific phoneme sound combinations, and the behavioral speech processing of sequential sets of phoneme sounds.

The auditory raw data consists of the output of sound-receiving microphones that are sensitive to the auditory frequency range of zero to 20,000 cycles per second (cps). These microphones generate a sequence of frequencies and amplitudes as a function of time, which are then inputted to the interface circuit. The interface circuit processes these a-f-t data into collective modalities that are characteristic of 120 phoneme-sound combinations present in the English language. The collective modalities are tuned to the selected phoneme sound combinations using a spectrum analyzer. The interface circuit also performs behavioral speech processing on sequential sets of phoneme sounds, identifying and recognizing these sequences as words or sentences formed by the person speaking to the microphones.

### Section D—the RRC Processing Stage: Training the System to Respond to the 3D-Photometric Image (Steps 7 and 8)

The RRC processing stage involves the training of the RRC to respond to the 3D-photometric image and the processed auditory data. This stage is crucial for enabling the RRC-Humanoid Robot to achieve human-like levels of intelligence. The RRC is designed to relate, correlate, prioritize, and remember input patterns, thereby enabling the robot to identify, recognize, visualize, and comprehend the input signals and patterns as does a human.

The RRC uses a multi-dimensional nodal map module to store and process the input data. The nodal map module is a high-dimensional representation of the input data, which is used to form a contextual form of self-location and identification knowledge. The RRC also uses a hierarchical task diagram (HTD) to organize and prioritize the tasks that the robot can perform. Each task in the HTD has a programmed task selector module (TSM) associated with it, which consists of pattern recognition circuits that are trained to recognize and prioritize input-signal task initiating triggers (TITs).

The training of the RRC involves the use of experiential programming techniques, where the robot is trained to perform specific tasks based on the input data. For example, the robot is trained to repeat and remember the words and sentences spoken by the trainer-supervisor, and to identify and recognize objects in the FOV. The RRC is also trained to control the robotic body and limbs, and the verbal phoneme sound generator, in relation to the words and sentences applied to the multi-dimensional nodal map module.

### Section E—Robotic Visual “Seeing:”

The robotic visual "seeing" capabilities of the RRC-Humanoid Robot are a critical aspect of its human-like intelligence. The robot is designed to "see" the environment in a manner similar to a human, using a 3D-photometric image that is a high-fidelity representation of the objects in the FOV. The visual system of the RRC-Humanoid Robot is validated through a series of reduction to practice experiments, which demonstrate that the robot can detect, identify, and manipulate objects in the FOV, and navigate through complex environments.

The validation experiments involve the use of a motorized robotic arm-wheelchair system, which is equipped with a set of video cameras and tactile sensors. The robotic arm is used to behaviorally program the self-location and identification coordinate frame into the system, and to control the motion of the wheelchair. The video cameras are used to form a 3D-photometric image of the environment, which is processed by the RRC to enable the robot to "see" and navigate through the environment.

The RRC-Humanoid Robot is designed to achieve human-like levels of visual intelligence, enabling it to detect and identify objects with a high level of discrimination and resolution. The robot is also capable of using the 3D-photometric image to navigate through complex environments, such as crowded sidewalks and heavy city traffic. The validation experiments demonstrate that the RRC-Humanoid Robot can achieve human-like levels of visual intelligence, making it a valuable tool in applications where human-like perception and navigation are required.
