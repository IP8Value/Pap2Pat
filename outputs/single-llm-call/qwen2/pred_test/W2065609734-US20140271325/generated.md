# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of materials science has long sought efficient and effective methods for the consolidation of powders into dense, high-performance materials. Traditional sintering techniques often require high temperatures and extended periods, leading to significant energy consumption and potential degradation of material properties. Moreover, achieving full density and maintaining fine-grained structures in sintered materials has been challenging, particularly for refractory metals like tungsten. The present invention addresses these issues by introducing a novel method for the rapid and efficient sintering of nanocrystalline tungsten alloy powders. This method leverages the unique properties of nanocrystalline structures and alloy supersaturation to achieve accelerated sintering, resulting in fully dense materials with ultrafine grain sizes.

## SUMMARY

The invention pertains to a method for the rapid and efficient sintering of nanocrystalline tungsten alloy powders. The method involves mechanically alloying tungsten with a secondary element, such as chromium, to create a supersaturated solid solution with a nanoscale grain structure. The resulting powder is then compacted and sintered under specific conditions that promote phase separation and the formation of a rapid transport layer, leading to accelerated densification. The invention also encompasses the resulting sintered materials, which exhibit high density and ultrafine grain sizes, making them suitable for a wide range of high-performance applications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a method for the rapid and efficient sintering of nanocrystalline tungsten alloy powders. The method involves several key steps: mechanical alloying, compaction, and sintering. Each step is crucial for achieving the desired properties of the final sintered material.

### Mechanical Alloying

The first step in the method is the mechanical alloying of tungsten with a secondary element, such as chromium. This process involves high-energy ball milling of tungsten and the secondary element to create a supersaturated solid solution with a nanoscale grain structure. The resulting powder particles are micron-sized, with each particle containing nanoscale grains. The high-energy ball milling process ensures that the secondary element is fully dissolved in the tungsten matrix, creating a metastable solid solution poised for phase separation upon heating.

### Compaction

Once the nanocrystalline tungsten alloy powder is produced, it is compacted into a desired shape, such as a cylindrical disk. The compaction is typically performed using cold uniaxial pressing at a high pressure, such as 360 MPa, to form a green compact. The green compact is then ready for sintering.

### Sintering

The sintering process is conducted under controlled conditions to promote phase separation and accelerated densification. The green compact is heated in a thermomechanical analyzer (TMA) under a flowing high-purity argon gas containing 3% hydrogen. The heating rate is typically set at 10°C/min, and the temperature is increased to a point where phase separation begins, typically around 950°C. As the temperature increases, a chromium-rich phase precipitates from the supersaturated solid solution, forming necks between the compact particles. This phase separation creates a rapid transport layer that facilitates the diffusion of tungsten and chromium, leading to accelerated densification.

### Kinetics of Nanophase Separation Sintering

The kinetics of the sintering process are influenced by the unique structural changes that occur during heating. The nanocrystalline grain size within the powder particles provides ample short-circuit diffusion pathways, allowing the chromium to out of the particle centers and decorate their surfaces. The chromium-rich phase that precipitates at the particle surfaces and interparticle necks acts as a rapid transport layer, significantly enhancing the rate of densification. The sintering activation energy for this process is approximately 373 kJ/mol, which is close to the activation energy for the diffusion of tungsten in chromium (386 ± 33 kJ/mol). This indicates that the flow of tungsten through the chromium-rich phase is a kinetically rate-limiting process for sintering.

### Control Experiments

To validate the effectiveness of the nanophase separation sintering method, a series of control experiments were conducted. These experiments involved comparing the sintering behavior of various tungsten-chromium materials with different combinations of nanocrystallinity and alloy supersaturation. The results showed that accelerated sintering only occurs when the powders possess both nanocrystalline internal grain sizes and alloy supersaturation. Other conditions, such as nanocrystalline tungsten without chromium, nanocrystalline tungsten with undissolved chromium, and coarse-grained tungsten with dissolved chromium, did not exhibit the same level of accelerated densification.

### Applications

The sintered materials produced using the nanophase separation sintering method exhibit high density and ultrafine grain sizes, making them suitable for a wide range of high-performance applications. These applications include, but are not limited to, high-temperature structural materials, wear-resistant coatings, and components for advanced manufacturing processes. The ability to produce fully dense materials with controlled grain sizes opens up new possibilities for the development of next-generation materials with superior mechanical and thermal properties.

### Non-Limiting Working Examples

#### Example 1: Sintering of W-15 at% Cr

In this example, tungsten and chromium powders were mechanically alloyed using a high-energy ball mill for 20 hours to create a nanocrystalline W-15 at% Cr powder. The resulting powder was compacted into a cylindrical disk and sintered under a flowing high-purity argon gas containing 3% hydrogen. The heating rate was set at 10°C/min, and the temperature was increased to 1500°C. The compact began to noticeably densify at around 950°C and reached nearly full density (>98%) by the time 1500°C was reached. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analyses confirmed the formation of a chromium-rich phase at the particle surfaces and interparticle necks, facilitating rapid densification.

#### Example 2: Sintering of W-35Ti-10Cr

In this example, tungsten, titanium, and chromium powders were mechanically alloyed using a high-energy ball mill for 30 hours to create a nanocrystalline W-35Ti-10Cr powder. The resulting powder was compacted into a cylindrical disk and sintered under the same conditions as Example 1. The addition of titanium promoted the stabilization of the grain structure, resulting in a fully dense material with an ultrafine grain size of 100 nm. This material demonstrated excellent mechanical properties and potential for high-temperature applications.

#### Example 3: Sintering of Cr-15 at% Ni

In this example, chromium and nickel powders were mechanically alloyed using a high-energy ball mill for 15 hours to create a nanocrystalline Cr-15 at% Ni powder. The resulting powder was compacted into a cylindrical disk and sintered under the same conditions as Example 1. The sintering activation energy for this system was measured as 258 kJ/mol, close to the activation energy for the diffusion of chromium in nickel (272 kJ/mol). The micrographs showed that nickel precipitated around chromium necks, leading to accelerated densification.

These examples demonstrate the versatility and effectiveness of the nanophase separation sintering method for producing fully dense, ultrafine-grained materials with superior properties.
