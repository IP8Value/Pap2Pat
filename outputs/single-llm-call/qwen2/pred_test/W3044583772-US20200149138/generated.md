# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

### Technical Field

The present invention relates to the field of metallurgy, specifically to a method for producing a low-carbon steel with a hardened surface layer through sub-rapid solidification. The invention provides a novel approach to achieving high surface hardness and strength in low-carbon steels without the need for additional processing steps such as alloying, rolling, heat treatment, or surface treatment.

### Description of the Related Art

Low-carbon steels are widely used in various industrial applications due to their high ductility and toughness. However, their strength and hardness, particularly surface hardness, are generally inferior to those of other steels. Surface hardening methods such as laser hardening, induction hardening, carburizing, and nitriding have been developed to enhance the surface properties of low-carbon steels. These methods, however, often require additional processing steps, which increase the cost and complexity of production.

In a previous study, a method was developed to produce an as-cast low-carbon steel with a hardened surface layer without introducing extra processing steps. The average Vickers hardness in the surface area of this steel was 420 HV, comparable to the hardness of martensite. Additionally, the steel maintained similar ductility and toughness to ordinary low-carbon steels.

The microstructure of the hardened surface layer in low-carbon steels can significantly influence their mechanical properties. Different microstructures, such as lath martensite, upper bainite, and acicular ferrite, exhibit varying degrees of hardness and strength. Understanding and characterizing these microstructures is crucial for optimizing the mechanical properties of the steel.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for producing a low-carbon steel with a hardened surface layer through sub-rapid solidification. The method involves heating a low-carbon steel to a high temperature, rapidly solidifying it using a cooling mechanism, and then quenching the solidified steel. The resulting steel has a surface layer with a unique microstructure consisting of lath martensite, upper bainite, and acicular ferrite, which contributes to its high surface hardness and strength.

The invention also includes a detailed characterization of the microstructures in the hardened surface layer, including their phase, morphology, and mechanical properties. The method allows for the production of low-carbon steels with enhanced surface properties, making them suitable for applications requiring high wear resistance and strength.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Example 1: Production of Low-Carbon Steel with Hardened Surface Layer

#### Materials and Methods

Rods of TG30 steel were heated to 1600°C in an induction furnace. FeO was added to adjust the free oxygen content to 35-50 ppm. Fe-Ti was added to the melt to adjust the titanium content to 0.01-0.02%. Fe-Mn and Fe-Si were sequentially added to adjust the manganese and silicon contents (see Table 1 for chemical compositions).

Two 9-mm-thick copper plates with a 2.5-mm-wide chamber between them were pressed into the melt and removed. This step took 0.24 seconds, during which heat transferred from the center to the surface and then to the copper plates, resulting in a flat steel sheet measuring 2.5 × 75 × 100 mm³. The sheet was then subjected to 30 seconds of air cooling and finally quenched in water. The solidification rates, as simulated using Procast software (Version 2018, ESI Group, Paris, France), were about 900-1000 K/s at 0-50 µm depth and about 400-900 K/s at 50-200 µm depth. These rates fall within the sub-rapid solidification range.

A 10 mm × 5 mm × 2.5 mm sample was cut from the sheet. The cross-sectional plane of the sample was milled and then polished using a 0.05 µm Al₂O₃ suspension. The prior austenitic grains were etched using a picric acid solution and measured using the linear intercept method. The average prior austenitic grain size was 152 µm near the surface. The sample was then repolished and re-etched using a 4% nital solution.

Microstructure images were obtained using a Hitachi SU1510/JSM-6700F SEM (Hitachi Group, Tokyo, Japan). Small specimens (4 µm × 2 µm × 50 nm) were extracted from each of the three main microstructure types found at the surface using a Helios nanolab 600 Focused Ion Beam (FIB, FEI company, Hillsboro, OR, USA). Selected Area Electron Diffraction (SAED) patterns of each microstructure in each specimen were identified using a JEM-ARM200cF TEM (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Nanoindentation tests were conducted using an Agilent U9820A Nano Indenter G200 (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a TB-13989 Berkovich-type diamond head. The Poisson's ratio (ν) was set at 0.3. To ensure the reliability of test results, the thermal drift effect during pressing was automatically deducted. Eight test points were indented on each microstructure, and the indentations were arranged in a dot array. The centers of test points were placed 20 µm apart. For each indentation, nanohardness readings were taken directly from the software of the nanoindentation equipment. Nanohardness-displacement curves were plotted for each indentation. After nanoindentation tests, the cross-sectional plane of the sample was etched again to screen the nanoindentations on target microstructures.

#### Results

##### Microstructure

Based on SEM and TEM micrographs and SAED patterns from samples made by sub-rapid solidification, three microstructures were identified on the surface area of the sample, down to a depth of 200 µm:
1. **Lath Martensite (M)**: Consisting of fibers with fusiform grains randomly distributed between them.
2. **Upper Bainite (UB)**: Consisting of roughly parallel laths arranged within randomly oriented blocks.
3. **Acicular Ferrite (AF)**: Consisting of needles with high-angle misorientations.

The average width of all grains (laths or needles) was less than 1 µm. Martensite usually has a body-centered tetragonal (bct) structure, characterized by deformation in the a-axis and c-axis. SAED patterns indicated a difference of about 0.4% between the interplanar distances of the (110) plane and the (011) plane, suggesting the presence of lattice distortion.

Using the following formulas, the lattice distortion caused by carbon atoms in martensite was calculated:
\[ c = a_0 + \alpha p \]
\[ a = a_0 + \beta p \]

Where \( c \) and \( a \) are lattice constants of martensite, \( a_0 \) is the lattice constant of α-Fe (2.8664 Å), \( p \) is the carbon content (wt%), \( \alpha = 0.116 \pm 0.002 \), and \( \beta = 0.013 \pm 0.002 \). Given that the carbon content of the steel is 0.023 wt%, the value for \( c \) should be about 2.869 Å and the value for \( a \) should be about 2.866 Å. Thus, the calculated difference between the interplanar distances of the (110) plane (2.0266 Å) and the (011) plane (2.0277 Å) was 0.05%.

However, the actual difference in martensite was higher than 0.05% due to carbon segregation in quenched low-carbon steel. The carbon content in martensite was estimated to be about 0.14 wt% (6 times the average carbon content of the steel). Therefore, the calculated difference between the interplanar distances of (110) and (011) planes could reach 0.31%, which is similar to the value calculated from SAED patterns.

TEM micrographs confirmed the presence of lath martensite, characterized by ultrafine parallel laths with an average width of 128 nm. The straight grain boundaries between laths, a characteristic of martensite, were clearly visible. TEM micrographs of upper bainite showed the characteristic structure of lath-like ferrite with cementite (θ) between the laths. SAED patterns indicated the presence of bcc α-Fe and the secondary phase strips of cementite with an Isaichev relationship ({110}α//{103}θ, <111>α//<010>θ) between the two phases. TEM micrographs of acicular ferrite showed relatively long and coarse grains, all indexed to be α-Fe.

Three of the observed constituents mixed with one another in the surface layer. In the area of 0-50 µm depth, the microstructure consisted of lath martensite and upper bainite with large aspect ratios, referred to as B1. In the area of 50-200 µm depth, the microstructure consisted of acicular ferrite and upper bainite with low aspect ratios, referred to as B2. B1 appeared as large blocks with over 15 roughly parallel laths, and the length/width ratios of the laths were larger than 30. B2 appeared as an assortment of small blocks with several parallel laths, and the length/width ratios of the laths were less than 10.

##### Nanohardness and Yield Strength

Nanoindentation tests were performed to measure the surface mechanical properties of each microstructure. The average nanohardness was much higher for martensite than for the ferrites: 9.87 ± 0.51 GPa for lath martensite, 4.18 ± 0.39 GPa for upper bainite, and 2.93 ± 0.30 GPa for acicular ferrite. The high nanohardness of martensite in this low-carbon steel, despite its low carbon content (0.023 wt%), was attributed to the fineness of the laths, which had an average width of 128 nm.

The nanohardness of upper bainite fell within the range of 3-5 GPa reported by other researchers. The nanohardness of acicular ferrite was higher than previously reported values, likely due to the inclusion of grain boundary effects in the indentation tests. The average nanohardness of upper bainite was over 1 GPa higher than that of acicular ferrite, primarily due to the smaller grain size and the precipitation strengthening effect of cementite.

Using the tested average nanohardness values and the equation derived by Rodriguez et al., the yield strength in each microstructure of the surface layer was calculated:
\[ \sigma_Y = 3H \]

It was about 2378 ± 123 MPa for lath martensite, 1007 ± 94 MPa for upper bainite, and 706 ± 72 MPa for acicular ferrite. The high yield strength of martensite, particularly in the surface layer, provided the steel with exceptional resistance to abrasion, a property that would be unachievable without additional heat treatment in other steels with similar carbon content.

### Example 2: Formation Process of Mixed Microstructure

The formation process of the mixed microstructure in the surface layer of the sub-rapid-solidified low-carbon steel was elucidated through a detailed analysis of the cooling rates and phase transformations.

During the cooling process, the steel sheet experienced two key phase transformation stages:
1. **Liquid to Solid Transformation**: The cooling rates during solidification were calculated to be about 900-1000 K/s at 0-50 µm depth and about 400-900 K/s at 50-200 µm depth. The high cooling rate at this stage resulted in a refined high-temperature microstructure in the surface area, with an average austenite grain size of 152 µm.
2. **Austenite to Martensite or Ferrite (Room-Temperature Microstructure)**: The cooling rates at the second stage were estimated to be about 50-100 K/s at 0-50 µm and about 10-30 K/s at 50-200 µm.

In the 0-50 µm depth area, the ferrite laths of upper bainite nucleated on austenite grain boundaries on the surface and grew inward. Carbon was expelled from bainitic ferrite into the surrounding austenite due to the difference in solubility of carbon in the two phases. As the bainitic ferrite laths grew, the supersaturated carbon between the laths in the same block precipitated in the form of nanosized cementite strips, while the carbon expelled outside the block enriched the carbon content in the surrounding austenite. The carbon-rich austenite subsequently transformed diffusionlessly into lath martensite as a result of a cooling rate of 50-100 K/s. This resulted in a mixed microstructure of lath martensite and B1 upper bainite.

In the 50-200 µm depth area, the cooling rate was about 10-30 K/s, which was in the acicular ferrite and bainitic ferrite transformation zone. Acicular ferrite nucleated and grew on inclusions inside austenite grains prior to bainitic ferrite, effectively separating the austenite grains into small regions. The needles of acicular ferrite provided nucleation sites for bainite laths and also limited the growth of the laths. This resulted in a mixed microstructure of acicular ferrite with B2 upper bainite laths in the 50-200 µm depth area.

The present invention provides a method for producing a low-carbon steel with a hardened surface layer through sub-rapid solidification. The method involves heating the steel to a high temperature, rapidly solidifying it using a cooling mechanism, and quenching the solidified steel. The resulting steel has a surface layer with a unique microstructure consisting of lath martensite, upper bainite, and acicular ferrite, which contributes to its high surface hardness and strength. The method allows for the production of low-carbon steels with enhanced surface properties, making them suitable for applications requiring high wear resistance and strength.
