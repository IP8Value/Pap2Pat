# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Radio frequency identification (RFID) technology has revolutionized various sectors, including transportation, logistics, and healthcare, due to its cost-effectiveness and robustness. RFID tags, which operate wirelessly, collect energy from a nearby reader's interrogating radio waves, making them highly versatile and tolerant to extreme conditions such as high and low temperatures, pressure, and solvents. This technology has been widely adopted in the form of cards, clothing, and even implants in animals and humans. For instance, RFID tags are used in mass transit fare payments, highway toll collection, and even in medical applications like the "digital pill" for monitoring medication adherence in chronic conditions. The global RFID market, encompassing tags, readers, software, and services, was valued at US$11.2 billion in 2017 and is projected to reach US$18.68 billion by 2026, growing at an annual rate of 10%.

In recent years, the integration of RFID technology into healthcare has gained significant attention. Implantable RFID microchips have been used for positive identification in both animals and humans. In the medical field, RFID has been applied to develop oral "digital pills" that emit a radiofrequency signal upon activation by gastric pH, providing real-time ingestion data and enabling timely interventions. These applications highlight the potential of RFID in advancing biomedical research and clinical practices. However, the broader application of RFID in biological tissues, particularly in tissue culture, has been limited due to the challenges of incorporating microchips without compromising the native structure and function of the tissues.

Human organoids, which are three-dimensional (3D) structures derived from human stem cells, have emerged as a powerful tool for in vitro studies of human organs. These organoids closely mimic the structure and function of human tissues, making them valuable for drug screening, disease modeling, and personalized medicine. The self-assembly process of organoids, which involves the formation of a polarized structure surrounded by a basement membrane, presents a unique opportunity for the integration of miniature RFID chips. This integration could enable the digitalization of organoids, facilitating their tracking and analysis in various experimental settings.

The present invention addresses the need for a method to integrate ultracompact RFID chips into biological tissues, specifically human iPSC-derived organoids, without affecting their native structure and function. This innovation opens new avenues for the application of RFID technology in biomedical research and clinical diagnostics.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a method for integrating ultracompact RFID chips into biological tissues, particularly human iPSC-derived organoids, to create RFID-integrated organoids (RiOs). The method involves the following steps:

1. Differentiating human iPSCs into specific organoid types, such as liver organoids, using established protocols.
2. Dissociating the differentiated organoids and re-aggregating them in the presence of ultracompact RFID chips (O-Chips).
3. Inducing self-assembly of the re-aggregated tissues to form RiOs, wherein the O-Chips are internalized within the organoids.
4. Verifying the functionality and integrity of the RiOs through various assays, including gene expression analysis, protein secretion, and functional tests.

The invention also includes a device for simultaneous detection of RFID signals and fluorescence imaging of RiOs, enabling high-throughput phenotyping and screening of multiple donor-derived organoids. This device facilitates the identification of specific RiOs based on their RFID signals and the quantification of their phenotypic characteristics, such as lipid accumulation.

The RFID-integrated organoids (RiOs) produced by this method maintain their native structure and function, making them suitable for various applications, including drug screening, disease modeling, and personalized medicine. The invention provides a scalable and cost-effective approach to digitalizing biological tissues, enhancing the capabilities of in vitro studies and clinical diagnostics.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Definitions

- **RFID (Radio Frequency Identification)**: A technology that uses electromagnetic fields to automatically identify and track tags attached to objects. The tags contain electronically stored information and can be read from a distance without the need for direct contact.
- **Organoid**: A three-dimensional (3D) structure derived from human stem cells that mimics the structure and function of a specific organ.
- **iPSC (Induced Pluripotent Stem Cell)**: A type of pluripotent stem cell that can be generated directly from adult cells. iPSCs have the ability to differentiate into any cell type in the body.
- **RiO (RFID-integrated Organoid)**: An organoid that contains an ultracompact RFID chip (O-Chip) integrated within its structure.
- **O-Chip**: An ultracompact RFID chip designed for integration into biological tissues. The O-Chip is 460 × 480 μm in size and has a 512-bit memory area.
- **Self-assembly**: The process by which disordered parts of a system spontaneously form an organized structure.
- **Cavitation**: The formation of vapor-filled cavities in a liquid, typically as a result of the rapid movement of a solid object through the liquid.
- **Phenotyping**: The process of determining the observable characteristics or traits of an organism, resulting from the interaction of its genotype with the environment.

### Examples

#### Example 1: Integration of O-Chips into iPSC-Derived Liver Organoids

**Objective**: To demonstrate the successful integration of ultracompact RFID chips (O-Chips) into human iPSC-derived liver organoids (LOs) without compromising their native structure and function.

**Materials and Methods**:
- **Human iPSCs**: Obtained from a commercial source or derived from healthy donors.
- **Differentiation Protocol**: Human iPSCs were differentiated into posterior foregut organoids using a sequential protocol involving Activin and FGF4/CHIR 99021 exposure.
- **O-Chip**: Commercially available ultracompact RFID chip (460 × 480 μm, 512-bit memory area).
- **Re-aggregation Culture**: At day 6 of differentiation, foregut organoids were dissociated and re-seeded into 96-well plates, followed by the addition of O-Chips.
- **Self-assembly Induction**: Re-aggregated tissues were treated with a 2% laminin-rich basement membrane matrix to induce self-assembly and polarization.
- **Morphology and Functionality Assays**: RiOs were compared to control LOs using morphological analysis, gene expression analysis, protein secretion assays, and functional tests.

**Results**:
- **Morphology**: RiOs exhibited no morphological abnormalities compared to control LOs, indicating successful integration of O-Chips without affecting the organoid structure.
- **Gene Expression**: qPCR analysis revealed that RiOs expressed equivalent levels of liver-specific markers (ALB, AFP, AAT) compared to control LOs.
- **Protein Secretion**: ELISA of culture supernatants showed comparable levels of albumin (ALB) secretion between RiOs and control LOs.
- **Immunofluorescence Staining**: Immunofluorescent whole-mount staining confirmed the presence of ALB+ cells and HNF4a in the nucleus of RiOs.
- **Functional Tests**: RiOs demonstrated normal bile transport and fat accumulation capacities, similar to control LOs, as evidenced by CLF and rhodamine123 uptake and BODIPY staining.

**Conclusion**: The integration of O-Chips into iPSC-derived liver organoids did not compromise their native structure and function, validating the feasibility of this approach.

#### Example 2: Cryopreservation and Thawing of RiOs

**Objective**: To assess the cryopreservation potential of RiOs and their functionality after thawing.

**Materials and Methods**:
- **RiOs**: Generated as described in Example 1.
- **Cryopreservation Protocols**: RiOs were subjected to various cryopreservation methods, including slow freezing and vitrification, using different cryoprotectants.
- **Thawing and Recovery**: Frozen RiOs were thawed and assessed for morphology, O-Chip functionality, and functional capacity.

**Results**:
- **Morphology**: RiOs maintained their morphology after thawing, with no visible signs of damage.
- **O-Chip Functionality**: The O-Chips remained functional after cryopreservation, as confirmed by wireless detection.
- **Functional Capacity**: Thawed RiOs retained their ability to accumulate lipid droplets, as demonstrated by BODIPY staining.

**Conclusion**: RiOs can be effectively cryopreserved and thawed without losing their structural integrity or functional capacity, making them suitable for long-term storage and future use.

#### Example 3: High-Throughput Phenotyping and Screening of RiOs

**Objective**: To develop a high-throughput device for simultaneous detection of RFID signals and fluorescence imaging of RiOs, enabling efficient phenotyping and screening of multiple donor-derived organoids.

**Materials and Methods**:
- **Device Design**: A custom device was developed, consisting of a microliter-scale syringe pump, a flow path, a detection probe for RFID, and a fluorescence microscope.
- **Workflow**: RiOs were treated with fatty acids to induce steatosis and then passed through the flow path using the syringe pump. The RFID signal and fluorescence intensity were simultaneously recorded.
- **Validation**: The device was validated using RiOs derived from different iPSC lines, including those from patients with Wolman disease.

**Results**:
- **Simultaneous Detection**: The device successfully recorded the RFID signals and fluorescence intensity of passing RiOs.
- **Phenotyping**: RiOs derived from patients with Wolman disease showed significantly higher lipid accumulation compared to control RiOs, as indicated by BODIPY staining and quantification.

**Conclusion**: The developed device enables high-throughput phenotyping and screening of RiOs, facilitating the identification of specific organoids based on their RFID signals and phenotypic characteristics.

#### Example 4: Application of RiOs in Forward Cellomics

**Objective**: To demonstrate the application of RiOs in a forward cellomics approach for determining the personalized phenotypes of multiple donor-derived organoids.

**Materials and Methods**:
- **RiO Pool**: RiOs were generated from seven different iPSC lines, including two from patients with Wolman disease.
- **Pooling and Treatment**: The RiOs were pooled together and treated with fatty acids to induce steatosis.
- **Phenotyping and Identification**: The pooled RiOs were analyzed using the high-throughput device developed in Example 3 to quantify lipid accumulation and identify each RiO based on its RFID signal.

**Results**:
- **Phenotypic Variations**: RiOs derived from patients with Wolman disease showed significantly higher lipid accumulation compared to control RiOs.
- **Identification**: Each RiO was accurately identified based on its RFID signal, confirming the effectiveness of the forward cellomics approach.

**Conclusion**: The use of RiOs in a forward cellomics approach enables efficient and accurate determination of personalized phenotypes, facilitating the study of genetic and cellular bases of human variations and diseases.

These examples illustrate the versatility and potential of the invention in various applications, from basic research to clinical diagnostics. The integration of ultracompact RFID chips into biological tissues, particularly organoids, opens new possibilities for the digitalization and high-throughput analysis of biological samples, enhancing the capabilities of in vitro studies and personalized medicine.
