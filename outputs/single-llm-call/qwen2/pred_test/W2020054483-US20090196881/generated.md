# DESCRIPTION

## INTRODUCTION

Spring viremia of carp virus (SVCV) is a rhabdoviral pathogen that poses a significant threat to common carp (Cyprinus carpio carpio) populations, particularly in Europe. High prevalence rates have been reported in countries such as Russia, Romania, Netherlands, Moldavia, Georgia, Germany, France, United Kingdom, and Denmark. Infections by SVCV lead to severe inflammation, hemorrhaging, and swelling of internal organs, ultimately resulting in necrosis and death. Outbreaks typically occur in the spring when water temperatures begin to rise after a cold winter period, with the highest mortalities occurring between 11 and 17°C. While common carp are the primary hosts, natural infections have also been documented in other cyprinid fish species, including koi (Cyprinus carpio koi), goldfish (Carassius auratus), crucian carp (Carassius carassius), silver carp (Hypopthalmichthys molitrix), bighead carp (Aristichthys nobilis), grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella), orfe (Leuciscus idus), and tench (Tinca tinca). Additionally, experimental infections have been reported in roach (Rutilus rutilius), zebrafish (Danio rerio), and fathead minnow (Pimephales promelas). The highly infectious nature of SVCV necessitates immediate notification to the Office of International Epizootic (OIE) within 48 hours of detection.

In 2002, SVCV emerged in the United States, leading to significant mortalities in koi and common carp. Subsequent outbreaks in North America have highlighted the potential for SVCV to establish itself and cause widespread damage to vulnerable fish stocks. Current control measures, such as eradication of infected fish and hygiene practices, have proven insufficient. There is no commercially available SVCV vaccine, and previous attempts with inactivated vaccines have been limited by incomplete activation, regulatory hurdles, and high production costs. DNA vaccines offer a promising alternative due to their straightforward design, heat stability, low production costs, and long-term storage capabilities. However, early efforts to develop an SVCV DNA vaccine have yielded mixed results, with limited efficacy reported.

This invention describes a novel SVCV DNA vaccine utilizing the glycoprotein (G) gene from the North Carolina (nc) SVCV isolate. The vaccine has been rigorously tested in multiple trials, demonstrating significant protection against SVCV in koi. The development of this vaccine represents a significant advancement in the prevention and control of SVCV, particularly in North America.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a DNA vaccine for preventing and controlling Spring viremia of carp virus (SVCV) in fish. The vaccine comprises a recombinant plasmid expressing the glycoprotein (G) gene of the North Carolina (nc) SVCV isolate. The vaccine has been shown to provide significant protection against SVCV in koi, with relative percent survival (RPS) values ranging from 50 to 88%. The vaccine is administered via intramuscular injection and has been tested under various conditions, including different host ages and virus challenge doses. The invention also includes methods for developing a reliable challenge model to assess vaccine efficacy, including the use of a rapid cold water stressor and the selection of koi as the susceptible fish host species.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Susceptibility Testing of Goldfish and Koi

To develop a reliable challenge model for testing the SVCV DNA vaccine, the susceptibility of goldfish and koi to the North Carolina (nc) SVCV isolate was evaluated. Goldfish and koi were subjected to both injection and immersion challenges with SVCVnc. The results indicated that goldfish had little to no susceptibility to SVCVnc, with 96% survival in both challenge experiments. In contrast, all koi died after the SVCVnc injection challenge, and 75% of the koi succumbed after immersion challenge in the second koi susceptibility experiment. These findings validated the use of koi as the positive control fish species in virus challenges and as a potential cyprinid host for SVCVnc vaccine development.

### Example 2

To further refine the challenge model, the effect of cold water stress on koi susceptibility to SVCVnc was investigated. Koi were subjected to two different cold water stress treatments: a slow acclimation to colder water temperatures over a 2-week period and a rapid transfer from stock water temperatures to challenge temperatures on the day of the virus challenge. The results showed no significant difference in mortality between the two cold water stress treatments. The cumulative percent mortalities were identical for both immersion (75% CPM) and injection (100%) challenges. Given the efficiency of the rapid cold stressor, it was adopted into the SVCV challenge model protocol.

### Example 3

Four vaccine trials were conducted to evaluate the efficacy of the SVCV DNA vaccine (pSGnc) in koi. Each trial included three treatment groups: the SVCV DNA vaccine (pSGnc), a mock luciferase vaccine (pLuc), and a non-vaccinated (NV) control. Fish were vaccinated via intramuscular injection 28 days before virus challenge. The challenge conditions varied across the trials, including different host ages and virus challenge doses. The results demonstrated that the pSGnc vaccinated fish had significantly lower mortalities compared to the pLuc vaccinated fish and non-vaccinated control fish. The RPS values ranged from 50 to 88%, indicating significant protection against SVCV. Virus was detected in 98% of the dead fish, confirming that the mortalities were due to SVCV infection. Among the survivors, the prevalence and virus concentration were lower than in the dead fish, with many survivors testing negative for virus. These findings highlight the potential of the pSGnc DNA vaccine as a prophylactic tool to prevent the spread and establishment of SVCV in North America.
