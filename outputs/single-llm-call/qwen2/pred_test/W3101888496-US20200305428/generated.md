# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of plant defense mechanisms against plant-parasitic nematodes (PPN). Specifically, the invention pertains to the identification and utilization of anti-nematode phytochemicals (ANPs) derived from the phenylpropanoid pathway, particularly focusing on the role of hydroxycinnamic acids (HAs) and their derivatives in conferring resistance to PPN in various plant species.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Plant-parasitic nematodes (PPN) are significant agricultural pests that cause substantial yield losses by diverting nutrients, disrupting water transport, increasing susceptibility to secondary infections, and acting as vectors for viruses. Despite the diversity of PPN species, most economic losses are caused by a handful of sedentary PPN genera, including root-knot nematodes (Meloidogyne spp.) and cyst nematodes (Heterodera spp. and Globodera spp.). Effective nematode control is challenging and typically requires an integrated approach combining chemicals, cultural practices, biocontrol, and resistant varieties.

One defense mechanism against PPN is the production of metabolites with anti-nematode activity, known as anti-nematode phytochemicals (ANPs). These ANPs can be broadly categorized into primary and secondary metabolites. Secondary metabolites, such as those derived from the phenylpropanoid pathway, play a crucial role in plant defense against PPN. The phenylpropanoid pathway produces a wide array of secondary metabolites, including hydroxycinnamic acids (HAs), flavonoids, and stilbenoids, which have been implicated in nematode resistance.

Hydroxycinnamic acids (HAs) are hydroxy derivatives of (E)-cinnamic acid and are abundant in many plants. Chlorogenic acid, a notable HA, has been shown to accumulate in sites of PPN infection and correlate with nematode resistance in various plant species. However, the exact mechanisms by which HAs confer resistance to PPN are not fully understood. Understanding these mechanisms and identifying specific HAs with potent anti-nematode activity can lead to the development of novel strategies for PPN control.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for enhancing plant resistance to plant-parasitic nematodes (PPN) by modulating the production of hydroxycinnamic acids (HAs) and their derivatives. The invention is based on the discovery that specific HAs, such as chlorogenic acid, play a crucial role in conferring resistance to PPN in various plant species. The method involves increasing the biosynthesis of HAs through genetic engineering, chemical induction, or breeding programs.

In one embodiment, the invention provides a transgenic plant with enhanced resistance to PPN, wherein the plant is engineered to overexpress genes involved in the biosynthesis of HAs. In another embodiment, the invention provides a method for inducing the production of HAs in plants through the application of resistance inducers, such as salicylic acid (SA) or methyl jasmonate (MeJA).

The invention also encompasses the use of HAs and their derivatives as botanical nematicides. These compounds can be extracted from plants or synthesized chemically and applied to crops to protect them from PPN infestation. Additionally, the invention provides a method for using HAs as biomarkers in nematode resistance breeding programs, allowing for the high-throughput screening of breeding lines for resistance to PPN.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Identification of Hydroxycinnamic Acids (HAs) as Anti-Nematode Phytochemicals

Hydroxycinnamic acids (HAs) are a class of secondary metabolites derived from the phenylpropanoid pathway. They are hydroxy derivatives of (E)-cinnamic acid and include compounds such as chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid, and ferulic acid. HAs have been implicated in plant defense against various pests and diseases, including PPN.

#### Chlorogenic Acid

Chlorogenic acid, a prominent HA, has been shown to accumulate in sites of PPN infection and correlate with nematode resistance in several plant species. Studies have demonstrated that chlorogenic acid levels are higher in resistant plant varieties compared to susceptible ones. For example, in soybean, chlorogenic acid accumulates near the head region of soybean cyst nematodes (H. glycines) in resistant cultivars but not in susceptible ones. Similarly, in poplar, chlorogenic acid accumulation is strongly repressed in Meloidogyne incognita galls, suggesting that suppression of chlorogenic acid biosynthesis might be a pathogenesis strategy employed by the nematode.

### Mechanisms of Action

The exact mechanisms by which HAs confer resistance to PPN are not fully understood, but several hypotheses have been proposed:

1. **Direct Toxicity**: HAs may directly inhibit nematode growth and reproduction by disrupting cellular processes. For instance, chlorogenic acid has been shown to be weakly nematicidal in vitro, and its derivatives, such as caffeic acid quinone, exhibit stronger nematicidal activity.

2. **Induced Immune Response**: HAs may act as signaling molecules that trigger the plant's immune response against PPN. For example, salicylic acid (SA), a plant hormone involved in the regulation of immune responses, has been shown to enhance plant resistance to PPN. Pre-inoculation treatment with SA or chemical analogs thereof enhances plant resistance to subsequent nematode infection.

3. **Physical Barriers**: HAs may contribute to the formation of physical barriers that prevent nematode penetration. For instance, caffeic acid is involved in lignification, which can strengthen cell walls and make them less permeable to nematode invasion.

### Genetic Engineering for Enhanced HA Production

One embodiment of the invention involves the genetic engineering of plants to overexpress genes involved in the biosynthesis of HAs. Key genes in the phenylpropanoid pathway, such as PHENYLALANINE AMMONIA LYASE (PAL), CINNAMIC ACID-4-HYDROXYLASE (C4H), and 4-COUMARATE-CoA LIGASE (4CL), can be overexpressed to increase the production of HAs. Transgenic plants with enhanced HA production have been shown to exhibit increased resistance to PPN.

### Chemical Induction of HA Production

Another embodiment of the invention involves the chemical induction of HA production in plants. Resistance inducers such as salicylic acid (SA) and methyl jasmonate (MeJA) can be applied to plants to stimulate the production of HAs. Pre-treatment with these inducers has been shown to enhance plant resistance to PPN by upregulating the expression of genes involved in the phenylpropanoid pathway.

### Use of HAs as Botanical Nematicides

The invention also encompasses the use of HAs and their derivatives as botanical nematicides. These compounds can be extracted from plants or synthesized chemically and applied to crops to protect them from PPN infestation. For example, chlorogenic acid and its derivatives can be formulated into nematicidal sprays or soil amendments to control PPN populations.

### Biomarkers for Nematode Resistance Breeding

HAs can serve as biomarkers in nematode resistance breeding programs. High-throughput targeted metabolomics can be used to screen breeding lines for the presence of specific HAs associated with PPN resistance. This approach allows for the rapid identification of resistant genotypes, reducing the need for laborious and time-consuming nematode resistance assays.

## EXAMPLES

### Effect of C4H Inhibition on Growth and Development in Uninfected Crops

In this example, the effect of CINNAMIC ACID-4-HYDROXYLASE (C4H) inhibition on the growth and development of uninfected crops was investigated. C4H is a key enzyme in the phenylpropanoid pathway responsible for the conversion of (E)-cinnamic acid to para-coumaric acid, a precursor to HAs. The inhibition of C4H was achieved using specific inhibitors, and the resulting changes in plant growth and development were monitored.

**Materials and Methods:**
- **Plant Material:** Soybean (Glycine max) and poplar (Populus tremula × Populus alba) were used as model crops.
- **C4H Inhibition:** Plants were treated with a specific C4H inhibitor at different concentrations.
- **Growth Analysis:** Plant height, leaf area, and biomass were measured at regular intervals.
- **HA Quantification:** Levels of HAs, including chlorogenic acid, were quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC).

**Results:**
- **Growth Parameters:** C4H inhibition did not significantly affect the growth and development of uninfected crops. Plant height, leaf area, and biomass remained comparable to those of control plants.
- **HA Levels:** The levels of HAs, particularly chlorogenic acid, were significantly reduced in C4H-inhibited plants.

**Conclusion:**
C4H inhibition did not adversely affect the growth and development of uninfected crops, indicating that the phenylpropanoid pathway can be modulated without compromising plant health. This finding supports the feasibility of using genetic engineering or chemical induction to enhance HA production in crops for PPN resistance.

### C4H Inhibitors Have No Effect on Yield and Fertility in Wheat

In this example, the effect of C4H inhibitors on the yield and fertility of wheat (Triticum aestivum) was investigated. The goal was to determine whether the modulation of the phenylpropanoid pathway through C4H inhibition affects the agronomic performance of wheat.

**Materials and Methods:**
- **Plant Material:** Wheat (Triticum aestivum) was used as the model crop.
- **C4H Inhibition:** Wheat plants were treated with a specific C4H inhibitor at different concentrations.
- **Yield Analysis:** Grain yield, grain size, and fertility were measured at harvest.
- **HA Quantification:** Levels of HAs, including chlorogenic acid, were quantified using HPLC.

**Results:**
- **Yield Parameters:** C4H inhibition did not significantly affect the grain yield or grain size of wheat. The fertility of the plants, as measured by the number of viable seeds, remained comparable to that of control plants.
- **HA Levels:** The levels of HAs, particularly chlorogenic acid, were significantly reduced in C4H-inhibited plants.

**Conclusion:**
C4H inhibition did not adversely affect the yield and fertility of wheat, suggesting that the phenylpropanoid pathway can be modulated without compromising the agronomic performance of the crop. This finding supports the potential of using C4H inhibitors in breeding programs to enhance PPN resistance without negative impacts on yield.

### The Early Phenylpropanoid Pathway and Rice Defense

In this example, the role of the early phenylpropanoid pathway in the defense of rice (Oryza sativa) against PPN was investigated. The focus was on the accumulation of HAs and their derivatives in response to nematode infection.

**Materials and Methods:**
- **Plant Material:** Rice (Oryza sativa) was used as the model crop.
- **Nematode Infection:** Rice plants were inoculated with the root-knot nematode Meloidogyne incognita.
- **HA Quantification:** Levels of HAs, including chlorogenic acid, were quantified using HPLC at different time points post-inoculation.
- **Gene Expression Analysis:** The expression of genes involved in the phenylpropanoid pathway, such as PAL, C4H, and 4CL, was analyzed using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR).

**Results:**
- **HA Accumulation:** HAs, particularly chlorogenic acid, accumulated significantly in rice roots 5 days post-inoculation with M. incognita. The levels of HAs were higher in resistant rice varieties compared to susceptible ones.
- **Gene Expression:** The expression of genes involved in the phenylpropanoid pathway, such as PAL, C4H, and 4CL, was upregulated in response to nematode infection, particularly in resistant varieties.

**Conclusion:**
The early phenylpropanoid pathway plays a crucial role in the defense of rice against PPN. The accumulation of HAs, particularly chlorogenic acid, and the upregulation of genes involved in their biosynthesis are key factors in conferring resistance to M. incognita. This finding supports the potential of enhancing HA production in rice to improve PPN resistance.

### Second Infection Experiment in Rice

In this example, a second infection experiment was conducted to further validate the role of HAs in the defense of rice against PPN. The experiment aimed to confirm the findings from the previous study and explore the long-term effects of HA accumulation on nematode resistance.

**Materials and Methods:**
- **Plant Material:** Rice (Oryza sativa) was used as the model crop.
- **Nematode Infection:** Rice plants were inoculated with the root-knot nematode Meloidogyne incognita.
- **HA Quantification:** Levels of HAs, including chlorogenic acid, were quantified using HPLC at different time points post-inoculation.
- **Nematode Population Analysis:** The number of nematodes in the roots and soil was quantified at harvest.

**Results:**
- **HA Accumulation:** HAs, particularly chlorogenic acid, accumulated significantly in rice roots 5 days post-inoculation with M. incognita. The levels of HAs remained elevated throughout the infection period in resistant varieties.
- **Nematode Population:** The number of nematodes in the roots and soil was significantly lower in resistant rice varieties compared to susceptible ones. The reduction in nematode population correlated with the levels of HAs in the roots.

**Conclusion:**
The second infection experiment confirmed the role of HAs in the defense of rice against PPN. The sustained accumulation of HAs, particularly chlorogenic acid, in resistant varieties was associated with a significant reduction in nematode population. This finding further supports the potential of enhancing HA production in rice to improve PPN resistance.

### Example 5

In this example, the use of HAs as biomarkers in nematode resistance breeding programs was explored. The goal was to develop a high-throughput method for screening breeding lines for the presence of specific HAs associated with PPN resistance.

**Materials and Methods:**
- **Plant Material:** A diverse panel of rice (Oryza sativa) varieties with varying levels of susceptibility to PPN was used.
- **HA Quantification:** Levels of HAs, including chlorogenic acid, were quantified using HPLC in root extracts of the rice varieties.
- **Nematode Resistance Assay:** The resistance of the rice varieties to PPN was assessed using a standard nematode infection assay.
- **Correlation Analysis:** The correlation between HA levels and nematode resistance was analyzed using statistical methods.

**Results:**
- **HA Levels:** The levels of HAs, particularly chlorogenic acid, varied significantly among the rice varieties. Resistant varieties showed higher levels of HAs compared to susceptible ones.
- **Nematode Resistance:** The resistance of the rice varieties to PPN was consistent with the levels of HAs. Varieties with higher levels of HAs showed greater resistance to nematode infection.
- **Correlation:** A strong positive correlation was observed between the levels of HAs and nematode resistance.

**Conclusion:**
HAs, particularly chlorogenic acid, can serve as reliable biomarkers for nematode resistance in rice. High-throughput targeted metabolomics can be used to screen breeding lines for the presence of specific HAs associated with PPN resistance, facilitating the rapid identification of resistant genotypes. This approach has the potential to streamline nematode resistance breeding programs and accelerate the development of resistant crop varieties.
