# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE DISCLOSURE

Transcatheter procedures have become the predominant and increasingly favored treatment approach for a wide variety of structural heart diseases, including atrial septal defect and patent foramen ovale closure, valvular repair/replacement, left atrial appendage closure, and the deployment of hemodynamic monitoring devices. These procedures are typically performed under X-ray fluoroscopy, which is the most common imaging modality due to its real-time imaging capabilities and the ability to visualize radiopaque markers on transcatheter devices. However, while fluoroscopy can identify the silhouette of the heart, intracardiac structures remain transparent, necessitating the use of contrast agents to visualize the relative position of the catheter to surrounding tissues. Additionally, fluoroscopy provides only a 2D projection of the heart, catheter, and other devices, offering no depth information, which increases the complexity and uncertainty of the procedures.

Echocardiography, particularly transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), can directly image heart tissue and blood flow, and is often used as a complementary imaging modality. However, TEE requires skilled operators and specialized equipment, which are not readily available in all hospitals. Preoperative imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance (MR) imaging provide detailed 3D anatomical information, which can be displayed on separate screens or overlaid on real-time imaging modalities to improve image-guided interventions. Despite these advancements, the method of displaying hybrid images often obstructs the view of the real-time image during the procedure and fails to provide adequate depth perception.

Automated image registration of different modalities is a non-trivial endeavor but is crucial for various applications, including diagnostics, surgical planning, and image-guided surgery. Several algorithms have been developed for image registration, including intensity-, gradient-, and feature-based methods. Intensity-based methods, such as those that maximize mutual information, have been applied to register CT and MR images. Gradient-based methods, which use the differential total variation, have also been proposed for registration of MR images. Feature-based methods, utilizing descriptors like SIFT, SURF, and BRIEF, are preferred due to their broader applicability but face challenges in accurately segmenting cardiac features from different image modalities.

Augmented reality (AR) technology has gained significant interest in the medical community for enhancing visualization. AR systems, such as OpenSight, have been approved by the FDA for pre-operative planning in orthopedic and neurosurgical procedures. However, these systems lack real-time monitoring and quantitative feedback, which are essential for guiding cardiac interventions.

To address these limitations, a novel AR-assisted image guidance system is proposed. This system displays high-resolution 3D holographic renderings of the catheter and the patient's heart in real-time using AR devices. The geometry of the patient's heart is generated from preoperative CT scans, and the real-time position and orientation of the catheter are automatically detected by processing fluoroscopic images captured from two angles. The registration of the fluoroscopic images with the CT scans is achieved using the spine as a universal fiduciary marker, ensuring that the heart and catheter renderings are set into the same coordinate system and displayed on the AR device.

## SUMMARY OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present disclosure relates to a novel AR-assisted 3D visualization system for image-guided percutaneous cardiac interventional procedures. The system includes a method for generating a 3D model of the patient's heart from preoperative CT images and a method for detecting the real-time position and orientation of a catheter using fluoroscopic images. The system further includes a method for registering the fluoroscopic images with the 3D heart model using the spine as a fiduciary marker and a method for rendering the catheter and heart as holograms on an AR device.

The 3D anatomical structures of the heart and spine are segmented from diagnostic CT scans using a 3D medical image processing software. The segmented heart volume and spine are combined in a coordinate system to create a unified 3D model. The spine is detected in both CT and fluoroscopic images, and the two image modalities are registered using a Fourier-based method. The catheter's position is detected from fluoroscopic images captured at two different angles, and the 3D position of the catheter is calculated. The catheter and heart are then rendered as holograms on an AR device, providing real-time, high-resolution 3D visualization and quantitative feedback for guiding the interventional procedure.

The disclosed system offers several advantages over existing technologies. It provides a 3D visualization of the heart and catheter, which enhances the interventionalist's understanding of the heart anatomy and improves hand-eye coordination. The system is user-friendly and can be used for pre-procedural planning, practicing complicated procedures, and as a teaching/training tool for residents and fellows. The system also facilitates the development of new therapeutic procedures and lowers the learning curve for existing procedures.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Segmentation of 3D Anatomical Structure from CT Images

The 3D anatomical structures of the heart and spine are segmented from diagnostic CT scans to provide a direct visualization of the patient's heart during the interventional procedure. The original CT images are imported into a 3D medical image processing software, such as Materialise Mimics. Each slice of the CT images is binarized by manually selecting an appropriate threshold value according to the target anatomical structure of interest. This threshold processing typically generates a rough segmentation that contains many isolated 3D objects due to the low contrast and low resolution of the CT images. To remove the noise, only the largest object is selected, and its inner holes are filled to generate the 3D model. The largest object is then modified by setting a 3D mask and further adding or removing target regions for each slice. The reconstructed 3D model is smoothed and exported as an STL file. Both the segmented heart volume and spine are combined in 3-matics software to unify all meshes in the same coordinate system.

After the segmentation from the volumetric CT images, the 3D CADs are imported into another editing software, such as Geomagic Wrap, to further trim away undesired structures, including ribs, chest bones, or small peripheral blood vessels. These features are trimmed to produce models that are practical to 3D print and visualize in the AR environment. For specific percutaneous transcatheter procedures, such as transseptal puncture, a preplanned path and target rings are added to the CADs to provide visual guidance to the interventionalists.

### Spine Detection in Fluoroscopic and CT Images

To detect the catheter's position and provide 3D visual guidance in real-time, the first step is to register the coordinate systems of the fluoroscopic images and CT scan. The spine is selected as a universal fiduciary marker because it is relatively clear in both imaging modalities, with minimal or no movement, and is stable between the time a preoperative CT is acquired and a transcatheter procedure is performed. The patients are required to lie in a supine position for both CT imaging and X-ray fluoroscopic procedures to ensure the consistency of spine appearance. The algorithm does not make any assumptions regarding the orientation of the spine, minimizing sensitivity to changes due to patient positioning.

In the experiments, five thoracic vertebrae (T4-T8) are selected as the fiduciary marker because they are close to the atriums of the heart and are visible during the transseptal puncture procedure. The vertebral bodies are selected as the intrinsic fiducial marker because they are the most visible in a fluoroscopic image, minimizing the needed amount of radiation.

Spine detection in CT images is achieved by taking a projection view from the 3D segmented models, such that a 2D image of the spine with a similar outline as the fluoroscopic image is obtained. The projection angle is set equivalent to the capturing angle of the C-arm as recorded in the metadata of the fluoroscopic image. The fluoroscopic image and projection image of the spines are registered using a Fourier-based method.

To detect the spine from fluoroscopy, the noise of the original image is first reduced through Gaussian smoothing and binarized by applying Otsu's adaptive thresholding. A bounding box calculated from the largest object is used to generate a region of interest for the refined binarization. A morphological close operation is then performed to remove noise and small particles that may be present in the region of interest. The segmented spine from a fluoroscopic image is used for further processing.

### Image Registration Based on Fourier Transform

Since the spine is a rigid object and its relative position inside the patient is consistent, a rigid registration is used to determine the transformation matrix between the fluoroscopic images and the 3D heart model. A 2D projection image of the segmented 3D spine model is denoted as the fixed reference image (Ic), whereas the X-ray fluoroscopic images captured at the same angle are denoted as the moving image (If). The registration of the two image modalities enables determining the transform matrix (Tf) such that the catheter and heart can be put into a single coordinate system with the scaling factor k, rotation angle θ, and translation (tx, ty).

where Φ(u, v) is the spectral phase change depending on scaling, rotation, and translation. The spectral magnitude is translation-invariant:
\[ \left| \mathbf{F}_{c}(x,y) \middle| = k^{- 2}\left| \mathbf{F}_{f}\left\lbrack k^{- 1}\left( u\mspace{720mu}\text{cos}\mspace{720mu}\alpha + v\mspace{720mu}\text{sin}\mspace{720mu}\alpha \right),k^{- 1}\left( - u\mspace{720mu}\text{sin}\mspace{720mu}\alpha + v\mspace{720mu}\text{cos}\mspace{720mu}\alpha \right) \right\rbrack \right| \right. \]
The spectral amplitude relationship indicates that the rotation of the spine results in the same rotation by the same angle in the spectral amplitude images, and the scaling by k scales the spectral amplitude by k−1. The rotation and scaling can be further decoupled by defining the spectral amplitudes in polar coordinates (θ, ρ):
\[ \mathbf{F}_{c}(\theta,\rho) = k^{- 2}\mathbf{F}_{f}(\theta - \alpha,\rho/k) \]
By using a logarithmic scale for the radial coordinate, the scaling is converted to a translation:
\[ \mathbf{F}_{c}(\theta,\gamma) = k^{- 2}\mathbf{F}_{f}(\theta - \alpha,\gamma - \kappa) \]
where γ = log(ρ) and κ = log(k). Using the polar-logarithmic representation, both rotation and translation are converted to translations. By applying Fourier transform on the polar-logarithmic representations, one obtains:
\[ \mathbf{F}_{c}(\chi,\psi) = k^{- 2}e^{- j2\pi(\chi\alpha + \psi\kappa)}\mathbf{F}_{f}(\chi,\psi) \]
where rotation and scaling are represented as phase correlations e−j2π(χα+ψκ). In the ideal case, the inverse Fourier transformation of the phase shifts is a Dirac δ-function at (α, κ). In real cases, the rotation and scaling factors are determined by finding the maximum location from the inverse Fourier transformation image. With the same scale and rotation, the phase correlation method is used again to determine the translation factor (tx, ty). The combination of the Fourier and polar-logarithmic transformation is also called the Fourier-Mellin method.

### Detection of Catheter Position and 3D Visualization

After the two image modalities are registered into the same coordinate system, 3D rendering occurs in three steps: 1) The catheter is detected from fluoroscopic images captured at two different angles; 2) The third-dimensional location (i.e., the depth information) is calculated from the pair of 2D locations with the known rotation angle; and 3) The catheter's position and orientation within the heart are rendered in AR.

#### Detection of Catheter from Fluoroscopic Images

A 12-French catheter is used for demonstration of the transseptal puncture procedure. Since the catheter is relatively large (4 mm in diameter) and has a higher X-ray absorption rate than soft tissue and bones, it appears darker than the surrounding area in fluoroscopic images. A pixel intensity-based method is developed to determine the catheter's position. The detection of the catheter starts from the bottom edge of the image with the direction initialized along the Y axis, as the catheter is often inserted from the inferior vena cava in the case of a transseptal puncture procedure.

The ten rows of pixels at the bottom of the image are first binarized using an adaptive thresholding method. A 50×10 region of interest (ROI) is then extracted with the center located in the middle of the dark object (i.e., the catheter) in the bottom ten rows. Within the ROI, the edges of the catheter are detected by calculating the derivatives along the direction perpendicular to the axial direction of the catheter. The centerline of the catheter is then determined as the middle points between two edges. Following the centerline direction, a new ROI is updated on top of the previous ROI, and the detection of the catheter centerline is repeated in the updated ROI. The final results of the detected centerline of the catheter are displayed.

#### Calculation of the 3D Position from Two Fluoroscopic Images

The catheter's position in 3D space is determined by processing the fluoroscopic images captured from two distinct angles. In the experiment, the C-arm is rotated in the axial plane (i.e., around the main axis of the body). The positions of the catheter in the two images have the relationship:
\[ P_{1} = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ y_{1} \\ \mathbf{z}_{1} \end{bmatrix} = T_{3d} \cdot P_{2} = \begin{bmatrix} {\mspace{720mu}\text{cos}\mspace{720mu}\theta} & 0 & {\mspace{720mu}\text{sin}\mspace{720mu}\theta} \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ {- \mspace{720mu}\text{sin}\mspace{720mu}\theta} & 0 & {\mspace{720mu}\text{cos}\mspace{720mu}\theta} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x_{2} \\ y_{2} \\ \mathbf{z}_{2} \end{bmatrix} \]
where P1 and P2 are the catheter's position in the two images, and θ is the rotation angle. With the detected position of the catheter (x1, y1 and x2, y2) and the known rotation angle, the third-dimensional positions (z1, z2) are solved. The 3D positions of the catheter are determined from the fluoroscopic images.

#### Enhanced Visualization on Augmented Reality Device

To provide enhanced visualization, the patient's heart, spine, and catheter are rendered as holograms and displayed on the augmented reality device. The HoloLens (Microsoft Corporation) is selected as the AR device for three reasons: (i) The HoloLens is a pair of video see-through goggles that do not block the surgical view of the medical practitioners and therefore do not disturb the normal interventional procedures. (ii) The HoloLens is able to simultaneously project the results onto the wearable goggles to generate true stereo holograms for 3D AR visualization. (iii) The HoloLens is integrated with a wireless communication module that can be used to seamlessly receive the processed results from the server computer via a shared Wi-Fi network.

The 3D model of the heart and spine are imported into the HoloLens system. The movement trajectory of the catheter is planned by experienced interventionalists and plotted as a virtual path in the 3D model. The target transseptal puncture site is modified as concentric rings to provide additional references. The catheter position (detected offline) from nine sets of fluoroscopic images is rendered on the HoloLens. Only ten key points are selected and rendered as spherical objects with a diameter of 4 mm. A blue virtual catheter is rendered along the trajectory of the red spherical objects. To assist the operator in understanding the catheter's location relative to the heart, a virtual camera is generated at the distal tip of the catheter, with the view angle aligned with the orientation of the catheter's tip.

The system is also embedded with voice recognition and hand gesture detection. The system allows the operator to use two fingers to grab the 3D renderings and reposition or rotate them to any desired locations or orientations. The 3D renderings can be changed by voice command. When the operator says 'open', the system creates a cross-sectional view to provide more detailed information about the inner geometry of the heart anatomy. Several standard views/orientations, such as AP, LAO30, and RAO30, are preloaded in the system and can be easily accessed by voice command.

### Performance of Fourier-Based Registration

#### Average Registration Errors

The image registration algorithm is evaluated by quantifying the registration errors between the fluoroscopy images and the projectional images derived from the CT model. The registration error is defined as the distance between the centroid of the same vertebrae in the two registered image modalities. The registration method is tested in five different groups depending on the imaging angles: anterior-posterior (AP), left anterior oblique view 30 and 60 degrees (LAO30, LAO60), and right anterior oblique view 30 and 60 degrees (RAO30, RAO60). The experimental results indicate that the misalignment errors are consistently below one millimeter for all five groups.

#### Comparison with Other Registration Methods

The Fourier-based registration algorithm is evaluated by comparing it with other feature-based registration methods. The SIFT (scale-invariant feature transform) method and the SURF (speed up robust feature) method are applied to extract local features for image registration. The paired feature points are then used to determine an optimal transform matrix. The performance of the two feature-based methods and the present Fourier-based method is evaluated by measuring the registration success rate and the average registration errors. The Fourier-based method has the highest success rate (100%) and the lowest misalignment error (0.425 mm) compared to the SIFT and SURF methods.

### Detection of Catheter and 3D Rendering

In the experiment, the catheter is inserted into a 3D-printed model to simulate the transseptal puncture procedure. The experimental setup with varied catheter positions is imaged at 15 fps using an interventional X-ray system. After image registration and detection of the catheter from the fluoroscopic images, the catheter's position relative to the phantom heart model is displayed on the HoloLens to provide an augmented visualization.

The catheter is inserted into the phantom heart model at fixed positions and imaged from three different angles (i.e., AP, LAO30, and RAO30). The paired images captured from two angles are used to calculate the 3D position of the catheter's endpoint. The precision of the localization of the catheter in 3D space is quantified by using a mean standard error (MSE). The experimental results indicate that the MSE for 1000 points on the catheter is below 2 pixels (i.e., 0.5 mm). The average MSE for all five locations is 0.298 mm. During a transseptal puncture procedure for an adult patient, an interventionalist expects the catheter to be placed within a ∼5 mm safe region centered at the ideal puncture site. The experimental results indicate a high precision on the static phantom model, although the effectiveness of using the AR guidance for real-time procedures requires further investigation with more realistic patient data that involves cardiac and respiratory motion.

### Discussion

The present AR image-guidance system determines the 3D position of the catheter by processing two fluoroscopic images and uses the spine as a fiduciary marker to register the intraoperative fluoroscopic images with the 3D heart model. The visualization of the patient's anatomic models has been adopted in clinics for the diagnosis of diseases, planning of surgical procedures, and intraoperative guidance. However, the visualizations are typically displayed on 2D computer screens, requiring the operator to frequently rotate the models using a computer mouse. The development of AR and VR technologies enables a more intuitive solution for 3D visualization and provides a transformative human-machine interface. The smart AR glasses used in the present system allow the user to use both eyes to directly view the 3D models, and the virtual 3D heart models can be rendered at any position/orientation that is comfortable to the interventionalists. This direct projection/rendering enabled by AR technology allows for improved hand-eye coordination.

The limitations of the proposed system include the static nature of the 3D-printed heart model, which does not recapitulate the dynamic geometry and location of the heart due to contractions and respiration. Future work will need to overcome these challenges by gating the CT model with multiple phased CT scans with an ECG signal. Real-time phase matching between 2D fluoroscopic images and 3D CT images can compensate for respiratory motion, and pseudo 4D images can facilitate the rendering of a dynamic heart model. Continuous tracking is possible for hospitals with bi-plane C-arms, but updates for renderings are limited by the speed at which a C-arm can switch between the two fluoroscopic angles. Advanced image processing and machine learning algorithms will be needed to effectively segment spines in real-time on patient images.
