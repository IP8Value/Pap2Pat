## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of microbiology and pharmacology, specifically to the discovery and characterization of small-molecule inhibitors that activate the quorum sensing (QS) system in Vibrio cholerae and other Vibrio species. These inhibitors target the LuxO protein, a central regulator in the QS pathway, and have the potential to be developed into anti-virulence agents for the treatment of bacterial infections caused by pathogenic vibrios.

## BACKGROUND

Quorum sensing (QS) is a process of bacterial cell-cell communication that relies on the production, release, detection, and response to extracellular signaling molecules called autoinducers. QS allows groups of bacteria to synchronously alter behavior in response to changes in the population density and species composition of the surrounding community. QS controls collective behaviors including bioluminescence, sporulation, virulence factor production, and biofilm formation. Impairing virulence factor production or function has gained increasing attention as a method to control bacterial pathogenicity. The advantage of anti-virulence strategies over traditional antibiotics is presumed to be reduced pressure on bacteria to develop resistance. Because QS controls virulence in many clinically relevant pathogens, disrupting QS is viewed as a promising possibility for this type of novel therapeutic development.

Many pathogenic Gram-negative bacteria use acylhomoserine lactones (HSLs) as QS autoinducers, which are detected by either cytoplasmic LuxR-type or membrane-bound LuxN-type receptors. Efforts to interfere with HSL QS in Gram-negative bacteria have yielded several potent antagonists. However, some globally important Gram-negative pathogens do not use HSLs as autoinducers, necessitating additional strategies that target non-HSL based QS systems.

Vibrio cholerae, the causative agent of cholera, produces and detects two QS autoinducer molecules called CAI-1 and AI-2. Detection of these autoinducers occurs through transmembrane receptors CqsS and LuxPQ, respectively. At low cell density (LCD), these receptors function as kinases, resulting in the phosphorylation of the response regulator LuxO. Phosphorylated LuxO activates the transcription of four genes encoding small regulatory RNAs (sRNAs), Qrr1-4, which target the mRNAs encoding the quorum-sensing master transcriptional regulators AphA and HapR. At LCD, AphA protein is made while HapR protein is not. When autoinducer concentration increases above the threshold required for detection (high cell density, HCD), binding of the autoinducers to their cognate receptors switches the receptors from kinases to phosphatases, leading to dephosphorylation and inactivation of LuxO. This results in the cessation of Qrr1-4 transcription, derepression of HapR translation, and activation of the QS HCD gene expression program.

In pathogens that cause persistent infections, QS commonly activates virulence factor production at HCD. However, in V. cholerae, which causes an acute disease, HapR production at HCD represses genes important for biofilm formation and virulence factor production. This unique pattern of virulence gene regulation can be understood in terms of the disease caused by V. cholerae. Following successful V. cholerae infection, the ensuing diarrhea washes huge numbers of bacteria from the human intestine into the environment. Thus, expression of genes for virulence and biofilm formation at LCD promotes infection, while repression of these genes by autoinducers at HCD promotes dissemination. Therefore, molecules that activate QS have the potential to repress virulence in V. cholerae and other pathogenic vibrios.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a set of small-molecule inhibitors that activate the QS system of V. cholerae by targeting the LuxO protein. These inhibitors function uncompetitively to perturb LuxO ATPase activity, thereby activating QS and repressing virulence factor production. The invention includes the identification, synthesis, and characterization of these inhibitors, as well as their use in methods for controlling virulence in pathogenic vibrios.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Materials and Methods

#### Bacterial Strains and Culture Conditions

All V. cholerae strains are derivatives of wild type C6706str. All V. harveyi strains are derivatives of wild type V. harveyi BB120. V. parahaemolyticus strains were generously provided by Dr. Linda McCarter. Escherichia coli S17-1 pir, DH5α, and Top10 were used for cloning. The relevant genotypes of all plasmids and strains are provided in Table 1. Unless specified, E. coli and V. cholerae were grown in LB medium at 37°C and 30°C with shaking, respectively. V. harveyi and V. parahaemolyticus were grown in LM medium at 30°C with shaking. Colony opacity of V. parahaemolyticus was monitored on LM with 2% agar. Antibiotic concentrations are as follows: ampicillin, gentamicin, and kanamycin, 100 mg/L; chloramphenicol and tetracycline, 10 mg/L; streptomycin, 5 g/L; polymyxin B, 50 U/L.

#### Screening for V. cholerae QS-Activating Molecules

The 90,000 molecule library was supplied by the High-Throughput Screening Resource Center of the Rockefeller University. The V. cholerae strains BH1578 (ΔcqsA ΔluxS pBB1) and BH1651 (luxO D47E pBB1) were grown overnight in LB medium with tetracycline and diluted 25-fold. The diluted cultures were dispensed into 384-well microtiter plates containing screening molecules that were previously added to each well. The final concentration of each compound was ∼20 µM. Light production was measured on an Envison Multilabel Reader after 6-hour incubation at 30°C without shaking. Compounds that induced light production >100-fold were reordered from suppliers and tested.

#### Bioluminescence Assays for V. cholerae and V. harveyi

Overnight cultures of reporter strains were grown in LM medium (for V. harveyi) or LB with tetracycline (for V. cholerae carrying pBB1) and diluted 20-fold with sterile medium. Bioluminescence and OD600 were measured in an Envison Multilabel Reader following 4-hour incubation at 30°C with shaking. Synthetic molecules were dissolved in DMSO and supplied at varying concentrations to the reporter strains. DMSO was used as the negative control.

#### Protein Purification

The open reading frame encoding V. cholerae LuxO D47E was amplified by PCR and cloned into plasmid pET28B that had been previously digested with NdeI and BamHI. The resulting plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21 Gold (DE3) resulting in strain WN133. Strain WN133 was grown in LB with kanamycin at 30°C with shaking until the OD600 of the culture reached ∼1.0. IPTG was added at a final concentration of 200 µM, and the culture was incubated for an additional 4 hours at 30°C with shaking. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, suspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Sodium phosphate buffer pH 7.4, 0.5 M NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 5 mM imidazole), and lysed using a Cell Cracker. Soluble materials were loaded onto a HiTrap chelating column charged with nickel, the column was washed extensively with lysis buffer, and His6-tagged V. cholerae LuxO D47E enzyme was eluted using a linear gradient of increasing concentration of imidazole dissolved in lysis buffer. Fractions containing LuxO D47E were pooled and concentrated with an Amicon Untra-15 filter. Protein was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. Protein concentrations were determined by UV absorbance at 280 nm. E. coli NtrC and other LuxO D47E variants were purified using the same method.

## Results

### Investigation of the Targets of the QS Activating Compounds

To determine which QS component each compound acts on, we tested the identified Class 1 and Class 2 compounds against V. cholerae mutants that lack only the CqsS receptor or only the LuxPQ receptor. All eight Class 1 compounds induced light production in the ΔluxPQ strain but not the ΔcqsS strain, indicating that these molecules function as CqsS agonists. The three Class 2 compounds, which are structurally homologous, likely act downstream of the QS receptors. These compounds are structurally homologous and may function by an identical mechanism. We focused on the compound displaying the highest potency (compound 11). GFP production from a qrr4-gfp transcriptional fusion decreased ∼3-fold when the luxO D47E strain was treated with compound 11, suggesting that compound 11 targets either LuxO or σ54. Further analysis revealed that compound 11 targets LuxO.

### Structure-Activity-Relationship of Class 2 Compounds

The three identified Class 2 compounds share a 5-thio-6-azauracil core and only their side chains vary. Differences in the hydrocarbon side chains must be responsible for the corresponding differences in potency. Increasing steric bulk by incorporation of a tert-butyl carbinol side chain led to a 3-fold enhancement in potency (compound 12). Thus, the activity of the 5-thio-6-azauracil compounds within this series is highly sensitive to the structural features of the alkyl side chain. A bulky, hydrophobic terminal t-butyl moiety is optimal.

### Class 2 Compounds Inhibit the LuxO ATPase Activity

NtrC-type response regulators including LuxO possess three biochemical activities: phosphoryl-group accepting activity, DNA-binding activity, and ATP hydrolyzing activity. We investigated which of these activities is inhibited by compounds 11 and 12. Both compounds inhibit ATP hydrolysis in a dose-dependent manner. Using traditional Michaelis-Menten enzyme kinetic analyses, we found that both compounds decrease the K_m and the V_max of the LuxO ATPase reaction. The Lineweaver-Burk plots of curves derived from control reactions and from inhibitor-containing reactions display parallel slopes (K_m/V_max), indicating that compounds 11 and 12 function as uncompetitive inhibitors, suggesting they bind to the pre-formed LuxO-ATP complex to inhibit ATP hydrolysis.

### A Genetic Screen to Identify LuxO Mutants Resistant to Compound 12

To determine where compounds 11 and 12 bind, we screened for LuxO mutants refractory to inhibition. Four such mutants were identified, carrying I211F, L215F, L242F, or V294L alterations. These residues map to three regions that abut the Walker B motif, suggesting that the inhibitors bind to a region close to the ATP-binding pocket.

### Broad Spectrum Activation of Vibrio QS

LuxO is a conserved member of vibrio QS circuits. We tested whether compounds 11 and 12 could activate QS in other Vibrio species. Treatment of V. harveyi luxO D47E with compounds 11 and 12 induced light production 10,000-fold, indicating that these compounds are active in V. harneyi. Similarly, treatment of V. parahaemolyticus luxO* with compounds 11 and 12 resulted in a switch from translucent to opaque colonies, indicating that these compounds inhibit V. parahaemolyticus LuxO from repressing the OpaR-dependent QS program.

### New Chemical Tools for Controlling Virulence in Vibrios

In pathogenic vibrios, HapR and its homologs function as repressors of virulence factor production at HCD. We assayed the effects of compounds 11 and 12 on TcpA production in V. cholerae and production and secretion of VopD in V. parahaemolyticus. Western blot analysis showed that, in a V. cholerae luxO D47E strain, HapR and TcpA levels increased and decreased, respectively, in the presence of compound 12. Similarly, exposing the V. parahaemolyticus luxO* mutant to compound 12 resulted in decreased production and secretion of VopD. Cytotoxicity assays revealed that treatment with compound 12 significantly reduced the cytotoxicity of V. parahaemolyticus luxO* mutant towards HeLa cells.

## Discussion of Results

The identification of small-molecule inhibitors that activate QS in V. cholerae and other Vibrio species represents a significant advance in the development of anti-virulence agents. These inhibitors, which target the LuxO protein, function by uncompetitively inhibiting the ATPase activity of LuxO, thereby activating QS and repressing virulence factor production. The inhibitors possess favorable drug-like characteristics, including potent inhibition, water solubility, good stability, and cell permeability. They also display low host-cell cytotoxicity, making them promising candidates for further development as therapeutic agents.

The broad spectrum activity of these inhibitors in multiple Vibrio species highlights their potential utility in controlling virulence factor production in a variety of pathogenic vibrios. Future improvements to these molecules will focus on optimizing their structure-activity relationships and expanding their spectrum to target other NtrC-type response regulators. The discovery of these inhibitors opens new avenues for the development of anti-TCS drugs and provides valuable tools for studying QS signal transduction mechanisms.
