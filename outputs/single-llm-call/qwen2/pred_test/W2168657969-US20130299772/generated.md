# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of semiconductor nanocrystals, specifically to the synthesis and characterization of heavily doped InAs nanocrystals. The invention encompasses methods for synthesizing InAs nanocrystals and subsequently doping them with metal atoms such as copper (Cu), silver (Ag), and gold (Au). The invention further includes the characterization of the doped nanocrystals using various spectroscopic and microscopic techniques to elucidate the effects of doping on the optical and structural properties of the nanocrystals.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Semiconductor nanocrystals, also known as quantum dots, have garnered significant attention due to their unique optical and electronic properties, which differ from those of bulk materials. These properties are primarily attributed to quantum confinement effects, which occur when the dimensions of the nanocrystals are comparable to or smaller than the exciton Bohr radius of the material. InAs nanocrystals, in particular, have been widely studied due to their narrow bandgap and high electron mobility, making them promising candidates for various applications in optoelectronics, photovoltaics, and biomedical imaging.

However, the performance of InAs nanocrystals in these applications can be further enhanced through controlled doping with metal atoms. Doping introduces additional charge carriers into the nanocrystals, which can significantly alter their electronic and optical properties. For instance, n-type doping with elements like copper (Cu) can increase the electron concentration, while p-type doping with elements like silver (Ag) can increase the hole concentration. Gold (Au) doping, on the other hand, can introduce isovalent impurities that do not significantly alter the charge carrier concentration but can affect the lattice structure and electronic levels.

Despite the potential benefits of doping, the precise control of dopant incorporation and the understanding of the resulting changes in the nanocrystal properties remain challenging. Traditional methods for doping bulk semiconductors, such as ion implantation and diffusion, are not directly applicable to nanocrystals due to their small size and high surface-to-volume ratio. Therefore, there is a need for new methods and techniques to achieve controlled and uniform doping of InAs nanocrystals.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for synthesizing and doping InAs nanocrystals with metal atoms, specifically copper (Cu), silver (Ag), and gold (Au). The method involves the following steps:

1. **Synthesis of InAs Nanocrystals**: InAs nanocrystals are synthesized using a hot injection technique under an inert atmosphere. The synthesis involves the reaction of indium chloride (InCl3) and tris(trimethylsilyl) arsenide (TMS3As) in the presence of trioctylphosphine (TOP) as a solvent. The reaction is carried out at high temperatures, and the growth of the nanocrystals is monitored by absorption spectroscopy.

2. **Doping of InAs Nanocrystals**: The synthesized InAs nanocrystals are doped with metal atoms by adding a solution of the metal salt (CuCl2, AgNO3, AgCl, or AuCl3) to a toluene solution of the nanocrystals. The metal solution is prepared by dissolving the metal salt in toluene along with didodecyldimethylammonium bromide (DDAB) and dodecylamine (DDA) as surfactants. The doping process is carried out under inert conditions, and the resulting doped nanocrystals are precipitated using methanol or acetone.

3. **Characterization of Doped InAs Nanocrystals**: The doped InAs nanocrystals are characterized using various techniques, including UV-visible-near infrared (UV-Vis-NIR) spectroscopy, photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), powder X-ray diffraction (XRD), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). These techniques provide insights into the optical and structural changes induced by doping.

The invention also includes the determination of the number of metal atoms per nanocrystal using inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) and the theoretical modeling of the effects of doping on the electronic and optical properties of the nanocrystals.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

### Synthesis of InAs Nanocrystals

The synthesis of InAs nanocrystals is based on a hot injection technique carried out under an inert atmosphere using standard Schlenck techniques. In a typical synthesis, a mixture of indium and arsenic precursors is prepared by adding 0.3 grams (1 mmol) of tris(trimethylsilyl) arsenide (TMS3As) to 1.7 grams of a 1.4 M InCl3 trioctylphosphine (TOP) solution (2 mmol in total). This solution is then injected into a three-neck flask containing 2 ml of TOP at 300°C under vigorous stirring. The temperature is subsequently reduced to 260°C, and further precursor solution is added to achieve particle growth. The growth of the nanocrystals is monitored by taking absorption spectra of aliquots extracted from the reaction solution. Upon reaching the desired size, the reaction mixture is allowed to cool to room temperature and transferred into a glovebox. Anhydrous toluene is added to the reaction solution, and the nanocrystals are precipitated by adding anhydrous methanol. The size distribution of the nanocrystals in a typical reaction is on the order of 10%, which can be improved using size-selective precipitation with toluene and methanol as the solvent and anti-solvent, respectively.

### Doping of InAs Nanocrystals

In a typical doping reaction, a metal solution is prepared by dissolving 10 mg of the metal salt (CuCl2, AgNO3, AgCl, or AuCl3), 80 mg of didodecyldimethylammonium bromide (DDAB), and 120 mg of dodecylamine (DDA) in 10 ml of toluene. The resulting solutions of Cu and Ag are blue, colorless, and yellow, respectively. The metal solution is then added dropwise to a stirred 2 ml toluene solution of InAs nanocrystals. After 15 minutes, the absorption and emission spectra of the solutions are measured. The Cu and Au samples are precipitated with methanol, while the Ag sample is precipitated with acetone. The entire metal treatment procedure is carried out under inert conditions. The ratio of metal atoms to nanocrystals in solution is estimated from the literature values of InAs nanocrystal absorption cross-sections.

### Characterization of Doped InAs Nanocrystals

#### UV-Visible-Near Infrared (UV-Vis-NIR) Spectroscopy

UV-Vis-NIR spectroscopy is performed on a JASCO V-570 spectrometer using glass cuvettes. The spectra provide information about the absorption and extinction properties of the doped nanocrystals. The treatment of InAs nanocrystals with Cu solution leads to an increase in the first exciton energy, while the emission energy remains unchanged. Ag treatment, on the other hand, leads to a decrease in both the first exciton energy and the emission energy. All metal treatments result in some quenching of the emission, which is totally quenched after precipitation.

#### Photoluminescence (PL) Spectroscopy

Photoluminescence (PL) measurements are carried out using an Ocean Optics NIR-512 spectrophotometer with a 633 nm HeNe laser excitation. The PL spectra provide insights into the emission properties of the doped nanocrystals. The treatment of InAs nanocrystals with Cu leads to a blue shift in the PL spectrum, while Ag treatment results in a red shift. The PL spectra of the doped nanocrystals exhibit quenching, which is more pronounced after precipitation.

#### Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) is performed on a Tecnai T12 G2 Spirit at 120 keV. TEM grids are prepared by evaporating one drop of the metal-treated nanocrystal solution onto a standard carbon-covered grid, followed by washing the grid with several drops of methanol for Cu and Au samples and acetone for Ag samples. TEM images provide information about the size, shape, and morphology of the doped nanocrystals. No metal domains are observed on the surface of the nanocrystals, indicating that the metal atoms are uniformly distributed within the nanocrystals.

#### Powder X-ray Diffraction (XRD)

Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns are obtained using Cu Kα photons from a Phillips PW1830/40 diffractometer operated at 40 kV and 30 mA. Samples are deposited as a thin layer on a low-background scattering quartz substrate. The XRD data undergo background subtraction. The XRD patterns of the doped nanocrystals show the main InAs reflections, indicating that the InAs crystal structure is generally preserved. However, evidence of disorder is observed in the broadening of the peaks at higher angles, and a tail appears around 2θ = 30° for Cu-doped samples.

#### X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) is carried out using a Kratos Analytical Axis Ultra XPS instrument. Data are obtained with Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV). Survey spectra are collected with 160 eV pass energy detection. The measurements are performed on nanocrystal films of monolayer thickness deposited on a Si wafer. The XPS spectra provide information about the chemical composition and oxidation state of the doped nanocrystals. The presence of Cu, Ag, and Au is confirmed by the detection of their characteristic peaks in the XPS spectra. The Cu-doped samples show additional peaks assigned to In2O3 and As2O3, indicating the presence of oxidation.

### Determination of Metal Impurity Concentration by ICP-AES

The number of metal atoms per nanocrystal is determined using inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES). Metal treatment is carried out as described above on a range of nanocrystal sizes and at different metal concentrations. The nanocrystals are precipitated, dissolved using concentrated HNO3, and diluted with triply distilled water. The relative quantities of In, As, Cu, Ag, and Au in these solutions are measured by ICP-AES. The amount of Cu/Ag/Au per nanocrystal is extrapolated using the ratio of In and As to Cu/Ag/Au atoms together with the estimated number of In and As atoms per nanocrystal based on the nanocrystal size. The results are presented in graphs showing the number of metal atoms per nanocrystal against the metal:nanocrystal solution ratio.

### Theoretical Modeling of Doped InAs Nanocrystals

The effects of doping on the electronic and optical properties of InAs nanocrystals are theoretically modeled using three distinct approaches: the single hydrogenic impurity model, the Z-model, and the pseudo-crystal model.

#### Single Hydrogenic Impurity Model

The single hydrogenic impurity model describes the behavior of a single impurity within the nanocrystal. The model involves solving the spherically symmetric Schrödinger equation for a hydrogenic impurity at the center of a spherical finite well. The solutions are described in spherical coordinates, and the binding energy of the impurity is calculated. This model predicts red shifts in both the absorption and PL spectra due to the binding of the impurity to the nanocrystal.

#### Z-Model

The Z-model considers the effects of multiple strongly interacting impurities within the nanocrystal. The impurities are modeled as a central Coulomb potential with a valency Z. The model predicts blue shifts in the absorption spectrum at low impurity concentrations, which decrease in magnitude with increasing impurity concentration. This is due to the unbroken spherical symmetry of the model, which leads to high level degeneracy.

#### Pseudo-Crystal Model

The pseudo-crystal model describes the behavior of multiple scattered impurities within the nanocrystal. The impurities are parameterized as deep impurities, and their properties are combined into a pseudoband using the tight-binding method. The model predicts that impurity electrons are highly localized and mix strongly when impurities are close to each other. The results of the model closely resemble the experimental observations for Cu-doped nanocrystals.

### Band Tailing in Nanocrystals

The possibility of band tailing induced by doping is also considered. Band tailing refers to the distortion of the symmetric crystal structure by dopants, which can lead to broadening and shifting of the density of states into the gap region. The effect of band tailing on the density of states (DOS) of InAs nanocrystals is calculated using a real-space pseudopotential method. The results show that the shifts in the DOS increase with decreasing nanocrystal size and saturate at lower disorder levels for larger nanocrystals, consistent with experimental observations.
