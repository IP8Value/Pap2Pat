# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a system and method for ensuring patient safety in radiotherapy by providing real-time, independent verification of patient setup accuracy. More specifically, the invention involves an efficient, automatic patient safety system (PSS) that utilizes optical tracking technology to monitor and verify the position of a patient relative to the treatment isocenter during radiotherapy sessions.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Radiotherapy is a critical component of cancer treatment, involving the delivery of ionizing radiation to destroy cancer cells while minimizing damage to surrounding healthy tissues. The success of radiotherapy depends heavily on the precise and accurate delivery of radiation to the target volume. However, the process is complex and involves multiple steps, each of which can introduce potential errors. One of the most significant sources of errors in radiotherapy is geometric miss due to incorrect patient setup, which can lead to underdosing of the target and overdosing of healthy tissues, potentially resulting in severe complications or even death.

Despite the advent of advanced technologies such as intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) and volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT), which have improved the precision of radiation delivery, errors in patient setup remain a persistent issue. Several studies have highlighted the prevalence of geometric misses caused by incorrect patient setup, with some reports indicating that such errors account for a significant percentage of radiotherapy incidents.

To address these issues, various image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) systems have been developed, including cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), continuous tracking systems like AlignRT, and other optical tracking systems. While these technologies have significantly reduced setup errors, they are not foolproof and can still be subject to human errors, technical limitations, and workflow inefficiencies. For instance, CBCT systems do not provide real-time tracking and can be affected by patient movement after imaging. Continuous tracking systems, while effective, often require multiple markers and can be cumbersome to use in a clinical setting.

Therefore, there is a need for an efficient, automatic, and general-purpose patient safety system that can provide continuous and independent verification of patient setup accuracy, ensuring patient safety in radiotherapy. The present invention aims to fulfill this need by introducing a novel PSS that utilizes a single infrared reflective marker (IRRM) and optical tracking technology to monitor patient position in real-time.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides an efficient, automatic patient safety system (PSS) for ensuring patient safety in radiotherapy by providing real-time, independent verification of patient setup accuracy. The system comprises a pair of Polaris CCD cameras mounted on the treatment room ceiling, a single IRRM affixed on the patient's skin or immobilization device, and a set of in-house developed software.

The primary function of the PSS is to track the position of the IRRM relative to the treatment room isocenter and provide continuous feedback to the therapists. The system is designed to be general-purpose, applicable for all disease sites, and to complement existing IGRT systems by offering an additional layer of safety.

Key features of the PSS include:
1. **Optical Tracking Technology**: The system uses a pair of Polaris CCD cameras to track the position of a single IRRM. The IRRM can be affixed on the patient's skin or immobilization device, depending on the treatment site.
2. **Real-Time Verification**: The PSS continuously monitors the position of the IRRM and provides real-time feedback to the therapists, allowing for immediate corrective actions if the patient is not positioned correctly.
3. **Automated Workflow**: The system is designed to integrate seamlessly with the existing clinical workflow, minimizing the workload for therapists and reducing the potential for human errors. Patient information is automatically loaded from the treatment planning system (TPS) and record and verify (R&V) system, and the IRRM is tracked without the need for manual intervention.
4. **Independent Verification**: The PSS operates independently of the treatment room, treatment machine, couch top design, immobilization device, or patient internal anatomy, ensuring that it can be used as a reliable safety check regardless of the specific treatment setup.
5. **High Precision and Reliability**: The system has been tested for accuracy and stability, demonstrating the ability to detect gross setup errors with high precision and reliability.

The PSS is particularly useful in preventing setup-related errors that can occur in unexpected and unusual circumstances, such as when treating patients with large lateral offsets, using unconventional indexing positions, or treating patients on different linear accelerators. By providing immediate and independent verification of patient setup, the PSS enhances patient safety and ensures the accurate delivery of radiotherapy.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### System Overview

The patient safety system (PSS) consists of a pair of Polaris CCD cameras (Northern Digital Inc., Waterloo, Ontario) mounted on the treatment room ceiling, a single infrared reflective marker (IRRM) affixed on the patient's skin or immobilization device, and a set of in-house developed software. The software communicates with the control box of the cameras through a regular serial cable and a proprietary data cable. An internal processing unit conducts a process called triangulation to determine the coordinates of the IRRM in its native coordinate system. The three-dimensional (3D) coordinates of up to 50 IRRMs can be sent back to the computer at the same time.

### System Calibration

A calibration procedure was developed to convert the camera native coordinate system to the absolute room coordinate system. A calibration jig consisting of five commercial IRRMs was used for this purpose. The coordinates of each IRRM relative to the center of the jig were determined via CT scan and collected in a 5×3 matrix, A. Under the guidance of CBCT, the jig was then placed on the treatment couch with its center aligned with the imaging isocenter. Congruence of the imaging isocenter and machine isocenter was confirmed with a ball bearing phantom. The coordinates of the IRRMs were sampled by the cameras and collected into another matrix, B. A rotation matrix, R, and a translation (shift) matrix, S, satisfying \(A = \text{BR} + S\) were solved by minimizing the following expression: \(\varepsilon^{2} = \left\| {A - \text{BR} - S} \right\|\).

This is known as the relative pose problem in computer vision and has been studied extensively. In this work, a general analytical solution via singular value decomposition (SVD) was employed, and the steps were summarized as follows:
1. The centroids (Ac) and (Bc) were subtracted from A and B, respectively, to align the origins of the two coordinate systems.
2. The SVD of \(B^{T}A\) was computed as \(B^{T}A = \text{UWV}^{T}\) where U and V are unitary matrices, and W is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are equal to the singular values of \(B^{T}A\).
3. The solutions of R and S were given as:
   \[
   \begin{matrix}
   {R = U\begin{pmatrix}
   1 & 0 & 0 \\
   0 & 1 & 0 \\
   0 & 0 & {\text{det}(UV^{T})}
   \end{pmatrix}V^{T}} \\
   {\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{and},} \\
   {S = A_{c} - B_{c}R}
   \end{matrix}
   \]

### System Workflow

#### Preparation Stage

The preparation stage involves several steps to ensure that the PSS is ready for use during the treatment session. These steps include:
1. **CT Simulation**: Prior to CT simulation, a CT ball bearing (BB) is placed on the patient's surface at a location close to the intended treatment area to ensure that it is included in the scan. After the scan, the BB is removed and its location is marked with a skin tattoo.
2. **Information Transfer**: The CT BB is identified as a point of interest (POI) in the treatment planning system (TPS) and its coordinates are sent to a designated network folder along with each treatment isocenter. The approved treatment plan in TPS is transferred to the record and verify (R&V) system, where treatment beams and treatment sites are appropriately labeled and finalized before physics approval.
3. **Data Import**: The approved plan (including treatment beams and their associated sites) is then exported from the R&V in radiation treatment plan (RTP) format to the same network folder. The data import module of the PSS associates each treatment site in the RTP file with POIs exported from TPS, including the treatment isocenter and the CT BB. The CT BB coordinates relative to the respective isocenter of each treatment site predict the room coordinates of the CT BB, which will be replaced with an IRRM at treatment.

#### Treatment Stage

At the treatment stage, the PSS is used to verify the patient's setup accuracy in real-time. The workflow is as follows:
1. **Patient Setup**: When setting up the patient for treatment, therapists will locate the skin tattoo indicating the CT BB position and place a specially designed disposable flat-surface IRRM. The IRRM is fabricated by covering one side of a piece of round double-sided sticky tape (6 mm diameter and 1 mm height) with reflective tape. The other side is protected by a peelable sheet, making mounting of the IRRM easy.
2. **Continuous Monitoring**: The PSS uses the IRRM as a surrogate to verify patient setup. The discrepancy (in three dimensions) between its room position and reference position is continuously monitored and displayed on a computer monitor inside the treatment room. The same information is displayed on another computer monitor in the control area. If the discrepancy exceeds prespecified thresholds, the therapists can take appropriate corrective action with assistance from a physicist.
3. **Noncoplanar Treatment**: To cope with noncoplanar treatment, the vector connecting the isocenter and IRRM is rotated around the vertical axis in the same way (direction and angle) as the couch rotates to calculate the reference location of the IRRM.

#### Workflow Automation

To minimize the workload and avoid potential errors, the IRRM can be permanently mounted on the patient's immobilization device. This is easy to achieve for patients treated with aquaplastic face masks (for brain or head and neck disease). The CT BB can be directly affixed on the face mask during CT scan and replaced with an IRRM afterwards. Its location is selected to be over the patient's chin area, ensuring its visibility to both cameras in the treatment room. With this approach, the IRRM is readily available before treatment starts, and the system workflow is fully automated, requiring no intervention from the therapist in the treatment room.

For patients treated with body molds (for diseases not in the brain or head and neck area), the IRRM can be permanently mounted on the body mold. For patients with diseases in their extremities, a spot on the body mold close to the disease can be conveniently selected to place a CT BB. For patients with diseases in the abdominal-pelvic area, an alternative workflow is adopted. The CT BB is placed on the patient's middle anterior surface during CT scan and marked with a skin tattoo afterwards. In the first treatment session, therapists will locate the tattoo and temporarily place an IRRM for initial verification. Once the verification is successful, a convenient location on the body mold can be selected to permanently mount an IRRM. The new location of the IRRM is then captured as a reference for future treatment sessions.

### Daily Quality Assurance

A daily quality assurance (QA) procedure is necessary to ensure the accuracy and consistency of the PSS. A simple and efficient daily QA procedure has been developed, which can be integrated with other daily QA activities. At the time of initial system calibration, an IRRM is permanently mounted on the end side of the daily QA device, facing the cameras. After aligning the daily QA device to the room isocenter with the CBCT system, the reference position of the IRRM is captured and associated with a daily QA beam in the R&V system. As a result of daily IGRT QA, the daily QA device is aligned to the room isocenter with CBCT guidance. When the daily QA beam is loaded in the R&V system, the consistency of the PSS, indicated by the position variation of the IRRM, is immediately displayed.

### System Accuracy Evaluation

#### System Stability

The short-term stability of the system was evaluated by monitoring the IRRM mounted on a daily QA device for a consecutive 10 minutes. The Polaris cameras were sufficiently warmed up before the study, and the daily QA device was kept stationary on the couch. Real-time position of the IRRM was captured, approximately 15 frames per second with the cameras, and recorded in a log file for analysis. Variations of the IRRM position in the 10-minute period in each orthogonal direction were used to evaluate the short-term stability of the system. Long-term stability of the system was also evaluated with the daily QA device, using the same setup for daily QA. The device was aligned to the room isocenter with CBCT guidance once a week over a 12-week period. Long-term stability of the system was assessed with position variations of the IRRM in these tests.

#### Phantom Study

The accuracy of the PSS was first evaluated with an anthropomorphic head phantom in an end-to-end test. The head phantom was scanned on a multislice CT scanner. To investigate the effect of different CT BB locations, five CT BBs were affixed on the phantom for CT scan. One CT BB was located right over the chin area, and the other four were at random locations on the phantom surface. All CT BBs were replaced with IRRMs after the scan. An isocenter was arbitrarily defined inside the head phantom in the TPS. Its coordinates were then sent to the PSS, along with those of the CT BBs. In the treatment room, the head phantom was positioned to the treatment isocenter under the guidance of CBCT. The PSS used one IRRM at a time, with the other four IRRMs blocked from the camera, to verify setup accuracy.

To evaluate the ability of the system to detect couch shifts of various magnitudes, the phantom was positioned to 30 different locations by shifting the couch ±0.5, ±1, ±2, ±5, or ±10 cm along each axis. The PSS-observed couch shifts were compared to nominal couch shifts. The IRRM over the chin area was used in this study. Its room coordinates before couch shifts were captured as a baseline. This experiment was repeated with a 60° couch rotation to evaluate system performance under noncoplanar treatment conditions.

#### Patient Study: Head and Neck Patients

The PSS has been implemented in four treatment rooms at the institution since April 2012. Setup of all patients in each treatment session has been verified with the system. Twenty patients with disease in the head and neck area were included in this retrospective study to evaluate system accuracy. The patients were immobilized with an H&N immobilization system. The indexed immobilization system related CT BBs embedded in the CT couch top to the treatment isocenter through indexing bars. This couch indexing system (CIS) provided initial target localization, which was subsequently refined with in-room CBCT guidance. The PSS started automatic position verification at the moment the face mask was placed on the patient. Position deviations of the IRRM, observed with the PSS before and after CBCT-guided couch adjustment, were used to evaluate system accuracy against the CIS and CBCT system, respectively. Differences between PSS-observed couch shifts and couch shifts from CBCT registration were also reported.

#### Patient Study: Abdominal-Pelvic Patients

For patients with disease in the thoracic or abdominal-pelvic region, the IRRM would be placed directly on the patient's chest or abdomen in the first treatment session. With the automated workflow, the IRRM would be transferred to the patient's immobilization device. However, if it is desirable to continuously monitor the patient during treatment, or when a body mold is not used in patient treatment (e.g., for palliative purposes), the IRRM could be affixed to the patient's skin in each session. Real-time position of the IRRM is subject to breathing motion when directly placed on the patient's chest or abdomen. Maximum range of external surface motion can be over 20 mm depending on factors such as the patient's health condition and breathing pattern, which directly impacts the action level selection in the PSS. To evaluate the effect of breathing motion, twenty patients with disease in the abdominal-pelvic region were included in this study. The IRRM was placed directly over the abdominal area where breathing motion mostly occurred. Motion range throughout the entire treatment session and accuracy of the system when compared to the CIS and CBCT systems were reported for these patients.

### Results

#### System Workflow Efficiency

The expected total time in patient preparation for the use of the PSS is less than 5 minutes. For brain, head and neck, and extremity patients, the CT BB can be placed on immobilization devices and replaced with an IRRM after CT scan, and no skin tattoo is needed. For abdominal-pelvic patients, the CT BB has to be affixed on the patient's skin, thus requiring a skin tattoo. In the current practice, two sets of skin tattoos are made for each abdominal-pelvic patient to assist patient setup (mainly body rotation correction). Each set includes one anterior and two side tattoos, with one on each side. Either of the two anterior tattoos, when included in the CT scan, can be selected to affix the CT BB. Therefore, no extra tattoo needs to be made for the use of the PSS. Information transfer from the TPS to the PSS, implemented with scripting language and standard FTP (file transfer protocol), takes a single button click in the TPS. An efficient software module is implemented to combine information from the TPS and R&V and import it into the PSS database.

At the treatment stage, if an IRRM has been mounted on immobilization devices, no therapist intervention is needed. Otherwise, therapists will locate the selected anterior skin tattoo for initial verification, select a location on the immobilization device to permanently mount an IRRM, and capture the new location with the PSS. This procedure adds approximately 2 to 3 minutes to the patient setup time in the first treatment session. No intervention is needed in future treatment sessions.

#### System Stability

The results of the short-term stability test are shown in Table 1 (line A). The standard deviation of the position variation in a 10-minute period was close to 0 in all directions. Maximum variation was 0.3 mm along the longitudinal direction. This variation was in part due to vibrations in the ceiling. Table 1 (line B) shows the results of the long-term stability test, in which the IRRM was positioned to the same location weekly along with the daily QA device under CBCT guidance. An evenly distributed deviation was observed along the three axes with mean errors of 0.5±0.4 mm, 0.5±0.5 mm, and 0.6±0.5 mm for the lateral, longitudinal, and anteroposterior directions, respectively. The combined 3D deviation was 1.0±0.6 mm on average, with a maximum of 2.0 mm. The deviation included uncertainty from CBCT image registration, as well as automatic couch movement. The accuracy of both the CBCT image registration and automatic couch movement was reported to be on the order of 1.0 mm.

#### System Accuracy: Phantom Study

The results of the phantom study are shown in Table 2. In the end-to-end test with five individual IRRMs, mean error on all three axes was within 1.5 mm; the maximum deviation was 2.3 mm for both the longitudinal and anteroposterior axes (Table 2 line A). The combined 3D deviation was 2.2±0.8 mm, on average, with a maximum of 3.1 mm. Variation (less than 2.3 mm on all three axes) was observed among individual IRRMs mounted at different locations. The IRRM situated over the chin area was selected in the remaining studies due to its superior visibility. In the end-to-end test, deviation with this marker was 0.8 mm, 0.6 mm, and 0.1 mm along the lateral, longitudinal, and anteroposterior axis, respectively.

Table 2 line B shows the difference between nominal couch shifts and PSS-observed shifts. In this test, 30 combinations of shifts between 0.5 cm and 10 cm were applied to the couch along each axis. Maximum difference on individual axis was less than 1.5 mm, and maximum combined 3D difference was less than 2.0 mm. When there was a 60° couch rotation, maximum difference on individual axis and combined 3D difference increased to 1.7 mm (along the anteroposterior axis) and 2.8 mm, respectively, as shown in Table 2 line C. A possible reason for the increased deviation with couch rotation is that the PSS tracks the IRRM instead of the treatment isocenter. Small uncertainty in couch rotation can translate into large spatial deviation, depending on the distance between the IRRM and the treatment isocenter. For example, a couch rotation error of 1° (typical uncertainty of couch rotation) results in an effective spatial displacement of 1.7 mm when the IRRM is 10 cm away from the isocenter.

#### System Accuracy: Head and Neck Patients

The results of system accuracy evaluated retrospectively on 20 head and neck patients are shown in Table 3. Table 3 lines A and B show deviations of the PSS with respect to the CIS and CBCT systems, respectively. Results in the table were obtained by analyzing recorded real-time marker positions before and after CBCT-guided automatic couch shift. Mean deviation of the system along each axis was less than 2.5 mm with respect to both systems, but the maximum deviation reached 3.8 mm (lateral direction) when compared with the CIS, and 5.5 mm (anteroposterior direction) when compared with the CBCT system. The maximum combined 3D deviation of the system was less than 8.0 mm when compared to both systems. The system showed better agreement with the CIS than with the CBCT system. One of the reasons is that, unlike the CBCT system, the PSS and CIS do not account for daily setup variation, as well as patient internal anatomy change. For this reason, the CBCT system represents the desired treatment position and is used to guide treatment. Table 3 lines C and D show intended couch shifts based on CBCT image registration and residual errors as observed with the PSS, respectively. Intended couch shifts were evenly distributed among three axes, with a mean between 2.4 mm and 3.5 mm and a maximum between 5.6 mm and 7.0 mm. Combined 3D shift had an average of 5.8±2.1 mm, with a maximum of 9.0 mm. Residual errors, observed with the PSS, had an average less than 1.0 mm and a maximum less than 2.0 mm along all three axes. The maximum combined 3D residual error was less than 2.0 mm. These results show the ability of the PSS to (1) predict patient treatment position with sufficient accuracy and (2) accurately track automatic couch motion, which lacks independent verification in current practice.

#### System Accuracy: Abdominal-Pelvic Patients

Table 4 shows the results for abdominal-pelvic patients. The IRRM was placed directly on the patient's abdominal area in this study. Due to significant breathing motion, the average IRRM position of at least four breathing cycles (~20 seconds) was used in the analysis. When compared with the CIS, average deviations were between 2.8 mm and 3.9 mm along all three axes, as shown in Table 4 line A. The maximum deviations were less than 6.0 mm for each axis. Average deviations along each axis, in general, were approximately 1 to 2 mm larger for these patients than for head and neck patients. While breathing motion partially contributed to these larger deviations, system accuracy for these patients was mainly limited by the accuracy in placing the IRRM on the moving patient surface. Additional uncertainty of approximately 1 to 2 mm could have been introduced when placing the 6 mm diameter IRRM over the tattoo on the patient's abdomen, compared to placing an IRRM on a stationary face mask. Situated on loose skin, the relative position of the tattoo with respect to the treatment isocenter could vary from day to day, and from the time of CT simulation. Comparison of the PSS with the CBCT system yielded similar results as shown in Table 4 line B, except the maximum deviation along the longitudinal direction, which was 4 mm larger. Table 4 line C shows the intended couch shifts resulting from CBCT registration. These results were comparable to head and neck patients along the lateral and anteroposterior directions, but twice as large along the longitudinal direction, with an average of 4.5 mm and a maximum of 12.5 mm. Average residual errors of couch shift observed with the PSS were below 2.0 mm for all three axes, as displayed in Table 4 line D. A maximum difference of 3.7 mm was observed along the anteroposterior direction. Table 4 line E shows the range of patient motion, indicated by the motion of the IRRM. While the motion along either the lateral or longitudinal direction was no more than 6.0 mm, average motion of 9.7±3.3 mm was observed along the anteroposterior direction, with the maximum being 16.2 mm. The combined 3D motion had a maximum of 17.2 mm for these patients.

### Discussion

#### System Accuracy for Gross Error Detection

The results demonstrate that the system has adequate accuracy in detecting gross setup errors. The system displayed good reproducibility, with short-term and long-term variation within 0.5 mm and 2.0 mm, respectively. The end-to-end test with an anthropomorphic head phantom revealed system accuracy on the order of approximately 1 to 2 mm when compared with the CBCT. It accurately tracked couch motions along each axis to within 1.2 mm when there was no couch rotation, and within 1.7 mm when there was a 60° couch rotation. These results show that the system is able to accurately predict patient treatment position and detect unintended couch shifts. On head and neck patients, system tracking accuracy slightly decreased due to finite CT slice thickness (3 mm), which affected POI identification of the CT BB in the TPS and accuracy of IRRM placement on real patients. However, the accuracy was generally within 6.0 mm in each orthogonal direction when compared to both the CIS and CBCT systems, indicating that it is capable of detecting gross setup errors.

The feasibility of placing the IRRM on the abdominal area for abdominal-pelvic patients has been investigated. Maximum deviation of the system with respect to the CIS was less than 6.0 mm, but reached 9.9 mm when compared with the CBCT system. For the patients in this study, maximum daily CBCT shift along one direction was as high as 12.5 mm, and patient breathing motion range reached 16.2 mm. These results have implications for the ability of the system to detect gross setup errors (when defined as unintended shifts greater than 10 mm). The system may generate frequent false alarms due to CBCT-determined large shifts or breathing motion for patients with heavy breathing. To alleviate false alarms resulting from large CBCT shifts, the action level needs to be relaxed (for example, to 15.0 mm) along the longitudinal direction. There are two ways to deal with false alarms resulting from large breathing motion. One is to place the CT BB (and IRRM) on the patient's chest area where less motion occurs. There are two issues with this method. Firstly, it may require an extra skin tattoo on the patient at the time of CT scan, which means extra work in the workflow and a potential source of error in IRRM placement later at treatment. In addition, the chest area may not be included in the CT scan for all abdominal-pelvic patients. Another way is to permanently transfer the IRRM from the patient's skin to the immobilization device in the first treatment fraction, as suggested by the authors. The drawback of this method is that, though accidental couch movement can be detected in real-time, accidental patient motion cannot be monitored. A major advantage of this method is a streamlined, fully automated workflow. No therapist intervention is needed after the first treatment session.

#### Importance of Smooth Clinic Workflow

Significant emphasis was placed on achieving a smooth clinic workflow in the development of the system. The streamlined workflow was achieved through synchronization with the R&V system and mounting specially designed IRRMs directly on patient immobilization devices. The former allows for automatic loading of patient treatment information. The latter enables immediate and automatic tracking. These automations not only minimize therapists' workload but also mitigate potential human errors in loading patient data or mounting the IRRM. A general concern with the use of reflective markers on patient skin for localization is the uncertainty associated with the placement of markers. The method of permanently mounting IRRMs on patient immobilization devices minimizes such uncertainty.

In the opinion of the inventors, a smooth clinic workflow is crucial for the successful employment of a safety system on a large scale. Nowadays, the overall incident rate in radiotherapy is very low, and gross setup errors are even rarer, due to the widespread use of IGRT systems. A safety system must be efficient in detecting these rare errors. At the institution, the proposal of the safety system met initial resistance and reluctance from some therapists who claimed that the system would be redundant because of the use of daily CBCT imaging guidance. They were also concerned about the additional workload that would be added to an already busy schedule. However, the safety system was well adopted after deployment for two reasons. First, because of the smooth workflow, therapists gain extra confidence in patient setup at almost no expense. Second, the system caught several near-misses in treatment rooms equipped with CBCT systems and proved its effectiveness in ensuring patient safety.

#### Error Detection

In the experience of the inventors, setup-related accidents typically do not occur in routine treatments but occur in unexpected and unusual circumstances. Quality assurance and quality control systems are well designed to ensure patient safety in routine treatments. These systems may get bypassed under unexpected and unusual circumstances. For example, when treating patients having diseases with a large lateral offset (> 10 cm) from midline, collision between the CBCT imaging source (or panel) and the treatment couch (or patient) may be inevitable. In these cases, CBCT imaging can only be performed with a lateral couch offset less than planned by a few centimeters (e.g., 5 cm). After CBCT imaging, the difference can be accounted for through manual couch adjustment. This process needs additional human involvement and can be error-prone. As another example, if a posterior beam goes through a metal component in the treatment couch top, an unconventional indexing position may be necessary as a result of shifting the patient immobilization device longitudinally on the treatment table. If this information is not successfully communicated to substitute therapists in future treatment sessions, the patient can be set up to a wrong longitudinal position. Similarly, when treating patients on a different linear accelerator than planned (for example, due to a machine being down) with different couch tops, different couch parameters are needed. The R&V system provides therapists the flexibility to override couch parameters at the treatment console without double approval. This can be a source of error if the wrong couch parameters are used for initial patient setup. As a final example, image-guided patient alignment using CBCT imaging or orthogonal filming may rely on image fusion of patient bony structures (e.g., spine and femur); anatomical similarities of these bony structures along the longitudinal direction may lead to misregistration and become a source of error. Manual registration or automatic software registration using a smaller region of interest than desired can significantly increase the likelihood of such errors.

Some errors resulting from these unusual circumstances can be detected before treatment starts, at the expense of treatment delay and unnecessary imaging exposure to the patient, while some errors (e.g., inadvertent couch shift after imaging and incorrect image registration) can go undetected and lead to treatment of the wrong sites. The PSS, providing independent and continuous patient position verification, can detect all these unusual errors when they occur, thus avoiding unnecessary imaging exposure and preventing severe treatment errors. The system is independent in the sense that it verifies patient setup by ensuring the position of the IRRM relative to the treatment room isocenter. It is independent of the treatment room, treatment machine, couch top design, immobilization device, or patient internal anatomy.

Since the deployment in April 2012 at the institution, the safety system has caught three near-misses (potential gross setup errors). Without the safety system, two errors would be eventually caught by CBCT. In the first case, the indexing bar for a head and neck patient was placed at a wrong indexing hole (off by 7 cm) by a substitute therapist in CT scan, resulting in the wrong calculated linac couch positions. The second case was also related to the couch indexing system. In the institution, three of the four couch tops are the same; the fourth leads to approximately 14 cm difference in longitudinal couch parameter for the same treatment. When one machine breaks down and patients have to be temporarily treated on another machine, the difference is accounted for by creating setup beams with new couch parameters for the new machine. Instead of changing the couch parameters for all treatment beams, therapists are allowed to override them at the console after CBCT verification. In this case, the setup beams for the original machine were accidentally loaded for patient setup, causing a 14 cm longitudinal offset in patient position. Both errors were caught by the PSS before CBCT imaging, preventing patients from unnecessary CBCT exposure and potential treatment of the wrong sites. In the third case, a large (~1 cm laterally) couch adjustment was needed after CBCT imaging. Due to concern of collision between the gantry head and patient arms (lung patient with arms up position), the treatment couch was arbitrarily moved laterally (~5 cm) such that the gantry could go to the intended treatment position. However, the couch was not moved back, and its parameters in the R&V system were overridden. The offset in patient position was immediately caught by the PSS at the time of couch shift and caught the therapists' attention before treatment. Without the PSS, this error could have gone undetected and resulted in treating a completely different site.

#### Comparison with Other Tracking Systems

Commercially available IGRT systems capable of continuous tracking, such as the ExacTrac Optical-Tracking System, Frameless SonArray, and AlignRT, have long been used for patient setup and monitoring due to their high precision and continuous tracking ability. However, they all come with limitations compared to the PSS when trying to use them on a large scale as a general-purpose patient setup system. Designed to monitor patient motion (both translation and rotation) during treatment, the ExacTrac Optical-Tracking System requires at least four IRRMs to be affixed on the patient in each treatment session, which not only introduces significant workload to therapists but also may act as a source of error. The Frameless SonArray system, which connects IRRMs in a fixed pattern to the patient through a bite block, is only employed in the treatment of brain targets. Similarly, 3D surface imaging-based systems like AlignRT and C-Rad Sentinel are not for general purpose and are predominantly utilized only in the treatment of brain, head and neck, or breast patients. Most importantly, all these systems lack a streamlined workflow. For each treatment, therapists need to manually load the right patient under treatment and the correct treatment site, and provide couch angle for individual beams in the case of noncoplanar treatment (e.g., in AlignRT). Frequent therapist interventions not only disrupt the clinic workflow but may also introduce human errors, which discourages their large-scale use as a patient safety system in a busy clinic.

#### Limitations of the System

There are a few limitations of the system that need to be considered. Instead of using commercially available spherical IRRMs, the PSS uses flat-surface, disposable IRRMs. These IRRMs are easier to fabricate and mount. However, they are subject to visibility issues due to their small volume and flat surface. The view of the cameras can be obstructed by patient skin slope (in chest area) or when the IRRM surface is angled away from the cameras. For patients treated with face masks, the problem is overcome by affixing the IRRM over the patient's chin area and tilting the reflective surface toward the cameras. For other patients treated with body molds, the IRRM is shifted from the patient's skin onto a spot on the body mold. The flat surface of the IRRM is tilted to ensure visibility. Another benefit of doing so is the streamlined workflow, but the tradeoff is the loss of the ability to track patient motion throughout the treatment. To mount the IRRM on the patient's immobilization device instead of the patient's skin for tracking, reproducibility of the patient lying in the body mold is assumed. In the institution, this is ensured by aligning the two sets of skin tattoos (intended for correcting patient rotation) with marks on the body mold made at CT simulation.
