# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of computer graphics and image-based rendering, specifically to the animation and real-time rendering of deformable three-dimensional objects with complex, spatially varying reflectance properties under arbitrary and dynamically changing illumination conditions. More particularly, the invention provides a novel method and system for preserving the perceptual appearance of material reflectance characteristics—such as anisotropic specular highlights, diffuse interreflections, self-shadowing, and subsurface scattering effects—when a surface reflectance field is subjected to nonrigid geometric deformations. The invention operates within the domain of hybrid image-based rendering systems that utilize an approximate impostor geometry to represent the shape of a real-world object while capturing its full bidirectional reflectance behavior across multiple viewing and lighting configurations. The method enables the deformation of such objects in animation sequences without requiring explicit knowledge of the object’s true surface normals or microstructure, making it applicable to objects scanned via 3D photography techniques where high-fidelity geometry is unavailable or impractical to acquire. The invention is particularly suited for applications in photorealistic computer animation, virtual production, digital archiving of cultural artifacts, e-commerce product visualization, and immersive virtual environments where dynamic lighting and realistic material behavior are critical to visual fidelity.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The accurate depiction of real-world objects in computer graphics has long been a central challenge, particularly when the goal is to preserve not only geometric shape but also the nuanced optical behavior of materials under varying lighting conditions. Traditional approaches to this problem fall into three broad categories: parametric modeling, pure image-based rendering, and hybrid representations. Parametric models, such as those based on analytical Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Functions (BRDFs), offer efficient rendering performance and compatibility with standard animation pipelines, including skinning and vertex blending. However, these models are fundamentally limited in their ability to capture the rich complexity of real materials, including non-Lambertian diffuse responses, interreflections between surface features, self-shadowing, translucency, and subsurface scattering. As a result, they often fail to reproduce the subtle visual cues that distinguish realistic materials from synthetic approximations.

Pure image-based rendering techniques, in contrast, circumvent the need for parametric modeling by directly capturing and interpolating between observed images of an object under various lighting and viewing conditions. Methods such as the lumigraph, surface light fields, and opacity light fields have demonstrated remarkable success in reproducing complex global illumination effects without requiring explicit material models. However, these approaches are inherently constrained by their static nature: they are typically acquired under fixed illumination and cannot be easily re-lit or deformed without introducing severe visual artifacts. The lack of an explicit geometric model that can be animated—such as a mesh or point cloud with deformable topology—renders these representations unsuitable for dynamic scenes, where objects must be articulated, stretched, bent, or otherwise transformed over time.

Hybrid approaches attempt to bridge this gap by combining the visual fidelity of image-based data with the animatability of geometric representations. The surface reflectance field, as introduced in prior art, represents a significant advancement in this direction. It captures the full reflectance behavior of an object across a dense sampling of viewing directions and illumination configurations, enabling the object to be rendered under arbitrary new lighting conditions. This representation is typically stored on an impostor geometry—a simplified, approximate surface model derived from silhouette constraints or visual hull techniques—which serves as a scaffold for interpolating the acquired reflectance data during rendering. While this method allows for relighting, it has historically been limited to static objects, as no robust mechanism existed to preserve the material appearance when the impostor geometry is deformed.

Prior attempts to animate image-based representations have focused primarily on surface light fields, which are restricted to fixed illumination and lack the ability to simulate new lighting environments. One such method, proposed by Wood et al., applies arbitrary deformations to the impostor geometry and interpolates the stored images accordingly. However, this approach fails to preserve the angular relationships between incident lighting, viewing direction, and surface orientation, leading to incorrect shading of diffuse components and an inability to maintain the shape of specular lobes. Other techniques, such as feature-based light field morphing, require extensive manual annotation of corresponding features between source and target configurations and are not scalable to continuous animation. A more recent approach by Furukawa et al. introduces the animation of Bidirectional Texture Functions (BTFs) using tensor product compression and a tightly fitted impostor geometry. While this method achieves relightable deformations, it relies on precise geometric acquisition via range scanning, which is not always feasible, and does not explicitly address the preservation of reflectance characteristics under nonuniform or skewed deformations where the impostor geometry diverges significantly from the true object surface.

A critical limitation common to all prior methods is their inability to maintain the spatially varying BRDFs of the original object during deformation. When an impostor geometry is warped, the mapping between the deformed surface point, the incident lighting direction, and the viewing direction must be transformed back into the original acquisition space to retrieve the correct reflectance value. Naive inversion of the deformation warp—such as applying the inverse transformation directly to lighting and viewing vectors—fails to preserve the geometric relationships necessary for accurate shading. This results in distorted specular highlights, misaligned anisotropic reflections, and incorrect shadowing patterns that break the illusion of realism. Furthermore, existing systems do not account for the fact that the impostor geometry is an approximation of the true object surface, and thus, the surface normals used for shading are not exact. Consequently, any shading scheme that assumes perfect correspondence between the impostor and the real object will produce artifacts under nonuniform deformations.

Additionally, the computational burden of shading a deformed surface reflectance field is immense. Each point on the deformed impostor requires multiple lookups into a large database of reflectance images, each of which may be stored at high dynamic range and sampled across hundreds of viewing and lighting configurations. The resulting data volume often exceeds the capacity of conventional memory, necessitating frequent disk access. Without optimization, the rendering pipeline becomes prohibitively slow, especially when animating complex objects with high-resolution reflectance data. Existing systems lack a coherent strategy for managing this data access, leading to poor cache utilization and inefficient shading execution.

Finally, the integration of dynamic lighting into deformed surface reflectance fields remains an unsolved problem. Environment mapping, a common technique for simulating complex illumination, requires filtering of the environment map according to the spatial resolution of the reflectance data to avoid aliasing. However, when the impostor is deformed, the mapping of lighting directions changes per surface point, requiring a unique filtered version of the environment map for each query—an operation that is computationally infeasible in real time. Alternative methods, such as approximating environment maps with dense point light sources, have been proposed but lack integration with the core shading mechanism of surface reflectance fields and do not address the underlying issue of appearance preservation under deformation.

Thus, there exists a critical need for a method that enables the animation of surface reflectance fields under arbitrary geometric deformations while preserving the perceptual integrity of material reflectance properties, without requiring precise surface normals or high-fidelity geometry, and that simultaneously provides a computationally efficient shading framework capable of handling the massive data throughput required for real-time or near-real-time rendering. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel local parameterization of the impostor geometry that enables the approximate preservation of spatially varying BRDFs during deformation, coupled with a deferred shading scheme optimized for cache coherence and efficient data retrieval.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method and system for animating surface reflectance fields under arbitrary geometric deformations while preserving the perceptual appearance of material reflectance properties. The invention operates on a hybrid image-based representation comprising a dense collection of reflectance images acquired under multiple viewing and lighting configurations, mapped onto an approximate impostor geometry that serves as a deformable scaffold for rendering. Unlike prior approaches, the invention does not require knowledge of the true object surface normals or microstructure, making it applicable to objects captured via 3D photography techniques where high-fidelity geometry is unavailable. The core innovation lies in the introduction of a local impostor parameterization that encodes, at each point of the impostor geometry, a set of geometric and orientational attributes sufficient to reconstruct an appearance-preserving mapping from the deformed space back to the acquisition space. This mapping ensures that the angular relationships between incident lighting, viewing direction, and surface orientation are preserved during deformation, thereby maintaining the shape and orientation of specular lobes, diffuse reflectance patterns, and anisotropic material responses.

The local impostor parameterization consists of four key components stored per impostor point: the original position of the point in acquisition space, a fixed rotation matrix representing the local tangential frame in acquisition space, and two deforming tangential vectors that describe the local surface orientation in the current deformed configuration. These parameters are computed during preprocessing and updated during deformation without requiring an explicit inverse warp function. During rendering, the system computes a local rigid transformation—comprising a translation and a rotation—that maps the deformed query point and its associated lighting and viewing directions back into the acquisition space. The translation aligns the deformed point with its original position, while the rotation aligns the local tangential frame of the deformed point with the fixed acquisition frame, ensuring that the relative angles between lighting, viewing, and surface orientation are preserved. This transformation is derived entirely from the stored parameters and the local deformation field, and it does not rely on the true object geometry or surface normals, making it robust to nonuniform, skewed, and large-scale deformations.

To enable efficient shading of the deformed surface reflectance field, the invention introduces a deferred shading scheme that decomposes the rendering process into minimal, cache-coherent shading operations. Each operation consists of a single lookup into the reflectance database, triggered by a transformed query point, lighting direction, and viewing direction. These operations are collected in a buffer and sorted according to their spatial locality in the reflectance data storage, minimizing disk access and maximizing cache utilization. By reordering the evaluation sequence to group together queries that access nearby reflectance images, the system reduces the number of disk reads by an order of magnitude, enabling real-time or near-real-time rendering even with massive reflectance datasets that exceed main memory capacity.

The invention further provides a novel method for simulating dynamic and environment lighting on deformed surface reflectance fields without requiring per-point filtering of environment maps. Instead of directly applying environment maps, the system approximates the lighting environment using a dense set of point light sources, each positioned at the original acquisition lighting directions and colored according to the corresponding environment map intensity. These point light sources are then used to illuminate the deformed impostor geometry, with each lighting query transformed via the appearance-preserving mapping described above. This approach eliminates the need for expensive refiltering operations while preserving the spatial frequency content of the lighting environment through the inherent interpolation properties of the surface reflectance field. The method naturally supports both static and dynamic lighting scenarios, including colored, moving, and flickering light sources, without compromising visual fidelity.

The invention is compatible with any impostor geometry representation, including point-sampled surfels, triangular meshes, or voxel grids, and is particularly well-suited for point-based representations where tangential frames can be directly encoded as part of the point attributes. The use of elliptical surface splats, defined by position and two nonorthogonal tangential vectors, allows for seamless rendering of deformed regions without visible holes or texture distortion, even under extreme warping. The system also incorporates a view- and light-source interpolation scheme based on unstructured lumigraph principles, enabling smooth transitions between discrete acquisition samples in both viewing and lighting dimensions, even when the queried directions fall outside the original sampling grid.

The invention achieves a level of appearance preservation previously unattainable in the animation of surface reflectance fields. It correctly maintains the spatial variation of BRDFs across the object surface, preserves the orientation of anisotropic reflections under shear and stretch, and avoids the common artifacts of rotated specular highlights and misaligned diffuse shading that plague prior methods. The method is computationally efficient, scalable to large datasets, and requires no manual annotation or feature correspondence. It is especially effective for objects with complex material properties such as specular metals, translucent fabrics, and self-shadowing geometries, and it enables the realistic animation of real-world objects in arbitrary lighting environments, opening new possibilities for photorealistic virtual production, digital heritage preservation, and immersive interactive media.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

### Approximate BRDF Preservation

The fundamental challenge in animating a surface reflectance field lies in preserving the perceptual appearance of material reflectance properties when the underlying impostor geometry undergoes deformation. A surface reflectance field, as defined herein, is a discrete sampling of the object’s bidirectional reflectance behavior across a dense set of viewing and lighting configurations, stored on an approximate geometric representation that does not necessarily coincide with the true object surface. The reflectance at any point on the impostor geometry is determined by querying a multidimensional function that maps a point location, an incident lighting direction, and a viewing direction to a radiance value. When the impostor geometry is deformed, the spatial relationships between these three components change, and a naive application of the inverse deformation warp to the lighting and viewing directions fails to preserve the angular relationships that define the material’s reflectance characteristics. This results in distorted specular highlights, misaligned anisotropic reflections, and incorrect diffuse shading that break the illusion of realism.

To address this, the present invention introduces a novel appearance-preserving mapping that transforms a query in the deformed object space back into the acquisition space in such a way that the essential geometric relationships governing reflectance are maintained. This mapping is not intended to model the physical changes in material microstructure that occur during deformation—such as the rearrangement of fibers, the stretching of microfacets, or the alteration of subsurface scattering paths—but rather to preserve the observed reflectance behavior as it was captured during acquisition. The method assumes that the object’s visual appearance can be adequately described by its local spatially varying BRDFs, and that the deformation does not alter the intrinsic reflectance properties of the material itself, only its geometric orientation in space.

The desired mapping, denoted as L, takes as input a deformed point p*, a deformed lighting direction l*, and a deformed viewing direction v*, and outputs a corresponding point p, lighting direction l, and viewing direction v in the acquisition space. The mapping is constructed to satisfy three critical conditions for appearance preservation. First, the viewing ray in object space, defined as p* + s v* for s ∈ ℝ, should intersect the deformed object at a point q*, and the corresponding viewing ray in acquisition space, p + t v for t ∈ ℝ, should intersect the original object at the corresponding point q = É⁻¹(q*). Although the true object geometry is unknown, the impostor point p = É⁻¹(p*) is used as a proxy for q, and the viewing ray is anchored at this point. This approximation is justified by the fact that, for a well-constructed impostor geometry, p lies in close proximity to the true surface point q, and the error introduced is negligible compared to the spatial sampling resolution of the reflectance data.

Second, the angles between the incident lighting direction and the surface normal, and between the viewing direction and the surface normal, must be preserved. That is, the angle between l* and the deformed surface normal m* must equal the angle between l and the original surface normal m, and similarly for v* and v. This condition ensures that the shape and orientation of the BRDF lobes—the characteristic patterns of reflected light—are maintained. For example, a specular highlight that appears as a narrow, elongated streak on a brushed metal surface must retain its shape and orientation even after the surface is stretched or twisted. This condition is particularly critical for anisotropic materials, where the reflectance varies significantly with azimuthal orientation around the surface normal.

Third, the azimuthal orientation of the lighting and viewing directions relative to the local tangential frame must be preserved. This means that the direction of l* and v* within the plane tangent to the surface at q* must correspond to the direction of l and v within the plane tangent to the surface at q. This ensures that anisotropic effects, such as the directional sheen of a velvet fabric or the linear highlights of a scratched metal surface, remain aligned with the local surface features rather than rotating arbitrarily with the deformation.

To satisfy these conditions, the mapping L is decomposed into a rigid transformation consisting of a translation and a rotation. The translation component moves the deformed point p* back to its original position p = É⁻¹(p*), aligning the spatial origin of the query with the acquisition space. The rotation component, denoted as L_p*, is a local isometry that aligns the deformed tangential frame at p* with the original tangential frame at p. This rotation must preserve angles and lengths, meaning it is an element of the special orthogonal group SO(3), and can be represented as a rotation matrix or, equivalently, as a Rodrigues vector for compact storage.

The key insight of the invention is that, although the true surface normal m is unknown, the normal of the impostor geometry at p can be used as a reliable approximation. This approximation is not used to compute reflectance directly—reflectance is retrieved from the stored images without reference to any normal—but rather to derive the rotational component of the mapping. The tangential frame at p is defined by two orthogonal vectors u₀ and v₀, which span the tangent plane and are stored as part of the local parameterization. Similarly, the deformed tangential frame at p* is defined by two vectors u and v, which are obtained by applying the deformation warp É to the original tangential vectors u₀ and v₀ using central differences to approximate the Jacobian of the warp. These deformed vectors may be nonorthogonal and nonunit length, reflecting the local distortion introduced by the deformation.

From these vectors, an orthogonalized tangential frame is computed by applying a Gram-Schmidt procedure to minimize the squared angular differences between the deformed and original frames. This yields two orthonormal vectors û and v̂ that define the best-fit local orientation of the deformed surface. The rotation L_p* is then constructed as the transformation that maps the original tangential basis (u₀, v₀, n₀) to the orthogonalized deformed basis (û, v̂, n̂), where n₀ and n̂ are the corresponding normals. This rotation ensures that the azimuthal orientation of lighting and viewing directions is preserved relative to the local surface frame, even under extreme shearing or stretching. The resulting transformation is rigid, meaning it does not introduce scaling or shearing into the lighting and viewing vectors, which would otherwise distort the BRDF shape.

This approach is fundamentally different from prior methods that apply the inverse warp directly to lighting and viewing directions without regard to the local surface orientation. Those methods produce artifacts because they treat lighting and viewing as free vectors in space, rather than as directions constrained by the local geometry. The present invention, by contrast, anchors the transformation to the local surface frame, ensuring that the reflectance properties are preserved relative to the object’s own coordinate system. The method is also independent of the specific form of the deformation warp É, requiring only that É be differentiable in the neighborhood of the impostor geometry, which is a weak constraint easily satisfied by most animation techniques, including skeletal skinning, mesh deformation, and fluid simulations.

The resulting mapping is not exact, as it cannot account for nonlocal effects such as interreflections or subsurface scattering, nor can it perfectly reconstruct the true object surface. However, it is the best possible approximation given the constraints of the surface reflectance field representation. In the limit, as the impostor geometry converges to the true object surface, the mapping becomes exact. Moreover, the method benefits from improvements in geometric acquisition—such as higher-resolution scanning or stereo reconstruction—without requiring any changes to the core algorithm. The preservation of BRDF characteristics under deformation is thus achieved not through physical modeling, but through geometric alignment, making the method both robust and generalizable across a wide range of materials and deformation types.

The invention further ensures that the mapping is computationally efficient and cache-friendly. The rotation L_p* is precomputed during preprocessing and stored as a Rodrigues vector, requiring only three floating-point values per impostor point. During rendering, the transformation is applied in a single step: the deformed lighting and viewing directions are rotated using the stored Rodrigues vector, and the deformed point is translated to its original position. This operation is performed per shading query and requires no matrix inversion, no iterative optimization, and no dependency on the global structure of the deformation. The entire process is local, deterministic, and requires minimal computational overhead, making it suitable for real-time applications even on modest hardware.

In summary, the approximate BRDF preservation method of the invention provides a principled, geometrically grounded solution to the problem of material appearance preservation under deformation. It does not require knowledge of the true object geometry, it is compatible with any deformable impostor representation, and it produces visually convincing results even under extreme nonuniform deformations. By treating the impostor normal as a proxy for the true surface normal and constructing a local rigid transformation that aligns the tangential frames, the invention successfully decouples the problem of geometric animation from the problem of material appearance, enabling the realistic animation of complex real-world objects under arbitrary lighting conditions.
