# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of immunotherapeutics for the prevention and treatment of influenza A virus infections in humans. More specifically, the invention provides naturally occurring human monoclonal antibodies that specifically bind to the extracellular domain of the matrix protein 2 (M2e) of influenza A virus, with high affinity and broad cross-reactivity across multiple influenza A subtypes, including but not limited to H1N1, H3N2, and H5N1 strains. These antibodies are capable of mediating antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) against influenza A virus-infected cells, thereby providing potent prophylactic and therapeutic efficacy in vivo. The invention further encompasses the use of these antibodies, or antigen-binding fragments thereof, in pharmaceutical compositions for passive immunization, particularly in individuals who are immunocompromised, elderly, or otherwise unable to mount an effective immune response to conventional influenza vaccines. The antibodies described herein represent a novel class of human-derived biologics that overcome the limitations of current influenza interventions, which are rendered ineffective by the antigenic drift and shift characteristic of influenza A viruses. Unlike traditional vaccines that target highly variable surface proteins such as hemagglutinin, the antibodies of this invention recognize a conserved epitope within the M2e region, which has remained structurally unchanged across human influenza A isolates for over a century. This conservation enables the development of a universal countermeasure against seasonal and pandemic influenza strains without the need for annual reformulation. The invention also includes methods for the production, purification, and formulation of these antibodies in clinically viable quantities, as well as dosing regimens for both prophylactic administration prior to exposure and therapeutic intervention following the onset of infection.

## RELATED ART

The global burden of influenza A virus infection remains substantial, with annual epidemics causing millions of severe illnesses and hundreds of thousands of deaths worldwide. Current preventive strategies rely primarily on seasonal vaccines that induce neutralizing antibodies against the hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) surface glycoproteins. However, these proteins undergo frequent antigenic drift through point mutations and occasional antigenic shift through reassortment of viral genome segments, necessitating continuous surveillance and annual reformulation of vaccines. Despite these efforts, vaccine effectiveness often falls below 50% in high-risk populations such as the elderly and immunocompromised individuals, due to diminished immune responsiveness and mismatch between circulating strains and vaccine components. Passive immunization with monoclonal antibodies has emerged as a promising alternative, particularly for rapid protection in high-risk scenarios such as post-exposure prophylaxis or outbreak containment. Several monoclonal antibodies targeting HA have been developed and shown to be effective in preclinical models and early clinical trials; however, their utility is inherently limited by the variability of HA, rendering them ineffective against newly emerged strains. In contrast, the matrix protein 2 (M2) of influenza A virus has long been recognized as a promising target for universal influenza interventions due to its exceptional conservation across strains. The extracellular domain of M2 (M2e), comprising 23 amino acids, exhibits minimal sequence variation among human isolates spanning nearly a century, largely due to its co-transcription with the matrix protein M1 and its low abundance on the virion surface. While M2e-specific antibodies do not neutralize free virions, they confer protection by engaging immune effector mechanisms, particularly antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), which leads to the lysis of infected host cells before viral progeny are released. Early studies demonstrated that passive transfer of murine monoclonal antibodies directed against M2e could protect mice from lethal influenza challenge, and subsequent work extended these findings to humanized and fully human antibodies generated through phage display or transchromosomic mouse platforms. However, these engineered antibodies, while functional, carry inherent risks of immunogenicity, suboptimal affinity maturation, and potential off-target reactivity due to non-natural pairing of variable domains. Moreover, no naturally occurring human monoclonal antibodies specific for M2e had been isolated prior to this invention, despite decades of research into M2e as a vaccine target. This absence reflects the low immunogenicity of M2e in natural infection and the technical challenges associated with isolating rare, high-affinity B cell clones from human donors. Conventional methods for antibody discovery, such as single-cell sorting and cloning from memory B cells, are labor-intensive, low-throughput, and prone to selection bias, often failing to capture the full diversity of natural antibody repertoires. Furthermore, many previously reported M2e-specific antibodies, even when humanized, exhibited limited cross-reactivity with emerging zoonotic strains such as H5N1, reducing their potential as broad-spectrum therapeutics. The lack of a naturally derived, high-affinity, broadly reactive human antibody against M2e has therefore represented a critical gap in the development of a universal influenza immunotherapy. The present invention fills this gap by disclosing the first naturally occurring human monoclonal antibodies isolated directly from the peripheral blood of a human donor with elevated M2e-specific immune responses, using a novel mammalian cell display platform that preserves native heavy and light chain pairings and enables efficient screening of millions of antibody variants in a physiologically relevant context. These antibodies exhibit unprecedented affinity for M2e, cross-reactivity with divergent strains, and potent in vivo efficacy in both prophylactic and therapeutic settings, establishing a new standard for influenza immunotherapy.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel class of naturally occurring human monoclonal antibodies that bind with high affinity to the conserved extracellular domain of the matrix protein 2 (M2e) of influenza A virus, and which are capable of mediating protective immune responses against influenza infection through antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity. These antibodies are derived from human B cells and retain the native heavy and light chain pairing that results from physiological affinity maturation, thereby minimizing immunogenicity and maximizing target specificity. The antibodies of the invention exhibit dissociation constants in the low nanomolar range for the M2e consensus peptide and demonstrate robust cross-reactivity with M2e sequences derived from multiple influenza A subtypes, including H1N1, H3N2, and H5N1, as confirmed by binding assays using synthetic peptides and native M2 protein expressed on the surface of infected cells. The antibodies are capable of recognizing M2 in its native conformation on the plasma membrane of virus-infected cells, and they mediate potent ADCC activity in vitro, as demonstrated by their ability to induce lysis of M2-expressing target cells by natural killer cells. In vivo, the antibodies provide complete protection against lethal influenza A challenge in a murine model when administered prophylactically, even at very low doses, and retain significant therapeutic efficacy when administered up to three days after infection. The invention further encompasses fully human IgG1 isotype variants of these antibodies, which are expressed at high yields in mammalian cell culture systems and retain full biological activity, including the ability to recruit human Fcγ receptors and activate effector functions in human immune cells. The antibodies of the invention are suitable for administration via intravenous, intramuscular, or subcutaneous routes and are formulated in stable, sterile, pyrogen-free solutions suitable for clinical use. The invention also includes pharmaceutical compositions comprising these antibodies in combination with pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, excipients, or stabilizers, as well as kits for diagnostic or therapeutic use. The antibodies described herein represent a breakthrough in influenza immunotherapy, offering a universal, off-the-shelf solution that does not require strain-specific updates and is effective in populations that respond poorly to conventional vaccines. Their discovery and characterization represent a significant advancement over prior art, which relied on engineered or non-human antibodies with limited cross-reactivity, suboptimal affinity, or increased immunogenic risk. The invention enables the development of a single therapeutic agent capable of protecting against seasonal epidemics and potential pandemic strains, thereby addressing a critical unmet medical need in global public health.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention disclosed herein is directed to a set of naturally occurring human monoclonal antibodies that bind with high specificity and affinity to the extracellular domain of the matrix protein 2 (M2e) of influenza A virus. These antibodies were isolated from the peripheral blood mononuclear cells of a human donor who exhibited elevated levels of M2e-specific immune responses, using a mammalian cell display system that preserves the native pairing of heavy and light chain variable regions. The antibodies are encoded by immunoglobulin genes that have undergone physiological affinity maturation within the human immune system, ensuring minimal immunogenicity and optimal target recognition. The heavy and light chain variable regions of the antibodies were cloned from single B cells and assembled into single-chain variable fragment (scFv) constructs, which were then displayed on the surface of Sindbis virus-infected BHK cells. Through iterative rounds of fluorescence-activated cell sorting using M2e-conjugated bait molecules, a diverse panel of M2e-specific scFv clones was isolated, with the majority of functional binders derived from κ light chain-expressing B cells. Three lead antibodies—designated D005, E040, and F052—were selected for detailed characterization based on their high binding affinity, robust expression levels, and strong reactivity with both consensus and variant M2e sequences. These antibodies were subsequently fused to the Fc region of mouse IgG2c to enable in vivo functional testing in murine models, and later reformulated as fully human IgG1κ isotypes for clinical translation. Binding affinity was quantified using an ELISA-based solution competition assay, which revealed dissociation constants (Kd) of approximately 4 to 5 nanomolar for all three antibodies when binding to the M2e consensus peptide. Cross-reactivity was confirmed through ELISA assays using M2e peptides derived from recent H5N1 isolates, where the antibodies demonstrated comparable binding kinetics and half-maximal effective concentrations (EC50) in the low picomolar range. Importantly, the antibodies were shown to recognize native M2 protein expressed on the surface of L929 cells engineered to stably express the full-length M2 protein from influenza A/PR/8/34, confirming their ability to bind the antigen in its natural conformation within a cellular membrane context. In functional assays, the antibodies induced significant lysis of M2-expressing target cells in the presence of human natural killer cells, demonstrating their capacity to mediate antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, the primary mechanism by which M2e-specific antibodies confer protection in vivo. In prophylactic mouse models, passive administration of 500 micrograms of scFv-Fc fusion proteins completely protected all treated animals from a lethal challenge with mouse-adapted influenza A/PR/8/34, whereas control animals receiving non-specific mouse IgG succumbed to infection within eight days. Dose-response studies revealed that as little as 20 micrograms of antibody D005 was sufficient to confer 100% survival, and even a dose of 6 micrograms resulted in 50% survival, indicating exceptional potency. Therapeutic administration of D005 up to three days after infection still provided substantial protection, with nearly all animals surviving when treated within two days and a significant proportion surviving even when treated on day three. The fully human IgG1κ version of D005 was produced at yields exceeding 70 milligrams per liter of culture supernatant and demonstrated identical protective efficacy to its scFv-Fc counterpart in vivo, confirming that the human Fc domain is fully functional in the murine system and capable of engaging murine Fcγ receptors to mediate ADCC. These findings establish that the antibodies of the invention are not only highly specific and potent but also amenable to large-scale manufacturing and clinical deployment. The invention further encompasses methods for the production of these antibodies in stable mammalian cell lines using Epstein-Barr virus-based episomal vectors, which enable long-term, high-yield expression without genomic integration. Purification is achieved through protein A affinity chromatography, resulting in highly pure, endotoxin-free preparations suitable for parenteral administration. Formulations are prepared in phosphate-buffered saline containing human serum albumin and polysorbate 80 to ensure stability during storage and transport. The antibodies may be administered as single-dose or multi-dose regimens depending on the clinical indication, with prophylactic use recommended for high-risk populations prior to influenza season or during outbreaks, and therapeutic use indicated for early-stage infection in hospitalized or immunocompromised patients. The invention further includes diagnostic applications, such as the use of these antibodies in immunoassays for the detection of M2e-expressing influenza A virus in clinical samples, or as capture reagents in point-of-care diagnostics. The antibodies are also suitable for conjugation to toxins, radionuclides, or fluorescent labels for targeted delivery or imaging applications. The structural and functional characterization of these antibodies represents a significant departure from prior art, which has relied on non-human or engineered antibodies with limited breadth and clinical translatability. The discovery of naturally occurring human antibodies with such high affinity and cross-reactivity against a conserved influenza epitope was previously unanticipated, given the low immunogenicity of M2e and the rarity of such B cell clones in the human repertoire. The invention therefore provides not only a novel therapeutic agent but also a paradigm for the discovery of antibodies against other conserved, poorly immunogenic viral targets.

### EXAMPLE 1

The isolation of human monoclonal antibodies specific for the M2e domain of influenza A virus was achieved through a mammalian cell display platform that enables the direct cloning and surface expression of antibody fragments derived from single antigen-specific B cells. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were collected from a human donor who had previously demonstrated elevated serum titers of M2e-reactive antibodies, as determined by ELISA using synthetic M2e peptides. These cells were stained with a panel of fluorescently labeled reagents to identify B cells expressing surface immunoglobulins that bound to M2e conjugated to virus-like particles. Specifically, M2e was chemically linked to the Qβ bacteriophage capsid to form a multivalent antigenic scaffold, which was then labeled with Alexa Fluor 647. To distinguish M2e-specific B cells from background, a secondary antibody recognizing Qβ was conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488, and a mouse monoclonal antibody known to bind M2 (clone 14C2) was used in combination with a fluorescently labeled anti-mouse secondary to confirm specificity. B cells were further gated on CD19 positivity, CD3 and CD14 negativity, and IgM/IgD expression profiles to ensure selection of mature, antigen-experienced memory B cells. Using fluorescence-activated cell sorting, a total of 334 M2e-specific B cells were isolated and immediately lysed for RNA extraction. Reverse transcription was performed using oligo(dT) primers, followed by nested polymerase chain reaction to amplify the variable regions of the heavy and light chain immunoglobulin genes. The heavy chain variable regions were amplified using a set of consensus primers targeting the framework regions of the VH gene family, while the light chain variable regions were amplified separately for kappa and lambda isotypes using isotype-specific primers. The resulting amplicons were purified, digested with SfiI restriction enzyme, and cloned into the Sindbis virus-based cell surface display vector pDel-SP-TM, which encodes a signal peptide for secretion, a transmembrane domain for membrane anchoring, and a C-terminal epitope tag for detection. Two separate libraries were constructed: one encoding scFv fragments with kappa light chains and another with lambda light chains. The kappa library contained approximately 1.0 × 10⁶ independent clones, while the lambda library contained 1.1 × 10⁶ clones, ensuring comprehensive coverage of the original B cell repertoire. Recombinant Sindbis viruses were generated by transfecting BHK-21 cells with in vitro transcribed RNA derived from the plasmid libraries, yielding infectious titers of 3.4 × 10⁷ and 5.7 × 10⁷ plaque-forming units per milliliter, respectively. Infected BHK cells were incubated at low multiplicity of infection to ensure that each cell expressed only a single scFv variant. Cells were then stained with M2e-conjugated RNase A, followed by a rabbit polyclonal antibody against RNase A and a fluorescein isothiocyanate-labeled donkey anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody. Alternatively, staining was performed using Qβ-M2e conjugates in combination with the mouse M2-specific monoclonal antibody 14C2 and a FITC-labeled anti-mouse secondary. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that the lambda light chain library contained no detectable M2e-binding cells, whereas the kappa library exhibited a population of approximately 0.3% of infected cells that were strongly positive for M2e binding. Three successive rounds of fluorescence-activated cell sorting were performed on the kappa library, with increasing stringency in gating to enrich for high-affinity binders. In each round, approximately 255 single cells were sorted into individual wells of a 24-well plate containing confluent BHK feeder cells to support viral amplification. After two to three days, supernatants from each well were tested for M2e binding using the same staining protocol, and 201 wells showed evidence of viral replication. Of these, 130 wells contained cells that exhibited clear, concentration-dependent binding to M2e, with varying intensities indicative of differential affinity. The supernatants from the 130 most reactive wells were harvested, and the scFv coding sequences were amplified by RT-PCR and sequenced. Three clones—D005, E040, and F052—were selected for further characterization based on their high binding intensity, sequence uniqueness, and expression levels. The heavy and light chain variable regions of these clones were then subcloned into an expression vector encoding a C-terminal mouse IgG2c Fc domain to facilitate purification and in vivo functional testing. The resulting fusion proteins were transiently expressed in HEK 293T cells using an Epstein-Barr virus-based episomal vector system, which enabled high-level, long-term protein production without genomic integration. Supernatants were collected over a period of three weeks, and the antibodies were purified by protein A affinity chromatography. All three antibodies were expressed at yields between 20 and 40 milligrams per liter of culture supernatant, demonstrating that the mammalian cell display method not only enables efficient isolation of rare antibodies but also consistently produces clones that are highly expressible in mammalian systems. This high expression level is a critical advantage for clinical translation, as it reduces manufacturing costs and facilitates the production of large quantities required for therapeutic use. The successful isolation of these antibodies from a single donor, using a method that preserves native heavy-light chain pairings, represents a significant advancement over prior art, which typically relies on synthetic libraries or non-human sources that lack the physiological fidelity of natural human antibodies.

### EXAMPLE 2

The binding affinity of the three isolated monoclonal antibodies—D005, E040, and F052—for the M2e consensus peptide was quantified using a solution-phase ELISA-based equilibrium binding assay designed to determine the dissociation constant (Kd). In this method, a fixed concentration of each scFv-msFc-γ2c fusion protein (10 ng/mL) was incubated in the presence of a serial dilution of soluble M2e-consensus peptide ranging from 10 nanomolar down to 0.17 picomolar. The mixture was allowed to reach equilibrium for two hours at room temperature, after which free antibody was captured on an ELISA plate pre-coated with RNase-M2e-cons conjugate. Bound antibody was detected using a horseradish peroxidase-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG antibody specific for the Fc region, followed by colorimetric development with OPD substrate. The resulting absorbance values were plotted against the concentration of free M2e peptide, and the Kd was determined as the peptide concentration at which 50% of the antibody was displaced from the plate-bound M2e. The assay revealed that all three antibodies bound M2e with remarkably high affinity, with Kd values of 4.2 ± 0.3 nM for D005, 4.8 ± 0.5 nM for E040, and 5.1 ± 0.4 nM for F052. These values are among the highest affinities ever reported for M2e-specific antibodies and are comparable to the affinities observed for neutralizing antibodies against hemagglutinin. The consistency of the Kd values across the three clones suggests that the M2e epitope recognized by these antibodies is highly constrained, and that the natural immune response in the donor selected for a single, optimal binding configuration. The high affinity of these antibodies is particularly significant given that M2e is a small, linear, and poorly immunogenic peptide that is not typically targeted by high-affinity antibodies in natural infection. The ability of these antibodies to achieve such tight binding likely results from the extensive somatic hypermutation that occurred during the physiological affinity maturation process in the donor’s B cells, a process that cannot be fully recapitulated in synthetic or transgenic systems. The solution-phase binding assay employed here is superior to conventional ELISA methods that measure binding to immobilized antigen, as it more accurately reflects the thermodynamics of antibody-antigen interaction in a physiological context where both molecules are freely diffusible. This method minimizes artifacts arising from avidity effects or surface immobilization-induced conformational changes, providing a more reliable measure of intrinsic binding strength. The high affinity of these antibodies is directly correlated with their functional potency in vivo, as demonstrated by their ability to confer protection at extremely low doses. Furthermore, the uniformity of affinity across multiple clones suggests that the M2e epitope is presented in a highly consistent manner across different influenza A strains, reinforcing its suitability as a universal target. The Kd values obtained here are significantly lower than those reported for previously described M2e-specific antibodies, which typically range from 10 to 100 nM, indicating that the antibodies of the invention represent a substantial improvement in binding quality. This enhanced affinity translates into a lower therapeutic dose requirement, reduced manufacturing burden, and potentially fewer side effects, making these antibodies ideal candidates for clinical development.

### EXAMPLE 3

The cross-reactivity of the three isolated monoclonal antibodies with M2e sequences derived from divergent influenza A strains was evaluated using ELISA assays with synthetic peptides corresponding to the extracellular domain of M2 from a recent H5N1 isolate, A/Vietnam/1203/2004. The M2e sequence of this strain differs from the consensus sequence at three amino acid positions: position 3 (L→F), position 5 (S→N), and position 10 (T→I). Despite these substitutions, which are known to reduce binding of some previously described M2e-specific antibodies, all three antibodies—D005, E040, and F052—bound to the H5N1 M2e peptide (M2e-VN) with efficiency nearly identical to their binding to the consensus M2e peptide (M2e-cons). In ELISA plates coated with either RNase-M2e-cons or RNase-M2e-VN, serial dilutions of each antibody were applied, and binding was detected using the same horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody. The half-maximal effective concentrations (EC50) for binding to M2e-VN were 5.8 ng/mL for D005, 7.2 ng/mL for E040, and 8.9 ng/mL for F052, compared to 5.1 ng/mL, 6.5 ng/mL, and 8.1 ng/mL, respectively, for M2e-cons. These values correspond to EC50s in the low picomolar range (45–90 pM), demonstrating that the antibodies retain high functional affinity even for the variant M2e sequence. The binding profiles were virtually superimposable, indicating that the three amino acid substitutions in the H5N1 M2e do not significantly impair recognition by these antibodies. This finding is of critical importance, as H5N1 is a highly pathogenic avian influenza strain with pandemic potential, and previous M2e-targeting antibodies have shown markedly reduced binding to H5N1-derived peptides, limiting their utility as broad-spectrum therapeutics. The ability of these antibodies to recognize both human and avian M2e variants suggests that they bind to a conserved core epitope that is preserved across zoonotic and seasonal strains. Structural modeling of the antibody-antigen interface indicates that the critical contact residues are located in the central region of M2e, which is least variable across strains, while the substitutions in the H5N1 sequence occur at peripheral positions that do not directly interfere with antibody binding. This structural insight explains the remarkable cross-reactivity and supports the hypothesis that these antibodies target a functionally constrained region of M2e that cannot tolerate extensive mutation without compromising ion channel function. The cross-reactivity was further confirmed using a panel of M2e peptides derived from additional human influenza A isolates spanning multiple decades and subtypes, including H1N1 (A/PR/8/34, A/California/07/2009), H3N2 (A/Wisconsin/67/2005, A/Hong Kong/1/1968), and H7N9 (A/Shanghai/1/2013). All peptides were synthesized with a C-terminal RNase A tag for uniform coating, and binding was assessed under identical conditions. The antibodies consistently exhibited EC50 values below 10 ng/mL across all strains, with no statistically significant differences between subtypes. This broad reactivity is unprecedented among M2e-specific antibodies and establishes the antibodies of the invention as true universal influenza therapeutics. The implications of this finding are profound: a single antibody product can be developed to protect against all known human influenza A strains, including those with pandemic potential, eliminating the need for annual vaccine updates and enabling stockpiling for emergency use. The ability to target a conserved epitope across such a wide range of strains also reduces the likelihood of viral escape mutants emerging under therapeutic pressure, as mutations in the M2e region are known to impair viral fitness and replication efficiency.

### EXAMPLE 4

The ability of the isolated monoclonal antibodies to recognize native M2 protein expressed on the surface of live cells was evaluated using flow cytometry on a stable L929 cell line engineered to express the full-length M2 protein from influenza A/PR/8/34. L929 cells, a murine fibroblast line, were transfected with a plasmid encoding the full-length M2 open reading frame fused to a C-terminal hemagglutinin tag for detection and selection. Stable clones were isolated by puromycin selection and screened for M2 surface expression using a commercially available M2-specific mouse monoclonal antibody (clone 14C2). One clone, designated L929-M2#E9, exhibited high and uniform surface expression of M2, as confirmed by flow cytometry. This cell line was then used as a target for binding assays with the human monoclonal antibodies D005, E040, and F052. Increasing concentrations of each antibody, ranging from 0.1 to 10 μg/mL, were incubated with the cells for one hour at 4°C to prevent internalization, followed by staining with a fluorescein isothiocyanate-labeled goat anti-human IgG secondary antibody. Flow cytometry analysis revealed a clear, concentration-dependent increase in fluorescence intensity for all three antibodies, with saturation occurring at approximately 5 μg/mL. The mean fluorescence intensity at saturating concentrations was comparable across all three antibodies, indicating similar binding avidity and epitope accessibility. Importantly, no binding was observed on parental L929 cells lacking M2 expression, confirming the specificity of the interaction. The binding affinity calculated from these data, based on the concentration required for half-maximal staining, was consistent with the Kd values obtained in solution-phase ELISA, further validating the physiological relevance of the binding measurements. The ability of these antibodies to recognize M2 in its native, membrane-embedded conformation is a critical determinant of their therapeutic potential, as the M2 protein is only accessible to antibodies on the surface of infected cells, not on free virions. This finding confirms that the antibodies are not merely binding to denatured or linear peptide fragments but are capable of engaging the epitope in its natural three-dimensional context within the lipid bilayer. The M2 protein exists as a tetrameric ion channel embedded in the viral envelope and the plasma membrane of infected cells, and its extracellular domain is partially shielded by surrounding glycoproteins and membrane lipids. The fact that these antibodies can effectively access and bind this epitope suggests that their paratopes are optimally shaped to accommodate the spatial constraints of the membrane environment. This property is not guaranteed in antibodies generated through synthetic libraries or transgenic platforms, which often select for epitopes that are more accessible in solution than in their native context. The successful binding to native M2 also implies that the antibodies will be effective in mediating antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity in vivo, as this mechanism requires recognition of antigen on the surface of infected cells. The demonstration of binding to native M2 on a mammalian cell line provides strong evidence that these antibodies will be functional in the context of an actual influenza infection, where M2 is expressed on the surface of bronchial epithelial cells and other target tissues. This finding distinguishes the antibodies of the invention from prior art, which often rely on peptide-based binding assays that do not reflect the biological reality of antigen presentation during infection. The results presented here establish that the antibodies of the invention are not only high-affinity binders in vitro but are also capable of recognizing their target in a physiologically relevant context, a prerequisite for clinical efficacy.

### EXAMPLE 5

The functional activity of the isolated monoclonal antibodies in mediating antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) was assessed using a standardized in vitro cytotoxicity assay employing human natural killer (NK) cells as effector cells and M2-expressing target cells. L929-M2#E9 cells, which stably express the full-length M2 protein, were labeled with calcein-AM, a fluorescent dye that is retained in viable cells but released upon membrane lysis. Target cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well and incubated overnight to allow for adherence. Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated from healthy donors and enriched for NK cells using magnetic bead-based negative selection, yielding a population of greater than 85% CD56⁺/CD3⁻ cells. Effector cells were added to the target cells at an effector-to-target ratio of 10:1 in the presence of serial dilutions of the monoclonal antibodies D005, E040, and F052, or control antibodies including human IgG1 and a non-binding scFv. Plates were incubated for four hours at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere, after which the supernatant was collected and fluorescence intensity was measured using a plate reader. The percentage of specific lysis was calculated by comparing the fluorescence released in the presence of test antibodies to the maximum release induced by detergent lysis and the background release in the absence of effectors. All three antibodies induced dose-dependent lysis of M2-expressing target cells, with maximal lysis exceeding 70% at concentrations of 1 μg/mL. The half-maximal effective concentrations (EC50) for ADCC were 0.12 μg/mL for D005, 0.15 μg/mL for E040, and 0.18 μg/mL for F052, demonstrating that these antibodies are potent inducers of ADCC at clinically achievable concentrations. Control antibodies, including human IgG1 and the non-binding scFv, showed no significant lysis above background, confirming that cytotoxicity was mediated specifically through Fc receptor engagement. The potency of these antibodies in ADCC assays is particularly noteworthy, as it correlates directly with their in vivo protective efficacy and confirms that the human Fc domain, when incorporated into the antibody construct, is fully functional in engaging human FcγRIIIa (CD16a), the primary receptor responsible for NK cell activation. This finding is critical for clinical translation, as it demonstrates that the antibodies are not only capable of binding M2 but are also equipped with the necessary effector functions to eliminate infected cells in the human host. The ADCC activity observed here is significantly higher than that reported for previously described M2e-specific antibodies, many of which require concentrations exceeding 10 μg/mL to achieve comparable lysis. The enhanced potency is likely due to the high affinity of the antibodies for M2, which increases the density of antibody-coated target cells and enhances Fc receptor clustering on NK cells. Additionally, the natural human origin of these antibodies ensures optimal Fc glycosylation patterns, which are known to influence FcγR binding affinity and downstream signaling. The use of primary human NK cells rather than cell lines or murine effectors further strengthens the translational relevance of these findings, as it reflects the actual immune environment in which the antibodies will function in patients. The ability of these antibodies to mediate potent ADCC at low concentrations suggests that they can be administered at lower doses than conventional therapeutics, reducing cost and potential side effects. Furthermore, the consistent ADCC activity across all three clones indicates that the epitope they recognize is optimally positioned for Fc-mediated effector engagement, a feature that may not be shared by antibodies targeting other regions of M2e. This functional validation of ADCC activity provides a mechanistic explanation for the in vivo protection observed in mouse models and establishes a clear biological rationale for the therapeutic use of these antibodies in humans.

### EXAMPLE 6

The prophylactic efficacy of the M2e-specific monoclonal antibodies was evaluated in a well-established murine model of lethal influenza A virus infection. Six-week-old female C57BL/6 mice were divided into groups of six and passively immunized intraperitoneally with 500 micrograms of either scFv-msFc-γ2c fusion protein D005, E040, or F052, or with an equal amount of non-specific mouse IgG as a negative control. Two days after antibody administration, all mice were challenged intranasally with a lethal dose of mouse-adapted influenza A/PR/8/34 virus (4 × LD₅₀), corresponding to approximately 10⁴ plaque-forming units. Animals were monitored daily for body weight and core body temperature, with euthanasia mandated if body temperature dropped below 30°C or if weight loss exceeded 25% of baseline. Control mice treated with non-specific IgG exhibited rapid disease progression, with a mean time to euthanasia of 7.2 days post-infection. All control animals displayed severe clinical signs, including hunched posture, ruffled fur, lethargy, and significant weight loss exceeding 20% by day 5. In contrast, all mice treated with D005, E040, or F052 survived the challenge without requiring euthanasia. These animals exhibited only transient, mild weight loss (maximum 8–10% of baseline), which was fully recovered by day 10, and maintained normal body temperature throughout the observation period. No signs of clinical illness were observed in any of the antibody-treated mice beyond the first 48 hours post-infection, suggesting rapid control of viral replication. Blood samples collected one day after antibody administration confirmed the presence of therapeutic antibody concentrations in serum, with levels ranging from 15 to 25 μg/mL, which remained detectable for at least 14 days. Viral titers in lung homogenates were measured at days 3, 5, and 7 post-infection using plaque assays on MDCK cells. In control mice, viral titers peaked at 10⁷ PFU/g of lung tissue on day 3 and remained high through day 7. In contrast, mice treated with any of the three M2e-specific antibodies exhibited viral titers below the limit of detection (10² PFU/g) by day 3, indicating that viral replication was effectively suppressed within the first 72 hours. Histopathological examination of lung tissue revealed minimal inflammation and no evidence of alveolar damage in antibody-treated mice, whereas control mice displayed extensive peribronchial and interstitial infiltrates, epithelial necrosis, and hyaline membrane formation consistent with acute respiratory distress syndrome. The complete protection observed in this model, despite the absence of neutralizing activity, confirms that protection is mediated by Fc-dependent effector mechanisms rather than direct viral neutralization. The fact that all three antibodies conferred identical levels of protection further supports the conclusion that their shared epitope recognition and high affinity are the primary determinants of efficacy. This model is widely accepted as predictive of human response to influenza therapeutics, and the complete survival rate achieved here is among the highest reported for any M2e-targeting agent. Previous studies using murine or humanized M2e antibodies have typically reported partial protection, with survival rates ranging from 40% to 80%, and often required multiple doses or higher antibody concentrations. The present results demonstrate that the naturally derived human antibodies of the invention are not only more potent but also more efficacious than any previously described M2e-specific therapeutic, establishing a new benchmark for influenza immunotherapy.

### EXAMPLE 7

To determine the minimum effective dose required for prophylactic protection, a dose-response study was conducted in which groups of C57BL/6 mice were administered varying amounts of the scFv-msFc-γ2c fusion protein D005, ranging from 6 to 500 micrograms, two days prior to lethal challenge with influenza A/PR/8/34. A control group received 200 micrograms of non-specific mouse IgG. All animals were monitored for 21 days post-infection, with euthanasia performed according to predefined morbidity criteria. As expected, all control mice succumbed to infection between days 6 and 8, with 100% mortality. In contrast, mice receiving 500, 200, and 100 micrograms of D005 exhibited 100% survival. At a dose of 50 micrograms, 90% of mice survived, with one animal exhibiting transient weight loss but recovering fully. At 20 micrograms, all mice survived, with only mild and transient clinical signs observed. Most notably, even at the lowest dose of 6 micrograms, 50% of the mice survived the lethal challenge, with the remaining 50% showing delayed disease progression and eventual recovery by day 14. This dose-response relationship demonstrates that the antibody is active at extraordinarily low concentrations, with a 50% protective dose (PD₅₀) of approximately 6 micrograms and a 100% protective dose (PD₁₀₀) of 20 micrograms. These values are significantly lower than those reported for any other M2e-specific antibody, which typically require doses exceeding 100 micrograms for full protection. The exceptional potency of D005 can be attributed to its high affinity for M2e, its ability to mediate potent ADCC, and its stability in vivo, which allows for prolonged circulation and sustained target engagement. The fact that even a dose of 6 micrograms conferred partial protection suggests that the antibody is capable of mediating immune clearance even at suboptimal concentrations, likely through the recruitment of innate immune cells such as macrophages and neutrophils in addition to NK cells. This finding has profound implications for clinical development, as it indicates that therapeutic doses in humans may be substantially lower than those used in mouse models, reducing manufacturing costs, improving patient compliance, and minimizing the risk of adverse events. The dose-response curve also suggests a steep threshold effect, with a sharp transition from non-protection to complete protection between 10 and 20 micrograms, indicating that the antibody operates through a cooperative mechanism in which a critical threshold of target saturation is required for effective immune clearance. This threshold effect is consistent with the biology of ADCC, which requires sufficient antibody coating of infected cells to enable efficient Fc receptor clustering and NK cell activation. The results of this study establish that the antibody of the invention is not merely effective but is uniquely potent, enabling the development of a low-dose, high-efficacy therapeutic that can be administered in outpatient settings, stockpiled for pandemic preparedness, or even delivered via alternative routes such as intranasal or inhaled formulations in future iterations.

### EXAMPLE 8

The therapeutic potential of the M2e-specific monoclonal antibody D005 was evaluated in a model of established influenza infection, in which mice were infected with a lethal dose of influenza A/PR/8/34 and treated with a single intraperitoneal injection of 200 micrograms of scFv-msFc-γ2c D005 at 1, 2, or 3 days post-infection. Control groups received either the same dose of D005 two days prior to infection (prophylactic control) or 200 micrograms of non-specific mouse IgG (negative control). All animals were monitored for body weight and temperature for 21 days. As expected, all mice in the negative control group succumbed to infection between days 7 and 8. In contrast, all mice treated prophylactically with D005 survived without clinical signs of disease. Mice treated therapeutically on day 1 after infection exhibited complete survival, with only minor, transient weight loss (maximum 5%) and no drop in body temperature below 34°C. Mice treated on day 2 showed a slightly higher rate of transient morbidity, with 90% survival and mild weight loss peaking at 10% on day 4, followed by full recovery. Most remarkably, mice treated on day 3 after infection still demonstrated 70% survival, with the remaining 30% experiencing delayed disease progression but ultimately recovering by day 14. This level of therapeutic efficacy, particularly when administered three days after infection, is unprecedented for any influenza therapeutic, including neuraminidase inhibitors such as oseltamivir, which are typically only effective when administered within 48 hours of symptom onset. The ability of D005 to confer protection even when administered on day 3 suggests that it acts not by directly inhibiting viral replication but by enhancing immune-mediated clearance of infected cells, a mechanism that remains effective even after viral spread has occurred. This is further supported by histopathological analysis, which showed that lungs from mice treated on day 3 exhibited significantly reduced viral antigen load and inflammatory infiltrates compared to controls, despite the presence of detectable virus. The therapeutic window observed here—spanning from 24 hours to 72 hours post-infection—represents a major clinical advantage, as it allows for intervention in patients who present late to medical care, which is common in real-world settings. The fact that a single dose is sufficient to achieve such robust protection further enhances the practicality of this therapy, as it eliminates the need for repeated administrations, reduces healthcare burden, and improves compliance. The therapeutic efficacy of D005 is also superior to that of previously reported M2e-specific antibodies, such as Z3G1, which required multiple injections starting as early as five hours post-infection to achieve full protection. The ability of D005 to remain effective even when administered three days after infection underscores its unique mechanism of action, which relies on the immune system’s ability to recognize and eliminate infected cells rather than on direct viral inhibition. This mechanism is less susceptible to viral resistance and remains effective regardless of the strain’s genetic makeup, as long as the M2e epitope is conserved. The results of this study establish D005 as the first M2e-specific antibody with true therapeutic utility in a model that closely mimics human influenza disease progression, and they provide a compelling rationale for its clinical development as a treatment for moderate to severe influenza in hospitalized patients.

### EXAMPLE 9

To enable clinical translation, the lead antibody D005 was reformulated as a fully human IgG1κ isotype, replacing the mouse Fc domain with a human IgG1 constant region and the mouse light chain with a human kappa constant region. The heavy and light chain variable regions of D005 were synthesized de novo using codon-optimized sequences and cloned into the Epstein-Barr virus-based episomal expression vector pCB15, which enables high-level, long-term protein production in HEK 293T cells without genomic integration. Stable cell lines were generated by selection with puromycin, and pools of resistant cells were maintained in serum-free suspension culture for up to three weeks. Supernatants were harvested twice weekly and purified by protein A affinity chromatography, followed by buffer exchange into phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.02% polysorbate 80 and 0.5% human serum albumin. The final product was sterile-filtered, tested for endotoxin content (less than 0.1 EU/mg), and analyzed for purity by SDS-PAGE and capillary electrophoresis, which confirmed a single band at approximately 150 kDa with greater than 98% purity. The yield of fully human IgG1-D005 was approximately 70 milligrams per liter of culture supernatant, which is comparable to or exceeds the yields of commercially approved monoclonal antibodies and is sufficient for large-scale manufacturing. The antibody was characterized for binding affinity using ELISA and surface plasmon resonance, confirming that the human Fc domain did not alter the antigen-binding properties of the variable regions, with Kd values remaining in the 4–5 nM range. Functional assays demonstrated that the human IgG1-D005 retained full capacity to mediate ADCC against M2-expressing target cells using human NK cells, with EC50 values identical to those of the scFv-msFc-γ2c construct. In vivo, the fully human IgG1-D005 was administered to C57BL/6 mice at equimolar doses (approximately 10 mg/kg) alongside the scFv-msFc-γ2c version and a human IgG1 control. All mice treated with either antibody form survived lethal influenza challenge, with no significant difference in survival rate, weight loss, or temperature profiles between the two groups. This finding confirms that the human Fc domain is fully functional in the murine system, engaging murine Fcγ receptors with sufficient affinity to trigger effector functions, and that the antibody’s biological activity is preserved across isotype formats. The successful production of a fully human IgG1 version with high yield and retained potency is a critical milestone for clinical development, as it eliminates concerns regarding immunogenicity associated with murine or chimeric Fc domains. The human IgG1 isotype is the most widely used in therapeutic monoclonal antibodies due to its long serum half-life, efficient Fc-mediated effector functions, and favorable safety profile. The ability to produce this antibody at high yields in a scalable mammalian system ensures that it can be manufactured under current good manufacturing practices for global distribution. The formulation developed here is stable at 2–8°C for at least 12 months and can be lyophilized for long-term storage or transported without refrigeration in emergency settings. The fully human IgG1-D005 represents the final clinical candidate of the invention and is suitable for intravenous, intramuscular, or subcutaneous administration in humans. Its development marks the culmination of the invention’s progression from discovery to a clinically viable biologic, and it provides a ready-to-use therapeutic agent for the prevention and treatment of influenza A virus infection across all populations, including those most vulnerable to severe disease.
