# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The monitoring of human activity has become an increasingly critical component in the development of ambient intelligence systems designed to support aging populations, individuals with mobility impairments, and those requiring continuous health surveillance without intrusive intervention. Traditional methods of activity detection have relied heavily on visual surveillance using cameras, wearable sensors such as accelerometers and gyroscopes, and more recently, frequency-modulated continuous wave (FMCW) radar systems. While these technologies offer varying degrees of accuracy and temporal resolution, they each suffer from significant practical limitations that hinder their widespread adoption in domestic and healthcare environments. Camera-based systems, for instance, require substantial computational resources to process video streams in real time, often necessitating high-performance edge computing or cloud connectivity. Moreover, the inherent privacy concerns associated with video monitoring render them unsuitable for use in private spaces such as bedrooms or bathrooms, where individuals expect a high degree of personal autonomy and confidentiality. Wearable sensors, though accurate in capturing motion data, impose a behavioral burden on users who must remember to wear, charge, and maintain the devices. This burden leads to inconsistent usage patterns, particularly among elderly or cognitively impaired individuals, resulting in unreliable data collection and reduced system efficacy over time. FMCW radar systems, while capable of detecting subtle physiological movements such as respiration and cardiac activity, remain prohibitively expensive for mass deployment, require specialized calibration, and are not readily available as commercial off-the-shelf components, limiting their scalability.

Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) technology has emerged as a compelling alternative due to its low cost, passive operation, and ability to operate without direct line-of-sight constraints under certain conditions. RFID systems function by transmitting a continuous wave from a reader antenna toward one or more passive tags, which modulate and reflect a portion of the incident signal back to the reader. The reflected signal contains information encoded in its received signal strength indicator (RSSI) and phase characteristics, which can be analyzed to infer the presence, location, and motion of objects or humans within the environment. Previous research has demonstrated that changes in RSSI and phase caused by human movement can be used to classify activities such as walking, sitting, or gesturing. However, these systems have been largely constrained by the physical limitations of commercially available RFID tags, which are predominantly linearly polarized. In typical deployments, circularly polarized reader antennas are used to ensure reliable communication regardless of tag orientation—a design choice optimized for inventory tracking in warehouses where tagged items may be rotated arbitrarily. Yet, in the context of human activity detection, this configuration is suboptimal. The dominant signal component in such systems is the direct backscatter from the tag itself, which overwhelms the much weaker reflections generated by the human body. As a result, the system’s sensitivity to human motion is severely limited, requiring the individual to be positioned very close to the tag-reader axis, effectively restricting the detection volume to a narrow corridor between the two antennas. This constraint renders the technology impractical for monitoring activities across an entire room, especially when the goal is to detect subtle, non-repetitive, or infrequent motions such as a slight head tilt, arm crossing, or shallow breathing.

Attempts to expand the detection area have involved deploying multiple linearly polarized tags in a spatially distributed array. While this approach increases coverage, it introduces significant logistical and operational challenges. Each additional tag increases system cost, requires careful placement to avoid mutual coupling and signal interference, and demands more complex signal processing to disambiguate contributions from individual tags. Furthermore, the need for manual calibration and maintenance of multiple tags reduces the system’s appeal as a truly “ambient” solution—one that operates unobtrusively and requires minimal user interaction. The fundamental limitation underlying these approaches lies in the polarization mismatch between the reader and tag antennas. In a co-polarized configuration, where both the reader and tag antennas share the same polarization state, the direct backscatter signal remains strong, while the body-reflected signal, which is inherently weaker and often distorted by multipath effects, struggles to be distinguished from noise. Alternative polarization pairings, such as linear reader with linear tag, fail to provide sufficient discrimination between direct and reflected signals because the reflection of linearly polarized waves off human tissue does not reliably alter the polarization state. Consequently, the reflected signal remains indistinguishable from background fluctuations, leading to poor signal-to-noise ratios and limited detection range.

There exists a critical gap in the literature and in commercial practice: no prior system has effectively leveraged the polarization transformation properties of electromagnetic waves upon reflection from biological tissues to enhance the detection of human activity using passive RFID infrastructure. While the phenomenon of circular polarization reversal upon reflection from conductive or high-permittivity surfaces is well documented in radar and remote sensing literature, its application to RFID-based human motion sensing has remained unexplored. The opportunity lies in deliberately configuring the reader and tag antennas to be cross-circularly polarized—such as a right-hand circularly polarized (RHCP) reader paired with a left-hand circularly polarized (LHCP) tag, or vice versa. In such a configuration, the direct backscatter signal is inherently suppressed due to polarization mismatch, reducing its power by more than 30 decibels relative to a co-polarized setup. Meanwhile, when an electromagnetic wave reflects off the human body, its handedness is inverted: an RHCP wave becomes LHCP, and an LHCP wave becomes RHCP. This reversal effectively aligns the reflected signal with the polarization state of the receiving antenna, thereby enabling its detection while simultaneously suppressing the dominant noise source. This polarization-based signal enhancement mechanism provides a novel and elegant solution to the longstanding problem of low signal-to-noise ratio in passive RFID sensing systems. It eliminates the need for multiple tags, reduces computational complexity, and extends the effective sensing range beyond the immediate line-of-sight region, enabling full-room coverage with a single tag-reader pair. Such a system would represent a paradigm shift in ambient sensing, transforming RFID from a proximity-based identification tool into a robust, non-intrusive, and scalable platform for continuous human activity monitoring in real-world environments.

The absence of a commercially viable circularly polarized passive RFID tag capable of operating within standard UHF ISM bands has further impeded progress in this area. While spiral antennas and other circularly polarized structures have been studied in the context of active RF systems, their integration into passive RFID tags requires careful impedance matching to the underlying chip, precise fabrication tolerances, and robust performance across the entire frequency band used for RFID communication. Existing commercial tags are optimized for maximum read range under co-polarized conditions, not for polarization discrimination in sensing applications. Therefore, the development of a custom-designed, low-cost, circularly polarized RFID tag that maintains compatibility with standard UHF RFID readers, while exhibiting the desired cross-polarization suppression and reflection-enhancement characteristics, constitutes a foundational technological advancement. When combined with appropriate signal preprocessing techniques to mitigate frequency hopping artifacts and multipath variability inherent in commercial RFID readers, such a system can achieve unprecedented sensitivity to subtle human movements—including respiration, hand gestures, and postural shifts—without requiring any wearable components, visual surveillance, or complex sensor arrays. This invention addresses the core limitations of existing technologies by introducing a hardware-level polarization engineering solution that fundamentally redefines the capabilities of passive RFID systems in ambient human activity monitoring.

## SUMMARY

This invention discloses a novel method and system for non-contact human activity detection using radio frequency identification (RFID) technology through the deliberate implementation of cross-circular polarization between a reader antenna and a custom-designed circularly polarized passive tag. The system exploits the inherent polarization reversal that occurs when circularly polarized electromagnetic waves reflect off human tissue, thereby transforming what was previously a dominant noise source—the direct backscatter from the tag—into a suppressed signal, while simultaneously amplifying the weak, motion-modulated reflections from the human body. This polarization-based signal enhancement mechanism enables the detection of subtle and non-repetitive human movements at significantly greater distances than previously possible with conventional linearly polarized RFID systems, eliminating the need for multiple tags, wearable sensors, or visual monitoring. The core innovation lies in the configuration of a right-hand circularly polarized (RHCP) reader antenna paired with a left-hand circularly polarized (LHCP) passive RFID tag, or vice versa, such that the direct line-of-sight backscatter is attenuated by more than thirty decibels due to polarization mismatch, while reflections from the human body, which reverse the handedness of the incident wave, become co-polarized with the receiving antenna and are therefore efficiently captured. This configuration results in a dramatic improvement in signal-to-noise ratio, allowing for the reliable detection of minute physiological and kinematic activities—including respiration, head nodding, hand waving, arm crossing, and torso rotation—across an entire room using only a single tag and a single reader.

The system comprises a commercially available UHF RFID reader equipped with a circularly polarized antenna, a custom-designed passive circularly polarized RFID tag fabricated on a low-cost FR4 substrate, and a data acquisition and preprocessing software module that calibrates the ambient electromagnetic environment and isolates motion-induced variations in RSSI and phase. The tag is constructed using an Archimedean spiral antenna structure with a radius of approximately thirteen centimeters, integrated with an Impinj Monza R6 RFID chip via a conjugate-matched T-shaped feed network designed to cancel the chip’s complex impedance of 20–145j ohms. The tag is fabricated using standard printed circuit board techniques, ensuring scalability, low unit cost, and mechanical durability suitable for permanent mounting on walls, ceilings, or furniture. The circular polarization of the tag is characterized by an axial ratio bandwidth exceeding 105 degrees across the UHF RFID band, ensuring consistent performance under varying incidence angles and environmental conditions. The reader operates in maximum throughput mode, collecting tag responses at a rate of approximately 250 reads per second, with output data including timestamped RSSI and phase measurements across 50 frequency channels within the 902–928 MHz band. To mitigate the effects of frequency hopping and multipath interference, a dynamic calibration procedure is employed prior to activity detection, wherein baseline RSSI and phase values are recorded over a ten-second interval while the environment is static. Subsequent measurements are referenced against this baseline through frequency-channel remapping and differential subtraction, yielding a flat, stable signal representation that isolates motion-induced deviations from environmental noise.

The system’s performance is validated through a series of controlled experiments conducted in both anechoic chambers and real-world indoor environments, including residential and office settings furnished with typical furniture and architectural features. In comparative tests against conventional linearly polarized tags, the cross-circular polarization configuration demonstrates a spatial coverage area exceeding 230% greater than that of the best-performing linear tag configuration, enabling detection of human motion throughout an entire room without requiring multiple tags or precise user positioning. The system reliably distinguishes between a variety of non-repetitive activities—including head nodding, head shaking, hand waving, and arm crossing—each of which produces unique signatures in the RSSI and phase time series. These signatures are sufficiently distinct to permit classification without requiring machine learning algorithms, although such techniques may be employed to enhance accuracy in complex environments. Repetitive activities such as respiration and rhythmic hand waving are detected with high fidelity, with the system accurately resolving respiratory rates of up to 14 breaths per minute and hand-waving frequencies of 45 cycles per minute, as confirmed by fast Fourier transform analysis. The signal-to-noise ratio of the cross-circular polarization system is shown to remain robust even at distances exceeding three meters from the tag-reader axis, whereas the performance of linearly polarized systems degrades to the noise floor at comparable ranges. Furthermore, the system exhibits consistent performance across multiple users, regardless of body size, movement speed, or gesture execution style, indicating that the underlying physics of polarization reversal governs the signal response rather than individual biomechanical variability.

The invention provides a fully passive, non-invasive, privacy-preserving, and cost-effective solution for ambient human activity monitoring that requires no wearable components, no visual surveillance, and no user participation beyond normal daily behavior. It is uniquely suited for deployment in homes, hospitals, assisted living facilities, and senior care environments where continuous monitoring of mobility, fall risk, and physiological status is essential but where traditional technologies are either intrusive, unreliable, or prohibitively expensive. The system’s reliance on a single tag-reader pair reduces installation complexity, maintenance burden, and total cost of ownership, making it scalable for widespread adoption. The invention further enables the development of intelligent ambient systems capable of detecting anomalies such as prolonged inactivity, irregular breathing patterns, or sudden falls, thereby facilitating proactive health interventions without compromising personal dignity or autonomy. By fundamentally reconfiguring the electromagnetic interaction between reader and tag through polarization engineering, this invention transforms passive RFID from a simple identification tool into a powerful sensing platform capable of discerning the subtlest human motions with unprecedented range and sensitivity.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The detailed implementation of the invention encompasses the structural, functional, and procedural components necessary to realize a fully operational, non-contact human activity detection system based on cross-circular polarization in a radio frequency identification (RFID) architecture. The system is composed of three primary subsystems: the hardware configuration of the reader and tag antennas, the signal acquisition and preprocessing methodology, and the analytical framework for interpreting motion-induced variations in RSSI and phase. Each of these components is designed to operate in concert to achieve a level of sensitivity, range, and reliability that is unattainable through conventional RFID sensing approaches. The foundation of the invention lies in the deliberate polarization mismatch between the reader and the tag, which is not a design flaw to be corrected, but rather a carefully engineered feature that enables the suppression of the dominant noise source while selectively enhancing the desired signal. This inversion of conventional design principles—where polarization alignment is typically sought to maximize communication range—is what distinguishes this invention from all prior art and forms the basis of its novel functionality.

The reader component of the system is implemented using a commercially available Impinj R420 UHF RFID reader, a device widely employed in inventory and logistics applications for its high read rate, robust signal processing capabilities, and compatibility with standard EPC Gen2 tags. The reader is interfaced with a circularly polarized antenna, specifically the S9028PC model, which exhibits a right-hand circular polarization (RHCP) characteristic with a gain of 9 dBi and a radiation pattern optimized for wide angular coverage. The antenna is mounted at a height of 1.2 meters above the floor, positioned on one wall of the monitoring environment, and oriented such that its main lobe is directed toward the center of the room. The reader is connected via Ethernet to a host computer running a Java-based application developed using the Impinj Octane SDK, which continuously streams tag interrogation data at a rate of 250 reads per second in MAXTHROUGHPUT mode. The reader transmits at a fixed power level of 31.5 dBm and operates across the full UHF band from 902 to 928 MHz, employing frequency hopping spread spectrum (FHSS) to comply with regulatory requirements and minimize interference with other readers. Each tag response contains a timestamp, a unique electronic product code (EPC), the received signal strength indicator (RSSI) in decibels relative to one milliwatt, and the phase angle of the backscattered signal in radians, measured at each of the 50 frequency channels within the band. These parameters are sampled at intervals of approximately 4 milliseconds, providing a high temporal resolution sufficient to capture rapid human movements such as hand gestures and head motions.

The tag component is a custom-designed, passive, circularly polarized RFID tag fabricated on a 0.8 mm thick FR4 substrate, chosen for its low cost, mechanical rigidity, and compatibility with standard printed circuit board manufacturing processes. The antenna structure is an Archimedean spiral with a total radius of 13 centimeters, designed to maintain circular polarization across the entire UHF RFID band. The spiral consists of two symmetric arms that extend outward from the center, forming a continuous conductive path that terminates at the feed point where the Impinj Monza R6 RFID chip is mounted. The chip, identical to that used in commercial linearly polarized tags, has a complex input impedance of approximately 20–145j ohms at the center frequency of 915 MHz. To ensure maximum power transfer from the antenna to the chip, a conjugate-matching network is implemented using a T-shaped feed structure, wherein each arm of the spiral is extended outward in a symmetric fashion to form a balanced transmission line that cancels the reactive component of the chip’s impedance. This matching network is simulated using electromagnetic field solvers to optimize the return loss and axial ratio across the operating band, and the resulting design achieves a realized gain of 3.3 dBi and an axial ratio bandwidth of 105 degrees, indicating consistent circular polarization over a wide angular range. The tag is fabricated using standard photolithographic etching techniques, with copper traces deposited on the FR4 substrate and protected by a thin layer of solder mask. The final tag measures approximately 26 centimeters in diameter, making it compact enough to be discreetly mounted on walls, ceilings, or furniture without obstructing normal human activity. Unlike commercial tags, which are optimized for co-polarized communication, this tag is specifically engineered to exhibit strong cross-polarization rejection when interrogated by a circularly polarized reader of the opposite handedness.

The operational principle of the system hinges on the polarization transformation that occurs when a circularly polarized electromagnetic wave reflects off a human body. Human tissue, due to its high water content and conductive properties, behaves as a lossy dielectric medium with a relative permittivity exceeding 40 at UHF frequencies. When a right-hand circularly polarized (RHCP) wave from the reader impinges upon the human body, the reflection process inverts the handedness of the wave, converting it into a left-hand circularly polarized (LHCP) wave. This phenomenon is well established in electromagnetic theory and has been previously exploited in Doppler radar systems for vital sign monitoring. In the context of this invention, the tag is designed to be LHCP, meaning that the direct backscatter from the tag—incident RHCP wave reflected by the tag antenna—is orthogonal to the reader’s polarization and thus severely attenuated. The polarization mismatch loss between RHCP and LHCP is measured to exceed 30 dB in controlled anechoic chamber tests, reducing the direct signal to near the noise floor of the reader’s receiver. However, when a human body enters the field of view and reflects the incident wave, the reflected signal becomes LHCP, which now matches the polarization of the tag antenna. This alignment enables the reflected signal to be efficiently re-radiated by the tag and received by the reader, resulting in a measurable increase in RSSI and a discernible phase shift. The result is a system in which the direct signal is suppressed and the body-reflected signal is amplified, effectively inverting the signal-to-noise ratio that plagues conventional RFID sensing systems. This mechanism allows the system to detect human motion not by the presence of the tag, but by the perturbation of the electromagnetic field caused by the human body, rendering the tag a passive reflector rather than an active emitter.

To isolate motion-induced variations from environmental noise, a dynamic calibration procedure is executed prior to any activity monitoring. The system first records a baseline measurement over a ten-second interval during which the environment is completely static—no human movement, no opening of doors, no operation of appliances. During this interval, the reader collects RSSI and phase data across all 50 frequency channels, and the mean RSSI value for each channel is stored as a reference. For phase data, a remapping algorithm is applied to correct for the frequency hopping artifacts inherent in the reader’s operation. This algorithm, derived from prior work in phase-based RFID sensing, maps the phase measurements from each frequency channel to a fixed reference frequency chosen to minimize phase wrapping and maximize the dynamic range of the phase response. The reference frequency is selected as the one whose initial phase is closest to π radians, ensuring that the remapped phase response spans the full [0, 2π] interval without discontinuities. Subsequent measurements are then subtracted from the baseline values on a per-channel basis, yielding a differential signal that represents only the changes induced by human motion. This preprocessing step is critical, as raw RSSI and phase data are dominated by multipath interference, temperature drift, and minor environmental fluctuations that would otherwise obscure the subtle variations caused by human activity. The calibrated signal, by contrast, exhibits a flat baseline with minimal drift, allowing even minute deviations—on the order of 0.1 dB in RSSI or 0.1 radians in phase—to be reliably detected as meaningful events.

The spatial sensitivity of the system is demonstrated through a series of experiments conducted in a typical indoor environment measuring approximately 4 meters by 5 meters, furnished with a table, chairs, shelves, and a standard ceiling and floor. The tag is mounted on one wall, and the reader on the opposing wall, separated by a distance of 1.5 meters. A grid of 25 measurement points is established across the room, excluding the direct line-of-sight region between the tag and reader, where the direct signal would dominate. At each point, a subject performs a standardized head-nodding motion at a rate of once every seven seconds for one minute. The RSSI variation at each location is recorded and analyzed using a sliding window algorithm with a window size of 10 samples (40 milliseconds) and a threshold of 0.3 dB. An activity is registered only if the mean RSSI deviation exceeds the threshold for five consecutive windows, ensuring that transient noise spikes are rejected. The resulting heatmap of detection sensitivity reveals that the cross-circular polarization system detects motion across nearly the entire room, with the lowest sensitivity occurring near the corners farthest from the tag-reader axis. In contrast, a conventional linearly polarized tag, even when oriented vertically to maximize its donut-shaped radiation pattern, fails to detect motion beyond a radius of 1.2 meters from the tag-reader line. The spatial coverage area of the cross-circular system is measured to be 230% greater than that of the best-performing linear configuration, demonstrating a fundamental expansion in the effective sensing volume enabled by the polarization-based signal enhancement.

The system’s ability to distinguish between different types of human activity is evaluated through a series of non-repetitive gesture tests. At a fixed location—designated as C4 in the spatial grid—the subject performs four distinct motions: head nodding, head shaking, hand waving, and arm crossing. Each motion is executed with natural variability in speed and amplitude to simulate real-world behavior. The RSSI and phase responses are recorded for each activity and compared against the responses obtained using a vertically oriented linearly polarized tag. The results show that the linear tag produces negligible variation in both RSSI and phase, with deviations consistently below 0.2 dB and 0.05 radians, respectively, indistinguishable from baseline noise. In contrast, the cross-circular polarization system produces clear, repeatable signatures for each activity. Head nodding induces a periodic rise and fall in RSSI corresponding to the vertical motion of the torso and head, while head shaking produces a broader, more erratic fluctuation due to the lateral displacement of the upper body. Hand waving generates a series of sharp, transient peaks corresponding to the motion of the arm through the sensing field, and arm crossing results in a sustained dip in RSSI as the torso blocks the reflection path. These signatures are sufficiently distinct that they can be visually identified without machine learning, although clustering algorithms such as k-means or dynamic time warping could be applied to automate classification. The phase response, while less sensitive than RSSI, still exhibits discernible patterns that corroborate the RSSI observations, particularly during larger movements. Importantly, these patterns are consistent across three different subjects of varying height, weight, and movement style, indicating that the system responds to the physical interaction of the human body with the electromagnetic field rather than to individual biomechanical idiosyncrasies.

Repetitive motions are analyzed using fast Fourier transform (FFT) to extract frequency-domain features. In one experiment, a subject sits at position C4 and performs hand waving at a controlled rate of 45 cycles per minute, or 0.75 Hz. The RSSI time series is analyzed over a 60-second window, and the FFT reveals a dominant peak at 0.75 Hz, with harmonics at 1.5 Hz and 2.25 Hz, corresponding to the expected frequency of the motion. The signal-to-noise ratio of this peak exceeds 15 dB, whereas the same experiment with a linear tag yields no discernible peak, with the energy distributed uniformly across the frequency spectrum. In a second experiment, the subject sits at position C6, directly in front of the reader antenna, and breathes naturally. The RSSI signal reveals a clear periodic modulation with a frequency of 14 breaths per minute, or approximately 0.23 Hz, with a peak-to-peak variation of 0.8 dB. The FFT confirms this frequency with high precision, and the waveform exhibits the characteristic shape of respiratory motion—slow rise during inhalation, gradual fall during exhalation. The same experiment with a linear tag produces no resolvable pattern, with the signal indistinguishable from background noise. These results demonstrate that the system is capable of detecting not only gross motor movements but also fine physiological motions that are typically the domain of specialized medical sensors.

The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the system is quantified by analyzing the head-nodding activity across multiple locations, from the closest point (C5) to the farthest (G1), which lies at a distance of over three meters from the tag-reader axis. The SNR is defined as the ratio of the amplitude of the fundamental frequency component (0.14 Hz, corresponding to one nod every seven seconds) to the average amplitude of all other non-harmonic peaks in the FFT spectrum. At the closest location, both the linear and cross-circular systems exhibit similar SNR values, as the direct signal is still strong enough to be detectable. However, as the distance increases, the SNR of the linear system degrades rapidly, falling below 3 dB at G1, where the signal is buried in noise. In contrast, the cross-circular system maintains an SNR above 10 dB even at the farthest point, demonstrating a clear advantage in long-range detection capability. This performance differential is not attributable to increased antenna gain or transmit power, but rather to the fundamental suppression of the direct backscatter noise, which allows the weak body-reflected signal to emerge from the background.

The system’s robustness is further validated by testing its performance under varying environmental conditions. The tag and reader are subjected to temperature fluctuations ranging from 18°C to 30°C, and the baseline calibration is re-executed after each change. The system retains its ability to detect motion with no significant degradation in sensitivity, indicating that the polarization-based signal enhancement is not temperature-dependent. The tag is also tested for mechanical stability: it is mounted on a wall, subjected to minor vibrations from nearby foot traffic, and exposed to ambient electromagnetic noise from Wi-Fi routers, Bluetooth devices, and microwave ovens. In all cases, the system continues to function reliably, with the preprocessing algorithm effectively filtering out extraneous interference. The tag’s performance remains consistent even when partially occluded by furniture or when the reader is reoriented by up to 45 degrees, demonstrating that the system is tolerant of real-world deployment conditions.

The invention’s practical utility is further enhanced by its passive nature and lack of user interaction. Unlike wearable sensors, the tag requires no batteries, no charging, and no maintenance beyond occasional cleaning. It can be permanently mounted on walls, ceilings, or under furniture, making it invisible to occupants and unobtrusive to daily routines. The reader, which is typically installed in a central location such as a hallway or living room, operates continuously in the background, consuming minimal power and transmitting no visual or audio signals. The entire system is compliant with FCC and ETSI regulations for UHF RFID operation and does not interfere with other wireless systems. The data output is purely numerical—RSSI and phase values—and contains no identifiable personal information, ensuring compliance with privacy regulations such as HIPAA and GDPR. There is no video recording, no audio capture, no biometric data collection, and no location tracking beyond the general area of motion detection. This makes the system uniquely suitable for deployment in private residences, where privacy is paramount, and in healthcare settings, where ethical and legal constraints limit the use of invasive monitoring technologies.

In summary, the invention provides a complete, self-contained, and commercially viable solution for non-contact human activity detection that overcomes the fundamental limitations of all prior approaches. By leveraging the physics of polarization reversal upon reflection from biological tissue, it transforms a passive RFID tag from a simple identifier into a sensitive motion detector capable of discerning a wide range of human activities across an entire room. The system requires no wearable components, no visual surveillance, no complex sensor arrays, and no user participation beyond normal behavior. It is low-cost, scalable, privacy-preserving, and robust to environmental variability. Its applications span healthcare, elder care, smart homes, assisted living, and industrial safety monitoring, offering a transformative capability that was previously unattainable with existing technologies. The invention represents a paradigm shift in ambient sensing, establishing a new standard for non-intrusive, continuous, and reliable human activity monitoring.
