## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of heterogeneous catalysis for the oxidation of hydrocarbons in exhaust gas streams, specifically to a highly stable and sulfur-resistant catalyst for the complete oxidation of methane in the exhaust of lean-burn natural gas engines. Such engines, while offering significant reductions in carbon dioxide emissions compared to diesel or gasoline-powered counterparts, suffer from the persistent emission of unburned methane, a greenhouse gas with a global warming potential approximately 86 times greater than carbon dioxide over a twenty-year time horizon. The catalytic oxidation of methane remains a formidable challenge due to the exceptional stability of its C–H bonds, which require high activation energies for cleavage and are particularly susceptible to inhibition by water vapor and poisoning by sulfur dioxide, both of which are invariably present in the exhaust streams of lean-burn natural gas vehicles. Conventional catalysts based on palladium supported on alumina or silica have demonstrated high initial activity under ideal laboratory conditions but rapidly degrade under realistic operating environments due to the formation of stable palladium hydroxide species and the irreversible adsorption of sulfur compounds onto active metal surfaces. The present invention addresses these limitations by introducing a novel catalyst architecture in which a mesoporous SBA-15 silica support is modified with zirconium oxide, and then loaded with a precise combination of palladium and platinum metals. This configuration results in a material that not only resists deactivation by water and sulfur but actively enhances the catalytic turnover rate for methane oxidation even after prolonged exposure to harsh thermal and chemical conditions. The invention is particularly suited for integration into aftertreatment systems for heavy-duty transportation, stationary power generation, and marine propulsion systems that utilize natural gas as a primary fuel source. The catalyst operates effectively within the temperature range of 250 to 600 degrees Celsius, which corresponds to the typical exhaust gas temperatures encountered downstream of the engine and upstream of the tailpipe, making it ideal for direct incorporation into existing exhaust line configurations without requiring significant system redesign. The invention further provides a method of preparation that ensures reproducible and scalable synthesis of the catalyst, enabling commercial deployment in industrial and automotive applications where long-term reliability and cost-effectiveness are paramount considerations.

## BACKGROUND

The environmental imperative to reduce greenhouse gas emissions has led to the increasing adoption of lean-burn natural gas engines in heavy-duty transportation, where they offer a favorable balance between fuel efficiency, operational cost, and lower carbon dioxide output compared to conventional diesel engines. However, a persistent technical challenge associated with these engines is the emission of unburned methane, commonly referred to as methane slip, which arises from incomplete combustion due to cold spots in the combustion chamber, poor fuel-air mixing, or transient operating conditions. Methane, due to its strong tetrahedral symmetry and high bond dissociation energy of approximately 435 kJ/mol for the first C–H bond, is exceptionally resistant to oxidation at low temperatures, requiring temperatures exceeding 1000 degrees Celsius for complete thermal combustion. Catalytic oxidation has emerged as the most viable solution for mitigating methane slip at the lower exhaust temperatures characteristic of lean-burn engines, and palladium-based catalysts have long been recognized as the most active materials for this purpose. Despite their superior intrinsic activity, palladium catalysts are notoriously susceptible to deactivation under real-world exhaust conditions, where the presence of water vapor and sulfur dioxide leads to irreversible performance loss. Water vapor inhibits catalytic activity by forming stable surface hydroxyl groups that block oxygen exchange between the support and the palladium active sites, effectively passivating the catalyst surface. Sulfur dioxide, even at low concentrations of a few parts per million, reacts with palladium to form inactive palladium sulfite or sulfate species that permanently reduce the number of available active sites. These deactivation mechanisms have severely limited the practical application of palladium catalysts in natural gas vehicle aftertreatment systems, despite decades of research and development. Prior efforts to mitigate these issues have included the use of hydrophobic supports such as mesoporous silica, which reduces water adsorption, and the addition of platinum to palladium to enhance sulfur tolerance through competitive adsorption or alloy formation. However, these approaches have yielded only marginal improvements. For instance, palladium supported on SBA-15, while exhibiting improved hydrothermal stability due to the hydrophobic nature of silica, still suffers from rapid sulfur poisoning because silica lacks the chemical affinity to bind sulfur species. Similarly, the addition of platinum to palladium on alumina has been shown to improve sulfur resistance by preferentially reacting with sulfur dioxide, thereby protecting palladium sites; however, alumina itself is prone to sintering under hydrothermal conditions and promotes the formation of stable palladium hydroxides. Other attempts to enhance stability have involved doping with titanium oxide, which can act as a sulfur scavenger, but this approach introduces acidic surface sites that increase water adsorption, counteracting the intended benefit. The use of zirconia as a sulfur trap has been explored in prior art, particularly in conjunction with palladium on alumina or titania supports, but zirconia alone lacks the high surface area necessary for effective dispersion of noble metals, resulting in low catalytic activity. Furthermore, no prior catalyst system has successfully combined the high surface area and structural integrity of SBA-15 with the sulfur-trapping capability of zirconium oxide and the redox-enhancing properties of platinum in a single, stable, and reproducible formulation. The critical gap in the prior art lies in the absence of a catalyst that simultaneously achieves high methane conversion efficiency, resistance to both water inhibition and sulfur poisoning, and long-term hydrothermal stability under realistic exhaust conditions. The present invention fills this gap by introducing a novel catalyst structure in which zirconium is incorporated into the SBA-15 framework at a controlled concentration, creating a composite support that not only resists sintering and hydration but also actively sequesters sulfur species, thereby preserving the integrity and reactivity of the palladium and platinum active phases. This innovation represents a fundamental departure from previous strategies that treated support modification and metal loading as independent variables, instead recognizing that the electronic and chemical interaction between zirconium, platinum, and the silica matrix is the key to unlocking unprecedented catalytic durability.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a methane oxidation catalyst comprising a mesoporous SBA-15 silica support doped with zirconium in a weight percentage ranging from 5 to 15 percent, impregnated with palladium at a concentration of 2 weight percent and platinum at a concentration of 4 weight percent, wherein the catalyst exhibits enhanced resistance to deactivation by water vapor and sulfur dioxide, sustained catalytic activity over extended periods under hydrothermal aging conditions, and a lower temperature for 50 percent methane conversion compared to any previously disclosed catalyst system under identical test conditions. The catalyst is characterized by the presence of palladium predominantly in the +2 oxidation state and platinum distributed across multiple oxidation states, including metallic platinum (0), platinum(II), and platinum(IV), with the proportion of higher oxidation states of platinum increasing with the zirconium content in the support, thereby enhancing the polarization of methane molecules and facilitating the rate-limiting step of C–H bond cleavage. The zirconium-doped SBA-15 support functions as both a structural stabilizer and a sacrificial sulfur trap, preferentially binding sulfur dioxide to form stable zirconium sulfates, thereby preventing the poisoning of palladium and platinum active sites. The catalyst maintains a high surface area and mesoporous structure even after exposure to high-temperature aging in the presence of water and sulfur, with pore volumes and surface areas remaining above 400 square meters per gram and 0.5 cubic centimeters per gram, respectively, after 40 hours of aging at 500 degrees Celsius. The catalyst demonstrates a temperature for 50 percent methane conversion, or T₅₀, of less than 500 degrees Celsius after aging, representing a reduction of more than 120 degrees Celsius compared to undoped palladium-platinum catalysts on SBA-15 under the same conditions. The invention further encompasses a method of preparing the catalyst through sequential impregnation of the zirconium-doped SBA-15 support with palladium and platinum precursors, followed by controlled calcination at 550 degrees Celsius, which ensures the formation of discrete, non-alloyed palladium oxide and platinum particles, with no evidence of Pd–Pt intermetallic compound formation, thereby preserving the individual catalytic functionalities of each metal. The catalyst exhibits no significant loss in activity after repeated thermal cycling between 250 and 600 degrees Celsius and maintains its sulfur resistance over multiple exposure cycles, making it uniquely suited for continuous operation in real-world natural gas engine exhaust systems. The invention further provides for the use of the catalyst in an exhaust aftertreatment system, wherein the catalyst is positioned downstream of the engine and upstream of the tailpipe, and is exposed to an exhaust stream containing between 0.5 and 2 volume percent methane, 5 to 15 volume percent oxygen, 5 to 15 volume percent water vapor, and 5 to 20 parts per million sulfur dioxide, with the catalyst achieving at least 80 percent methane conversion at temperatures below 550 degrees Celsius. The invention represents a significant advancement over prior art catalysts by integrating the structural stability of SBA-15, the sulfur-trapping capability of zirconium, and the redox-enhancing properties of platinum into a single, synergistic system that overcomes the fundamental limitations of conventional palladium-based methane oxidation catalysts.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention relates to a novel methane oxidation catalyst designed for use in the exhaust systems of lean-burn natural gas engines, wherein the catalyst comprises a mesoporous SBA-15 silica support doped with zirconium in a weight percentage ranging from 5 to 15 percent, impregnated with palladium at a concentration of 2 weight percent and platinum at a concentration of 4 weight percent. The catalyst is engineered to overcome the persistent challenges of water inhibition and sulfur poisoning that have historically limited the practical application of palladium-based catalysts in real-world exhaust environments. The SBA-15 support, characterized by its well-defined two-dimensional hexagonal mesoporous structure, thick pore walls, and high specific surface area, provides a stable and robust framework for the dispersion of noble metal nanoparticles. Unlike conventional alumina supports, which are prone to phase transitions and sintering under hydrothermal conditions, SBA-15 retains its structural integrity even after prolonged exposure to steam at elevated temperatures, thereby preserving the accessibility of active sites. However, the inherent hydrophobicity of silica, while beneficial for reducing water adsorption, also renders it chemically inert toward sulfur dioxide, allowing sulfur to directly interact with and poison the palladium and platinum active phases. To address this limitation, the present invention introduces zirconium into the SBA-15 framework at a controlled concentration, creating a composite support that combines the structural advantages of silica with the chemical affinity of zirconia for sulfur species. The zirconium is introduced via incipient wetness impregnation using an aqueous solution of zirconium(IV) oxynitrate hydrate, followed by calcination at 550 degrees Celsius, which results in the formation of tetragonal zirconia crystallites that are uniformly distributed throughout the mesoporous network without collapsing the pore structure. The resulting support exhibits a surface area between 400 and 800 square meters per gram, depending on the zirconium loading, and a pore volume between 0.5 and 0.8 cubic centimeters per gram, ensuring that the noble metal components remain highly dispersed and accessible. The palladium and platinum are then sequentially loaded onto the zirconium-doped SBA-15 support using aqueous solutions of palladium(II) nitrate and tetraammineplatinum(II) nitrate, respectively, with each metal loading followed by drying at 120 degrees Celsius and calcination at 550 degrees Celsius to ensure complete decomposition of the precursor salts and formation of stable oxide phases. The sequential loading protocol is critical to the invention, as it prevents the premature interaction between palladium and platinum during impregnation, thereby avoiding the formation of Pd–Pt alloys, which have been shown in prior art to reduce catalytic activity under sulfur-containing conditions. The final catalyst composition, denoted as 2Pd/4Pt/xZr/SBA-15 where x is the weight percentage of zirconium, contains no detectable Pd–Pt intermetallic phases, as confirmed by X-ray diffraction and transmission electron microscopy, indicating that the two metals remain as distinct, independent phases on the support. The palladium is predominantly present in the +2 oxidation state as PdO, as evidenced by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, which reveals that over 80 percent of the palladium exists in this form, with minor contributions from Pd⁰ and Pd⁴⁺. The platinum, however, exhibits a more complex oxidation state distribution that is strongly influenced by the zirconium content. In catalysts containing 5 and 10 weight percent zirconium, platinum is predominantly metallic (Pt⁰), with less than 7 percent present as Pt²⁺ and negligible amounts of Pt⁴⁺. In contrast, the catalyst containing 15 weight percent zirconium exhibits a significantly altered platinum oxidation state distribution, with 27.3 percent Pt⁰, 43.6 percent Pt²⁺, and 29.1 percent Pt⁴⁺. This shift toward higher oxidation states is attributed to the strong interaction between platinum and zirconium oxide, which stabilizes platinum in its oxidized forms through the formation of Pt–O–Zr interfacial bonds. The presence of these higher oxidation states of platinum is a key innovation of the invention, as it enhances the polarization of adsorbed methane molecules through the creation of strong electric dipoles at the platinum surface, thereby weakening the C–H bond and lowering the activation energy required for the rate-limiting step of hydrogen abstraction. This effect is not observed in catalysts lacking zirconium or containing lower zirconium loadings, and it is directly correlated with the observed increase in methane oxidation activity. The catalyst’s resistance to sulfur poisoning is primarily attributed to the ability of zirconium to act as a sacrificial sulfur trap. Sulfur dioxide in the exhaust stream preferentially reacts with zirconium oxide to form stable zirconium sulfate species, thereby preventing sulfur from reaching and poisoning the palladium and platinum active sites. This mechanism is confirmed by outlet gas analysis, which shows a dramatic reduction in sulfur dioxide concentration at the reactor outlet for zirconium-doped catalysts compared to undoped controls, with the most pronounced effect observed in the 15 weight percent zirconium catalyst. The sulfur binding capacity of the support is directly proportional to the zirconium content, with the 15 weight percent catalyst capable of sequestering more than 80 percent of the incoming sulfur dioxide at temperatures below 400 degrees Celsius. Even after prolonged exposure to sulfur-containing exhaust for 40 hours at 500 degrees Celsius, the catalyst retains over 85 percent of its initial methane conversion activity, whereas undoped catalysts lose more than 50 percent of their activity within the first hour. The hydrothermal stability of the catalyst is further enhanced by the thick pore walls of SBA-15, which resist collapse under steam exposure, and by the absence of phase transitions in the zirconium-doped support, which remain in the tetragonal phase even after aging. The catalyst maintains its mesoporous structure, with pore size distributions centered at 3.8 and 5.6 nanometers, and exhibits no significant reduction in surface area or pore volume after aging, indicating that the support structure remains intact. The catalyst is prepared using a scalable and reproducible method that involves the synthesis of SBA-15 via a templating process using Pluronic P123 surfactant, followed by zirconium impregnation, sequential noble metal loading, and controlled calcination. The entire process is conducted under ambient pressure and does not require specialized equipment or extreme conditions, making it suitable for large-scale industrial production. The catalyst is designed for use in exhaust aftertreatment systems downstream of lean-burn natural gas engines, where it is exposed to a gas stream containing 0.5 to 2 volume percent methane, 5 to 15 volume percent oxygen, 5 to 15 volume percent water vapor, 5 to 20 parts per million sulfur dioxide, and the balance nitrogen. Under these conditions, the catalyst achieves a methane conversion efficiency of at least 80 percent at temperatures below 550 degrees Celsius, with a T₅₀ of less than 500 degrees Celsius after aging, representing a substantial improvement over all previously disclosed catalyst systems. The invention further encompasses the use of the catalyst in a monolithic or pelletized form within a catalytic converter, wherein the catalyst is coated onto a ceramic or metallic substrate with a washcoat layer to enhance mechanical durability and thermal conductivity. The catalyst may also be integrated into a two-stage aftertreatment system, wherein a first catalyst layer is optimized for low-temperature methane oxidation and a second layer is optimized for nitrogen oxide reduction, with the methane oxidation catalyst positioned upstream to ensure complete hydrocarbon removal before downstream processing. The invention further provides for the use of the catalyst in stationary power generation systems, marine propulsion systems, and other applications where lean-burn natural gas engines are employed and methane slip must be minimized to meet stringent environmental regulations. The catalyst’s ability to maintain high activity under continuous operation, its resistance to thermal degradation, and its compatibility with existing exhaust system architectures make it a transformative solution for the widespread adoption of natural gas as a clean transportation fuel.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1: Catalysts with Pd and Pt on a Lanthanum Doped Alumina Exhibit Enhanced Methane Conversion after Aging in the Presence of Sulfur and Water

In a comparative study designed to evaluate the performance of alternative support materials, a series of catalysts were prepared using lanthanum-doped alumina as the support instead of zirconium-doped SBA-15. These catalysts were synthesized using identical metal loadings of 2 weight percent palladium and 4 weight percent platinum, with lanthanum doping levels of 5, 10, and 15 weight percent, prepared via incipient wetness impregnation of lanthanum nitrate onto gamma-alumina followed by calcination at 550 degrees Celsius. The resulting catalysts, designated as 2Pd/4Pt/xLa/Al₂O₃, were subjected to the same aging protocol as the zirconium-doped SBA-15 catalysts, involving 40 hours of exposure to a simulated lean-burn natural gas exhaust stream containing 10 volume percent water vapor and 10 parts per million sulfur dioxide at a temperature of 500 degrees Celsius. Following aging, the catalysts were evaluated for methane oxidation activity using temperature gradient runs from 75 to 600 degrees Celsius at a heating rate of 3 degrees Celsius per minute. The results revealed that, despite the known sulfur-trapping capability of lanthanum oxide, the lanthanum-doped alumina catalysts exhibited significantly lower methane conversion efficiency compared to the zirconium-doped SBA-15 catalysts. The catalyst with 15 weight percent lanthanum achieved a T₅₀ of 523 degrees Celsius after aging, which was 42 degrees Celsius higher than the corresponding zirconium-doped SBA-15 catalyst. Moreover, the lanthanum-doped catalysts demonstrated a pronounced and irreversible drop in activity during the initial 20 minutes of aging, with methane conversion falling from approximately 80 percent to below 40 percent within the first hour, whereas the zirconium-doped SBA-15 catalysts maintained conversion above 60 percent throughout the entire aging period. Post-aging characterization using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy revealed that the palladium in the lanthanum-doped catalysts was predominantly present as PdO, but a significant portion of the platinum had been reduced to metallic Pt⁰, indicating that the lanthanum oxide support was unable to stabilize platinum in higher oxidation states. Additionally, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy mapping of the aged catalyst surfaces showed that sulfur had penetrated deeply into the alumina matrix, indicating that the lanthanum oxide was overwhelmed by the sulfur load and could not fully protect the noble metal sites. Transmission electron microscopy confirmed extensive sintering of both palladium and platinum nanoparticles on the lanthanum-doped alumina support, with average particle sizes increasing from 3.2 nanometers to over 12 nanometers after aging, whereas the particle sizes on the zirconium-doped SBA-15 support remained below 5 nanometers. The surface area of the lanthanum-doped alumina catalysts decreased by more than 40 percent after aging, from an initial value of 150 square meters per gram to less than 90 square meters per gram, whereas the zirconium-doped SBA-15 catalysts retained over 70 percent of their initial surface area. These findings demonstrate that lanthanum doping of alumina, while providing some sulfur resistance, is fundamentally inadequate for maintaining catalytic activity under prolonged hydrothermal and sulfur-rich conditions due to the inherent instability of the alumina support and its inability to stabilize platinum in the higher oxidation states necessary for optimal methane activation. The superior performance of the zirconium-doped SBA-15 catalysts is therefore not merely a function of sulfur trapping but is the result of a synergistic combination of structural stability, support-metal interaction, and oxidation state control that cannot be replicated using lanthanum-doped alumina.

### Example 2: A Catalyst with a Lanthanum Doped Alumina Support Exhibits Higher Activity in the Presence of Excess Water Vapour than a Catalyst with an Alumina Support not Doped with Lanthanum

A series of experiments were conducted to evaluate the effect of water vapor on the catalytic activity of lanthanum-doped alumina versus undoped alumina-supported catalysts under conditions simulating high-humidity exhaust environments. Catalysts containing 2 weight percent palladium and 4 weight percent platinum were prepared on both undoped gamma-alumina and on alumina doped with 15 weight percent lanthanum, following the same synthesis protocol as described in Example 1. The catalysts were tested in a fixed-bed reactor under identical conditions, with the only variable being the water vapor concentration in the feed gas, which was varied from 5 to 20 volume percent while maintaining constant methane, oxygen, and sulfur dioxide concentrations. The temperature for 50 percent methane conversion, or T₅₀, was recorded at each water vapor level. For the undoped alumina-supported catalyst, the T₅₀ increased from 487 degrees Celsius at 5 percent water vapor to 612 degrees Celsius at 20 percent water vapor, representing a 125-degree Celsius increase in activation temperature due solely to the presence of excess water. In contrast, the lanthanum-doped alumina catalyst exhibited a smaller but still significant increase in T₅₀, from 465 degrees Celsius at 5 percent water vapor to 578 degrees Celsius at 20 percent water vapor, indicating a 113-degree Celsius increase. While this represents a modest improvement over the undoped catalyst, the absolute performance of the lanthanum-doped catalyst remained inferior to that of the zirconium-doped SBA-15 catalyst, which exhibited a T₅₀ increase of only 48 degrees Celsius over the same water vapor range, from 432 to 480 degrees Celsius. The difference in behavior was attributed to the hydrophilic nature of the alumina support, which, even when doped with lanthanum, continues to adsorb water molecules on its surface, forming stable hydroxyl groups that block oxygen mobility and inhibit methane adsorption. In contrast, the SBA-15 support, being inherently hydrophobic, minimizes water adsorption, and the presence of zirconium oxide further enhances the catalyst’s ability to repel water by creating a surface chemistry that favors the desorption of hydroxyl species. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy of the aged catalysts revealed that the surface hydroxyl concentration on the lanthanum-doped alumina was nearly twice that of the zirconium-doped SBA-15 catalyst, confirming that water inhibition remains a dominant deactivation mechanism even in lanthanum-modified alumina systems. Furthermore, the lanthanum-doped catalysts exhibited a slower recovery of activity when the water vapor concentration was temporarily reduced, indicating that the hydroxyl groups formed on the alumina surface were more strongly bound and less reversible than those on the SBA-15-based catalysts. The data clearly demonstrate that while lanthanum doping provides marginal improvement in water tolerance compared to undoped alumina, it does not eliminate the fundamental limitation imposed by the hydrophilic nature of the alumina support. The zirconium-doped SBA-15 catalyst, by virtue of its hydrophobic support and the stabilizing influence of zirconium on platinum oxidation states, achieves a level of water resistance that is unattainable with any alumina-based system, regardless of lanthanum doping.

### Example 3: Sulfur Resistance and Hydrothermal Stability of Catalysts having an Alumina Support Doped with Lanthanum at Different Weight Ratios of Platinum and Palladium

To further investigate the limitations of lanthanum-doped alumina as a support for methane oxidation catalysts, a series of catalysts were prepared with varying platinum-to-palladium weight ratios on a 15 weight percent lanthanum-doped alumina support. The catalysts included formulations with 1 weight percent platinum and 5 weight percent palladium, 3 weight percent platinum and 3 weight percent palladium, and 5 weight percent platinum and 1 weight percent palladium, all supported on lanthanum-doped alumina. These catalysts were subjected to the same 40-hour aging protocol in a stream containing 10 volume percent water vapor and 10 parts per million sulfur dioxide at 500 degrees Celsius, followed by methane oxidation testing. The results revealed that increasing the platinum content did not significantly improve sulfur resistance or hydrothermal stability. The catalyst with the highest platinum content (5 wt% Pt, 1 wt% Pd) exhibited a T₅₀ of 518 degrees Celsius after aging, which was only 5 degrees Celsius lower than the catalyst with equal loadings of platinum and palladium (3 wt% each), and 10 degrees Celsius lower than the catalyst with the highest palladium content (1 wt% Pt, 5 wt% Pd). In contrast, the zirconium-doped SBA-15 catalyst with 2 wt% Pd and 4 wt% Pt achieved a T₅₀ of 481 degrees Celsius under identical conditions. Post-aging X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy showed that the platinum in all lanthanum-doped alumina catalysts was predominantly in the metallic state, with less than 5 percent present as Pt²⁺, regardless of the initial platinum loading. This indicates that the lanthanum-doped alumina support is incapable of stabilizing platinum in higher oxidation states, even when platinum is present in excess. Furthermore, sulfur analysis using X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy revealed that sulfur was distributed throughout the alumina matrix, with concentrations exceeding 2 weight percent sulfur on the surface of the catalysts, indicating that the lanthanum oxide had been saturated and could no longer act as an effective sulfur trap. The surface area of all lanthanum-doped alumina catalysts decreased by more than 35 percent after aging, with the highest platinum-containing catalyst showing the greatest degree of sintering, likely due to the increased mobility of platinum nanoparticles under hydrothermal conditions. The data demonstrate that increasing the platinum content on a lanthanum-doped alumina support does not confer the same benefits observed with zirconium-doped SBA-15, because the underlying support material lacks the necessary chemical and structural properties to stabilize platinum in its active oxidized forms or to effectively sequester sulfur. The performance of these catalysts is therefore limited by the inherent instability of the alumina support and its inability to interact favorably with platinum, regardless of the metal loading ratio. The zirconium-doped SBA-15 catalyst, by contrast, achieves superior sulfur resistance and hydrothermal stability not through increased metal loading but through the synergistic interaction between the support and the metal phases, a mechanism that cannot be replicated by altering the platinum-to-palladium ratio on an incompatible support.

### Example 4: Effect of Calcination Temperature on Catalyst Activity

The calcination temperature employed during the preparation of the catalyst was systematically varied to determine its influence on the catalytic performance, particularly with respect to the oxidation state distribution of platinum and the structural integrity of the support. A series of catalysts were prepared using the same composition of 2 weight percent palladium, 4 weight percent platinum, and 15 weight percent zirconium on SBA-15, but calcined at temperatures of 450, 500, 550, 600, and 650 degrees Celsius for six hours each. All other synthesis parameters, including impregnation sequence, drying conditions, and precursor concentrations, were held constant. The catalysts were then aged under identical conditions and tested for methane oxidation activity. The results revealed a strong dependence of catalytic performance on calcination temperature. Catalysts calcined at 450 and 500 degrees Celsius exhibited lower methane conversion efficiency, with T₅₀ values of 512 and 498 degrees Celsius, respectively, after aging. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy showed that platinum in these catalysts was predominantly in the metallic state, with less than 20 percent present as Pt²⁺ and negligible amounts of Pt⁴⁺, indicating insufficient thermal energy to fully oxidize platinum and establish the Pt–O–Zr interfacial bonds necessary for methane activation. Catalysts calcined at 550 degrees Celsius exhibited the optimal balance of metal dispersion and oxidation state control, with platinum distributed as 27.3 percent Pt⁰, 43.6 percent Pt²⁺, and 29.1 percent Pt⁴⁺, and a T₅₀ of 481 degrees Celsius after aging. Catalysts calcined at 600 and 650 degrees Celsius, while exhibiting higher crystallinity of zirconia and stronger metal-support interactions, showed a significant decrease in surface area—from 432 to 315 square meters per gram—and an increase in platinum particle size from 4.1 to 8.7 nanometers, as determined by transmission electron microscopy. This sintering led to a reduction in active site density and a corresponding increase in T₅₀ to 495 and 508 degrees Celsius, respectively. The decline in performance at higher calcination temperatures was attributed to the collapse of mesopores in the SBA-15 support and the aggregation of platinum nanoparticles, which reduced accessibility to active sites despite the higher oxidation state of platinum. The data demonstrate that calcination at 550 degrees Celsius is the optimal temperature for achieving the desired balance between metal oxidation state control, support stability, and nanoparticle dispersion. Calcination at lower temperatures fails to fully oxidize platinum and establish the critical Pt–O–Zr interface, while calcination at higher temperatures induces structural degradation of the support and excessive metal sintering, both of which diminish catalytic activity. The invention therefore specifies calcination at 550 degrees Celsius as the preferred condition for maximizing the synergistic effects between the zirconium-doped support and the noble metal components.

### Example 5: Effect of Method Preparation on Catalyst Activity

The method of catalyst preparation was systematically varied to determine its impact on catalytic performance, focusing on the sequence of metal impregnation, the use of co-impregnation versus sequential impregnation, and the inclusion of intermediate calcination steps. Four different preparation methods were employed for catalysts containing 2 weight percent palladium, 4 weight percent platinum, and 15 weight percent zirconium on SBA-15. Method A involved the sequential impregnation of palladium followed by platinum, with calcination at 550 degrees Celsius between each step. Method B employed co-impregnation of both palladium and platinum precursors simultaneously, followed by a single calcination step. Method C used sequential impregnation but omitted the intermediate calcination step, drying only at 120 degrees Celsius between metal loadings. Method D involved the impregnation of platinum first, followed by palladium, with intermediate calcination. All catalysts were aged under identical conditions and tested for methane oxidation activity. The results showed that Method A, the sequential impregnation with intermediate calcination, yielded the highest methane conversion efficiency, with a T₅₀ of 481 degrees Celsius after aging. Method B, the co-impregnation approach, resulted in a T₅₀ of 503 degrees Celsius, with X-ray diffraction revealing the formation of small Pd–Pt alloy particles, which were absent in the other methods. These alloy particles, while beneficial in some catalytic applications, were found to be more susceptible to sulfur poisoning and exhibited lower activity for methane oxidation under wet, sulfur-containing conditions. Method C, which omitted intermediate calcination, produced a catalyst with a T₅₀ of 518 degrees Celsius, with X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy indicating a higher proportion of palladium in the metallic state and a lower proportion of platinum in the +4 oxidation state, suggesting that incomplete decomposition of the precursors led to suboptimal metal dispersion and oxidation state control. Method D, which reversed the impregnation order, resulted in a T₅₀ of 497 degrees Celsius, with evidence of palladium migration into the zirconium-rich regions of the support, leading to partial encapsulation of palladium sites and reduced accessibility. The data demonstrate that the sequence of metal loading and the inclusion of intermediate calcination steps are critical to achieving the desired catalyst structure. Sequential impregnation with calcination between steps ensures that each metal is fully oxidized and anchored to the support before the next metal is introduced, preventing alloy formation and promoting the formation of discrete, well-dispersed PdO and Pt species. The intermediate calcination also removes residual chloride and nitrate ions that could otherwise interfere with metal-support interactions. The invention therefore specifies that the catalyst must be prepared using sequential impregnation of palladium followed by platinum, with calcination at 550 degrees Celsius after each metal loading, to achieve the optimal combination of activity, stability, and sulfur resistance. Any deviation from this method, including co-impregnation, omission of intermediate calcination, or reversal of impregnation order, results in a catalyst with inferior performance under real-world exhaust conditions.
