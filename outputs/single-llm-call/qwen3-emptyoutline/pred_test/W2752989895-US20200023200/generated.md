## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention resides in the field of medical radiation therapy, specifically in the subfield of electron beam delivery systems for conformal cancer treatment. More particularly, the invention relates to a passive, non-mechanical, non-electronic device designed to spatially modulate the intensity of a therapeutic electron beam without the need for dynamic collimation, real-time beam scanning, or electronic control systems. The invention enables the creation of complex, patient-specific, three-dimensional dose distributions using a static, physically fabricated component that is inserted into the electron beam path between the accelerator’s final collimator and the patient’s surface. This device, termed the Passive Radiotherapy Intensity Modulator for Electrons (PRIME), operates by selectively attenuating portions of the incident electron fluence through an array of high-density micro-structures—either solid island blocks or apertures—arranged in a precisely calculated spatial pattern within a collimating plane. The modulation arises not from direct blocking alone, but from the controlled interaction of multiple Coulomb scattering with the geometric arrangement of these micro-structures, such that the resulting fluence distribution at depth in tissue mimics a continuous, smoothly varying intensity profile. The invention is particularly suited for integration with existing electron therapy platforms, including those employing bolus-based conformal therapy, segmented-field techniques, and multi-field modulated radiation therapy, without requiring modifications to the linear accelerator, treatment planning system, or radiation detection hardware. The PRIME system represents a paradigm shift from active, high-cost, mechanically complex intensity modulation systems to a low-cost, robust, and clinically deployable passive solution that leverages fundamental physics of charged particle transport to achieve therapeutic precision.

## BACKGROUND AND DESCRIPTION OF PRIOR ART

Historically, intensity modulation in radiotherapy has been achieved through dynamic multileaf collimators for photon beams, scanned proton beams, and, more recently, electronically controlled electron multileaf collimators. In the context of electron therapy, the challenge of achieving spatially heterogeneous dose distributions has been persistently hindered by the inherent physical properties of electrons, particularly their propensity for multiple Coulomb scattering in air and tissue, which causes beam broadening and limits the sharpness of field edges. Conventional electron collimation relies on fixed apertures or custom-cut lead shields, which provide uniform attenuation across the entire field and are incapable of generating gradients in intensity. Attempts to introduce modulation have focused on adapting technologies developed for photon therapy, such as multileaf collimators, to electron beams. However, these efforts have encountered significant limitations. Electron multileaf collimators (eMLCs) require extended air gaps to accommodate leaf movement, which exacerbates scattering and results in unacceptably broad penumbras. Furthermore, the integration of eMLCs into clinical workflows demands helium-filled treatment heads to reduce scattering, increasing system complexity and cost. Commercially available eMLC systems, while technically functional, have not achieved widespread adoption due to their high acquisition cost, limited clinical utility given the relatively small proportion of patients treated with electrons, and the absence of seamless integration with standard treatment planning systems. Alternative approaches, such as the use of scanned electron beams, require sophisticated beam steering mechanisms and are incompatible with most existing clinical accelerators. Other methods, including the use of compensators for photon therapy, have not been successfully translated to electrons due to the inability of traditional metal compensators to account for the non-linear, depth-dependent scattering effects that govern electron transport. Prior art has also included the use of single, large-diameter shielding elements to protect sensitive structures, such as the lens of the eye during retinal irradiation, but these applications involve uniform attenuation over a single region and do not constitute true intensity modulation across a field. Similarly, saw-toothed collimator edges have been employed to match penumbras between abutting electron fields of differing energies, but these techniques are limited to edge blending and cannot generate internal intensity gradients. No prior system has demonstrated the capability to generate a continuous, spatially varying intensity distribution across an entire electron field using an array of micro-scale, high-density elements arranged in a non-uniform, non-periodic pattern, where the size, spacing, and density of each element are optimized through inverse planning algorithms to produce a desired dose distribution at a specified depth in tissue. Furthermore, no prior system has established a predictive mathematical framework relating the geometric parameters of these micro-structures—diameter, spacing, and arrangement—to the resulting fluence modulation at clinically relevant depths, nor has any prior system provided a practical, manufacturable method for fabricating such a device using materials compatible with clinical radiation environments. The absence of such a system has left a critical gap in the clinical toolbox for electron therapy, particularly in applications requiring high conformity, such as bolus-based treatments for head and neck cancers, postmastectomy chest wall irradiation, and extremity sarcomas, where irregular patient surfaces and shallow target volumes make precise dose sculpting essential. The prior art, therefore, fails to disclose, suggest, or render obvious the specific combination of structural elements, physical principles, and computational methodologies embodied in the present invention.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention discloses a novel, passive, non-electronic device for intensity modulation of therapeutic electron beams, known as the Passive Radiotherapy Intensity Modulator for Electrons (PRIME). The PRIME system comprises a rigid, planar structure positioned within the electron beam path, containing an array of micro-scale, high-density elements arranged in a spatially non-uniform pattern. These elements consist of either solid island blocks composed of a high-atomic-number material, such as tungsten alloy or lead-based compound, or apertures of varying diameter etched into a collimating insert of matching thickness and material. The island blocks and apertures are arranged on a geometric grid, preferably hexagonal, with each element sized and positioned according to a calculated prescription derived from an inverse planning algorithm that accounts for the physics of multiple Coulomb scattering in air and tissue. The size of each island block or aperture is determined by the desired local intensity reduction factor, which is defined as the ratio of the intended fluence at a specified depth to the unmodulated fluence. For intensity reductions between 70% and 100%, island blocks are employed, with their diameters and inter-element spacing selected to allow sufficient scattering to fill in the attenuated regions while maintaining a uniform, predictable dose profile. For intensity reductions between 0% and 50%, island apertures are used, where the unblocked fraction of the beam is modulated by the size and spacing of the apertures. The thickness of each island block and the collimating insert is sufficient to fully attenuate electrons from the highest energy beam used in clinical practice, typically up to 20 MeV, ensuring that no primary electrons penetrate beyond the modulator. The cross-sectional shape of each element is preferably circular to minimize surface area and reduce secondary electron emission and bremsstrahlung production. The axes of the island blocks and apertures are aligned to coincide with the diverging rays emanating from the virtual source of the electron beam, thereby minimizing edge effects and maintaining geometric fidelity across the field. The PRIME system operates without any moving parts, power requirements, or real-time control systems, and is designed to be inserted into the treatment head as a replaceable component, analogous to a conventional electron applicator or custom cutout. The invention further includes a computational methodology for determining the optimal configuration of island blocks and apertures based on a desired three-dimensional dose distribution, using a pencil beam algorithm or Monte Carlo simulation to model the scattering effects and iteratively adjust the geometric parameters until the predicted fluence at depth matches the clinical prescription. The system enables the creation of highly conformal dose distributions for applications such as intensity-modulated bolus electron conformal therapy, segmented-field electron conformal therapy, and modulated electron radiation therapy, without requiring modifications to the linear accelerator or treatment planning system beyond the addition of a dose calculation module capable of accounting for the PRIME modulator’s influence. The invention provides a clinically viable, low-cost, and robust solution to a long-standing problem in electron therapy, offering a practical pathway to achieving the same level of dose conformity and homogeneity currently available in photon and proton therapy.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The Passive Radiotherapy Intensity Modulator for Electrons (PRIME) is a static, physically fabricated component designed to be inserted into the electron beam path of a clinical linear accelerator, positioned between the final collimating insert and the patient’s surface. The device consists of a rigid, planar substrate, typically fabricated from a low-density, radiation-transparent material such as polystyrene foam or polyethylene, which serves as a structural matrix for the precise placement of high-density micro-elements. These micro-elements are either solid island blocks or apertures, each with a defined cross-sectional area and spatial location, arranged in a pattern that, when combined with the physics of multiple Coulomb scattering, produces a spatially modulated electron fluence distribution at a specified depth in tissue. The island blocks are cylindrical or right oblique cylindrical structures composed of a high-atomic-number material, such as tungsten alloy, lead-tin-bismuth alloy, or copper, with a diameter ranging from 0.1 mm to 1.5 mm and a thickness equal to that of the collimating insert, typically between 1.5 cm and 2.5 cm, sufficient to fully attenuate electrons from the highest clinical energy (up to 20 MeV). The apertures are circular holes of corresponding diameter, machined through the collimating insert itself, which is constructed from the same high-density material as the island blocks. The spatial arrangement of these elements follows a hexagonal lattice, although other regular or irregular patterns may be employed if they achieve the desired fluence modulation with acceptable fidelity. The pitch, or center-to-center spacing, between adjacent elements is selected based on the desired intensity reduction factor and the electron energy, with typical values ranging from 0.5 mm to 2.0 mm. The selection of diameter and spacing is governed by a mathematical relationship derived from the physics of electron transport: for island blocks, the local intensity reduction factor IRF is given by the equation IRF = 1 − (π / 2√3) × (d / r)², where d is the diameter of the island block and r is the pitch of the hexagonal grid; for island apertures, the local intensity is given by IRF = (π / 2√3) × (d / r)². These equations are valid under the assumption that the electron fluence is sufficiently broadened by multiple Coulomb scattering such that the attenuation from each individual element overlaps with its neighbors, creating a continuous, smoothed intensity profile rather than a discrete, pixelated pattern. The thickness of the substrate material is chosen to be minimal, typically less than 5 mm, to avoid introducing additional scattering or attenuation that would interfere with the intended modulation. The island blocks are embedded within the substrate using a precision machining or molding process, ensuring that their axial orientation aligns with the diverging rays from the virtual source of the electron beam, which is typically located 80 cm to 105 cm from the patient’s surface, depending on the accelerator model. This alignment ensures that the effective cross-section of each element remains perpendicular to the incident beam direction across the entire field, minimizing angular dependence and edge effects. The collimating insert, whether containing apertures or serving as the base for island blocks, is manufactured to have a uniform thickness throughout, with its upstream surface flush with the upstream surface of the substrate. The entire assembly is designed to fit within the standard collimating space of a clinical electron applicator, allowing for easy insertion and removal without requiring modification to the accelerator head or beam delivery system. The device is calibrated using a reference electron beam, and its performance is validated through a combination of Monte Carlo simulations and measurements using radiochromic film, ionization chambers, or diode detectors placed at clinically relevant depths in a water phantom. The PRIME system is intended for use in conjunction with a treatment planning system that has been augmented with a specialized dose calculation engine capable of modeling the scattering effects induced by the modulator. This engine accepts as input the three-dimensional geometry of the PRIME device, including the position, diameter, and material composition of each island block or aperture, and computes the resulting fluence distribution at depth by convolving the incident beam profile with a scattering kernel derived from empirical measurements and theoretical models of electron transport. The optimization of the PRIME geometry is performed using an inverse planning algorithm that iteratively adjusts the parameters of the island blocks and apertures to minimize the difference between the calculated dose distribution and the prescribed clinical target volume coverage, while respecting constraints on maximum and minimum fluence levels, dose homogeneity, and organ-at-risk sparing. The algorithm employs a gradient-based or genetic optimization technique, constrained by the physical limits of manufacturability, such as minimum feature size, maximum density of elements, and tolerance for positional error. The resulting design is then fabricated using precision micro-machining techniques, such as laser drilling, electrical discharge machining, or micro-stereolithography, depending on the material and required resolution. The fabricated PRIME device is then mounted onto a standard electron applicator or inserted into a custom carrier that ensures reproducible positioning relative to the beam axis and patient surface. During treatment, the PRIME device remains stationary, and the electron beam is delivered as a single, static field, with the intensity modulation achieved entirely through the passive geometry of the device. The system is compatible with all standard electron energies, from 6 MeV to 20 MeV, and can be used with or without a bolus, depending on the clinical application. The PRIME system does not require helium, vacuum chambers, or real-time beam control, and its operation is independent of the accelerator’s gantry angle, couch position, or monitor unit count. The device is designed for single-patient use or limited reuse, with sterilization and quality assurance protocols established to ensure clinical safety and reproducibility. The invention further encompasses a method for generating a library of pre-fabricated PRIME devices for common clinical scenarios, such as head and neck bolus therapy, chest wall irradiation, or extremity sarcoma treatment, where the geometric parameters of the island blocks and apertures are pre-calculated and stored in a database, allowing for rapid selection and deployment without requiring custom design for each patient. The PRIME system thus represents a fundamental departure from active, dynamic, and electronically controlled intensity modulation systems, offering a passive, mechanically simple, and clinically practical solution to the longstanding challenge of achieving conformal electron therapy.

### EXAMPLE 1

In a clinical scenario involving the treatment of a patient with a superficial tumor of the right buccal mucosa, the PRIME system was employed to improve dose homogeneity within the planning target volume (PTV) while minimizing dose to adjacent critical structures, including the mandible and oral mucosa. The patient’s anatomy was imaged using a computed tomography (CT) scanner with a slice thickness of 2.5 mm, and the target volume was contoured by a radiation oncologist based on clinical and radiographic criteria. A bolus of 1.5 cm thickness was designed to conform to the irregular surface of the cheek, with its distal surface shaped to match the 90% isodose line of the prescribed dose. However, due to the complexity of the patient’s surface contour, the bolus introduced significant dose heterogeneity, with hot spots exceeding 115% of the prescribed dose and cold spots dropping below 85%, resulting in a dose spread of 30%. To correct this, a PRIME device was designed to modulate the electron fluence such that the dose distribution at a depth of 2.0 cm in water, corresponding to the depth of the PTV, would achieve a homogeneity of ±10% around the prescribed dose of 50 Gy. An inverse planning algorithm was executed using a pencil beam algorithm calibrated for 16 MeV electrons at a source-to-surface distance (SSD) of 103 cm. The algorithm began with an initial guess of a uniform fluence distribution and iteratively adjusted the diameter and position of island blocks on a hexagonal grid with a pitch of 0.5 cm. The optimization constraints included a minimum island block diameter of 0.1 mm, a maximum diameter of 0.8 mm, and a maximum density of 100 elements per square centimeter to ensure manufacturability. The algorithm converged after 42 iterations, producing a spatial map of island block diameters ranging from 0.12 mm to 0.78 mm, with a total of 1,203 elements distributed across a 12 cm × 12 cm field. The resulting fluence distribution, calculated at 2.0 cm depth, showed a dose homogeneity of 92% to 108%, with a maximum dose reduction of 8% in regions corresponding to the bolus’s thickest regions and a minimum reduction of 1% in regions corresponding to the bolus’s thinnest regions. The PRIME device was fabricated using a precision laser drilling system on a 2.0 cm thick substrate of polystyrene foam, into which 1,203 cylindrical tungsten alloy pins, each 0.2 mm in diameter and 2.0 cm in length, were embedded with a positional accuracy of ±0.05 mm. The tungsten alloy had a density of 17.3 g/cm³ and a melting point of 3,422°C, ensuring structural stability under clinical conditions. The fabricated device was mounted onto a standard electron applicator and inserted into the treatment head of an Elekta Infinity linear accelerator. A water phantom was positioned at 103 cm SSD, and a p-type electron dosimetry diode with an active volume of 0.2 mm diameter and 0.06 mm thickness was used to measure the off-axis dose profile along the central axis and at 1 cm intervals perpendicular to it. Measurements were taken at a depth of 2.0 cm, with a 10 cm × 10 cm field size and 16 MeV energy. The measured dose profile showed an average deviation of 1.2% from the predicted profile, with a maximum local deviation of 2.8% occurring at the transition between regions of high and low modulation. The minimum relative dose measured was 0.92, and the maximum was 1.08, confirming the achievement of the desired homogeneity. The device was then used in the patient’s treatment, with a total of 1,250 monitor units delivered over a single field. Post-treatment verification using radiochromic film placed at the depth of the PTV confirmed a dose distribution within ±5% of the planned values, with no clinically significant hot or cold spots observed. The patient completed treatment without adverse effects, and follow-up imaging showed complete tumor regression with no evidence of normal tissue toxicity. This example demonstrates the clinical feasibility and therapeutic efficacy of the PRIME system in improving dose homogeneity in bolus electron conformal therapy without requiring changes to the treatment planning system beyond the addition of a dose calculation module capable of modeling the modulator’s effect.

### EXAMPLE 2

In a second clinical application, the PRIME system was employed to address the challenge of dose heterogeneity in segmented-field electron conformal therapy for a patient with a postmastectomy chest wall recurrence. The treatment plan required two abutting electron fields: a 12 MeV field targeting the medial chest wall and a 16 MeV field targeting the lateral chest wall. Due to the difference in energy, the penumbra of the 16 MeV field was significantly sharper than that of the 12 MeV field, resulting in a dose gradient of 25% across the abutting region, with a cold spot of 82% and a hot spot of 107%. To mitigate this, a PRIME device was designed to be placed at the edge of the 16 MeV field to broaden its penumbra to match that of the 12 MeV field. The PRIME device consisted of an array of island apertures arranged in a hexagonal grid with a pitch of 1.2 mm, positioned along the lateral edge of the 16 MeV field over a 3 cm width. The apertures were machined into a 2.0 cm thick copper collimating insert, with diameters ranging from 0.15 mm to 0.65 mm, increasing linearly from the field edge toward the center of the field. The optimization algorithm was configured to maximize the fluence gradient in the transition zone while maintaining a uniform dose in the central regions of both fields. The algorithm used a Monte Carlo simulation to model the electron transport through the apertures and surrounding air, with a voxel size of 0.1 mm in the transverse plane and 0.5 mm in depth. The simulation accounted for the energy dependence of multiple Coulomb scattering and the angular distribution of scattered electrons. After 31 iterations, the algorithm produced a spatial map of aperture diameters that, when placed at the edge of the 16 MeV field, resulted in a penumbra width (80% to 20%) of 1.8 cm, matching that of the 12 MeV field. The PRIME device was fabricated using an electrical discharge machining process on a 2.0 cm thick copper plate, with 897 apertures drilled to the specified diameters. The device was mounted onto a custom electron applicator and aligned with the field edge using a laser positioning system. Measurements were performed in a water phantom using a 0.1 mm diameter ionization chamber, with profiles taken at 0.5 cm intervals from the field edge. The measured penumbra width was 1.75 cm, within 3% of the predicted value. The dose gradient across the abutting region was reduced to 8% (92% to 100%), eliminating the clinically significant cold and hot spots. The patient was treated with the modified plan, receiving a total dose of 50 Gy over 25 fractions. Follow-up imaging and clinical assessment showed no evidence of skin toxicity or recurrence at the abutting region, and the patient reported improved comfort during treatment due to the elimination of hot spots. This example demonstrates the ability of the PRIME system to solve a specific clinical problem in segmented-field electron therapy—penumbra mismatch—through a passive, non-dynamic, and cost-effective solution that requires no modification to the accelerator or treatment planning system beyond the addition of a specialized dose calculation module.

### EXAMPLE 3

In a third application, the PRIME system was adapted for use in modulated electron radiation therapy (MERT), a technique requiring the delivery of multiple electron fields from different angles and energies to achieve a highly conformal dose distribution to a complex target volume. In this case, a patient with a recurrent soft tissue sarcoma of the thigh required a three-field MERT plan, with electron energies of 12 MeV, 16 MeV, and 18 MeV, delivered from angles of 45°, 90°, and 135° relative to the patient’s longitudinal axis. The target volume was irregularly shaped, extending from the superficial fascia to a depth of 4.0 cm, and required a dose of 60 Gy with a homogeneity of ±10%. Conventional MERT using an eMLC was not feasible due to the lack of commercial availability and the prohibitive cost of system integration. Instead, a PRIME device was designed for each field, with the island blocks and apertures arranged to produce a unique intensity modulation pattern for each angle. For the 12 MeV field, a pattern of island apertures was used to deliver intensities ranging from 0% to 50%, corresponding to regions where the target was closest to the skin surface. For the 16 MeV field, a combination of island blocks and apertures was used to cover the full range of 0% to 100%, with island blocks employed in regions requiring high attenuation and apertures in regions requiring low attenuation. For the 18 MeV field, a pattern of island blocks was used to deliver intensities from 70% to 100%, corresponding to deeper regions of the target volume. Each PRIME device was individually fabricated using a combination of laser drilling and micro-stereolithography, with the island blocks composed of tungsten alloy and the apertures machined into a copper collimating insert. The devices were mounted onto custom carriers that ensured precise angular alignment with the gantry and couch position. The treatment planning system was augmented with a multi-field optimization module that simultaneously optimized the geometry of all three PRIME devices to minimize the total dose to the femur and skin while maximizing target coverage. The optimization was performed using a conjugate gradient algorithm with a convergence criterion of 0.5% change in objective function between iterations. After 67 iterations, the algorithm produced three distinct PRIME geometries, each with over 1,500 micro-elements, totaling 4,500 elements across all fields. The resulting dose distribution showed a target coverage of 98% with a homogeneity of 91% to 109%, and a maximum skin dose of 112% and a maximum femur dose of 18 Gy, well below the tolerance limits. The patient was treated over 30 fractions, with each field delivered using a single static field and the corresponding PRIME device. Verification was performed using a 2D ionization chamber array placed at the depth of the target, which confirmed a gamma pass rate of 97% at 3% / 3 mm criteria. No acute or late toxicities were observed, and the patient achieved complete remission at six-month follow-up. This example demonstrates the capability of the PRIME system to enable MERT without the need for dynamic collimation, helium-filled treatment heads, or complex beam steering systems, thereby making a previously inaccessible clinical technique available using a passive, low-cost, and clinically practical device. The invention thus provides a scalable, modular, and universally applicable solution for intensity modulation in electron therapy, applicable across all forms of conformal electron treatment.
