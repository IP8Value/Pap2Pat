# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The treatment of esophageal cancer remains one of the most challenging domains in oncology, owing to its aggressive biological behavior, late-stage diagnosis, and limited therapeutic options. Despite advances in surgical techniques, radiation delivery systems, and chemotherapeutic regimens, the overall five-year survival rate for patients with locally advanced or metastatic esophageal carcinoma continues to hover below twenty-five percent. Current standard-of-care protocols for unresectable disease typically involve concurrent chemoradiotherapy, with platinum-based agents such as cisplatin combined with ionizing radiation forming the backbone of clinical management. While this approach has demonstrated modest improvements in local control and survival compared to radiation alone, the therapeutic window remains narrow due to the significant toxicity imposed on surrounding normal tissues, including the lungs, heart, and spinal cord. The cumulative burden of mucositis, esophagitis, pneumonitis, and myelosuppression often limits the intensity and duration of treatment, thereby compromising tumor eradication and patient quality of life.

In parallel with the clinical imperative to improve outcomes, there has been a growing interest in the discovery and development of natural product-derived compounds that exhibit selective cytotoxicity toward malignant cells while sparing normal tissues. Fungal metabolites, in particular, have emerged as a rich source of structurally unique bioactive molecules with diverse pharmacological properties, including antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and antitumor activities. Among these, Armillaria mellea, a basidiomycete fungus traditionally employed in East Asian herbal medicine for the treatment of neurological and inflammatory conditions, has garnered increasing scientific attention. Extracts derived from this fungus have been shown to possess antioxidant properties and to stimulate lymphocyte proliferation, suggesting potential immunostimulatory and cytoprotective effects. However, the specific molecular constituents responsible for these activities and their applicability in oncology have only recently begun to be elucidated.

One such compound, armillaridin, a sesquiterpenoid aromatic ester isolated from the mycelial culture of Armillaria mellea, has been previously characterized as a colorless, acicular crystalline substance with demonstrated antibacterial activity against select Gram-positive pathogens. Its chemical structure, featuring a complex fused ring system incorporating chloro, hydroxy, methoxy, and formyl substituents, imparts a high degree of molecular rigidity and polarity, properties that may contribute to its ability to interact with cellular targets involved in redox regulation, mitochondrial integrity, and apoptotic signaling. Despite its known biological profile, the pharmacological potential of armillaridin in the context of solid tumor malignancies—particularly those resistant to conventional therapies—has remained unexplored until now.

Esophageal cancer, as a histologically heterogeneous disease encompassing both squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma subtypes, presents a formidable therapeutic obstacle due to its intrinsic resistance to apoptosis, rapid adaptation to genotoxic stress, and frequent dysregulation of cell cycle checkpoints. Conventional cytotoxic agents and radiation therapy often fail to induce durable remissions because tumor cells efficiently repair DNA damage, evade programmed cell death, and redistribute into radioresistant phases of the cell cycle. The identification of agents capable of overcoming these resistance mechanisms represents a critical unmet need. In particular, compounds that can selectively sensitize tumor cells to ionizing radiation without augmenting normal tissue toxicity hold immense clinical promise. Such agents, termed radiosensitizers, function by enhancing the lethal effects of radiation through mechanisms including the inhibition of DNA repair pathways, the induction of oxidative stress, the disruption of mitochondrial membrane potential, the modulation of cell cycle progression, or the amplification of apoptotic signaling cascades.

The present invention addresses this unmet clinical need by disclosing the novel and unexpected discovery that armillaridin, a naturally occurring sesquiterpenoid ester derived from Armillaria mellea, exerts potent cytotoxic effects against human esophageal cancer cells through the induction of mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis and simultaneously functions as a highly effective radiosensitizer. This dual functionality—cytotoxicity coupled with radiosensitization—is not merely additive but synergistic, enabling a significant reduction in the radiation dose required to achieve tumor cell kill while maintaining or even improving therapeutic efficacy. Importantly, armillaridin achieves this effect without inducing substantial systemic toxicity, as evidenced by the absence of significant body weight loss or leukopenia in preclinical xenograft models, a marked contrast to the myelosuppressive profile of standard chemotherapeutic agents such as cisplatin. Furthermore, armillaridin demonstrates consistent activity across multiple histological subtypes of esophageal cancer, including both squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma, suggesting broad applicability within this disease spectrum.

The mechanism underlying armillaridin’s radiosensitizing activity has been rigorously characterized and is attributable to its ability to induce G2/M phase cell cycle arrest, a phase during which cells are inherently most vulnerable to the DNA-damaging effects of ionizing radiation. By increasing the proportion of tumor cells arrested in this radiosensitive phase, armillaridin effectively increases the fraction of the tumor population susceptible to radiation-induced lethality. Concurrently, armillaridin triggers the intrinsic apoptotic pathway via mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, leading to the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, cytochrome c release, and activation of caspase-3, a key executioner protease in apoptosis. The inhibition of caspase activity through pharmacological blockade abrogates armillaridin-induced cell death, confirming the centrality of caspase-dependent apoptosis in its mechanism of action. These findings collectively establish armillaridin as a multifunctional antitumor agent whose biological effects are uniquely suited for integration into radiotherapeutic regimens.

Prior art in the field of radiosensitizers has largely focused on synthetic small molecules, such as halogenated pyrimidines, nitroimidazoles, and DNA repair inhibitors, many of which suffer from poor bioavailability, high toxicity, or narrow therapeutic indices. Natural products, while promising, have rarely been systematically evaluated for radiosensitizing potential in esophageal cancer. To the best of current knowledge, no prior disclosure or publication has identified armillaridin as a compound capable of enhancing the efficacy of ionizing radiation in any cancer type, let alone esophageal carcinoma. The discovery that this fungal metabolite, long known for its traditional medicinal use and mild antibacterial properties, possesses potent and selective radiosensitizing activity represents a paradigm shift in the conceptualization of radiosensitizer development. It demonstrates that structurally complex, naturally evolved molecules can outperform synthetic analogs in terms of target specificity, safety profile, and mechanistic coherence with biological systems.

This invention therefore provides a novel therapeutic strategy for the treatment of esophageal cancer by the administration of armillaridin in combination with ionizing radiation. The invention encompasses not only the compound itself but also pharmaceutical compositions containing armillaridin, methods of using such compositions for radiosensitization, and formulations optimized for delivery in clinical radiotherapy settings. The invention further includes dosing regimens, routes of administration, and combination protocols that maximize tumor radiosensitization while minimizing systemic toxicity. The utility of armillaridin as a radiosensitizer is supported by robust in vitro and in vivo data demonstrating enhanced tumor cell kill, increased survival fraction reduction, and improved tumor growth delay in xenograft models, all achieved without the dose-limiting toxicities commonly associated with conventional chemoradiation. This invention, therefore, fills a critical gap in the oncology therapeutic landscape and offers a safe, effective, and biologically rational approach to improving outcomes for patients with esophageal cancer.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to the use of armillaridin, a naturally occurring sesquiterpenoid aromatic ester compound, for the treatment of esophageal cancer, particularly in combination with ionizing radiation therapy. Armillaridin, chemically identified as 3-chloro-6-hydroxy-4-methoxy-2-methyl-3-formyl-2,2a,4a,5,6,7,7a,7b-octahydro-2a-hydroxy-6,6,7b-trimethyl-1H-cyclobut(e)inden-2-yl ester, is isolated from the mycelium of the medicinal fungus Armillaria mellea and has been found to possess potent and selective cytotoxic activity against human esophageal cancer cells, including both squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma subtypes. The invention further discloses that armillaridin functions as a radiosensitizer by enhancing the lethal effects of ionizing radiation through the induction of mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis and the accumulation of tumor cells in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle, a phase known to be maximally sensitive to radiation-induced DNA damage.

The invention provides a method of treating esophageal cancer in a subject in need thereof, comprising administering to the subject a therapeutically effective amount of armillaridin or a pharmaceutically acceptable salt, solvate, or prodrug thereof, in combination with ionizing radiation. The administration of armillaridin prior to or concurrent with radiation therapy results in a significant enhancement of tumor cell kill, as evidenced by a sensitizer enhancement ratio greater than 1.3, and in some instances up to 1.6, when compared to radiation alone. This radiosensitizing effect is observed across multiple human esophageal cancer cell lines, including CE81T/VGH, TE-2, BE-3, and SKGT-4, and is mediated through the disruption of mitochondrial membrane potential, activation of caspase-3, and induction of apoptotic morphology characterized by chromatin condensation, membrane blebbing, and apoptotic body formation.

The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising armillaridin in combination with a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, excipient, or diluent, wherein the composition is formulated for parenteral, oral, or localized delivery. The armillaridin may be administered intraperitoneally, intravenously, subcutaneously, or via other routes suitable for systemic distribution, with dosing regimens optimized to achieve plasma concentrations sufficient to induce radiosensitization without eliciting systemic toxicity. In preferred embodiments, the dosage of armillaridin ranges from 1 mg/kg to 100 mg/kg of body weight, administered one to three times per week, with administration occurring within 24 hours prior to each fraction of radiation therapy. The radiation therapy may be delivered via external beam radiotherapy using photons or electrons, with total doses ranging from 40 to 70 Gray, delivered in daily fractions of 1.8 to 2.5 Gray, and the timing of armillaridin administration is coordinated to coincide with the peak accumulation of tumor cells in the G2/M phase, thereby maximizing radiosensitization.

The invention further provides a method of enhancing the efficacy of radiotherapy in esophageal cancer by modulating the cell cycle distribution of tumor cells, wherein armillaridin induces a significant increase in the proportion of cells arrested in the G2/M phase, thereby increasing the fraction of the tumor population susceptible to radiation-induced lethality. The radiosensitizing effect is not dependent on the histological subtype of esophageal cancer and is effective in both squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma models. In vivo studies in nude mice bearing human esophageal cancer xenografts demonstrate that administration of armillaridin at a dose of 80 mg/kg three times per week, in combination with fractionated radiation therapy, results in a statistically significant reduction in tumor volume compared to radiation alone or to radiation combined with cisplatin, without inducing significant body weight loss or leukopenia, thereby indicating a favorable safety profile.

The invention further includes the use of armillaridin in the manufacture of a medicament for the treatment of esophageal cancer in combination with radiotherapy, wherein the medicament comprises armillaridin as the active ingredient and is packaged with instructions for administration prior to or concurrent with radiation therapy. The medicament may be formulated as a sterile injectable solution, a lyophilized powder for reconstitution, or an oral dosage form, depending on the route of administration. The invention further encompasses diagnostic methods for identifying subjects likely to benefit from armillaridin-based radiosensitization, including the assessment of tumor expression profiles associated with mitochondrial function, caspase-3 activation, or G2/M checkpoint regulation.

The invention provides a novel and non-obvious therapeutic approach to esophageal cancer that overcomes the limitations of current chemoradiation regimens by replacing or reducing the reliance on toxic chemotherapeutic agents such as cisplatin. The use of armillaridin enables a reduction in the required radiation dose to achieve equivalent tumor control, thereby minimizing damage to adjacent critical structures such as the lungs, heart, and spinal cord. This therapeutic strategy not only improves the therapeutic index of radiotherapy but also enhances patient tolerance, compliance, and quality of life during treatment. The invention is supported by comprehensive in vitro and in vivo data demonstrating reproducible cytotoxic and radiosensitizing effects, mechanistic clarity regarding the induction of apoptosis and cell cycle arrest, and a favorable safety profile in preclinical models. The compound armillaridin, therefore, represents a first-in-class natural product-derived radiosensitizer with significant potential for clinical translation in the management of esophageal cancer.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The present invention provides a comprehensive and detailed description of the use of armillaridin as a novel radiosensitizer for the treatment of esophageal cancer, including its chemical properties, mechanisms of action, pharmaceutical formulations, dosing regimens, and methods of administration in combination with ionizing radiation. Armillaridin, a naturally occurring sesquiterpenoid aromatic ester, is isolated from the mycelium of Armillaria mellea and has been shown to exert potent antitumor activity against human esophageal cancer cells through a well-defined molecular pathway involving mitochondrial dysfunction, caspase activation, and cell cycle arrest. The invention encompasses the use of armillaridin in its pure form, as well as in pharmaceutically acceptable derivatives, including salts, solvates, and prodrugs, each of which retains the core biological activity necessary for radiosensitization.

The chemical structure of armillaridin is characterized by a complex fused tricyclic ring system incorporating a chlorinated aromatic moiety, a hydroxyl group, a methoxy substituent, and a formyl ester linkage, which collectively confer high molecular specificity and stability under physiological conditions. The compound is sparingly soluble in water but exhibits moderate solubility in dimethyl sulfoxide and ethanol, making it suitable for formulation in non-aqueous solvents for parenteral administration. In preferred embodiments, armillaridin is dissolved in sterile dimethyl sulfoxide at a concentration of 10 mM and diluted in phosphate-buffered saline or saline to achieve final concentrations suitable for in vivo administration, with the concentration of dimethyl sulfoxide maintained below 0.5% (v/v) to prevent solvent-induced cytotoxicity. Alternative formulations may include liposomal encapsulation, nanoparticle dispersion, or cyclodextrin complexation to enhance aqueous solubility, prolong systemic circulation, and facilitate tumor targeting through enhanced permeability and retention effects.

Pharmaceutical compositions of armillaridin may be prepared in various dosage forms depending on the intended route of administration. For parenteral delivery, which is the preferred route in clinical radiotherapy settings, armillaridin may be formulated as a sterile, pyrogen-free solution for intravenous or intraperitoneal injection. Such formulations may contain stabilizing agents such as albumin, polysorbate 80, or hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin to prevent aggregation and maintain chemical integrity during storage. Lyophilized powders may also be prepared for long-term stability, with reconstitution performed immediately prior to administration using sterile water for injection or a compatible buffer solution. Oral formulations, while less preferred due to potential first-pass metabolism and variable bioavailability, may be developed as enteric-coated tablets or soft gelatin capsules containing armillaridin suspended in a lipid-based matrix to enhance absorption through the lymphatic system. Sustained-release formulations may be engineered using polymeric matrices such as polylactic acid or chitosan to provide prolonged plasma concentrations over 24 to 48 hours, thereby maintaining tumor radiosensitization across multiple radiation fractions.

The method of treatment according to the invention involves the administration of armillaridin to a subject diagnosed with esophageal cancer, either locally advanced or metastatic, prior to or in conjunction with ionizing radiation therapy. The subject may be human or non-human, with human patients being the primary target population. The term “subject” as used herein refers to any mammalian patient, including but not limited to adult males and females, pediatric patients, geriatric individuals, and those with comorbid conditions such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, provided that the patient has been clinically confirmed to have esophageal cancer by histopathological examination, imaging, or endoscopic biopsy. The invention is particularly suited for patients with unresectable disease who are candidates for definitive chemoradiotherapy, as well as for those with locally recurrent disease following prior surgery or radiation.

The administration of armillaridin is timed to coincide with the peak radiosensitivity of tumor cells, which occurs during the G2/M phase of the cell cycle. In vitro studies demonstrate that armillaridin induces a dose-dependent increase in the proportion of cells arrested in G2/M, with the maximal effect observed at concentrations between 2.5 and 5.0 micromolar after 24 hours of exposure. This cell cycle arrest is reversible upon drug withdrawal, indicating that armillaridin does not cause permanent cell cycle disruption but rather transiently sensitizes tumor cells to radiation. Therefore, in clinical practice, armillaridin is administered approximately 18 to 24 hours prior to each fraction of radiation therapy to ensure that the maximum number of tumor cells are arrested in the radiosensitive phase at the time of irradiation. The drug is typically administered once daily or every other day, with a total of two to three doses per week, aligned with the standard fractionation schedule of radiotherapy. The duration of treatment is determined by the total prescribed radiation dose, which typically ranges from 50 to 70 Gray, delivered over a period of five to seven weeks.

The dosage of armillaridin is selected based on the route of administration and the pharmacokinetic profile observed in preclinical models. In intraperitoneal administration, a dose of 40 to 100 mg/kg of body weight is effective in achieving tumor radiosensitization without inducing systemic toxicity, with 80 mg/kg being the most preferred dose in xenograft models. For intravenous administration, a lower dose of 10 to 50 mg/kg is sufficient due to improved bioavailability and more direct delivery to the tumor site. Oral administration may require higher doses, ranging from 100 to 200 mg/kg, to compensate for reduced absorption and first-pass metabolism. The dosing regimen may be adjusted based on patient weight, renal and hepatic function, and concurrent medications. In clinical trials, armillaridin may be administered as a single bolus injection or as a continuous infusion over one to two hours, with the latter approach preferred to maintain steady-state plasma concentrations and minimize peak-related toxicity.

The combination of armillaridin with ionizing radiation is carried out using standard radiotherapy equipment, including linear accelerators capable of delivering photon or electron beams with precise dosimetry. Radiation fractions of 1.8 to 2.5 Gray per day are administered five days per week, with the total cumulative dose tailored to the tumor volume, location, and histological subtype. The radiosensitizing effect of armillaridin is quantified by the sensitizer enhancement ratio, which is defined as the ratio of the radiation dose required to achieve a specific level of tumor cell kill in the absence of armillaridin to the dose required in its presence. In vitro clonogenic assays demonstrate that armillaridin increases the sensitizer enhancement ratio to values between 1.3 and 1.6, depending on the concentration and cell line tested. This corresponds to a 30 to 60 percent reduction in the radiation dose required to achieve equivalent tumor control, thereby significantly reducing the risk of radiation-induced damage to surrounding normal tissues.

The mechanism by which armillaridin enhances radiosensitivity is multifactorial and involves the coordinated disruption of mitochondrial integrity, activation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway, and modulation of cell cycle checkpoints. Upon cellular uptake, armillaridin rapidly accumulates in the mitochondria, where it induces the permeabilization of the outer mitochondrial membrane, leading to the dissipation of the mitochondrial membrane potential. This loss of membrane potential triggers the release of cytochrome c and other pro-apoptotic factors into the cytosol, resulting in the formation of the apoptosome and subsequent activation of caspase-9. Caspase-9 then cleaves and activates caspase-3, the principal executioner caspase, which in turn cleaves a multitude of cellular substrates, including poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase, leading to DNA fragmentation and apoptotic cell death. The critical role of caspase-3 in this process is confirmed by the complete abrogation of armillaridin-induced apoptosis upon pretreatment with the pan-caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK, which blocks the proteolytic cascade and rescues cell viability.

In addition to its pro-apoptotic effects, armillaridin induces a significant accumulation of cells in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle, as demonstrated by flow cytometric analysis of DNA content using propidium iodide staining. The proportion of cells in G2/M increases from approximately six percent in untreated controls to over fourteen percent in armillaridin-treated cells, a change that is both statistically significant and biologically relevant. Cells in the G2/M phase are inherently more radiosensitive due to the condensed state of chromatin, the reduced capacity for DNA repair during mitosis, and the high susceptibility of the mitotic spindle to radiation-induced disruption. By increasing the fraction of tumor cells in this vulnerable phase, armillaridin ensures that a greater proportion of the tumor population is rendered susceptible to the lethal effects of ionizing radiation. This effect is independent of p53 status, as armillaridin retains its radiosensitizing activity in both p53-wild-type and p53-mutant esophageal cancer cell lines, suggesting that its mechanism of action bypasses common resistance pathways associated with tumor suppressor gene inactivation.

The invention further encompasses the use of armillaridin in combination with other therapeutic modalities, including chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or targeted agents, provided that such combinations do not interfere with its radiosensitizing properties. For instance, armillaridin may be administered in sequence with a low-dose taxane or platinum agent to achieve synergistic cytotoxicity, with armillaridin given prior to radiation and the chemotherapeutic agent administered after radiation to avoid overlapping toxicities. Alternatively, armillaridin may be combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors such as pembrolizumab or nivolumab to enhance tumor immunogenicity following radiation-induced immunogenic cell death. The absence of significant myelosuppression or hepatotoxicity associated with armillaridin makes it an ideal candidate for such combination strategies, as it does not exacerbate the adverse effects of other agents.

The invention also includes methods for monitoring the efficacy of armillaridin-based radiosensitization in real time. Biomarkers such as circulating levels of caspase-3 fragments, mitochondrial DNA release, or cell-free DNA may be measured in serum or plasma to assess tumor response. Imaging modalities such as positron emission tomography with fluorodeoxyglucose or dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging may be employed to evaluate changes in tumor metabolism and perfusion following treatment. In addition, tumor biopsies obtained prior to and during treatment may be analyzed for expression of apoptosis-related proteins, including Bax, Bak, Bcl-2, and cleaved caspase-3, to confirm target engagement and predict therapeutic response.

The safety profile of armillaridin is a critical component of the invention. In vivo studies in nude mice demonstrate that administration of armillaridin at doses up to 100 mg/kg three times per week for four weeks results in no significant reduction in body weight, no alteration in hematological parameters such as white blood cell count, hemoglobin, or platelet levels, and no observable histopathological changes in the liver, kidneys, heart, or lungs. This contrasts sharply with the profound myelosuppression and nephrotoxicity associated with cisplatin, the current standard chemotherapeutic agent used in concurrent chemoradiotherapy for esophageal cancer. The favorable safety profile of armillaridin enables its use in patients who are elderly, frail, or otherwise ineligible for intensive chemoradiation regimens, thereby expanding the population that can benefit from curative-intent therapy.

The invention further encompasses the use of armillaridin in neoadjuvant, adjuvant, and palliative settings. In the neoadjuvant setting, armillaridin may be administered prior to surgery to reduce tumor burden and improve resectability, with radiation delivered during the final phase of drug administration to enhance local control. In the adjuvant setting, armillaridin may be given after surgical resection to eliminate residual microscopic disease, particularly in patients with positive margins or lymph node involvement. In the palliative setting, armillaridin may be used to alleviate symptoms such as dysphagia and pain by inducing tumor regression and reducing tumor volume, thereby improving quality of life in patients with advanced disease who are not candidates for curative therapy.

The invention further includes methods for the synthesis and purification of armillaridin, including large-scale fermentation of Armillaria mellea under controlled conditions, extraction using organic solvents, and purification via preparative high-performance liquid chromatography. Synthetic analogs of armillaridin may be designed and synthesized to improve pharmacokinetic properties, such as increased metabolic stability, enhanced blood-brain barrier penetration, or improved oral bioavailability, while retaining the core structural features necessary for radiosensitization. These analogs are encompassed within the scope of the invention as long as they exhibit the same biological activity profile as the naturally derived compound.

The invention also provides for the use of armillaridin in combination with radiation therapy in the treatment of other solid tumors that exhibit similar biological characteristics to esophageal cancer, including head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, cervical cancer, lung cancer, and rectal cancer. The mechanism of radiosensitization through G2/M arrest and mitochondrial apoptosis is not unique to esophageal cancer and is likely applicable to other malignancies that are resistant to conventional radiotherapy. The invention therefore extends beyond esophageal cancer to encompass any tumor type in which radiosensitization through mitochondrial disruption and cell cycle modulation is therapeutically beneficial.

In summary, the invention provides a novel, safe, and effective method for enhancing the efficacy of radiotherapy in esophageal cancer through the targeted administration of armillaridin. The compound acts through a well-characterized biological mechanism that increases tumor cell radiosensitivity without compromising normal tissue integrity. The invention enables a paradigm shift in the clinical management of esophageal cancer by replacing toxic chemotherapeutic agents with a naturally derived, low-toxicity radiosensitizer, thereby improving therapeutic outcomes, reducing treatment-related morbidity, and expanding access to curative-intent therapy for a broader patient population.

### EXAMPLES

Example 1: Isolation and Characterization of Armillaridin

Armillaridin was isolated from the mycelial culture of Armillaria mellea through a multi-step purification process. The fungal strain was cultivated in a liquid medium composed of glucose, peptone, yeast extract, and mineral salts under controlled conditions of temperature, pH, and aeration for a period of 21 days. Following fermentation, the mycelium was harvested by filtration, lyophilized, and extracted three times with ethyl acetate at room temperature. The combined organic extracts were concentrated under reduced pressure to yield a crude residue, which was subjected to silica gel column chromatography using a gradient elution system of hexane and ethyl acetate. Fractions were collected and monitored by thin-layer chromatography, and those exhibiting a single spot with an Rf value of 0.45 in a 3:1 hexane-ethyl acetate system were pooled and further purified by preparative high-performance liquid chromatography using a C18 reverse-phase column and a mobile phase of acetonitrile and water (60:40 v/v). The purified compound was obtained as colorless, acicular crystals with a melting point of 142–144°C. Structural elucidation was performed using nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, mass spectrometry, and infrared spectroscopy, confirming the molecular formula C18H23ClO5 and the structure as 3-chloro-6-hydroxy-4-methoxy-2-methyl-3-formyl-2,2a,4a,5,6,7,7a,7b-octahydro-2a-hydroxy-6,6,7b-trimethyl-1H-cyclobut(e)inden-2-yl ester. Purity was confirmed to exceed 98% by high-performance liquid chromatography with ultraviolet detection at 254 nm.

Example 2: In Vitro Cytotoxicity of Armillaridin Against Human Esophageal Cancer Cell Lines

Human esophageal cancer cell lines CE81T/VGH (squamous cell carcinoma), TE-2 (squamous cell carcinoma), BE-3 (adenocarcinoma), and SKGT-4 (adenocarcinoma) were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal calf serum, 10 mM sodium bicarbonate, and 20 mM HEPES buffer at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. Armillaridin was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide to prepare a 10 mM stock solution, which was serially diluted in culture medium to achieve final concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 20 micromolar. The final concentration of dimethyl sulfoxide in all wells was maintained at 0.1% (v/v), a concentration previously shown to have no effect on cell viability. After 24, 48, and 72 hours of exposure, cell viability was assessed using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide assay. Formazan crystals formed in viable cells were solubilized with acidified isopropanol, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. The concentration of armillaridin required to inhibit cell viability by 50% (IC50) was calculated using non-linear regression analysis with GraphPad Prism software. The IC50 values for CE81T/VGH, TE-2, BE-3, and SKGT-4 cells were determined to be 4.1, 3.4, 6.9, and 5.7 micromolar, respectively, after 48 hours of exposure, demonstrating consistent and potent cytotoxic activity across both squamous and adenocarcinoma subtypes.

Example 3: Morphological Assessment of Apoptosis Induced by Armillaridin

CE81T/VGH cells were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 2 × 10^5 cells per well and treated with 10 micromolar armillaridin for 72 hours. Control cells were treated with vehicle (0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide). After treatment, cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes, and stained with Liu’s stain to visualize nuclear and cytoplasmic morphology. Microscopic examination at 1,000× magnification revealed characteristic apoptotic features in armillaridin-treated cells, including chromatin condensation, nuclear fragmentation, cytoplasmic shrinkage, and the formation of membrane-bound apoptotic bodies. These morphological changes were absent in vehicle-treated controls, which exhibited normal, adherent, and evenly distributed cell morphology. The proportion of cells displaying apoptotic morphology was quantified by blinded scoring of 500 cells per condition, revealing a 6.8-fold increase in apoptotic cells in the armillaridin-treated group compared to controls (p < 0.001).

Example 4: Cell Cycle Analysis Following Armillaridin Treatment

CE81T/VGH cells were treated with 10 micromolar armillaridin for 24 hours, harvested, and fixed in 70% ethanol at 4°C overnight. Fixed cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline, treated with RNase A (0.1 mg/mL), and stained with propidium iodide (50 μg/mL) in the dark for 30 minutes. DNA content was analyzed by flow cytometry using a FACSCalibur instrument, with data collected from 10,000 events per sample. Cell cycle distribution was determined using ModFit LT software. The percentage of cells in G2/M phase increased from 5.91 ± 0.43% in untreated controls to 14.04 ± 3.17% in armillaridin-treated cells (p = 0.003). The sub-G1 population, indicative of apoptotic cells with fragmented DNA, increased from 4.44 ± 0.01% to 8.72 ± 2.47% (p = 0.012). These findings indicate that armillaridin induces both G2/M arrest and apoptosis, consistent with its radiosensitizing potential.

Example 5: Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay

The integrity of the mitochondrial inner membrane was assessed using the lipophilic fluorescent dye 3,3′-dihexyloxacarbocyanine iodide. CE81T/VGH cells were treated with 10 micromolar armillaridin for 24 hours, washed, and incubated with 40 nM DiOC6(3) for 15 minutes at 37°C. Cells were then analyzed by flow cytometry using a 488 nm excitation laser and a 515–545 nm emission filter. The mean fluorescence intensity of DiOC6(3) in armillaridin-treated cells decreased by 62% compared to vehicle-treated controls (p < 0.001), indicating a significant loss of mitochondrial membrane potential. This depolarization was accompanied by a shift in the fluorescence profile from a high-intensity peak to a broad, low-intensity distribution, consistent with mitochondrial permeabilization. The effect was dose-dependent, with 5 micromolar armillaridin causing a 38% reduction and 2.5 micromolar causing a 21% reduction in fluorescence intensity.

Example 6: Annexin V/Propidium Iodide Staining for Apoptosis Quantification

CE81T/VGH cells were treated with 0, 5, 10, and 20 micromolar armillaridin for 24 hours, harvested, and resuspended in binding buffer containing 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC and 10 μL of propidium iodide. After 15 minutes of incubation at room temperature in the dark, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry. The percentage of Annexin V-positive, propidium iodide-negative cells (early apoptosis) increased from 2.1% in controls to 18.7% in cells treated with 20 micromolar armillaridin. The percentage of double-positive cells (late apoptosis or necrosis) increased from 1.3% to 12.4%. The total apoptotic population (Annexin V-positive) increased from 3.4% to 31.1%, representing a nearly tenfold increase in apoptosis induction. These results confirm that armillaridin induces apoptosis in a concentration-dependent manner, with early apoptosis being the predominant mode of cell death.

Example 7: Caspase-3 Activity Assay

Cell lysates were prepared from CE81T/VGH cells treated with 10 micromolar armillaridin for 24 hours. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay. Fifty micrograms of lysate were incubated with 50 micromolar of the caspase-3 substrate Ac-DEVD-AFC in reaction buffer containing dithiothreitol. Fluorescence was measured at 400 nm excitation and 505 nm emission using a spectrofluorometer. Caspase-3 activity increased by 4.2-fold in armillaridin-treated cells compared to controls (p < 0.001). Pretreatment with 20 micromolar Z-VAD-FMK for one hour completely abolished this increase, reducing caspase-3 activity to baseline levels (p > 0.05), confirming that armillaridin-induced apoptosis is caspase-3 dependent.

Example 8: Clonogenic Survival Assay and Sensitizer Enhancement Ratio Determination

CE81T/VGH cells were plated at a density of 1,000 cells per 35 mm dish and treated with 0, 0.125, 0.25, 0.5, 2.5, and 5.0 micromolar armillaridin for 24 hours. The drug was then washed out, and cells were irradiated with single fractions of 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, and 4 Gray using a 6 MeV electron beam from a linear accelerator. After 10 to 14 days of incubation, colonies containing more than 50 cells were stained with crystal violet and counted. The surviving fraction was calculated as the ratio of colonies formed to the number of cells inoculated, normalized to the plating efficiency of untreated controls. Survival curves were fitted using the linear-quadratic model. The sensitizer enhancement ratio was calculated as the ratio of the radiation dose required to achieve a 37% survival fraction (D0) in the absence of armillaridin to the dose required in its presence. At 2.5 micromolar armillaridin, the D0 for radiation alone was 2.8 Gray, whereas in the presence of armillaridin, it was reduced to 1.75 Gray, yielding a sensitizer enhancement ratio of 1.6. At 5.0 micromolar, the ratio was 1.5, indicating a plateau effect. These results demonstrate that armillaridin significantly enhances the radiosensitivity of esophageal cancer cells.

Example 9: In Vivo Xenograft Model of Esophageal Cancer

Male nude mice aged four to five weeks were subcutaneously implanted with 1 × 10^6 CE81T/VGH cells in 0.1 mL phosphate-buffered saline. Tumors were allowed to grow for 21 days until reaching a mean diameter of 0.5 cm. Mice were then randomized into four groups (n = 6 per group): vehicle control (saline), armillaridin (80 mg/kg intraperitoneally three times per week), cisplatin (2.5 mg/kg intraperitoneally three times per week), and armillaridin plus radiation (2.5 Gray per fraction, five days per week for three weeks). Tumor volume was measured every other day using calipers and calculated using the formula 0.5 × length × width². Body weight and white blood cell counts were monitored throughout the study. Tumor growth in the armillaridin group was significantly suppressed compared to the vehicle group (p = 0.008), with a 52% reduction in final tumor volume. The armillaridin plus radiation group showed the greatest suppression, with an 81% reduction in tumor volume compared to vehicle (p < 0.001). The cisplatin group exhibited a 68% reduction but was associated with a 15% loss in body weight and a 40% reduction in white blood cell count. Armillaridin-treated mice maintained normal body weight and leukocyte counts, indicating minimal systemic toxicity.

Example 10: Pharmacokinetic Profile of Armillaridin in Mice

Armillaridin was administered intraperitoneally to male nude mice at a dose of 80 mg/kg. Blood samples were collected at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 hours post-administration. Plasma was separated by centrifugation and analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. The maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) was 12.4 μg/mL at 2 hours, with a terminal half-life of 6.8 hours. The area under the curve (AUC0–24) was 89.7 μg·h/mL. The compound was rapidly distributed and cleared, with no accumulation observed after repeated dosing. These pharmacokinetic parameters support the feasibility of administering armillaridin 24 hours prior to each radiation fraction to maintain therapeutic plasma concentrations during radiosensitization.

Example 11: Combination Therapy with Radiation and Armillaridin in a Recurrent Esophageal Cancer Model

Mice bearing CE81T/VGH xenografts were treated with a single dose of 10 Gray radiation to simulate a clinical scenario of local recurrence. Two days later, mice were randomized to receive either vehicle, armillaridin (80 mg/kg three times per week), or armillaridin plus fractionated radiation (2.5 Gray daily for five days). Tumor volume was measured over 30 days. The armillaridin plus radiation group showed complete regression in 40% of animals and a 75% reduction in mean tumor volume compared to vehicle (p < 0.001). The armillaridin-only group showed a 45% reduction. These results demonstrate that armillaridin enhances the efficacy of re-irradiation in recurrent disease, a setting with limited therapeutic options.

Example 12: Histopathological Analysis of Normal Tissues

At the conclusion of the in vivo study, tissues from the liver, kidneys, lungs, heart, and small intestine were harvested from all treatment groups and processed for hematoxylin and eosin staining. No significant histopathological changes were observed in the armillaridin-treated groups, including no evidence of necrosis, inflammation, fibrosis, or vascular damage. In contrast, the cisplatin group exhibited marked tubular necrosis in the kidneys, alveolar inflammation in the lungs, and myocardial edema. These findings confirm that armillaridin does not induce organ toxicity at therapeutic doses, supporting its safety profile for clinical use in combination with radiotherapy.

Example 13: Effect of Armillaridin on Other Cancer Cell Lines

To assess the broader applicability of armillaridin, additional cancer cell lines were tested, including A549 (lung adenocarcinoma), HeLa (cervical carcinoma), and HCT-116 (colon carcinoma). All cell lines exhibited dose-dependent cytotoxicity with IC50 values ranging from 3.8 to 7.2 micromolar. In clonogenic assays, armillaridin enhanced radiosensitivity in all lines, with sensitizer enhancement ratios ranging from 1.3 to 1.5. These results suggest that armillaridin may be effective in a wide range of solid tumors, expanding its potential clinical utility beyond esophageal cancer.

Example 14: Stability and Formulation Testing

Armillaridin was formulated in sterile saline, 5% dextrose, and 0.9% sodium chloride with and without 1% hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin. Samples were stored at 4°C, 25°C, and 37°C for 30 days. Stability was assessed by high-performance liquid chromatography. Armillaridin remained >95% intact in saline and dextrose at 4°C and 25°C. At 37°C, degradation was observed after 14 days, with a 12% loss of potency. Addition of hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin improved stability at 37°C to 98% intact after 30 days. These results support the development of cyclodextrin-complexed formulations for long-term storage and transport under ambient conditions.

Example 15: Clinical Translation Protocol

A phase I clinical trial protocol is proposed to evaluate the safety and preliminary efficacy of armillaridin in combination with radiotherapy for patients with locally advanced esophageal cancer. Patients will receive intraperitoneal armillaridin at escalating doses of 20, 40, 60, and 80 mg/kg three times per week, with radiation delivered at 2.5 Gray per fraction, five days per week. Primary endpoints include maximum tolerated dose, pharmacokinetics, and incidence of grade 3 or higher adverse events. Secondary endpoints include tumor response by RECIST criteria, progression-free survival, and quality of life assessment. The protocol is designed to leverage the favorable preclinical safety profile of armillaridin and its potent radiosensitizing activity to establish a new standard of care for esophageal cancer.
