# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of aluminum production, specifically to the protection of carbon anodes used in Hall-Héroult electrolytic cells against oxidative degradation during operation. More particularly, the invention concerns a method for substantially reducing the air reactivity and dust generation of carbon anodes through the controlled impregnation of boron-containing compounds into the porous structure of the anode material. The invention further relates to the resulting carbon anode product, which exhibits significantly enhanced resistance to oxidation at elevated temperatures encountered during aluminum smelting, typically between 400 and 600 degrees Celsius, while maintaining the structural integrity and electrical conductivity required for efficient electrolytic performance. The method described herein enables the uniform distribution of a protective boron oxide layer throughout the internal pore network of the anode, thereby transforming the mechanism of oxidation from surface-limited consumption to a slow, volumetric reaction that minimizes particulate emission and extends anode service life. This innovation addresses a long-standing industrial problem in aluminum production—excessive anode consumption due to air oxidation—which has persisted despite decades of research into surface coatings, material substitutions, and process modifications. The invention is particularly suited for application in large-scale industrial anodes, where conventional surface treatments have proven ineffective due to their inability to penetrate deeply into the porous matrix, and where boron doping at high concentrations has been deemed economically and metallurgically impractical due to contamination risks in the aluminum melt. By leveraging the solubility of boron oxide in aqueous media and its ability to precipitate within the anode’s microstructure during drying, the invention provides a scalable, cost-effective, and environmentally compatible solution that fundamentally alters the oxidation kinetics of carbon anodes without altering their bulk composition or requiring high-temperature processing.

## BACKGROUND

In the conventional Hall-Héroult process for aluminum production, carbon anodes are subjected to extreme thermal and chemical environments during electrolysis. The anodes, composed primarily of calcined petroleum coke, recycled anode butts, and coal tar pitch as a binder, serve as both the electrical conductor and the reactant in the electrochemical reduction of alumina to metallic aluminum. Despite their critical function, carbon anodes are inherently susceptible to oxidative degradation when exposed to ambient air during handling, preheating, and the intervals between anode changes in the electrolytic cell. At temperatures ranging from 400 to 600 degrees Celsius, which are typical for the exposed surfaces of anodes during operation, oxygen from the surrounding atmosphere reacts exothermically with the carbon matrix, producing carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide according to well-established thermodynamic pathways. These reactions are parasitic in nature, consuming valuable carbon material without contributing to aluminum production, thereby increasing operational costs and reducing energy efficiency. The rate of this air reactivity is among the most significant contributors to overall anode overconsumption, often accounting for more than 30% of total carbon loss in industrial operations. The binder phase, composed of coal tar pitch and fine carbon particles, is particularly vulnerable to oxidation due to its higher reactivity compared to the larger, more graphitic coke grains. As the binder oxidizes preferentially, it leads to the mechanical disintegration of the anode surface, resulting in the release of fine carbon dust—a phenomenon known as dusting—which not only exacerbates material loss but also poses environmental and health hazards, contaminates the electrolyte, and interferes with current distribution within the cell.

Numerous attempts have been made over the years to mitigate this problem. Surface coatings have been proposed as a physical barrier to oxygen diffusion. For instance, alumina-based sprays have been developed and tested, but their long-term durability under thermal cycling and mechanical abrasion has proven inadequate. Similarly, the practice of applying a layer of solidified cryolite bath over freshly installed anodes has been adopted in some smelters, yet this method offers only transient protection due to the porous and brittle nature of the solidified bath, which allows oxygen to permeate over time. Other approaches have focused on modifying the intrinsic reactivity of the carbon material itself. Doping with elements such as phosphorus and boron has been explored, but phosphorus is strictly prohibited due to its detrimental effect on current efficiency and aluminum purity, as it readily reduces in the molten electrolyte and contaminates the metal product. Boron, by contrast, has been considered a viable alternative due to its lower solubility in aluminum and its potential to form a protective oxide layer. However, prior attempts to introduce boron into anodes via high-temperature doping with elemental boron under inert atmospheres have been deemed economically unfeasible, as the required concentrations—often exceeding 1000 parts per million by weight—would be prohibitively expensive for a commodity material such as carbon anodes. Furthermore, the baking temperatures used in anode manufacturing (approximately 1200 degrees Celsius) are insufficient to induce significant graphitization or lattice substitution of boron into the carbon structure, thereby limiting the effectiveness of such doping strategies. Alternative methods involving the incorporation of boron-containing compounds into the pitch matrix prior to baking have also been investigated, but these approaches suffer from poor dispersion, incomplete reaction, and the risk of volatilization during the high-temperature curing process.

Patents issued by Moltech and Alcan have described the use of aqueous boron compound suspensions for impregnating anode surfaces. These methods involve applying a liquid suspension of boron oxide or boron-containing salts, followed by drying to precipitate the protective agent within the pores. While laboratory-scale tests on small cylindrical samples (typically 50 mm in diameter and 100 mm in length) have demonstrated promising reductions in mass loss—often exceeding 80% compared to untreated controls—these results have not translated into industrial adoption. The primary reason for this disconnect lies in the fundamental difference between laboratory-scale testing and real-world anode geometry. Small samples, due to their high surface-area-to-volume ratio, allow for rapid and uniform penetration of the impregnating solution throughout their entire volume. In contrast, industrial anodes are massive, rectangular blocks measuring up to one meter in length, with cross-sectional areas exceeding 0.5 square meters and volumes exceeding 100 liters. Under such conditions, the diffusion of aqueous solutions into the dense, heterogeneous pore network is severely limited by capillary forces, viscosity, and drying kinetics. As a result, conventional impregnation techniques applied to full-sized anodes yield only superficial coverage, with the protective layer confined to the outermost millimeters of the surface. This shallow penetration fails to protect the bulk of the anode, which continues to oxidize from within, leading to internal degradation, structural weakening, and eventual dusting. Moreover, existing methods have not addressed the critical issue of reaction kinetics over time. Most laboratory evaluations rely on endpoint mass loss measurements after fixed durations, providing no insight into the temporal evolution of oxidation, the development of reaction fronts, or the preservation of mechanical integrity. Consequently, the true protective capability of a treatment cannot be assessed, and the risk of catastrophic failure during prolonged service remains unquantified. The absence of microstructural characterization techniques such as X-ray computed tomography in prior studies has further obscured the mechanistic understanding of how boron impregnation alters the oxidation pathway. Without this understanding, optimization of impregnation parameters—such as solution concentration, temperature, duration, and drying profile—has been largely empirical, leading to inconsistent results and limited scalability. The present invention overcomes these limitations by introducing a novel methodology for deep, uniform, and controllable impregnation of boron oxide into the internal pore structure of large carbon anodes, coupled with a comprehensive analytical framework that quantifies the transformation of oxidation from a surface-dominated to a volumetrically controlled process, thereby enabling industrial implementation with predictable and reproducible outcomes.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for reducing the air reactivity of carbon anodes used in aluminum electrolysis cells by impregnating the anode with an aqueous solution of a boron-containing compound, wherein the solution is applied under controlled conditions of temperature, duration, and drying to achieve a uniform distribution of boron oxide throughout the anode’s internal pore network. The method comprises the steps of preparing an aqueous solution containing boron oxide or a precursor capable of forming boron oxide upon drying, immersing a carbon anode in the solution for a duration sufficient to permit penetration into the anode’s pore structure, removing the anode from the solution, and drying the anode at a temperature and for a time sufficient to precipitate boron oxide within the pores without causing significant volatilization or structural degradation. The carbon anode is characterized by a porous structure formed from calcined petroleum coke, recycled anode butts, and coal tar pitch, baked at temperatures between 1000 and 1300 degrees Celsius. The aqueous solution is prepared at a concentration ranging from 0.1 to 5.0 weight percent boron oxide equivalent, and the immersion is conducted at a temperature between 30 and 100 degrees Celsius for a period of 5 to 60 minutes. Following immersion, the anode is dried in a controlled atmosphere at a temperature between 100 and 180 degrees Celsius for a duration of 2 to 12 hours, such that the boron oxide precipitates as a continuous, nanoscale film lining the internal surfaces of the pores. The resulting anode exhibits a reduction in air reactivity of at least 75% compared to an untreated anode when subjected to isothermal oxidation at 500 degrees Celsius in a flowing air stream, with a mass loss rate of less than 0.5 percent per hour over extended exposure periods. Crucially, the oxidation of the impregnated anode occurs primarily within the interior of the anode rather than at its surface, as evidenced by the preservation of the anode’s external dimensions and structural integrity after prolonged exposure, in contrast to untreated anodes which exhibit significant surface shrinkage and disintegration into dust. The method further includes the step of characterizing the anode using X-ray computed tomography to confirm the uniformity of boron oxide distribution and to monitor the evolution of porosity during oxidation, thereby enabling process optimization based on microstructural response rather than endpoint mass loss alone. The invention also encompasses the carbon anode produced by this method, wherein the boron oxide is present in an amount sufficient to reduce the air reactivity by at least 75% and wherein the anode retains its original electrical resistivity, mechanical strength, and dimensional stability. The invention further provides a system for the continuous impregnation of anodes in an industrial setting, comprising a tank containing the aqueous boron solution, a lifting mechanism for submerging and extracting anodes, a drying chamber with controlled temperature and airflow, and a monitoring system for tracking boron loading and anode mass change during impregnation. The invention eliminates the need for high-temperature doping, eliminates the use of hazardous or expensive boron precursors, and avoids the application of brittle, non-adherent surface coatings. It achieves a fundamental shift in the mechanism of oxidation protection, transforming the anode from a material that burns from the outside in to one that reacts slowly and uniformly from within, thereby minimizing dust generation, extending anode life, reducing material consumption, and improving the environmental and economic performance of aluminum production.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention is directed to a method for the protection of carbon anodes against oxidative degradation during aluminum electrolysis by the controlled impregnation of boron oxide into the internal pore structure of the anode. Unlike previous approaches that rely on surface coatings or high-concentration doping, the present invention exploits the solubility of boron oxide in water to enable deep, uniform penetration of the protective agent throughout the anode’s complex, heterogeneous microstructure. The carbon anode, as used herein, refers to a baked anode composed of a composite matrix of calcined petroleum coke, recycled anode butts, and coal tar pitch, cured at temperatures between 1000 and 1300 degrees Celsius to form a rigid, porous structure with a specific surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution characteristic of industrial anodes. The anode may be in the form of a rectangular block, a cylindrical core, or any other geometry used in industrial electrolysis cells, provided that it contains sufficient porosity to permit the infiltration of the aqueous solution. The key innovation lies not in the chemical identity of the boron compound, but in the precise control of the impregnation and drying process to ensure that the boron oxide precipitates not merely on the surface, but throughout the entire volume of the anode’s pore network. This is achieved by selecting an aqueous solution of boron oxide or a boron precursor—such as boric acid, sodium tetraborate, or boron trioxide dissolved in water—that remains stable during immersion and does not induce undesirable chemical reactions with the carbon matrix. The solution concentration is maintained between 0.1 and 5.0 weight percent, with a preferred range of 0.5 to 2.0 weight percent, to ensure sufficient boron loading without inducing excessive viscosity or osmotic pressure that could hinder penetration. The solution is maintained at a temperature between 30 and 100 degrees Celsius during immersion, with optimal results obtained at temperatures between 60 and 90 degrees Celsius, as elevated temperatures enhance the diffusion rate of the solution into the pores without causing premature evaporation or decomposition of the boron species. The immersion time is carefully calibrated to allow for complete wetting and capillary-driven infiltration of the solution into the anode’s pore structure. For small laboratory samples, immersion times of 10 to 30 minutes are sufficient; however, for full-sized industrial anodes, immersion times of 15 to 60 minutes are required to achieve uniform penetration to depths exceeding 20 millimeters from the surface. The duration is determined empirically based on the anode’s porosity, density, and pore size distribution, and may be optimized using real-time monitoring of solution uptake or mass gain during immersion. Following immersion, the anode is removed from the solution and allowed to drain for a brief period to remove excess surface liquid. It is then transferred to a drying chamber where it is subjected to a controlled thermal profile. The drying temperature is maintained between 100 and 180 degrees Celsius, with a preferred range of 120 to 160 degrees Celsius, to ensure the gradual dehydration of boron hydroxide—formed upon dissolution of the boron precursor—to boron oxide without inducing thermal cracking, volatilization, or phase transformation that could compromise the protective layer. The drying duration is typically between 2 and 12 hours, depending on the anode’s size and moisture content, and is conducted in a low-humidity, low-airflow environment to prevent surface crust formation and to ensure uniform moisture removal from the interior. The drying process is critical: if performed too rapidly, a dense, impermeable crust of boron oxide forms on the surface, blocking further penetration of moisture and leaving the interior unprotected; if performed too slowly, the boron species may migrate toward the surface due to capillary flow, resulting in non-uniform distribution. The invention therefore encompasses a precisely controlled drying protocol that balances the rate of solvent evaporation with the rate of boron oxide precipitation, ensuring that the protective layer forms continuously throughout the pore network rather than as a discontinuous surface film. The resulting anode exhibits a boron oxide loading of between 0.05 and 0.30 grams per square centimeter of surface area, with a preferred range of 0.10 to 0.20 grams per square centimeter, corresponding to a total boron content of 50 to 300 parts per million by weight in the anode matrix. This loading is sufficient to inhibit oxidation without introducing excessive boron into the electrolyte during subsequent anode use, as the boron oxide remains chemically bound within the carbon matrix and is not readily soluble in the molten cryolite bath. The oxidation resistance of the impregnated anode is demonstrated by its behavior under isothermal air exposure at 500 degrees Celsius, where the mass loss rate is reduced by at least 75% compared to an untreated anode, and in many cases exceeds 90%. The oxidation mechanism is fundamentally altered: whereas untreated anodes undergo rapid surface consumption, leading to shrinkage, cracking, and dusting, the impregnated anodes exhibit minimal dimensional change and maintain their structural integrity even after prolonged exposure. X-ray computed tomography reveals that the oxidation of the impregnated anode proceeds from the interior outward, with the pore structure gradually enlarging as carbon is consumed, while the outer surface remains intact and uneroded. This indicates that the boron oxide layer effectively suppresses the intrinsic reactivity of the carbon matrix, slowing the oxidation kinetics to such an extent that oxygen diffusion through the pores becomes the rate-limiting step rather than the surface reaction itself. As a result, the CO₂/CO ratio within the pores remains relatively stable, allowing oxygen to penetrate deeply into the anode and react uniformly throughout its volume. This volumetric oxidation mode prevents the formation of a porous, friable surface layer that would otherwise flake off as dust, thereby dramatically reducing particulate emissions. The invention further includes a method for validating the effectiveness of the impregnation process through non-destructive microstructural analysis. Prior to and after oxidation testing, anodes are scanned using X-ray computed tomography to generate three-dimensional maps of porosity, density, and spatial distribution of the boron oxide phase. These scans enable the quantification of pore volume evolution, the identification of regions of incomplete impregnation, and the correlation of boron loading with oxidation resistance. The data obtained from these scans are used to refine the impregnation parameters—such as solution concentration, immersion time, and drying profile—for each batch of anodes, ensuring consistent performance across industrial production. The invention also encompasses the anode product itself, which is characterized by a uniform distribution of boron oxide within the pore network, a surface that remains dimensionally stable during oxidation, and a mass loss rate under air exposure at 500 degrees Celsius of less than 0.5 percent per hour over exposure durations exceeding 100 hours. The electrical resistivity of the impregnated anode remains within 5% of that of the untreated anode, confirming that the boron oxide layer does not interfere with electronic conduction through the carbon matrix. The mechanical strength, as measured by compressive strength or modulus of rupture, is preserved or even slightly enhanced due to the filling of microcracks and voids by the precipitated boron oxide. The anode may be used in any conventional Hall-Héroult cell without modification to the operating parameters, including current density, anode-cathode distance, or electrolyte composition. The invention further includes a system for continuous industrial implementation, comprising a series of immersion tanks, drying ovens, and automated handling equipment designed to process anodes at a rate consistent with the production schedule of an aluminum smelter. The system includes sensors to monitor the mass gain of the anode during immersion, the temperature profile during drying, and the humidity of the drying environment, with feedback control to adjust parameters in real time. The system may be integrated into existing anode production lines, with impregnation occurring after baking and before storage or installation in the electrolysis cell. The method is compatible with anodes of varying compositions, including those with high recycled content, and does not require additional binders, surfactants, or chemical additives. The invention thus provides a scalable, cost-effective, and environmentally sustainable solution to the long-standing problem of anode overconsumption, offering substantial economic benefits through reduced carbon consumption, lower dust emissions, extended anode service life, and improved operational efficiency.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1: Anode Preparation

A series of carbon anode samples were prepared from a commercial industrial anode block composed of 65 weight percent calcined petroleum coke, 22 weight percent recycled anode butts, and 13 weight percent coal tar pitch, baked at 1200 degrees Celsius. The anode block was sectioned using a diamond-core saw to produce cylindrical specimens with a diameter of 50 millimeters and a height of 30 millimeters, yielding individual samples with a volume of approximately 59 cubic centimeters and a mass of approximately 100 grams. Each sample was carefully labeled and stored in a dry environment to prevent moisture absorption prior to impregnation. A boron-containing aqueous solution was prepared by dissolving boric acid (H₃BO₃) in deionized water to achieve a concentration of 2.0 weight percent, corresponding to a boron oxide equivalent of approximately 1.7 weight percent, based on the stoichiometric conversion of boric acid to boron oxide upon dehydration. The solution was stirred continuously for 30 minutes at room temperature to ensure complete dissolution and homogeneity. The solution was then heated to 75 degrees Celsius in a stainless steel tank equipped with a temperature controller and a circulating pump to maintain uniform thermal conditions. The anode samples were placed on a perforated stainless steel rack and submerged vertically into the heated solution, ensuring full immersion and complete wetting of all surfaces. The immersion duration was varied across a range of 1 minute to 20 minutes, with eight samples subjected to different immersion times: 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, 12, 15, and 20 minutes. After immersion, each sample was carefully removed from the solution and allowed to drain for 5 minutes over a stainless steel tray to remove excess surface liquid. The samples were then transferred to a dedicated drying chamber maintained at a constant temperature of 140 degrees Celsius. The drying chamber was equipped with a low-flow air circulation system to ensure uniform moisture removal without inducing surface crust formation. Each sample was dried for a period of 8 hours, after which it was removed and allowed to cool to ambient temperature in a desiccator. The mass of each sample was recorded before and after impregnation and drying to determine the weight gain attributable to boron oxide uptake. The weight gain ranged from 0.074 grams to 0.157 grams per sample, corresponding to a boron oxide loading of 0.074 to 0.157 grams per 100-gram sample, or approximately 0.07 to 0.16 grams per square centimeter of surface area. The variation in weight gain was attributed to differences in local porosity and pore connectivity within the anode matrix, as confirmed by subsequent X-ray computed tomography. No visible cracks, surface discoloration, or structural deformation were observed in any of the samples following impregnation and drying, indicating that the process did not induce mechanical stress or thermal degradation. The impregnated samples were then stored in sealed plastic containers under ambient conditions until further testing. The untreated control samples, which were subjected to identical handling procedures but without exposure to the boron solution, were prepared in parallel and stored under identical conditions. The preparation protocol was repeated for multiple batches to ensure reproducibility, and all samples were processed under the same environmental conditions to minimize variability. The impregnation method described herein was designed to be scalable and compatible with industrial anode geometries, demonstrating that even large, high-volume anode blocks can be uniformly treated without requiring specialized equipment or extreme process conditions.

### Example 2: Anode Characterization

The impregnated and untreated anode samples were subjected to comprehensive microstructural characterization using X-ray computed tomography (CT) to evaluate the distribution of porosity and the presence of boron oxide within the internal structure. The CT scans were performed using a Siemens Somatom Sensation 64 scanner, operating at a voltage of 120 kV and a current of 200 µA, with a voxel resolution of 0.1 × 0.1 × 0.6 mm³. Each anode sample was mounted on a rotating platform and scanned in a helical mode to generate a complete three-dimensional dataset of the internal structure. The scans were acquired before and after the impregnation process to assess changes in pore morphology and density distribution. The raw CT data were reconstructed using proprietary software to generate volumetric images with grayscale values corresponding to material density. The untreated anode samples exhibited a heterogeneous pore structure characterized by irregularly shaped voids ranging from 10 to 500 micrometers in diameter, with a total porosity of approximately 22 percent by volume. The pores were predominantly interconnected, forming a complex network that extended throughout the entire sample volume. The binder phase, composed of coal tar pitch, appeared as a lower-density matrix surrounding the larger coke particles, with visible microcracks and voids at the interface between the coke and binder. Following impregnation, the CT images revealed a distinct change in the internal density profile. Regions of increased density, corresponding to the precipitation of boron oxide, were observed uniformly distributed along the walls of the pore network, particularly in the smaller pores with diameters below 100 micrometers. The boron oxide appeared as a thin, continuous film coating the internal surfaces of the pores, with no evidence of agglomeration, clustering, or surface-only deposition. The density increase in the pore walls was quantified as an average increase of 8 to 12 percent in grayscale intensity compared to the untreated samples, consistent with the expected density of boron oxide. Importantly, the overall porosity of the impregnated samples remained unchanged, indicating that the boron oxide filled the pore volume without collapsing the structure or reducing the available void space. The thickness of the boron oxide film was estimated to be between 1 and 5 micrometers, sufficient to inhibit oxygen access to the carbon surface while maintaining the anode’s permeability to gas diffusion. The spatial distribution of the boron oxide was found to be uniform across the entire sample volume, with no significant gradient observed from the surface to the core, even in samples with the shortest impregnation time of 1 minute. This uniformity was attributed to the capillary-driven infiltration of the aqueous solution and the controlled drying protocol, which prevented surface crust formation and allowed the solution to penetrate deeply before precipitation occurred. The CT images also revealed that the boron oxide was preferentially deposited in regions of higher surface area, such as the binder-rich zones and the interfaces between coke particles, which are known to be the most reactive sites during oxidation. This targeted deposition effectively passivates the most vulnerable regions of the anode, thereby providing maximum protection where it is most needed. The untreated control samples, in contrast, showed no change in density or pore morphology following the same handling and drying procedures, confirming that the observed changes were solely attributable to the boron impregnation process. The characterization results demonstrated that the impregnation method successfully delivers the protective agent to the critical internal surfaces of the anode, transforming the microstructure from one that is inherently reactive to one that is chemically passivated without compromising its structural or transport properties.

### Example 3: Air Reactivity Measurements

The air reactivity of the impregnated and untreated anode samples was evaluated using a high-precision isothermal oxidation test conducted in a muffle furnace under controlled atmospheric conditions. Each sample, weighing approximately 100 grams, was placed on a porous alumina wool support within a stainless steel tray to ensure uniform airflow around all surfaces. The furnace was purged with high-purity nitrogen for 30 minutes to eliminate residual oxygen, after which the temperature was ramped to 500 degrees Celsius at a rate of 5 degrees Celsius per minute and held constant for 10 minutes to stabilize thermal conditions. Following stabilization, the nitrogen flow was switched to a continuous stream of dry, high-purity air at a flow rate of 4 liters per minute, initiating the oxidation reaction. To monitor the reaction kinetics over time, the samples were removed from the furnace at regular intervals, cooled to ambient temperature in a desiccator, weighed on a high-precision analytical balance with a resolution of 0.001 gram, and then returned to the furnace for continued exposure. The first 24 hours of exposure were monitored at two-hour intervals, followed by 12-hour intervals for the subsequent 136 hours, resulting in a total exposure duration of 160 hours. The mass loss of each sample was calculated as a percentage of the initial mass, using the formula: gasification percentage = [(M₀ − Mₜ) / M₀] × 100, where M₀ is the initial mass and Mₜ is the mass at time t. The untreated anode samples exhibited a rapid and continuous mass loss, with an initial oxidation rate of approximately 4.0 percent per hour. After 24 hours of exposure, the mass loss exceeded 86 percent, and the samples had disintegrated into a fine, powdery residue, indicating complete surface degradation and dust generation. The oxidation rate remained high throughout the test, with no evidence of self-limiting behavior, and the samples could not be measured beyond 40 hours due to complete structural collapse. In contrast, the impregnated samples displayed a markedly reduced oxidation rate that was strongly dependent on the impregnation duration. The sample impregnated for 1 minute exhibited an oxidation rate of approximately 2.0 percent per hour, representing a 50 percent reduction compared to the untreated sample. The sample impregnated for 10 minutes showed a rate of 0.25 percent per hour, and the sample impregnated for 20 minutes exhibited the lowest rate of 0.20 percent per hour. After 160 hours of exposure, the 10-minute and 20-minute impregnated samples retained 30 percent and 40 percent of their original mass, respectively, while maintaining their original shape and structural integrity. Visual inspection confirmed that the surface of these samples remained smooth and intact, with no visible dusting, cracking, or flaking. The oxidation occurred uniformly throughout the volume, as evidenced by the gradual increase in porosity without surface shrinkage. The oxidation rate of the impregnated samples remained nearly constant throughout the entire exposure period, indicating that the protective effect was stable and not subject to depletion or degradation over time. The results demonstrated that the impregnation process fundamentally alters the oxidation mechanism, shifting it from a surface-controlled, rapid reaction to a slow, volumetric process governed by oxygen diffusion through the pore network. The consistency of the oxidation rate over time, coupled with the preservation of structural integrity, confirms that the boron oxide layer provides durable, long-term protection without requiring replenishment or reapplication. The data further indicate that the optimal impregnation duration lies between 10 and 20 minutes, as longer durations did not yield significant additional benefits, suggesting that the pore network becomes saturated with boron oxide within this range.

### Example 4: CO2 Reactivity Measurements

To evaluate the broader applicability of the impregnation method, the carbon dioxide reactivity of the impregnated and untreated anode samples was assessed under conditions simulating the gas atmosphere encountered in the anode zone of an electrolysis cell. The test was conducted in a vertical tube furnace equipped with a mass flow controller to regulate the composition of the reactive gas stream. The furnace was first purged with nitrogen for 30 minutes, then heated to 900 degrees Celsius at a rate of 5 degrees Celsius per minute. Once the target temperature was reached, the gas atmosphere was switched from nitrogen to a mixture of 70 percent carbon dioxide and 30 percent nitrogen, with a total flow rate of 3 liters per minute. The samples, each weighing approximately 50 grams and cut into cylindrical specimens of 30 millimeters in diameter and 20 millimeters in height, were placed on a ceramic crucible within the furnace. The mass loss of each sample was recorded at 30-minute intervals for a total duration of 12 hours, using the same gravimetric method employed in the air reactivity tests. The untreated anode samples exhibited a rapid CO₂ reactivity, with an initial mass loss rate of 1.8 percent per hour, reaching a total mass loss of 21.5 percent after 12 hours. The surface of the samples became highly porous and exhibited significant erosion, with visible pitting and cracking. In contrast, the anode samples impregnated for 15 minutes with the boron solution showed a marked reduction in CO₂ reactivity, with an initial mass loss rate of 0.45 percent per hour and a total mass loss of only 5.4 percent after 12 hours, representing a 75 percent reduction in reactivity. The impregnated samples retained their original shape and surface smoothness, with no evidence of surface spalling or dust generation. The reduction in CO₂ reactivity was consistent with the mechanism observed in air reactivity tests: the boron oxide layer inhibits the intrinsic reactivity of the carbon matrix, slowing the Boudouard reaction (C + CO₂ → 2CO) by passivating active sites and reducing the rate of carbon gasification. The fact that the same impregnation protocol was effective against both oxygen and carbon dioxide oxidation confirms that the protective mechanism is not specific to one oxidizing agent but rather acts as a general suppression of carbon reactivity. This is a significant advantage, as the anode is exposed to both gases during operation, with CO₂ being generated in situ from the anode reaction itself. The results demonstrate that the boron oxide impregnation method provides dual protection against the two primary oxidative pathways responsible for anode consumption, making it a comprehensive solution for extending anode life in industrial electrolysis cells. The consistency of the protective effect across different oxidizing environments further supports the hypothesis that the mechanism is rooted in the modification of the carbon’s intrinsic reactivity rather than the formation of a simple physical barrier.

### Example 5: Electrical Resistivity Measurements

The electrical resistivity of the impregnated and untreated anode samples was measured to ensure that the boron oxide impregnation process did not adversely affect the anode’s primary function as an electrical conductor in the Hall-Héroult cell. The resistivity was determined using a four-point probe method with a calibrated digital multimeter and a custom-designed probe fixture that ensured uniform contact pressure across the sample surface. Each sample, measuring 50 millimeters in diameter and 30 millimeters in height, was placed between two copper electrodes, with two additional probes positioned along the diameter to measure the voltage drop. A constant current of 10 milliamperes was applied, and the resulting voltage was recorded at multiple points along the sample’s length to account for any inhomogeneities. The resistivity was calculated using the standard formula for a cylindrical geometry, with corrections applied for probe spacing and sample dimensions. The untreated anode samples exhibited an average electrical resistivity of 12.4 microohm-meters at room temperature, consistent with typical values reported for industrial carbon anodes. The impregnated anode samples, following the same impregnation and drying protocol as described in Example 1, showed an average resistivity of 12.8 microohm-meters, representing an increase of only 3.2 percent. This minor increase is attributed to the partial filling of the smallest pores by the boron oxide film, which may slightly impede electron hopping between carbon particles in the binder phase. However, the increase is well within the acceptable tolerance for industrial anodes, which typically exhibit resistivity variations of up to 10 percent due to natural compositional heterogeneity. No correlation was observed between the level of boron oxide loading and the resistivity change, indicating that the effect is localized and does not scale with impregnation depth. The resistivity measurements were repeated after subjecting the impregnated samples to 100 hours of air oxidation at 500 degrees Celsius, and no significant change in resistivity was detected, confirming that the boron oxide layer remains stable and does not degrade or volatilize under operational conditions. The electrical performance of the impregnated anodes was further validated by comparing their voltage drop under simulated electrolysis conditions in a laboratory-scale cell. The impregnated anodes exhibited a voltage drop identical to that of untreated anodes, with no measurable increase in cell resistance or energy consumption. These results demonstrate that the impregnation process does not compromise the anode’s electrical conductivity, a critical requirement for industrial adoption. The boron oxide layer, while chemically effective in suppressing oxidation, is electrically transparent, allowing current to flow unimpeded through the carbon matrix. This combination of high oxidation resistance and unchanged electrical properties makes the invention uniquely suited for industrial implementation, as it enhances durability without introducing operational inefficiencies.
