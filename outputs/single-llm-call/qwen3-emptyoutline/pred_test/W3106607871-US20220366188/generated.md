# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Semantic role labeling has long been recognized as a foundational component in natural language understanding systems, serving as a bridge between syntactic structure and semantic interpretation. The task involves identifying the predicate-argument relationships within a sentence, assigning each argument a semantic role that describes its relationship to the predicate, such as agent, patient, instrument, location, or temporal modifier. This process enables machines to extract structured meaning from unstructured text, forming the basis for applications in question answering, information extraction, machine translation, and dialogue systems. Historically, semantic role labeling systems relied heavily on hand-engineered linguistic features, syntactic parse trees, and rule-based frameworks that required extensive domain expertise and were brittle when applied across domains or languages. The advent of deep learning revolutionized this field by enabling end-to-end models capable of automatically learning discriminative representations from raw textual input, significantly reducing reliance on external linguistic resources.

Early neural approaches to semantic role labeling treated the problem as a sequence labeling task, where each token in a sentence is assigned a label indicating its role with respect to a given predicate, often using BIO (Begin-Inside-Outside) tagging schemes to delineate argument spans. These models typically employed recurrent neural networks, particularly long short-term memory (LSTM) architectures, to capture sequential dependencies and contextual information. The integration of contextualized word embeddings, such as ELMo and later BERT, further enhanced performance by providing dynamic, context-sensitive representations that adapt based on the surrounding linguistic environment. These advancements led to state-of-the-art results on benchmark datasets like CoNLL 2005 and CoNLL 2012, where models achieved high F1 scores on in-domain evaluations. However, despite these improvements, persistent challenges remained, particularly in handling low-frequency predicates, rare argument configurations, and out-of-domain text where training data was sparse or mismatched.

A recurring limitation of purely neural approaches is their tendency to overfit to patterns prevalent in the training corpus, leading to poor generalization when confronted with lexical or syntactic variations not adequately represented during training. For instance, a predicate that appears only a handful of times in the training data may be assigned incorrect argument roles in test instances due to insufficient statistical evidence for its semantic behavior. Similarly, arguments that exhibit subtle contextual dependencies—such as a spatial phrase being interpreted as a location versus a direction—often confuse models that lack explicit mechanisms to reason about rare or exceptional cases. While syntax-aware models have demonstrated superior performance by incorporating dependency parses or constituency trees as structural priors, such approaches are inherently dependent on the quality and availability of syntactic parsers, which are not always reliable, especially in low-resource languages or noisy domains such as social media or conversational speech.

In parallel with the rise of deep learning, memory-based learning methods have emerged as a powerful paradigm for capturing long-tail patterns and adapting predictions at inference time. These methods store representations of previously observed examples in an external memory and retrieve relevant instances during prediction to inform or correct model outputs. One notable example is K-SRL, which leveraged syntactic features to compute distances between tokens and performed majority voting among the nearest neighbors to determine semantic roles. While effective, K-SRL was constrained by its reliance on manually designed distance metrics and did not exploit the rich, high-dimensional representations generated by modern neural architectures. More recently, continuous cache models and memory-augmented neural networks have shown promise in language modeling by dynamically adapting predictions based on contextual activations stored during training. These approaches demonstrated that even simple retrieval mechanisms, when combined with learned weighting schemes, could significantly improve performance on rare or ambiguous cases.

Despite these advances, no prior method has successfully integrated the high-capacity representation power of deep neural networks with a parameterized, learnable memory adaptation mechanism that operates directly on the internal activations of a trained semantic role labeling model. Existing memory-based techniques either operate at the token level without leveraging the full contextual representation of the model, or they require re-computation of classification parameters for each inference step, introducing prohibitive computational overhead. Furthermore, none of these methods explicitly learn how to combine multiple neighboring representations into a single, coherent adaptive signal that can be used to refine the final prediction. The absence of such a mechanism leaves a critical gap: even when a model’s internal representation is close to a known correct instance in the training data, there is no systematic way to extract and utilize that proximity to correct misclassifications.

This invention addresses this gap by introducing a Parameterized Neighborhood Memory Adaptive (PNMA) framework that operates as a post-training refinement module for any deep neural semantic role labeling system. Unlike prior approaches, PNMA does not require architectural modifications to the base model, nor does it depend on external syntactic resources or handcrafted features. Instead, it leverages the latent representations produced by the final layer of a pre-trained LSTM-based SRL model to construct a dense memory of token activations and their associated gold-standard labels. During inference, for each token in a new sentence, the system identifies its K nearest neighbors in this memory using a simple Euclidean distance metric. Rather than applying a fixed voting rule or averaging mechanism, PNMA introduces a novel parameterized function that learns to weigh and combine the representations of these neighbors into a single, context-sensitive adaptive vector. This vector is then concatenated with the original token representation and fed into a retrained classification layer, allowing the model to dynamically adjust its predictions based on the collective evidence from similar past examples.

The innovation lies not merely in the use of memory, but in the parameterization of the neighborhood aggregation process. By treating the combination of neighbors as a learnable transformation—rather than a static operation—the system can implicitly learn which neighbors are most informative, how their relative distances affect their influence, and how to resolve conflicts among conflicting labels. This enables the model to correct its own misclassifications by drawing upon the wisdom of the crowd encoded in the memory, even when those neighbors themselves were misclassified by the base model during initial training. Crucially, this adaptation occurs without altering the frozen base model weights, preserving the efficiency and stability of the original architecture while enhancing its ability to generalize to rare and exceptional cases. The result is a hybrid system that combines the representational power of deep learning with the adaptability of instance-based learning, achieving state-of-the-art performance on multiple benchmark datasets without requiring syntactic information or additional training data.

The significance of this invention extends beyond semantic role labeling. It introduces a generalizable framework for improving any sequence labeling model that suffers from data sparsity, label ambiguity, or domain shift. By decoupling representation learning from decision refinement, PNMA offers a plug-and-play enhancement that can be applied to any pre-trained model, making it particularly valuable in low-resource settings, domain adaptation scenarios, and applications where retraining the entire model is impractical. The method’s computational efficiency, minimal memory footprint, and compatibility with modern pretrained embeddings position it as a scalable solution for real-world deployment in production systems requiring robust, accurate, and adaptable natural language understanding.

## SUMMARY

The invention disclosed herein is a Parameterized Neighborhood Memory Adaptive (PNMA) system for enhancing the accuracy and robustness of semantic role labeling models through adaptive memory-based refinement. This system operates as a post-training augmentation module that does not require modification of the underlying neural architecture, enabling seamless integration with any pre-existing sequence labeling model trained for semantic role labeling. The core innovation lies in the construction of a dense memory of token representations derived from the final hidden layer of a trained base model, coupled with a learnable parameterized function that aggregates the representations of the K nearest neighbors of each token in the memory to produce a refined, contextually adaptive representation. This adaptive representation is then used to retrain the classification and conditional random field layers of the base model, resulting in a final model that significantly improves prediction accuracy, particularly for low-frequency predicates, rare argument configurations, and out-of-domain instances.

The system is implemented in two distinct phases. In the first phase, a base semantic role labeling model is trained using standard deep learning techniques, including contextualized word embeddings such as ELMo or BERT, alternating LSTM layers, and a conditional random field output layer. Upon completion of this training, the activations produced by the final LSTM layer for every token in the training corpus are stored in an external memory, along with their corresponding gold-standard semantic role labels. This memory serves as a repository of learned contextual representations, capturing both frequent patterns and rare exceptions encountered during training. In the second phase, the parameters of the base model—including the embedding layers, LSTM weights, and connection weights—are frozen, and a new set of learnable parameters is introduced to compute a parameterized neighborhood representation for each token. For each token in the training set, the system identifies its K nearest neighbors in the memory using Euclidean distance, computes the vector difference between the token’s own representation and each neighbor’s representation, and applies a learned linear transformation to each difference vector. These transformed vectors are then combined using a softmax-weighted sum to produce a compact, context-sensitive adaptive vector that encodes the collective influence of the neighborhood. This adaptive vector is concatenated with the original token representation and used as input to a retrained classification layer, which is optimized using the same training labels but with the new combined representation.

At inference time, the system operates without requiring any additional supervision or external resources. For each token in a new sentence, the base model generates its contextual representation, which is then compared against the precomputed memory to identify the K nearest neighbors. The same parameterized aggregation function is applied to these neighbors to compute the adaptive vector, which is combined with the token’s representation and passed through the retrained classification layer to produce the final semantic role label. The entire process is computationally efficient, with the memory lookup and neighbor aggregation steps being parallelizable and implementable on modern GPU architectures with less than a ten percent increase in inference time compared to the base model. The system does not require re-computation of parameters for each sentence or token, nor does it rely on syntactic parsers, external knowledge bases, or handcrafted features, making it highly portable and scalable.

The invention achieves substantial performance gains across multiple benchmark datasets, including CoNLL 2005, CoNLL 2012, and CoNLL 2009, consistently improving F1 scores for both in-domain and out-of-domain evaluations. Notably, the system demonstrates the most significant improvements on low-frequency predicates and rare argument roles, where traditional deep learning models typically fail due to insufficient training examples. The parameterized neighborhood mechanism enables the model to correct its own misclassifications by leveraging the collective evidence of similar instances stored in memory, even when those instances themselves were misclassified by the base model. This results in a model that is not only more accurate but also more robust to distributional shifts and lexical variation. The system outperforms all existing syntax-agnostic models and is competitive with syntax-aware models, despite requiring no syntactic information whatsoever. Furthermore, the method is fully compatible with state-of-the-art pretrained embeddings such as BERT, and its performance gains are additive, meaning that the improvements from PNMA are realized regardless of whether the base model uses randomly initialized embeddings, ELMo, or BERT.

The invention provides a novel and generalizable framework for enhancing sequence labeling models through adaptive memory-based refinement. It introduces a new paradigm in which the model’s predictions are dynamically refined at inference time using a learnable aggregation of its own past experiences, effectively embedding a form of instance-based learning within a deep neural architecture. This approach overcomes the limitations of purely parametric models by incorporating non-parametric memory, while avoiding the computational inefficiencies of per-inference parameter adaptation. The system’s simplicity, efficiency, and effectiveness make it suitable for deployment in real-world applications requiring high accuracy, low latency, and domain adaptability, including automated information extraction, conversational agents, legal document analysis, and multilingual natural language understanding systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The Parameterized Neighborhood Memory Adaptive (PNMA) system is a sophisticated framework designed to enhance the predictive accuracy of semantic role labeling models by integrating memory-based adaptation into the inference process without altering the underlying neural architecture. The system operates as a two-phase refinement protocol that begins with the training of a conventional deep neural model and culminates in the construction and deployment of a parameterized memory mechanism that dynamically refines predictions based on learned neighborhood relationships. The first phase involves the training of a base semantic role labeling model using standard deep learning techniques, including contextualized word embeddings, recurrent neural networks, and conditional random field decoding. This base model is trained end-to-end on labeled corpora such as CoNLL 2005, CoNLL 2012, and CoNLL 2009, using standard optimization procedures, dropout regularization, and learning rate scheduling to achieve optimal performance on the training data. The model architecture consists of multiple layers of alternating long short-term memory units, which capture sequential dependencies in the input sentence, followed by a classification layer that assigns a semantic role label to each token. The input to the model includes word embeddings derived from either randomly initialized vectors or pretrained contextualized representations such as ELMo or BERT, as well as a binary indicator for the position of the predicate within the sentence, which is known to improve argument classification performance. The model is trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 10^-3, which is halved after epochs 50 and 75, and a weight decay of 10^-4 is applied to prevent overfitting. Dropout is applied after each LSTM layer and the embedding layer to further regularize the training process, with values selected based on cross-validation to optimize performance on the validation set.

Upon completion of the first training phase, the system enters the second phase, which constitutes the core innovation of the invention. At this stage, the parameters of the base model—including the embedding weights, LSTM weights, and connection weights—are frozen to preserve the learned representations and prevent catastrophic forgetting. A new set of learnable parameters is introduced to compute a parameterized neighborhood representation for each token in the training corpus. The system first constructs a memory M by extracting the final hidden layer activations h_L(w) for every token w in the training set, along with their corresponding gold-standard semantic role labels. This memory is populated with a representative subset of tokens, typically comprising 15% of the training data, which has been empirically determined to provide optimal performance without incurring unnecessary memory overhead. The size of the memory is not constrained by theoretical limits but is instead optimized for computational efficiency and generalization, as increasing the memory size beyond this threshold yields diminishing returns in performance gains. For each token in the training set, the system computes its K nearest neighbors in the memory using Euclidean distance, where K is set to 64 based on empirical validation across multiple datasets. The selection of K balances the trade-off between capturing sufficient contextual evidence and maintaining computational tractability, as larger values introduce noise from distant neighbors while smaller values may fail to capture the diversity of relevant instances.

Once the nearest neighbors for each token are identified, the system computes a set of difference vectors that represent the separation between the token’s own representation and each of its K neighbors. These difference vectors are defined as m_i(w) = h_L(w) - h_L(n_i), where n_i denotes the i-th nearest neighbor of token w. Each difference vector is then passed through a distinct learnable linear transformation n_i ∈ R^d, where d is the dimensionality of the hidden representation, resulting in a transformed vector n_i * m_i(w). The use of distinct transformation parameters for each neighbor, rather than a shared parameter across all neighbors, is a critical design choice that allows the system to assign different weights to different neighbors based on their relative positions and semantic relevance. This approach enables the model to learn that some neighbors are more indicative of the correct label than others, even if they are equidistant in Euclidean space. The transformed vectors are then combined using a softmax-weighted sum, where the weights are computed as the inner product between each transformed vector and a learned attention vector, followed by a softmax normalization. This results in a compact, weighted representation n_K(w) ∈ R^d that encodes the collective influence of the K nearest neighbors, effectively forming an ensemble of contextualized representations that are semantically close to the current token.

This adaptive representation n_K(w) is then concatenated with the original token representation h_L(w) to form a combined representation [h_L(w); n_K(w)], which is fed into a retrained classification layer. The classification layer, which may consist of a feedforward neural network followed by a conditional random field (CRF) decoder, is reinitialized and trained from scratch using the same training labels but with the new combined representations as input. During this retraining phase, the system optimizes the parameters of the classification layer and the neighborhood transformation parameters {n_i} using a constant learning rate of 4 × 10^-4 for 20 epochs. The CRF layer is essential to ensure label consistency across the sequence, enforcing constraints such as valid BIO tag transitions and preventing invalid role assignments. Importantly, the original token representation h_L(w) is not used directly in this phase, as empirical experiments have shown that including it alongside the adaptive representation leads to suboptimal performance, likely due to redundancy or conflicting gradients. The retraining process is conducted on the same training set used in the first phase, ensuring that the system learns to correct the base model’s errors using the evidence encoded in the memory. The resulting model, referred to as the PNMA model, is the final output of the invention and is deployed for inference without further modification.

At inference time, the PNMA model operates in a manner that is fully compatible with standard deployment pipelines. For each token in a new sentence, the base model generates its contextual representation h_L(w) using the frozen embedding and LSTM layers. This representation is then compared against the precomputed memory M to identify the K nearest neighbors using the same Euclidean distance metric employed during training. The same parameterized transformation and weighted aggregation procedure is applied to these neighbors to compute the adaptive representation n_K(w), which is concatenated with h_L(w) and passed through the retrained classification and CRF layers to produce the final semantic role label. The entire process is computationally efficient, with the memory lookup and neighbor aggregation steps being highly parallelizable and implementable on modern GPU architectures. The overhead introduced by the PNMA mechanism is less than ten percent compared to the base model, making it suitable for real-time applications. The system does not require re-computation of parameters for each sentence or token, nor does it rely on external syntactic parsers, knowledge bases, or handcrafted features, making it highly portable and scalable across domains and languages.

The invention’s effectiveness stems from its ability to correct the base model’s misclassifications by leveraging the collective evidence of similar instances stored in memory. Empirical analysis reveals that for a large proportion of tokens misclassified by the base model, at least one of its nearest neighbors in the memory carries the correct label. The parameterized aggregation mechanism enables the system to learn which neighbors are most informative, even when those neighbors themselves were misclassified during training. This allows the model to overcome the limitations of purely parametric learning by incorporating non-parametric memory, effectively embedding a form of instance-based learning within a deep neural architecture. The system is particularly effective in addressing low-frequency predicates and rare argument configurations, where traditional models fail due to insufficient training data. For example, in the CoNLL 2005 dataset, the PNMA model significantly improves the prediction of core argument roles such as A1 and A2, which are frequently confused with each other or with adjunct roles such as LOC or ADV. The model reduces misclassification rates by leveraging neighbors that exhibit similar contextual patterns, even if those patterns are rare in the training corpus. This is evident in cases where a spatial phrase such as “at the foot of the bed” is incorrectly labeled as LOC by the base model but correctly classified as A2 by the PNMA model due to the presence of similar instances in memory where the same phrase was associated with the same argument role.

The system’s performance is further validated through extensive ablation studies and error analysis. When the parameterized neighborhood mechanism is removed and replaced with a simple average of neighbor representations, performance degrades significantly, demonstrating that the learnable transformations are essential to the system’s success. Similarly, when the distinct transformation parameters for each neighbor are replaced with a shared parameter, the model’s ability to resolve label conflicts diminishes, confirming that the diversity of neighbor-specific weights is critical. The system also demonstrates robustness to variations in memory size, K-value, and training subset selection, with optimal performance consistently achieved when the memory contains approximately 15% of the training tokens and K is set to 64. The PNMA model achieves new state-of-the-art F1 scores on all benchmark datasets, surpassing previous syntax-agnostic models by a significant margin and matching or exceeding the performance of syntax-aware models that rely on external parsers. On the out-of-domain Brown test set in CoNLL 2005, the PNMA model improves upon the previous best result by 2.0 F1 points, while on CoNLL 2009, the improvement reaches 5.0 F1 points, demonstrating its exceptional ability to generalize to unseen domains.

The invention’s compatibility with pretrained embeddings such as BERT further enhances its practical utility. When combined with BERT, the PNMA model achieves the highest reported F1 scores on both span-based and dependency-based semantic role labeling tasks, establishing a new benchmark for single-model performance. The performance gains are additive, meaning that the improvements from PNMA are realized regardless of whether the base model uses randomly initialized embeddings, ELMo, or BERT. This indicates that the memory adaptation mechanism operates independently of the representation quality, making it a universal enhancement for any sequence labeling model. The system’s efficiency, scalability, and portability make it suitable for deployment in a wide range of real-world applications, including automated information extraction from legal and medical documents, conversational agents requiring accurate semantic understanding, multilingual natural language processing systems, and domain-specific applications where labeled data is scarce. The PNMA framework introduces a novel paradigm in which the model’s predictions are dynamically refined at inference time using a learnable aggregation of its own past experiences, effectively embedding a form of instance-based learning within a deep neural architecture. This approach overcomes the limitations of purely parametric models by incorporating non-parametric memory, while avoiding the computational inefficiencies of per-inference parameter adaptation. The system’s simplicity, efficiency, and effectiveness make it suitable for deployment in real-world applications requiring high accuracy, low latency, and domain adaptability, including automated information extraction, conversational agents, legal document analysis, and multilingual natural language understanding systems.
