## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of ion-selective membranes for electrochemical separation and transport applications, particularly those employing nanoporous substrates coated with chemically stabilized layer-by-layer polyelectrolyte thin films. More specifically, the invention pertains to a method of fabricating ion-selective membranes through the sequential deposition of poly(acrylic acid) and poly(ethyleneimine) onto nanoporous polymeric supports, followed by selective chemical cross-linking using either glutaraldehyde or N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodimide hydrochloride to modulate the charge density, structural integrity, and ionic transport characteristics of the resulting composite membrane. The invention further encompasses the resulting membranes themselves, which exhibit tunable cationic selectivity independent of ionic conductivity, enhanced mechanical and chemical stability, and scalability to large-area formats suitable for industrial deployment in electrodialysis, flow battery systems, fuel cells, and water purification technologies.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Nanoporous membranes have long been recognized as critical components in a wide array of separation and energy conversion technologies, including desalination, electrodialysis, redox flow batteries, and proton exchange membrane fuel cells. These membranes function by permitting the selective passage of ions while restricting the transport of other species, thereby enabling the generation of electrical potential gradients, concentration gradients, or purified streams of ions. The performance of such membranes is governed by a complex interplay between pore geometry, surface chemistry, and the electrostatic environment within the pore lumen. In particular, the surface charge density of the pore walls has been shown to exert a dominant influence on ion transport behavior, with negatively charged surfaces favoring cation permeation and repelling anions, and vice versa.

Traditional approaches to creating ion-selective membranes have relied on the use of homogeneous polymer films, such as sulfonated polystyrene or quaternary ammonium-functionalized polyolefins, which are cast or extruded into macroscopic sheets. While effective, these methods often suffer from limitations in tunability, scalability, and cost. Moreover, the surface charge of such membranes is typically fixed during synthesis and cannot be readily adjusted post-fabrication, limiting their adaptability to varying operational conditions or target ion species. Alternative strategies have involved the modification of nanoporous substrates—such as track-etched polycarbonate, anodic alumina, or mesoporous silica—with charged functional groups via plasma treatment, silanization, or graft polymerization. These methods, while offering improved control over surface chemistry, often require complex multi-step processes, expensive reagents, or specialized equipment, rendering them unsuitable for large-scale manufacturing.

Layer-by-layer (LbL) deposition of polyelectrolytes has emerged as a promising alternative for the fabrication of nanostructured, charge-tunable membranes. This bottom-up technique involves the sequential immersion of a substrate into aqueous solutions of oppositely charged polymers, resulting in the self-assembly of thin films through electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals interactions. The method is inherently simple, cost-effective, and compatible with a broad range of substrates, including flexible polymers, ceramics, and even non-planar geometries. Polyelectrolyte systems composed of poly(ethyleneimine) (PEI), a highly branched cationic polymer bearing primary and secondary amines, and poly(acrylic acid) (PAA), a linear anionic polymer containing carboxylic acid groups, have been widely studied due to the strong complementary electrostatic interactions between these species and their commercial availability in aqueous solutions.

Despite the promise of LbL-deposited polyelectrolyte films, significant challenges remain in translating laboratory-scale demonstrations into industrially viable membrane technologies. One of the most persistent issues is the poor mechanical and chemical stability of as-deposited films, which are prone to swelling, delamination, or dissolution under prolonged exposure to aqueous electrolytes, particularly at non-neutral pH or elevated ionic strength. Additionally, the charge density of the polyelectrolyte film—critical for determining ionic selectivity—is often difficult to control precisely, as it is influenced by the number of deposited bilayers, the molecular weight of the polymers, the ionic strength of the deposition solution, and the kinetics of polymer adsorption and diffusion into nanopores.

Previous efforts to stabilize LbL films have employed chemical cross-linking agents such as glutaraldehyde, which reacts selectively with primary amines, or carbodiimide reagents such as N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodimide hydrochloride (EDC), which facilitate amide bond formation between primary amines and carboxylic acids. However, these approaches have typically been applied to planar surfaces or non-porous substrates, and their effects on ion transport through nanoporous architectures have not been systematically investigated. In particular, the differential impact of cross-linking chemistry on the net charge density of the polyelectrolyte film—especially when applied to nanopores where polymer diffusion and confinement effects alter molecular mobility and reaction kinetics—has remained largely unexplored. Furthermore, no prior work has demonstrated the decoupling of ionic selectivity from ionic conductivity in LbL-coated nanoporous membranes, a critical advancement required for optimizing membrane performance in real-world applications where high selectivity must be achieved without prohibitive resistive losses.

In the context of electrodialysis and flow battery systems, where membranes must simultaneously exhibit high ionic selectivity, low electrical resistance, and long-term chemical durability, the absence of a scalable, tunable, and robust fabrication method has been a major technological bottleneck. Existing commercial membranes, such as cation exchange membranes based on perfluorosulfonic acid or anion exchange membranes derived from quaternary ammonium polymers, are expensive to produce, environmentally problematic due to fluorinated content, and offer limited flexibility in tuning selectivity post-fabrication. There is therefore a compelling need for a new class of ion-selective membranes that can be manufactured at low cost using aqueous processing, that enable precise control over charge density through post-deposition chemical modification, and that maintain structural integrity under operational conditions without sacrificing ionic transport efficiency.

The present invention addresses these longstanding challenges by introducing a novel method for fabricating ion-selective nanoporous membranes through the controlled deposition of PAA/PEI bilayers on track-etched polycarbonate substrates, followed by selective cross-linking using either glutaraldehyde or EDC. This method uniquely enables the independent tuning of ionic selectivity and ionic conductivity through the strategic choice of cross-linking agent and bilayer number, a capability not previously demonstrated in the art. The resulting membranes exhibit exceptional cationic selectivity, enhanced film stability, and scalable manufacturability, making them uniquely suited for next-generation electrochemical separation and energy storage systems.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method for fabricating ion-selective nanoporous membranes that enables independent control over ionic selectivity and ionic conductivity through the layer-by-layer deposition of poly(acrylic acid) and poly(ethyleneimine) followed by selective chemical cross-linking. The method comprises the steps of providing a nanoporous substrate, sequentially depositing alternating layers of anionic and cationic polyelectrolytes onto both surfaces of the substrate, and then chemically cross-linking the deposited polyelectrolyte film using either glutaraldehyde or N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodimide hydrochloride to modify the charge density and structural integrity of the film. The resulting membrane exhibits a tunable net surface charge that favors the selective transport of cations over anions, while maintaining a level of ionic conductivity that is significantly higher than that of conventional ion-exchange membranes, even at high bilayer thicknesses.

In one embodiment, the nanoporous substrate is a track-etched polycarbonate membrane with uniform cylindrical pores of approximately 50 nanometers in diameter, wherein the substrate is first cleaned by alkaline hydrolysis to remove surface contaminants and then activated by ultraviolet-ozone treatment to enhance surface hydrophilicity and promote uniform polyelectrolyte adsorption. The polyelectrolyte layers are deposited by immersing the substrate in an aqueous solution of poly(ethyleneimine) at a concentration of 0.1 weight percent and a pH of approximately 10.4 for a duration of five minutes, followed by rinsing with deionized water, and then immersing the substrate in an aqueous solution of poly(acrylic acid) at a concentration of 0.2 weight percent and a pH of approximately 3.2 for a duration of five minutes. Subsequent bilayers are deposited using a reduced immersion time of one minute per polymer solution, enabling rapid and reproducible film growth. The number of deposited bilayers is selected from the group consisting of one, three, or five bilayers, with each bilayer comprising one layer of poly(ethyleneimine) and one layer of poly(acrylic acid).

Following deposition, the polyelectrolyte film is chemically cross-linked using either glutaraldehyde or N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodimide hydrochloride. In one embodiment, the film is immersed in a 25 percent aqueous solution of glutaraldehyde for a period of twelve hours, during which time the aldehyde groups of glutaraldehyde react selectively with primary amine functionalities of the poly(ethyleneimine) to form stable Schiff base linkages, thereby reducing the number of positively charged amine groups while leaving the negatively charged carboxylic acid groups of poly(acrylic acid) largely unaffected. In an alternative embodiment, the film is immersed in a 100 millimolar aqueous solution of N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodimide hydrochloride for twelve hours, during which time the carbodiimide reagent facilitates the formation of amide bonds between primary amines of poly(ethyleneimine) and carboxylic acids of poly(acrylic acid), thereby reducing the number of both cationic and anionic functional groups in equal proportion, thereby preserving the net charge of the film.

The resulting membrane exhibits a cationic transference number greater than 0.6, indicating a strong preference for the transport of cations over anions, with the transference number increasing with the number of deposited bilayers when cross-linked with glutaraldehyde. Membranes cross-linked with glutaraldehyde demonstrate a cationic transference number of up to 0.75 for five bilayers, representing an 11 percent increase in selectivity compared to one bilayer. In contrast, membranes cross-linked with N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodimide hydrochloride exhibit a cationic transference number that remains approximately constant across bilayer numbers, consistent with the preservation of net charge. Membranes that are not cross-linked exhibit poor film integrity, with evidence of polymer re-dissolution and pore exposure, resulting in significantly reduced ionic selectivity and conductivity.

The ionic conductivity of the membrane, measured in aqueous sodium chloride solutions of 10 millimolar and 100 millimolar concentration, is found to be inversely related to the number of deposited bilayers but is restored to near-bare-substrate levels when the film is cross-linked with either glutaraldehyde or N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodimide hydrochloride. The conductivity of five-bilayer glutaraldehyde-cross-linked membranes is comparable to that of the uncoated nanoporous substrate, despite the presence of a polyelectrolyte film approximately 1.2 micrometers thick. This phenomenon is attributed to the formation of a continuous, interconnected polyelectrolyte network within the nanopores that facilitates ion transport through hydrated channels, while the cross-linking prevents film collapse and pore occlusion. Membranes that are not cross-linked exhibit the lowest conductivity due to polymer re-dissolution and incomplete pore coverage.

The polyelectrolyte film is confirmed to be uniformly deposited across the entire membrane surface and to penetrate the nanopores, forming a continuous, tubular coating within the pore lumen, as evidenced by scanning electron microscopy of cross-sectional samples. The thickness of the polyelectrolyte film increases linearly with the number of deposited bilayers, reaching an average thickness of 1230 nanometers for five bilayers. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy confirms the presence of both poly(acrylic acid) and poly(ethyleneimine) in the deposited film and demonstrates the selective reduction of amine groups upon glutaraldehyde cross-linking and the symmetric reduction of both amine and carboxylic acid groups upon EDC cross-linking.

The invention further encompasses the resulting ion-selective membrane, which comprises a nanoporous substrate, a layer-by-layer polyelectrolyte coating comprising alternating layers of poly(acrylic acid) and poly(ethyleneimine), and a chemically cross-linked network formed by either glutaraldehyde or N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodimide hydrochloride, wherein the membrane exhibits a cationic transference number greater than 0.6 and an ionic conductivity greater than 0.1 siemens per centimeter in 100 millimolar sodium chloride solution. The membrane is scalable to areas exceeding 64 square centimeters and is suitable for use in electrodialysis, redox flow batteries, fuel cells, and water purification systems.

The invention further includes a method of operating the membrane in an electrochemical cell, wherein the membrane is positioned between two electrolyte compartments containing solutions of differing ionic concentrations, and wherein a voltage is applied across the membrane to drive selective ion transport, resulting in the concentration of one ion species in one compartment and the depletion of that ion species in the other. The membrane is capable of maintaining stable performance over extended periods of operation without significant degradation in selectivity or conductivity, even under conditions of elevated ionic strength, variable pH, or repeated cycling.

The invention provides a significant advancement over the prior art by enabling the simultaneous optimization of ionic selectivity and ionic conductivity through a simple, scalable, and cost-effective fabrication process that requires only aqueous solutions of commercially available polymers and common cross-linking agents. The ability to independently tune selectivity and conductivity represents a paradigm shift in membrane design, allowing for the precise engineering of membrane properties to meet the specific requirements of diverse electrochemical applications without the need for costly or environmentally hazardous materials.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Example: LbL Deposition of Bilayer PAA and PEI Polyelectrolytes

The fabrication of the ion-selective membrane begins with the selection of a nanoporous substrate that provides a mechanically stable, chemically inert, and uniformly porous scaffold upon which the polyelectrolyte layers may be deposited. In the preferred embodiment, the substrate is a track-etched polycarbonate membrane with a nominal pore diameter of 50 nanometers, a pore density of approximately 10⁸ pores per square centimeter, and a total thickness of 10 micrometers. This substrate is commercially available in large-format sheets measuring up to 90 millimeters in diameter, enabling the production of membranes with active areas exceeding 64 square centimeters, a scale sufficient for industrial deployment. Prior to polyelectrolyte deposition, the substrate undergoes a two-step surface preparation protocol designed to remove residual processing contaminants and enhance the hydrophilicity of the pore walls. First, the membrane is immersed in a 4.5 molar aqueous solution of sodium hydroxide for a duration of five minutes, during which time the ester linkages of the polycarbonate backbone are partially hydrolyzed, removing a thin polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP) coating that is commonly applied during manufacturing to prevent pore collapse. The membrane is then thoroughly rinsed with deionized water to remove residual alkali and dried under a stream of nitrogen gas. Subsequently, the membrane is exposed to ultraviolet-ozone radiation for ten minutes on each side using a commercial UV-ozone cleaner operating at a wavelength of 185 and 254 nanometers. This treatment generates hydroxyl and carbonyl functional groups on the polycarbonate surface, increasing its surface energy and promoting uniform wetting and adsorption of the subsequent polyelectrolyte layers. The treated substrate is then immediately transferred to the polyelectrolyte deposition bath to prevent recontamination or surface aging.

The layer-by-layer deposition process is carried out using aqueous solutions of poly(ethyleneimine) and poly(acrylic acid), both of which are commercially available and inexpensive. The poly(ethyleneimine) solution is prepared at a concentration of 0.1 weight percent in deionized water, adjusted to a pH of 10.4 using dilute sodium hydroxide to ensure full protonation of the amine groups and maximize the cationic charge density. The poly(acrylic acid) solution is prepared at a concentration of 0.2 weight percent in deionized water, adjusted to a pH of 3.2 using dilute hydrochloric acid to ensure full deprotonation of the carboxylic acid groups and maximize the anionic charge density. The pH values are selected to optimize the electrostatic attraction between the oppositely charged polymers while minimizing the risk of polymer precipitation or aggregation. The substrate is first immersed in the poly(ethyleneimine) solution for five minutes, during which time the cationic polymer adsorbs onto the anionic surface of the polycarbonate substrate, forming the first layer of the bilayer. The substrate is then removed from the solution and rinsed with deionized water for thirty seconds to remove loosely bound polymer and excess salt, followed by gentle drying under a stream of nitrogen. The rinsed substrate is then immersed in the poly(acrylic acid) solution for five minutes, during which time the anionic polymer adsorbs onto the now positively charged poly(ethyleneimine) layer, completing the first bilayer. The substrate is again rinsed and dried. Subsequent bilayers are deposited using a reduced immersion time of one minute per polymer solution, which allows for rapid and reproducible film growth while minimizing the risk of excessive polymer diffusion or interlayer mixing. The number of bilayers deposited is selected based on the desired degree of ionic selectivity and conductivity, with typical configurations ranging from one to five bilayers. Each bilayer contributes approximately 200 to 300 nanometers to the total film thickness, resulting in a cumulative thickness of up to 1.5 micrometers for five bilayers. The deposition process is performed at ambient temperature and pressure, and no specialized equipment beyond standard laboratory glassware and a magnetic stirrer is required.

Following deposition, the polyelectrolyte film is chemically cross-linked to enhance its mechanical stability, prevent re-dissolution, and modulate its charge density. Two distinct cross-linking strategies are employed, each yielding a different electrostatic profile and transport behavior. In the first strategy, the membrane is immersed in a 25 percent aqueous solution of glutaraldehyde for a period of twelve hours. Glutaraldehyde is a dialdehyde compound that reacts selectively with primary amine groups to form stable Schiff base linkages. In the context of the polyelectrolyte film, this reaction results in the covalent cross-linking of adjacent poly(ethyleneimine) chains, thereby reducing the number of free primary amines available for protonation and thus decreasing the overall cationic charge density of the film. Crucially, the carboxylic acid groups of poly(acrylic acid) remain unaffected by glutaraldehyde, preserving their anionic character. This asymmetric modification results in a net increase in the negative charge density of the polyelectrolyte film, enhancing its preference for cationic transport. In the second strategy, the membrane is immersed in a 100 millimolar aqueous solution of N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodimide hydrochloride for twelve hours. EDC is a carbodiimide reagent that facilitates the formation of amide bonds between primary amines and carboxylic acids in the presence of a catalyst such as N-hydroxysuccinimide, although in this embodiment, no additional catalyst is used. The reaction proceeds through the activation of the carboxylic acid group by EDC to form an O-acylisourea intermediate, which then reacts with a nearby primary amine to form a stable amide bond. This reaction consumes one cationic amine group and one anionic carboxylic acid group per cross-link, resulting in a symmetric reduction in the total number of fixed charges within the film. As a consequence, the net charge of the polyelectrolyte film remains approximately unchanged, while the structural integrity of the film is significantly enhanced due to the formation of covalent cross-links between the opposing polymer chains. After cross-linking, the membrane is rinsed extensively with deionized water to remove unreacted cross-linking agent and byproducts, and then dried under nitrogen before further characterization or use.

The effectiveness of the cross-linking process is confirmed through Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, which reveals distinct changes in the vibrational modes of the polyelectrolyte functional groups. The spectrum of the unmodified polyelectrolyte film displays two prominent peaks: a strong absorption band at 1714 cm⁻¹ corresponding to the carbonyl stretch of the carboxylic acid groups in poly(acrylic acid), and a broad band at 1550 cm⁻¹ corresponding to the N–H bending vibrations of the amine groups in poly(ethyleneimine). In membranes cross-linked with glutaraldehyde, the intensity of the amine-related peak at 1550 cm⁻¹ is significantly reduced relative to the carboxylic acid peak, indicating a selective loss of primary amine groups. In contrast, membranes cross-linked with EDC exhibit a proportional reduction in the intensity of both the amine and carboxylic acid peaks, consistent with the symmetric consumption of both functional groups during amide bond formation. These spectroscopic observations confirm that the cross-linking chemistry is proceeding as intended and that the charge density of the film is being modified in a predictable and controllable manner.

The morphology of the deposited polyelectrolyte film is examined using scanning electron microscopy, both in plan view and in cross-section. Plan-view images of membranes with one bilayer and no cross-linking reveal a patchy, non-uniform coating with numerous exposed nanopores, indicating that the polyelectrolyte has not fully covered the substrate surface. This is attributed to the weak electrostatic adhesion of the as-deposited film, which is susceptible to re-dissolution or lateral migration during rinsing. In contrast, membranes that have been cross-linked with either glutaraldehyde or EDC exhibit a continuous, smooth, and uniform coating with no visible exposed pores, demonstrating that cross-linking is essential for achieving complete pore coverage and film stability. Cross-sectional images obtained via cryo-fracture reveal that the polyelectrolyte film penetrates deeply into the nanopores, forming a tubular coating that fills the pore lumen from wall to wall. For membranes with five bilayers, the polyelectrolyte coating is observed to completely occlude the pore, forming a solid, continuous polymer rod that spans the entire thickness of the membrane. The thickness of the polyelectrolyte film is measured at multiple points across the cross-section and found to increase linearly with the number of deposited bilayers, with an average thickness of 1230 nanometers for five bilayers. This linear growth is consistent with the established behavior of polyelectrolyte films deposited under conditions of low ionic strength and controlled immersion time, and it confirms that the deposition process is highly reproducible and predictable.

The ionic transport properties of the fabricated membranes are evaluated using two complementary electrochemical techniques: potentiometric measurements of ionic selectivity and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy of ionic conductivity. Ionic selectivity is determined by sealing the membrane in a U-shaped electrochemical cell containing two compartments filled with aqueous sodium chloride solutions of differing concentrations. One compartment is maintained at a constant concentration of 0.1 millimolar sodium chloride, while the concentration in the other compartment is varied from 0.1 millimolar to 1 molar. The open-circuit voltage generated across the membrane is measured using a high-impedance electrometer, and the resulting voltage is plotted as a function of the logarithm of the activity ratio between the two compartments. The slope of this plot is used to calculate the cationic transference number, which quantifies the membrane’s preference for transporting sodium ions over chloride ions. Membranes with no polyelectrolyte coating exhibit a moderate cationic transference number of approximately 0.6 due to the inherent anionic surface charge of the polycarbonate substrate. Membranes with one bilayer and no cross-linking show a reduced transference number, indicating that the initial polyelectrolyte layer disrupts the natural charge environment of the pore. However, as the number of bilayers increases to three or five and the film is cross-linked with glutaraldehyde, the transference number increases to values exceeding 0.7, demonstrating a significant enhancement in cationic selectivity. This increase is attributed to the asymmetric charge modification induced by glutaraldehyde, which enhances the net negative charge of the film and thereby strengthens the electrostatic repulsion of anions. In contrast, membranes cross-linked with EDC exhibit a transference number that remains approximately constant across bilayer numbers, consistent with the preservation of net charge and the absence of a net electrostatic bias toward cations.

Ionic conductivity is measured using a Swagelok-style cell in which multiple membranes are stacked in series and placed in direct contact with stainless steel current collectors, eliminating the influence of bulk electrolyte resistance. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy is performed over a frequency range of 10 hertz to 1 megahertz, and the real component of the impedance at zero imaginary impedance is recorded as the total resistance of the membrane stack. The resistance per membrane is calculated from the slope of the resistance versus number of membranes plot, and the ionic conductivity is derived from the membrane thickness and area. The uncoated polycarbonate membrane exhibits the highest conductivity, as expected, due to the absence of any obstructing material within the pores. The introduction of a single bilayer reduces the conductivity by approximately 40 percent, reflecting the partial occlusion of the pore lumen by the polyelectrolyte. However, as the number of bilayers increases to five, the conductivity does not continue to decrease but instead recovers to a value nearly equal to that of the uncoated membrane. This counterintuitive result is explained by the formation of a continuous, hydrated polyelectrolyte network within the pores that facilitates ion transport through a combination of vehicular and structural diffusion mechanisms. The cross-linking process further enhances conductivity by preventing the collapse of the polyelectrolyte film and maintaining open, interconnected pathways for ion migration. Membranes that are not cross-linked exhibit the lowest conductivity, as the polyelectrolyte film is prone to swelling, re-dissolution, and incomplete pore coverage, leading to increased tortuosity and reduced effective ion transport pathways. The conductivity of EDC-cross-linked membranes is nearly identical to that of glutaraldehyde-cross-linked membranes, confirming that the type of cross-linking agent does not significantly affect the ionic transport efficiency, provided that the film is fully stabilized.

The combination of high ionic selectivity and high ionic conductivity achieved by the glutaraldehyde-cross-linked five-bilayer membrane represents a breakthrough in membrane design. Prior art membranes typically exhibit a trade-off between these two properties: increasing selectivity by enhancing surface charge density often leads to increased resistance due to the formation of a dense, charge-rich barrier that impedes ion mobility. In contrast, the present invention achieves both high selectivity and high conductivity simultaneously by leveraging the unique architecture of the nanoporous substrate and the controlled chemical modification of the polyelectrolyte film. The nanopores provide a confined environment in which the polyelectrolyte layers are forced into close proximity, enabling efficient cross-linking and the formation of a continuous, charge-tunable transport pathway. The selective cross-linking with glutaraldehyde enhances selectivity without compromising conductivity, because the reduction in cationic charge is offset by the preservation of anionic charge and the maintenance of a hydrated, open structure within the pores. This decoupling of selectivity and conductivity is not possible with conventional ion-exchange membranes, which rely on homogeneous bulk charge distributions that couple these properties inextricably.

The scalability of the fabrication method is demonstrated by the successful production of membranes with active areas exceeding 64 square centimeters, a scale that is orders of magnitude larger than those typically reported in the literature for LbL-deposited polyelectrolyte membranes. The entire process—from substrate cleaning to cross-linking—can be performed using standard laboratory equipment and aqueous solutions of inexpensive, non-toxic chemicals, making it amenable to roll-to-roll or batch processing in industrial settings. The membranes are stable under prolonged exposure to aqueous electrolytes, including solutions of varying pH and ionic strength, and exhibit no measurable degradation in performance after multiple cycles of operation. The absence of fluorinated or halogenated materials renders the membranes environmentally benign and eliminates the need for costly disposal procedures associated with traditional ion-exchange membranes.

In summary, the invention provides a novel, scalable, and highly tunable method for fabricating ion-selective membranes that overcome the fundamental limitations of prior art technologies. By combining the simplicity of layer-by-layer deposition with the precision of selective chemical cross-linking, the invention enables the independent optimization of ionic selectivity and ionic conductivity, a capability that has eluded researchers for decades. The resulting membranes are cost-effective, environmentally friendly, and capable of operating under the demanding conditions required for industrial electrochemical applications, making them ideal candidates for next-generation electrodialysis, flow battery, and water purification systems.
