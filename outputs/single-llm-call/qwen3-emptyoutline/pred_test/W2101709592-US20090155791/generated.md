# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of molecular biology and epigenetic analysis, specifically to methods and compositions for the accurate, proportional, and bias-free amplification of DNA sequences containing CpG dinucleotides following sodium bisulfite conversion. The invention provides novel primer designs and optimized polymerase chain reaction (PCR) protocols that enable the selective and balanced amplification of both methylated and unmethylated alleles from bisulfite-treated DNA templates, thereby overcoming the longstanding technical limitation of PCR amplification bias that has historically compromised the quantitative reliability of methylation analysis. The invention is particularly applicable to diagnostic, prognostic, and research applications in oncology, developmental biology, neurology, and other fields where precise quantification of DNA methylation patterns is critical for understanding disease mechanisms, monitoring therapeutic response, or detecting early epigenetic alterations. The methods described herein are compatible with downstream analytical techniques including nucleic acid sequencing, primer extension, restriction enzyme digestion, high-resolution melting curve analysis, and other post-amplification detection modalities, thereby offering a robust and universally applicable platform for methylation-sensitive assays that require fidelity to biological reality.

## BACKGROUND OF INVENTION

The epigenetic modification of DNA through the methylation of cytosine residues at CpG dinucleotides plays a fundamental role in the regulation of gene expression, genomic imprinting, X-chromosome inactivation, and the maintenance of cellular identity during development. Aberrant DNA methylation patterns—characterized by global hypomethylation and locus-specific hypermethylation—are among the most consistent and earliest molecular alterations observed in a wide spectrum of human diseases, particularly cancer. The detection and quantification of these methylation changes have therefore become central to the development of non-invasive diagnostic biomarkers, the stratification of patient risk, and the monitoring of disease progression or therapeutic efficacy. Consequently, numerous methodologies have been developed over the past two decades to assess DNA methylation status with increasing sensitivity and specificity. Among these, sodium bisulfite conversion remains the gold standard technique for distinguishing methylated from unmethylated cytosines at single-base resolution. This chemical treatment selectively deaminates unmethylated cytosines to uracils, which are subsequently amplified as thymines during PCR, while methylated cytosines remain unchanged and are amplified as cytosines. The resulting sequence divergence between methylated and unmethylated alleles allows for the design of molecular assays capable of detecting methylation at specific genomic loci.

Despite the widespread adoption of bisulfite conversion, the subsequent amplification step has remained a critical bottleneck in achieving accurate and reproducible methylation quantification. The majority of current methods rely on either methylation-specific PCR (MSP), which employs primers designed to bind exclusively to either methylated or unmethylated sequences based on the presence or absence of CpG sites within the primer binding region, or methylation-independent PCR (MIP), which uses primers designed to amplify both methylated and unmethylated templates equally, allowing for post-amplification analysis of methylation status through sequencing, melting curve analysis, restriction digestion, or other methods. While MSP offers high sensitivity and is widely used in clinical settings, it suffers from inherent limitations including the potential for false positives due to incomplete bisulfite conversion, primer cross-reactivity, and an inability to provide quantitative data on the relative proportions of methylated and unmethylated alleles. MIP-based methods, by contrast, are theoretically superior for quantitative analysis because they preserve the relative abundance of methylated and unmethylated templates in the amplified product, thereby reflecting the true biological state of the sample. However, the practical utility of MIP has been severely undermined by a persistent and poorly understood phenomenon known as PCR amplification bias.

This bias manifests as a consistent and disproportionate preferential amplification of unmethylated DNA sequences over methylated ones, even when primers are meticulously designed to avoid CpG dinucleotides entirely, as had long been recommended in the field. The origin of this bias was first systematically documented by Warnecke et al., who demonstrated that under standard PCR conditions, unmethylated templates were amplified with significantly greater efficiency than their methylated counterparts, leading to an underestimation of methylation levels that could be as severe as tenfold or greater. Subsequent studies confirmed this observation across diverse genomic regions and tissue types, yet the prevailing consensus in the literature continued to advocate for the exclusion of CpG sites from primer sequences as the primary strategy to achieve methylation independence. This recommendation, while logically sound in principle, proved empirically flawed: the very design criteria intended to ensure unbiased amplification instead exacerbated the bias by reducing the binding affinity of primers to methylated templates, which retain cytosines at CpG positions, while leaving primers optimally complementary to unmethylated templates, which contain thymines at those positions. As a result, many laboratories using MIP-based protocols—including bisulfite sequencing, methylation-sensitive high-resolution melting (MS-HRM), and restriction enzyme-based assays—were unknowingly generating data that misrepresented the true methylation landscape of their samples.

Efforts to mitigate this bias through optimization of PCR conditions, including variation in annealing temperature, magnesium concentration, polymerase type, and cycle number, yielded inconsistent results. The use of the Stoffel fragment of Taq polymerase, which lacks 5’→3’ exonuclease activity, was reported to reduce bias slightly, but this approach was not broadly adopted due to its limited efficacy and reduced amplification efficiency. Other strategies, such as the use of modified nucleotides, additives like betaine or DMSO, or the application of touchdown PCR protocols, provided marginal improvements at best and often introduced new sources of variability. The field thus found itself trapped in a paradox: the most widely accepted guidelines for primer design, intended to ensure quantitative accuracy, were in fact the primary cause of quantitative inaccuracy. This fundamental flaw rendered many published methylation studies unreliable, particularly those relying on MIP-based detection for clinical or translational applications where precise measurement of methylation levels is essential for interpretation.

The consequences of this bias extend beyond mere technical inconvenience. In cancer diagnostics, for example, the failure to detect low-level methylation in promoter regions of tumor suppressor genes can lead to false-negative results, delaying diagnosis or misclassifying patient risk. In developmental studies, the underestimation of methylation changes during cellular differentiation can obscure critical epigenetic transitions. In longitudinal monitoring of disease, the inability to accurately track methylation dynamics over time can invalidate conclusions regarding therapeutic response or disease progression. Moreover, the discrepancy between MSP and MIP results—where MSP frequently reports higher methylation positivity than MIP—has been attributed solely to the higher sensitivity of MSP, but this explanation fails to account for the possibility that MIP-based methods are systematically underestimating methylation levels due to amplification bias. This has led to confusion in the literature, inconsistent clinical interpretations, and a lack of reproducibility across laboratories.

The present invention resolves this long-standing problem by fundamentally rethinking the design of methylation-independent primers. Rather than avoiding CpG dinucleotides entirely, the invention demonstrates that the deliberate and controlled inclusion of one or a limited number of CpG sites within the primer sequence—strategically positioned away from the 3’ terminus—can restore the binding affinity of primers to methylated templates, thereby counterbalancing the inherent amplification bias that favors unmethylated sequences. This counterintuitive approach leverages the very sequence difference created by bisulfite conversion to create a thermodynamic equilibrium in primer binding: at optimal annealing temperatures, the primers bind with near-equal efficiency to both methylated and unmethylated templates, resulting in proportional amplification that accurately reflects the original methylation ratio in the sample. Furthermore, the invention establishes that the degree of bias correction is not fixed but is instead tunable through precise control of the annealing temperature, enabling researchers to select conditions that favor either balanced amplification, enrichment of methylated alleles, or enrichment of unmethylated alleles, depending on the analytical goal. This level of control has never before been achieved and represents a paradigm shift in the design and execution of methylation analysis.

The prior art, including commercial primer design software and published protocols, remains entirely incapable of generating such primers, as these tools are programmed to either exclude CpG sites entirely (for MIP) or to maximize CpG content at the 3’ end (for MSP). The present invention introduces a new class of primers—termed “bias-correcting methylation-independent primers”—that defy conventional design rules yet deliver unprecedented accuracy in methylation quantification. The invention further provides a comprehensive framework for validating these primers, including the use of spiked template mixtures, melting curve analysis, and control reactions with non-bisulfite-treated DNA to ensure specificity. This invention thus fills a critical gap in molecular diagnostics and epigenetic research, providing a reliable, reproducible, and universally applicable method for the accurate detection and quantification of DNA methylation that is essential for advancing precision medicine and understanding the epigenetic basis of human disease.

### Importance of DNA Methylation

DNA methylation is a covalent modification that involves the addition of a methyl group to the fifth carbon of cytosine residues, predominantly occurring at CpG dinucleotides in mammalian genomes. This epigenetic mark is dynamically regulated during development and is essential for normal cellular function, including the silencing of transposable elements, the establishment of genomic imprinting, and the regulation of tissue-specific gene expression. The precise spatial and temporal control of methylation patterns ensures that cells maintain their identity and function throughout the life cycle. Disruption of these patterns, whether through environmental exposure, aging, genetic mutations in methylation machinery, or disease processes, can lead to profound biological consequences. In cancer, for instance, hypermethylation of promoter regions of tumor suppressor genes such as BRCA1, MLH1, CDKN2A, and RASSF1A is a well-documented mechanism of gene inactivation that contributes to uncontrolled cell proliferation, evasion of apoptosis, and metastatic potential. Conversely, global hypomethylation, particularly in repetitive elements and intergenic regions, is associated with genomic instability, chromosomal rearrangements, and reactivation of silenced oncogenes.

Beyond oncology, aberrant DNA methylation has been implicated in a broad array of neurological disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, autism spectrum disorder, and schizophrenia, where methylation changes in neuronal genes correlate with cognitive decline and synaptic dysfunction. In autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus and rheumatoid arthritis, altered methylation profiles in immune cells have been linked to dysregulated cytokine production and loss of self-tolerance. In developmental disorders like Rett syndrome and Fragile X syndrome, mutations in genes encoding methyl-CpG-binding proteins or DNA methyltransferases directly disrupt methylation homeostasis, underscoring the centrality of this epigenetic mechanism to neurodevelopment. Even in aging, a progressive loss of methylation at specific loci has been proposed as a biomarker of biological age, with potential applications in longevity research and preventive medicine.

The clinical utility of DNA methylation as a biomarker is further enhanced by its stability in bodily fluids, including blood, urine, saliva, and cerebrospinal fluid, making it an ideal candidate for non-invasive liquid biopsy approaches. Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) carrying tumor-specific methylation signatures can be detected at very low frequencies, enabling early cancer detection, minimal residual disease monitoring, and real-time assessment of treatment response without the need for invasive tissue biopsies. For example, methylation of the SEPT9 gene in plasma has been approved as a screening tool for colorectal cancer, and methylation panels for lung, breast, and ovarian cancers are under active clinical validation. However, the accuracy of these tests is entirely dependent on the fidelity of the underlying methylation detection methodology. If the amplification step introduces systematic bias that underrepresents methylated alleles, then even the most sensitive detection platform will yield false-negative results, compromising diagnostic sensitivity and clinical utility.

Moreover, the quantitative nature of methylation changes is often as important as their presence or absence. In many contexts, the degree of methylation—not merely binary classification as methylated or unmethylated—correlates with disease severity, prognosis, or response to epigenetic therapies such as DNA methyltransferase inhibitors. For instance, in myelodysplastic syndromes, the extent of promoter hypermethylation predicts response to azacitidine, while in glioblastoma, methylation levels of the MGMT promoter determine sensitivity to temozolomide. In these cases, a method that fails to accurately reflect the proportion of methylated alleles cannot provide actionable clinical information. The current standard methods, despite their widespread use, are fundamentally inadequate for such quantitative applications due to the persistent amplification bias that distorts the true methylation ratio.

The invention described herein directly addresses this critical limitation by enabling the accurate, proportional, and reproducible amplification of methylated and unmethylated alleles from bisulfite-converted DNA. By restoring the balance between template types during PCR, the invention ensures that downstream analytical methods—whether sequencing, melting curve analysis, or restriction digestion—reflect the actual biological state of the sample. This is not merely a technical refinement but a foundational advancement that elevates methylation analysis from a qualitative or semi-quantitative tool to a truly quantitative and clinically reliable methodology. The ability to detect methylation at physiologically relevant levels, even when present in a minority of alleles, transforms the potential of epigenetic biomarkers in early diagnosis, risk stratification, and personalized therapeutic decision-making. The invention thus represents a pivotal step forward in the translation of epigenetic research into clinical practice, ensuring that the biological significance of DNA methylation is no longer obscured by technical artifacts.

## SUMMARY OF INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and non-obvious method for the accurate and proportional amplification of methylated and unmethylated DNA sequences following sodium bisulfite conversion, thereby overcoming the persistent and previously intractable problem of PCR amplification bias that has plagued methylation analysis for decades. The invention is based on the discovery that the deliberate and controlled inclusion of one or a limited number of CpG dinucleotides within the primer sequence—specifically positioned at least two nucleotides away from the 3’ terminus—can effectively counterbalance the inherent amplification bias that favors unmethylated templates, resulting in a near-equimolar amplification of both methylated and unmethylated alleles under optimized thermal cycling conditions. This represents a radical departure from conventional primer design guidelines, which have long mandated the complete exclusion of CpG sites from methylation-independent primers, and demonstrates that such exclusion is not only unnecessary but actively detrimental to quantitative accuracy.

The invention further provides a system for tuning the degree of bias correction through precise control of the annealing temperature during PCR amplification. By adjusting the annealing temperature within a defined range, typically between 55°C and 65°C, the relative binding efficiency of the primers to methylated versus unmethylated templates can be modulated, enabling the user to achieve one of three distinct analytical outcomes: (1) balanced amplification, where methylated and unmethylated templates are amplified in proportions that reflect their original abundance in the sample; (2) selective enrichment of methylated templates, where higher annealing temperatures favor binding to methylated sequences due to increased stability of C–C mismatches; or (3) selective enrichment of unmethylated templates, where lower annealing temperatures reduce stringency and allow non-specific binding, thereby amplifying more unmethylated DNA. This tunability provides unprecedented flexibility in experimental design, allowing researchers to optimize conditions for detection sensitivity, quantification accuracy, or enrichment of rare methylated alleles, depending on the specific application.

The invention further encompasses a set of design rules for the construction of bias-correcting methylation-independent primers, which include: (a) the inclusion of one to three CpG dinucleotides per primer, with preference for one; (b) the placement of these CpG sites at least two nucleotides from the 3’ end to prevent complete methylation specificity; (c) the calculation of salt-adjusted melting temperatures (Tm) to ensure optimal primer performance, with a target Tm of approximately 65°C to allow for PCR cycling at 60°C; (d) the incorporation of one or more thymine residues at or near the 3’ end of each primer to ensure specificity for bisulfite-converted DNA and to prevent amplification of unconverted genomic DNA; and (e) the validation of primer sets using spiked template mixtures of known methylated and unmethylated DNA, followed by analysis via high-resolution melting or sequencing to confirm proportional amplification across a range of annealing temperatures.

The invention also provides a novel class of primers specifically designed according to these rules, which are capable of amplifying bisulfite-converted DNA with minimal bias, even in complex biological samples such as whole blood, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissues, or cell-free DNA. These primers are compatible with standard PCR reagents and thermocyclers, require no specialized enzymes or modified nucleotides, and are readily integrated into existing workflows for methylation analysis, including bisulfite sequencing, primer extension, restriction enzyme digestion, and high-resolution melting curve analysis. The invention further includes a kit comprising these primers, along with control templates, buffer systems, and detailed protocols for validation and use, enabling widespread adoption in research and clinical laboratories.

The utility of the invention extends to the detection and quantification of methylation in a wide range of disorders, including but not limited to cancer, neurodegenerative diseases, autoimmune conditions, and developmental disorders. The method enables the reliable detection of low-abundance methylated alleles in heterogeneous samples, such as early-stage tumors or circulating tumor DNA, where previous methods failed due to amplification bias. Furthermore, the invention allows for the accurate comparison of methylation levels across multiple samples, time points, or treatment conditions, providing a robust platform for longitudinal monitoring and therapeutic assessment. The invention thus represents a transformative advancement in epigenetic diagnostics, enabling accurate, reproducible, and clinically meaningful methylation analysis that was previously unattainable.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### DEFINITIONS

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms shall have the meanings ascribed below, unless the context clearly indicates otherwise. The term “DNA methylation” refers to the covalent addition of a methyl group to the 5-carbon position of a cytosine residue within a CpG dinucleotide, resulting in the formation of 5-methylcytosine. The term “bisulfite conversion” refers to the chemical treatment of DNA with sodium bisulfite under acidic conditions, which selectively deaminates unmethylated cytosines to uracil, while leaving 5-methylcytosine unchanged, thereby creating sequence divergence between methylated and unmethylated alleles that can be exploited for downstream analysis. The term “methylation-independent primer” refers to a pair of oligonucleotide primers designed to bind to and amplify a target region of bisulfite-converted DNA regardless of the methylation status of CpG dinucleotides within the amplified region, such that the relative abundance of methylated and unmethylated alleles in the PCR product reflects their relative abundance in the original template. The term “bias-correcting methylation-independent primer” refers to a methylation-independent primer that contains one or more CpG dinucleotides strategically positioned within its sequence to counteract the preferential amplification of unmethylated templates during PCR, thereby enabling proportional amplification of both methylated and unmethylated alleles under optimized thermal cycling conditions.

The term “PCR amplification bias” refers to the non-equimolar amplification of methylated and unmethylated DNA templates following bisulfite conversion, wherein unmethylated templates are amplified with significantly greater efficiency than methylated templates, leading to an underrepresentation of methylated alleles in the final product. The term “annealing temperature” refers to the temperature at which primers bind to their complementary sequences on the DNA template during the PCR cycle, and which determines the stringency of hybridization. The term “salt-adjusted melting temperature” or “Tm” refers to the calculated temperature at which 50% of the primer-template duplexes are dissociated, adjusted for the concentration of monovalent cations (typically sodium) in the reaction buffer, and which provides a more accurate prediction of primer binding behavior under physiological PCR conditions than unadjusted Tm calculations. The term “CpG site” refers to a dinucleotide sequence in which a cytosine nucleotide is immediately followed by a guanine nucleotide, linked by a phosphodiester bond, and which is the primary site of DNA methylation in mammalian genomes. The term “3’ end” refers to the terminal nucleotide of an oligonucleotide at the hydroxyl group side of the sugar-phosphate backbone, which is the site of primer extension by DNA polymerase during PCR. The term “non-CpG C” refers to any cytosine residue within a DNA sequence that is not part of a CpG dinucleotide and which, following bisulfite conversion, is converted to uracil and subsequently amplified as thymine.

The term “proportional amplification” refers to the amplification of methylated and unmethylated DNA templates in a ratio that substantially corresponds to their relative abundance in the original sample, such that the methylation level measured in the PCR product is within ±10% of the true methylation level of the input DNA. The term “spiked template mixture” refers to a synthetic DNA sample prepared by mixing known quantities of fully methylated DNA with fully unmethylated DNA in defined proportions, such as 10%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 90% methylated, to serve as a control for assessing the accuracy of methylation quantification. The term “high-resolution melting curve analysis” refers to a post-PCR method for detecting sequence variations, including methylation differences, by monitoring the dissociation profile of double-stranded DNA as it is gradually heated, with small differences in melting temperature reflecting differences in base composition and methylation status. The term “methylation-specific PCR” or “MSP” refers to a PCR-based method that uses primers designed to bind exclusively to either methylated or unmethylated sequences, typically by incorporating CpG sites at or near the 3’ end to ensure specificity. The term “primer extension” refers to a method of methylation analysis in which a primer is annealed adjacent to a CpG site and extended by a DNA polymerase in the presence of labeled nucleotides, with the identity of the incorporated nucleotide indicating the methylation status of the template. The term “restriction enzyme digestion” refers to the cleavage of DNA by endonucleases that recognize specific sequences, some of which are methylation-sensitive and will only cut if the CpG site is unmethylated, thereby allowing methylation status to be inferred from fragment size.

The term “kit” refers to a packaged combination of reagents, primers, controls, and instructions provided for the performance of the methods described herein. The term “disorder” refers to any pathological condition in humans or animals in which aberrant DNA methylation patterns are associated with disease etiology, progression, diagnosis, prognosis, or response to therapy. The term “nucleic acid sequencing” refers to any method for determining the precise order of nucleotides in a DNA molecule, including Sanger sequencing, next-generation sequencing, pyrosequencing, or nanopore sequencing. The term “amplifying step” refers to the process of enzymatically replicating a target region of DNA using a polymerase, primers, nucleotides, and thermal cycling conditions. The term “unconverted DNA” refers to genomic DNA that has not been subjected to sodium bisulfite treatment and therefore retains its native cytosine residues, including those at CpG sites. The term “primer dimer” refers to a non-specific PCR product formed by the hybridization and extension of two primers to each other rather than to the target template. The term “secondary structure” refers to intramolecular folding of a primer, such as hairpins or stem-loops, that can interfere with efficient hybridization to the target DNA.

### Samples

The methods of the present invention are applicable to a wide variety of biological samples containing DNA that has been subjected to sodium bisulfite conversion. These samples may be derived from any tissue or biological fluid in which DNA methylation patterns are of diagnostic, prognostic, or research interest. Preferred sample types include peripheral blood, serum, plasma, cerebrospinal fluid, urine, sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, stool, and formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue sections. The invention is particularly well-suited for the analysis of cell-free DNA, including circulating tumor DNA, which is often present in very low quantities and requires highly sensitive and bias-free amplification to detect rare methylated alleles. The invention is also applicable to DNA extracted from fresh or frozen tissues, including tumor biopsies, normal adjacent tissue, and embryonic or stem cell cultures.

Prior to amplification, DNA must be extracted from the sample using standard methodologies, including phenol-chloroform extraction, silica-column-based purification, magnetic bead-based isolation, or automated extraction platforms. The quantity and quality of the extracted DNA should be assessed using spectrophotometry or fluorometry, and samples with a 260/280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 and a 260/230 ratio greater than 1.8 are preferred. DNA integrity may be evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis or capillary electrophoresis, with high molecular weight DNA being optimal for downstream applications. For samples with low DNA yield, such as cell-free DNA or microdissected tissue, whole genome amplification may be employed prior to bisulfite conversion, provided that the amplification method does not introduce methylation bias and that the resulting DNA is sufficiently representative of the original methylation landscape.

Following DNA extraction, the sample is subjected to sodium bisulfite conversion using commercially available kits or established protocols. The conversion process typically involves denaturation of DNA with alkali, followed by incubation with sodium bisulfite at a temperature between 50°C and 60°C for a duration of 4 to 16 hours. The reaction is then neutralized and desalted, and the converted DNA is purified using spin columns, magnetic beads, or ethanol precipitation. The efficiency of bisulfite conversion is critical and must be verified prior to amplification. This is typically achieved by amplifying a control region known to be completely unmethylated, such as the LINE-1 retrotransposon or the β-actin gene, and confirming that greater than 95% of cytosines outside of CpG contexts have been converted to thymines. Incomplete conversion may lead to false-positive signals and must be excluded from analysis.

The amount of bisulfite-converted DNA used in the amplification step is typically between 1 ng and 100 ng per reaction, with 10 ng being the preferred quantity for most applications. For highly degraded samples, such as archival formalin-fixed tissues, lower amounts may be used, but the total volume of DNA added to the PCR reaction should not exceed 10% of the total reaction volume to avoid inhibition. The invention is compatible with both singleplex and multiplex amplification formats, and the use of internal controls, such as reference genes with known methylation status, is recommended to ensure the reliability of results across samples. The invention may also be applied to samples containing mixed cell populations, such as heterogeneous tumors or blood samples containing leukocytes and tumor-derived DNA, where the methylation profile reflects a composite of multiple cellular sources. In such cases, the bias-correcting primers enable accurate quantification of methylation levels even when methylated alleles are present at low frequencies, such as 1% or less, thereby enhancing the sensitivity of detection in early-stage disease or minimal residual disease settings.

### Modification of DNA

The method of the present invention requires the prior modification of genomic DNA through sodium bisulfite treatment, which chemically distinguishes methylated from unmethylated cytosine residues by converting unmethylated cytosines to uracil while leaving 5-methylcytosine intact. This modification is a prerequisite for all downstream analyses that rely on sequence differences to infer methylation status. The bisulfite conversion process is performed under strictly controlled conditions to ensure complete and uniform conversion across all template molecules. The reaction is typically carried out in an acidic environment with a pH between 4.5 and 5.5, using a concentration of sodium bisulfite ranging from 2 M to 4 M, and incubated at temperatures between 50°C and 60°C for a period of 4 to 16 hours. The duration and temperature are selected based on the sample type and the desired balance between conversion efficiency and DNA degradation. Longer incubation times and higher temperatures increase the completeness of conversion but also increase the risk of DNA fragmentation, which may compromise amplification efficiency.

After conversion, the DNA is desalted and purified to remove residual bisulfite, which can inhibit subsequent enzymatic reactions. Purification is commonly achieved using silica membrane columns, magnetic beads, or ethanol precipitation, with the inclusion of carrier RNA or glycogen to improve recovery of low-concentration DNA. The purified DNA is then resuspended in a low-salt buffer, such as 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, and stored at –20°C until use. The quality of the converted DNA is assessed by measuring the conversion efficiency of non-CpG cytosines, which should exceed 95% to ensure that any remaining cytosines in the sequence are attributable to 5-methylcytosine rather than incomplete conversion. This is typically verified by sequencing or by amplifying a control region with known unmethylated status and confirming the absence of cytosine signals in the chromatogram.

The bisulfite modification fundamentally alters the sequence composition of the DNA, converting all unmethylated cytosines to thymines, while methylated cytosines remain as cytosines. As a result, the methylation status of a CpG site becomes encoded as a C/T polymorphism in the amplified product: a methylated CpG site will appear as a CC dinucleotide, whereas an unmethylated CpG site will appear as a CT or TT dinucleotide, depending on the strand. This creates a sequence divergence between methylated and unmethylated alleles that is exploited by the primers of the present invention. The primers are designed to bind to regions containing one or more CpG sites, and their binding affinity is determined by the degree of complementarity to the converted sequence. In conventional methylation-independent primers, which exclude CpG sites entirely, the primers are perfectly complementary to unmethylated templates but contain mismatches when binding to methylated templates, resulting in reduced binding efficiency and preferential amplification of unmethylated DNA. The invention overcomes this limitation by incorporating one or more CpG dinucleotides into the primer sequence, such that the primer contains cytosines at positions corresponding to CpG sites in the template. When the template is methylated, these cytosines in the primer form stable C–C pairs with the methylated cytosines in the template, whereas when the template is unmethylated, the primer cytosines form mismatched C–T pairs with thymines in the template. The strategic placement of these CpG sites within the primer allows for the fine-tuning of binding stability, enabling the primers to bind with near-equal efficiency to both methylated and unmethylated templates under optimized annealing conditions.

It is critical that the bisulfite conversion be complete and uniform across all molecules in the sample, as partial conversion can lead to ambiguous signals and false interpretations. To ensure this, the invention recommends the use of positive and negative controls in every experiment. A fully methylated control, such as in vitro methylated DNA from a bacterial source or synthetic methylated oligonucleotides, should be included to verify that the primers can bind to methylated templates. A fully unmethylated control, such as DNA from a cell line with known global hypomethylation or synthetic unmethylated oligonucleotides, should be included to verify that the primers can bind to unmethylated templates. Additionally, a no-template control should be included to detect contamination. The invention further requires that all samples be processed in parallel under identical conditions to minimize technical variability. The use of standardized protocols, calibrated instruments, and validated reagents is essential to ensure reproducibility across laboratories and over time.

### Methylation-Independent Primer

The methylation-independent primers of the present invention are oligonucleotides specifically designed to bind to bisulfite-converted DNA and amplify a target region containing one or more CpG dinucleotides, while enabling proportional amplification of both methylated and unmethylated alleles. Unlike conventional methylation-independent primers, which are designed to exclude all CpG dinucleotides to avoid methylation specificity, the primers of the present invention deliberately incorporate one to three CpG dinucleotides within their sequence, with a strong preference for a single CpG site per primer. The inclusion of these CpG sites is not arbitrary but is guided by a precise set of design criteria that ensure the primers do not become methylation-specific, yet are sufficiently complementary to methylated templates to counteract the inherent amplification bias that favors unmethylated sequences.

The CpG dinucleotides within the primer must be positioned at least two nucleotides away from the 3’ end to prevent complete methylation specificity. If a CpG site is located at or within one nucleotide of the 3’ terminus, the primer will bind almost exclusively to methylated templates, effectively converting the primer into a methylation-specific primer and negating the purpose of methylation-independent amplification. The optimal position for the CpG site is between the 5th and 15th nucleotide from the 3’ end, where the primer maintains sufficient flexibility to bind to both methylated and unmethylated templates depending on the annealing temperature. The primer length is typically between 18 and 30 nucleotides, with a preferred range of 22 to 26 nucleotides, to ensure sufficient specificity and melting temperature. The GC content of the primer should be between 40% and 60%, and the melting temperature (Tm) should be calculated using a salt-adjusted algorithm to account for the ionic strength of the PCR buffer, with a target Tm of approximately 65°C to allow for optimal PCR cycling at 60°C. The Tm of the forward and reverse primers should be matched within 1°C to ensure synchronous hybridization during amplification.

The primer sequence must also include at least one thymine residue at or near the 3’ end to ensure specificity for bisulfite-converted DNA. This thymine corresponds to a non-CpG cytosine in the original genomic sequence that has been converted to uracil and subsequently amplified as thymine. The presence of this thymine at the 3’ end prevents amplification of unconverted genomic DNA, which would otherwise contain cytosines at those positions and form mismatches with the primer. This feature is critical for avoiding false-positive signals and ensuring that the amplification is specific to bisulfite-converted material. The 3’ terminal nucleotide should not be a guanine, as G–T mismatches are relatively stable and may lead to non-specific extension. The preferred 3’ terminal nucleotide is a thymine or adenine, with thymine being most preferred.

The primers must be evaluated for secondary structure formation, including hairpins, self-dimers, and hetero-dimers, using established software tools such as OligoAnalyzer, Primer-BLAST, or Amplify. Primers with a predicted hairpin stability greater than –3 kcal/mol or a dimer formation energy greater than –5 kcal/mol should be discarded. The primers must also be checked for cross-reactivity with other genomic regions using BLAST or similar alignment tools to ensure specificity for the target locus. The primers of the present invention are designed to be used in pairs, with one forward and one reverse primer, and the amplicon length should be between 100 and 300 base pairs, with a preferred length of 150 to 250 base pairs, to ensure efficient amplification and compatibility with downstream analysis methods.

The design of these primers cannot be achieved using conventional primer design software, as these programs are programmed to either exclude CpG sites entirely (for MIP) or to maximize CpG content at the 3’ end (for MSP). The invention therefore requires manual design or the development of a specialized algorithm that incorporates the novel criteria described herein. The primers are synthesized using standard phosphoramidite chemistry and purified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) or polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) to ensure sequence fidelity. The primers are resuspended in TE buffer or nuclease-free water and stored at –20°C. The concentration of each primer in the PCR reaction is typically between 0.1 μM and 1.0 μM, with 0.2 μM being the preferred concentration for most applications.

### Amplifying Step

The amplifying step of the present invention is performed using standard polymerase chain reaction (PCR) conditions, with the critical distinction that the annealing temperature is carefully optimized to achieve proportional amplification of methylated and unmethylated templates. The PCR reaction mixture contains the bisulfite-converted DNA template, the bias-correcting methylation-independent primers, a thermostable DNA polymerase, deoxynucleotide triphosphates (dNTPs), a magnesium chloride solution, a reaction buffer, and optionally, additives such as bovine serum albumin or betaine to enhance amplification efficiency. The polymerase may be Taq DNA polymerase, a high-fidelity polymerase, or a blend of polymerases, with the Stoffel fragment of Taq polymerase being a preferred option due to its lack of 5’→3’ exonuclease activity, which reduces non-specific amplification. The concentration of dNTPs is typically 200 μM each, and the magnesium chloride concentration is optimized between 1.5 mM and 3.0 mM, with 2.0 mM being the preferred concentration for most primer sets.

The thermal cycling protocol consists of an initial denaturation step at 95°C for 3 to 5 minutes to fully denature the DNA and activate the polymerase, followed by 35 to 45 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 15 to 30 seconds, annealing at a temperature between 55°C and 65°C for 30 to 60 seconds, and extension at 72°C for 30 to 60 seconds per kilobase of amplicon length. A final extension step at 72°C for 5 to 10 minutes is included to ensure complete elongation of all amplicons. The number of cycles is kept to a minimum to avoid saturation and to preserve the proportional representation of methylated and unmethylated alleles. The annealing temperature is the most critical parameter in the amplifying step and must be empirically determined for each primer set using a series of temperature gradients. The optimal annealing temperature is identified as the temperature at which the amplification efficiency of methylated and unmethylated templates is balanced, as determined by high-resolution melting curve analysis or sequencing of the PCR product from a spiked template mixture containing known proportions of methylated and unmethylated DNA.

The invention demonstrates that at lower annealing temperatures (e.g., 55°C to 58°C), the primers bind with reduced stringency, allowing for non-specific hybridization and preferential amplification of unmethylated templates, which are inherently more complementary to the primer sequence due to the absence of CpG sites. At higher annealing temperatures (e.g., 62°C to 65°C), the primers bind with increased stringency, and the stability of C–C pairs formed between the primer cytosines and methylated template cytosines becomes dominant, leading to preferential amplification of methylated templates. At an intermediate annealing temperature (e.g., 59°C to 61°C), the binding affinity to methylated and unmethylated templates is nearly equal, resulting in proportional amplification that accurately reflects the original methylation ratio in the sample. This tunability allows the user to select the annealing temperature that best suits the analytical goal: for detection of rare methylated alleles in a background of unmethylated DNA, a higher annealing temperature may be selected to enrich for methylated templates; for quantitative comparison of methylation levels across samples, an intermediate annealing temperature is preferred to maintain proportionality.

The invention further requires that the amplification be performed in triplicate or greater to ensure reproducibility and that the amplification efficiency be monitored using a standard curve generated from serial dilutions of a fully methylated template spiked into an unmethylated background. The slope of the standard curve should be between –3.1 and –3.6, indicating an amplification efficiency of 90% to 100%. The coefficient of variation (CV) between replicates should be less than 10% for the methylation level to be considered reliable. The amplification products are visualized by agarose gel electrophoresis to confirm the presence of a single band of the expected size and to rule out primer dimers or non-specific amplification. The amplification step is performed in a thermal cycler with precise temperature control and uniform block heating to minimize well-to-well variability. The use of a hot-start polymerase is recommended to prevent non-specific amplification during reaction setup. The entire amplification process is conducted in a dedicated, contamination-free environment to prevent carryover contamination from previous PCR reactions, which could lead to false-positive results.

### Analysis of Amplified CpG-Containing Nucleic Acids

Following amplification, the resulting nucleic acid products containing CpG dinucleotides are analyzed using one or more downstream methods to determine the methylation status of the target region. The invention is compatible with a wide range of analytical techniques, including nucleic acid sequencing, primer extension, restriction enzyme digestion, high-resolution melting curve analysis, single-strand conformation polymorphism, and denaturing high-performance liquid chromatography. The choice of analytical method depends on the desired level of resolution, throughput, and quantification accuracy. The most preferred method is high-resolution melting curve analysis, as it allows for rapid, closed-tube, quantitative assessment of methylation levels without the need for additional processing steps.

High-resolution melting curve analysis is performed by gradually heating the amplified product from 65°C to 95°C at a rate of 0.02°C to 0.05°C per second while continuously monitoring the fluorescence of a double-stranded DNA-binding dye such as SYBR Green I, EvaGreen, or LCGreen Plus. The fluorescence signal decreases as the DNA strands dissociate, and the resulting melting curve is analyzed for shifts in the melting temperature (Tm), which reflect differences in base composition and methylation status. Methylated and unmethylated alleles, due to their differing C/T content, produce distinct melting profiles, and the proportion of each allele in the sample can be quantified by comparing the shape and Tm of the sample curve to those of reference standards with known methylation ratios. The invention enables the generation of highly reproducible melting curves due to the proportional amplification of both alleles, allowing for accurate quantification even at low methylation levels.

Nucleic acid sequencing, including Sanger sequencing or next-generation sequencing, may be performed to obtain base-pair resolution of methylation status across the entire amplified region. The amplified product is purified, cloned into a plasmid vector, and sequenced, or directly sequenced using pyrosequencing or bisulfite sequencing libraries. The sequencing data is analyzed using software that distinguishes C and T signals at CpG sites, and the percentage of methylation at each site is calculated as the ratio of C to (C+T) signals. The invention ensures that the sequencing results accurately reflect the true methylation levels in the original sample, as the amplification step does not introduce bias that would skew the relative abundance of methylated and unmethylated sequences.

Primer extension is performed by annealing a sequencing primer immediately adjacent to a CpG site of interest and extending it with a DNA polymerase in the presence of fluorescently labeled dideoxynucleotides or mass-modified nucleotides. The identity of the incorporated nucleotide indicates the methylation status of the template: a cytosine incorporation indicates a methylated CpG, while a thymine incorporation indicates an unmethylated CpG. The invention ensures that the primer extension signal is proportional to the abundance of each allele in the sample, as the amplification step did not distort the original ratio.

Restriction enzyme digestion is performed using methylation-sensitive restriction enzymes such as HpaII, BstUI, or AciI, which cleave DNA only if the CpG site is unmethylated. The digested products are separated by gel electrophoresis, and the relative intensities of the cut and uncut bands are used to estimate methylation levels. The invention ensures that both methylated and unmethylated templates are amplified in proportion, allowing for accurate estimation of the digestion ratio. The invention further enables the use of methylation-insensitive isoschizomers, such as MspI, as internal controls to normalize for DNA quantity and amplification efficiency.

All analytical methods require the inclusion of appropriate controls, including fully methylated, fully unmethylated, and no-template controls, to validate the accuracy and specificity of the results. The invention provides a robust platform for all of these methods by ensuring that the amplification step does not introduce quantitative distortion, thereby allowing the analytical method to reflect the true biological state of the sample.

### Nucleic Acid Sequencing

Nucleic acid sequencing of the amplified CpG-containing products is a preferred method for obtaining single-base resolution of methylation status across the entire target region. The invention enables the use of sequencing techniques such as Sanger sequencing, pyrosequencing, and next-generation bisulfite sequencing with unprecedented accuracy, as the amplification step does not introduce bias that would distort the relative abundance of methylated and unmethylated alleles. In Sanger sequencing, the amplified product is purified and directly sequenced using a sequencing primer that anneals within the amplicon. The resulting chromatogram displays overlapping C and T peaks at CpG sites, with the relative peak heights reflecting the proportion of methylated and unmethylated alleles. The methylation level at each CpG site is calculated as the ratio of the C peak height to the sum of the C and T peak heights. The invention ensures that these ratios are accurate and reproducible, as the amplification step did not preferentially amplify one allele over the other.

For samples with low methylation levels or heterogeneous cell populations, cloning of the amplified product into a bacterial vector followed by sequencing of individual clones may be performed. Each clone represents a single DNA molecule from the original sample, and the methylation pattern of each clone is determined by sequencing. The percentage of methylated clones provides a direct measure of the proportion of methylated alleles in the sample. The invention ensures that the distribution of methylation patterns among clones accurately reflects the original sample, as the amplification step did not favor the amplification of certain methylation states.

Pyrosequencing is a quantitative sequencing method that measures the release of pyrophosphate during nucleotide incorporation and is particularly well-suited for analyzing short regions containing a few CpG sites. The amplified product is immobilized on streptavidin-coated beads, denatured, and sequenced by sequential addition of nucleotides. The light signal generated during incorporation is proportional to the number of nucleotides added, allowing for precise quantification of C and T incorporation at each CpG site. The invention ensures that the quantification is accurate and linear across a wide range of methylation levels, from 1% to 100%, due to the proportional amplification of both alleles.

Next-generation bisulfite sequencing involves the preparation of sequencing libraries from the amplified products, followed by high-throughput sequencing on platforms such as Illumina or Ion Torrent. The methylation status of each cytosine in the sequence is determined by comparing the sequence reads to the reference genome, with C signals indicating methylated cytosines and T signals indicating unmethylated cytosines. The invention enables the generation of highly accurate methylation profiles across the entire genome or targeted regions, as the amplification step does not introduce the systematic bias that has historically plagued bisulfite sequencing studies. This is particularly important for studies of epigenetic heterogeneity in cancer, where methylation patterns can vary dramatically between individual cells or subclones. The invention thus provides a reliable foundation for the generation of high-resolution methylation maps that accurately reflect biological reality.

### Primer Extension

Primer extension is a targeted method for determining the methylation status of specific CpG sites within an amplified region. The invention enables the use of primer extension with high accuracy and quantitative precision by ensuring that the amplification step does not distort the relative abundance of methylated and unmethylated templates. In this method, a sequencing primer is designed to anneal immediately adjacent to the CpG site of interest, with the 3’ end of the primer positioned one nucleotide upstream of the cytosine to be interrogated. The primer is then extended in the presence of DNA polymerase and a mixture of dideoxynucleotides labeled with distinct fluorescent dyes, or with mass-modified nucleotides for mass spectrometry-based detection. The identity of the incorporated nucleotide—cytosine or thymine—indicates the methylation status of the template: incorporation of cytosine indicates a methylated CpG, while incorporation of thymine indicates an unmethylated CpG.

The amount of each nucleotide incorporated is proportional to the abundance of the corresponding allele in the template, allowing for the quantification of methylation levels as a percentage of total signal. The invention ensures that this quantification is accurate because the amplification step did not preferentially amplify one allele over the other. The method is particularly useful for analyzing single CpG sites or small clusters of CpGs, such as those found in promoter regions of tumor suppressor genes. The primer extension reaction is typically performed in a thermal cycler using a cycle of denaturation, annealing, and extension, followed by detection using capillary electrophoresis or mass spectrometry. The invention is compatible with both singleplex and multiplex primer extension formats, allowing for the simultaneous interrogation of multiple CpG sites in a single reaction.

The use of unlabeled nucleotides and subsequent detection by high-performance liquid chromatography or enzymatic methods is also possible. The invention further enables the use of allele-specific primer extension, in which the primer is designed to terminate at the CpG site and only extend if the template matches the primer sequence. The invention ensures that the extension efficiency is balanced between methylated and unmethylated templates, preventing the overrepresentation of one allele. This method is particularly valuable in clinical settings where rapid, quantitative, and cost-effective methylation analysis is required, such as in the monitoring of minimal residual disease or the assessment of response to epigenetic therapy.

### Restriction Enzyme Digestion

Restriction enzyme digestion is a well-established method for assessing DNA methylation status based on the differential cleavage of DNA by enzymes that are sensitive to the methylation state of specific CpG sites. The invention enables the use of restriction enzyme digestion with high accuracy and reliability by ensuring that the amplification step does not introduce bias that would distort the relative abundance of methylated and unmethylated templates. In this method, the amplified product is digested with a methylation-sensitive restriction enzyme such as HpaII, which recognizes the sequence CCGG and cleaves only when the internal cytosine is unmethylated. The enzyme will not cleave if the cytosine is methylated. The digested fragments are then separated by agarose or polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and the relative intensities of the cut and uncut bands are used to estimate the proportion of methylated alleles in the sample.

The invention ensures that the ratio of cut to uncut fragments accurately reflects the true methylation level in the original sample, as the amplification step did not preferentially amplify one allele over the other. This is in contrast to conventional methods, in which amplification bias leads to an overrepresentation of unmethylated templates and an underestimation of methylation levels. The invention further enables the use of methylation-insensitive isoschizomers, such as MspI, which recognizes the same sequence as HpaII but cleaves regardless of methylation status. The use of MspI as an internal control allows for normalization of DNA quantity and amplification efficiency, ensuring that differences in band intensity are attributable to methylation status rather than technical variability.

The digestion reaction is performed in a buffer optimized for the specific enzyme, with incubation at 37°C for 1 to 4 hours. The reaction is terminated by heat inactivation or the addition of EDTA, and the products are analyzed by electrophoresis. The intensity of the bands is quantified using densitometry or fluorescence imaging, and the percentage of methylation is calculated as the ratio of the uncut band intensity to the total band intensity (cut + uncut). The invention enables this calculation to be performed with high precision, even at low methylation levels, due to the proportional amplification of both alleles. The method is particularly useful for high-throughput screening of methylation status in large sample cohorts, as it is relatively low-cost and does not require specialized equipment beyond a standard thermal cycler and gel imaging system.

## Disorders

The methods and compositions of the present invention are applicable to the detection, diagnosis, prognosis, and monitoring of a wide range of human disorders in which aberrant DNA methylation plays a causal, contributory, or predictive role. The invention is particularly valuable for disorders in which methylation changes occur at low frequency, are present in heterogeneous cell populations, or require precise quantification for clinical decision-making. In oncology, the invention enables the accurate detection of promoter hypermethylation in tumor suppressor genes such as BRCA1, MLH1, CDKN2A, RASSF1A, APC, and GSTP1 in cancers of the breast, colon, lung, prostate, ovary, and pancreas. The invention allows for the detection of methylation in circulating tumor DNA at levels as low as 0.1%, enabling early diagnosis, monitoring of minimal residual disease, and assessment of therapeutic response to epigenetic drugs such as azacitidine and decitabine.

In neurodegenerative disorders, the invention enables the quantification of methylation changes in genes such as SNCA, PARK2, MAPT, and BIN1 in Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and frontotemporal dementia, where methylation alterations in brain tissue and peripheral blood correlate with disease progression and cognitive decline. In psychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, the invention allows for the detection of methylation changes in genes such as RELN, GAD1, and COMT in blood and postmortem brain samples, providing a molecular signature that may aid in diagnosis or stratification of treatment response.

In autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, and multiple sclerosis, the invention enables the detection of methylation dysregulation in immune cell populations, particularly in CD4+ T cells and monocytes, where hypomethylation of interferon-regulated genes contributes to autoimmunity. The invention allows for the monitoring of methylation changes over time, providing a biomarker for disease activity and response to immunomodulatory therapies.

In developmental and congenital disorders, the invention enables the detection of imprinting defects in syndromes such as Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome, Prader-Willi syndrome, and Angelman syndrome, where methylation at imprinting control regions is disrupted. The invention also enables the detection of methylation changes in placental tissue and cord blood as potential biomarkers of fetal exposure to environmental toxins or maternal stress.

In aging and age-related diseases, the invention enables the quantification of epigenetic clocks based on methylation levels at specific CpG sites in genes such as ELOVL2, FHL2, and KLF14, providing a measure of biological age that may predict longevity, frailty, or risk of age-related disease. The invention further enables the detection of methylation changes in stem cell populations and regenerative tissues, providing insights into cellular aging and the potential for rejuvenation therapies.

The invention is also applicable to infectious diseases, where pathogen-specific methylation patterns in host DNA may reflect immune response or disease stage, and to transplant medicine, where donor-derived cell-free DNA methylation signatures may be used to detect graft rejection. The invention thus provides a universal platform for methylation analysis across disciplines, enabling accurate, reproducible, and clinically meaningful detection of epigenetic alterations that were previously obscured by amplification bias.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

A primer set was designed to amplify a 192-base pair region of the promoter of the tumor suppressor gene CDKN2A, which contains three CpG sites at positions –120, –105, and –92 relative to the transcription start site. Conventional methylation-independent primers, designed according to published guidelines that exclude CpG sites, were tested alongside primers designed according to the invention. The conventional primers produced a strong amplification product but showed a marked bias toward unmethylated templates, as determined by high-resolution melting curve analysis of a 50:50 mixture of fully methylated and fully unmethylated DNA. The melting curve displayed a single dominant peak corresponding to unmethylated DNA, with a minor shoulder representing methylated DNA, indicating that the methylated allele was amplified at less than 20% of the efficiency of the unmethylated allele. In contrast, primers designed according to the invention included a single CpG dinucleotide at position 12 from the 3’ end of the forward primer and a single CpG at position 14 from the 3’ end of the reverse primer. When amplified at an annealing temperature of 60°C, the product from the same 50:50 mixture displayed a symmetric melting curve with a single peak at 82.5°C, indicating proportional amplification of both alleles. The methylation level was calculated as 49.8%, within 0.2% of the expected value. The same primers were tested on a series of spiked mixtures containing 0%, 10%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% methylated DNA, and the measured methylation levels showed a linear correlation (R² = 0.998) with the expected values across the entire range. The primers did not amplify unconverted genomic DNA, confirming specificity for bisulfite-converted material. The amplification efficiency was 97%, with a coefficient of variation between replicates of less than 5%. These results demonstrate that the primers of the invention enable accurate and proportional amplification of methylated and unmethylated alleles, overcoming the bias that plagues conventional methods.

### Example 2

A primer set was designed to amplify a 210-base pair region of the MLH1 promoter, which contains five CpG sites. Conventional primers, designed without CpG sites, were tested alongside primers of the invention, which included two CpG dinucleotides at positions 8 and 15 from the 3’ end of the forward primer and one CpG at position 10 from the 3’ end of the reverse primer. The conventional primers were unable to amplify the methylated template at any annealing temperature, producing no detectable product when tested on fully methylated DNA. In contrast, the primers of the invention amplified both methylated and unmethylated templates efficiently at an annealing temperature of 61°C. High-resolution melting analysis of a 10% methylated spike into an unmethylated background revealed a clear, quantifiable signal with a methylation level of 9.7%, compared to 2.1% measured using conventional primers. The same primers were tested on 12 clinical samples of colorectal cancer tissue and matched normal mucosa. The conventional primers failed to detect methylation in three samples that were later confirmed to be methylated by bisulfite sequencing. The primers of the invention detected methylation in all five samples known to be methylated and showed no false positives in the seven unmethylated samples. The correlation between the primers of the invention and bisulfite sequencing was R² = 0.98, compared to R² = 0.41 for conventional primers. These results demonstrate that the primers of the invention significantly improve sensitivity and accuracy in clinical samples.

### Example 3

A primer set was designed to amplify a 150-base pair region of the RASSF1A promoter, which contains four CpG sites. The primers of the invention included one CpG at position 10 from the 3’ end of the forward primer and one CpG at position 12 from the 3’ end of the reverse primer. The primers were tested across a range of annealing temperatures from 55°C to 65°C. At 55°C, the amplification was biased toward unmethylated templates, with a measured methylation level of 32% in a 50:50 mixture. At 65°C, the amplification was biased toward methylated templates, with a measured methylation level of 78%. At 60°C, the measured methylation level was 51%, within 2% of the expected value. The same primers were used to analyze 50 plasma samples from patients with non-small cell lung cancer and 50 healthy controls. Using conventional primers, only 12% of cancer samples were positive for methylation. Using the primers of the invention, 48% of cancer samples were positive, and the methylation level correlated significantly with tumor stage (p < 0.001). The primers were also tested on cell-free DNA from patients undergoing chemotherapy, and methylation levels decreased significantly after treatment, demonstrating utility for monitoring therapeutic response. These results demonstrate that the tunability of the annealing temperature allows for flexible application of the invention to different analytical needs.

### Example 5

The primers of the invention were used to analyze methylation in circulating tumor DNA from 100 patients with early-stage breast cancer and 100 age-matched healthy controls. The target region was the promoter of the BRCA1 gene, which contains six CpG sites. The primers included one CpG at position 9 from the 3’ end of the forward primer and one CpG at position 11 from the 3’ end of the reverse primer. Amplification was performed at 60.5°C. These results demonstrate that the invention enables the detection of clinically relevant methylation changes in cell-free DNA with high sensitivity and specificity, providing a powerful tool for non-invasive cancer detection and risk stratification.
