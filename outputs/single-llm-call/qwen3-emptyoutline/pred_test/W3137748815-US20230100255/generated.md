# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of medical image analysis and computer-aided diagnosis, specifically to a system and method for interactive three-dimensional segmentation of anatomical structures in medical imaging datasets using contextual deep learning. The invention enables a clinician to provide a single manual contour on one image slice of a volumetric dataset—such as a computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance (MR) scan—and automatically propagate that segmentation across the entire volume without requiring prior training on the specific anatomical structure being segmented. This is achieved through a novel deep neural network architecture that interprets contextual relationships between the provided manual contour and the surrounding image intensities, rather than relying on structure-specific features learned during training. The method is particularly suited for clinical applications where rapid, accurate, and adaptable segmentation is required, such as radiotherapy planning, surgical navigation, and diagnostic assessment, especially in cases where training data for the target structure is limited, unavailable, or structurally heterogeneous. The invention overcomes the limitations of conventional fully automated segmentation systems, which are constrained by their dependence on large, structure-specific training datasets, and surpasses the capabilities of traditional interactive segmentation tools, which require repeated manual input across multiple slices. By leveraging context as the primary determinant of segmentation, the invention provides a generalizable, user-guided framework that adapts to previously unseen anatomical entities with minimal human intervention, thereby significantly reducing contouring time while maintaining high segmentation fidelity.

## BACKGROUND OF INVENTION

### Image Segmentation

The accurate delineation of anatomical structures within medical images is a foundational task in modern clinical practice, underpinning critical applications such as radiotherapy treatment planning, surgical navigation, disease monitoring, and diagnostic imaging interpretation. In radiotherapy, for instance, precise segmentation of tumor volumes and adjacent critical organs is essential to maximize radiation dose delivery to malignant tissue while minimizing exposure to healthy structures, thereby improving therapeutic outcomes and reducing complications. Similarly, in neurosurgical planning, the delineation of tumors, blood vessels, and functional brain regions enables safer resection and preservation of neural integrity. Manual segmentation, although considered the gold standard in many clinical settings due to its flexibility and interpretability, is inherently time-intensive, laborious, and subject to substantial inter- and intra-observer variability. Studies have demonstrated that even experienced radiation oncologists may produce contours that differ by more than 15% in volume when segmenting the same structure across multiple sessions, introducing significant uncertainty into treatment protocols. The variability is exacerbated in cases involving irregularly shaped lesions, heterogeneous tissue densities, or structures with indistinct boundaries, such as lung tumors, pancreatic masses, or metastatic lymph nodes. To mitigate these challenges, automated and semi-automated segmentation techniques have been developed over the past two decades, with machine learning—particularly deep learning—emerging as the dominant paradigm. Convolutional neural networks, especially U-Net variants, have demonstrated remarkable success in generating segmentation masks that closely approximate expert-drawn contours when trained on large, curated datasets of annotated images. These models learn hierarchical representations of anatomical features by mapping pixel intensities to binary or probabilistic segmentation outputs, often achieving Dice similarity coefficients exceeding 0.90 for well-represented structures such as the heart, liver, or kidneys. However, this success is contingent upon the availability of extensive, high-quality training data that captures the full spectrum of anatomical variation within the target structure. When such data is lacking—due to rarity of the structure, ethical constraints on data collection, or the novelty of the clinical application—these models frequently fail to generalize, producing inaccurate or nonsensical segmentations that are clinically unusable.

### The Need for Interactive Segmentation

Given the limitations of fully automated approaches, interactive segmentation methods have gained increasing traction in clinical environments where adaptability and user control are paramount. These methods bridge the gap between manual contouring and fully automated systems by allowing a human expert to provide minimal, high-level guidance—such as a single brushstroke, a few seed points, or a single contour on one slice—and then relying on computational algorithms to propagate that guidance throughout the volume. The underlying premise is that human intuition, even when expressed in sparse form, contains sufficient contextual knowledge to direct the segmentation process effectively. Interactive techniques have been shown to significantly reduce the time required for segmentation compared to manual methods, often by more than 50%, while simultaneously improving consistency across different users. Moreover, because the final segmentation is refined through human oversight, the resulting contours are more clinically acceptable and less prone to the systematic errors that plague purely algorithmic approaches. Traditional interactive methods, such as region-growing, graph cuts, and level sets, rely on hand-crafted rules or energy minimization functions that are sensitive to image noise, intensity inhomogeneities, and boundary ambiguity. These methods often require multiple user interventions across multiple slices to achieve satisfactory results, particularly when the target structure exhibits complex morphology or is embedded in heterogeneous tissue. More recent approaches have integrated machine learning into interactive workflows, enabling the system to learn from user corrections and adapt its behavior over time. However, these systems remain largely structure-specific: a model trained to segment the prostate cannot be applied to segment a renal mass without retraining, and even minor anatomical variations—such as tumor invasion into adjacent organs or post-surgical distortion—can render the model ineffective. The fundamental limitation of these systems is their reliance on pre-defined anatomical templates or learned feature representations that are tied to specific structures. As a result, they lack the capacity to generalize to novel anatomical entities that were not explicitly represented in their training data, severely restricting their utility in dynamic clinical settings where the range of possible structures is vast and unpredictable.

### Application Examples of Segmentation:

The clinical utility of segmentation extends across numerous specialties and procedures. In radiotherapy, segmentation is indispensable for defining target volumes and organs at risk, directly influencing dose planning and treatment efficacy. For example, in lung cancer radiotherapy, the delineation of primary tumors, involved lymph nodes, and surrounding healthy lung tissue must be performed with millimeter precision to avoid underdosing the tumor or overdosing the contralateral lung. In head and neck cancer, segmentation of the parotid glands, submandibular glands, spinal cord, and mandible is critical to preserving salivary function and preventing radiation-induced xerostomia or myelopathy. In neuro-oncology, segmentation of glioblastomas, metastases, and peritumoral edema informs surgical resection boundaries and radiation boost volumes. In abdominal imaging, segmentation of the liver, pancreas, spleen, and kidneys is essential for assessing tumor burden, planning ablation procedures, and evaluating treatment response. In cardiovascular imaging, segmentation of the left ventricle, myocardium, and coronary arteries enables quantification of ejection fraction, wall motion abnormalities, and stenosis severity. In each of these applications, the need for rapid, accurate, and adaptable segmentation is acute. Clinicians often face scenarios in which a structure of interest has not been previously segmented in the institution’s database, or where the structure exhibits atypical morphology due to disease progression, prior treatment, or anatomical variation. In such cases, fully automated systems are frequently unreliable, and manual segmentation is prohibitively slow. The current state of the art offers no solution that can seamlessly bridge this gap: either the clinician must spend hours manually outlining the structure slice by slice, or they must accept an inaccurate automated segmentation that requires extensive manual correction. The invention disclosed herein addresses this unmet clinical need by enabling a clinician to provide a single contour on one slice and have the system generate a high-fidelity, volumetric segmentation of any anatomical structure—regardless of whether it was present in the training data—by interpreting the contextual relationship between the provided contour and the local image intensities across the volume.

## PRIOR ART

### Fully Automated Segmentation Using ML

Fully automated segmentation using machine learning has been the subject of intense research and commercial development over the past decade. Deep learning architectures, particularly convolutional neural networks, have achieved state-of-the-art performance in a wide array of medical imaging tasks. The U-Net architecture, introduced in 2015, became the de facto standard for biomedical image segmentation due to its encoder-decoder structure with skip connections that preserve spatial detail while enabling context-rich feature extraction. Subsequent variants, including residual U-Nets, attention U-Nets, and recurrent U-Nets, have further improved performance by incorporating mechanisms to enhance gradient flow, focus on salient regions, and model sequential dependencies. These models have been successfully applied to segment a wide range of anatomical structures, including the heart, liver, kidneys, lungs, prostate, and brain tumors, often achieving Dice similarity coefficients above 0.85 when evaluated on benchmark datasets such as the Medical Segmentation Decathlon or The Cancer Imaging Archive. However, the performance of these models is critically dependent on the quantity and diversity of training data. Models trained on large, homogeneous datasets of cardiac MRIs, for example, perform exceptionally well on new cardiac scans but fail catastrophically when presented with abdominal CT scans containing pancreatic tumors. The reason for this failure lies in the nature of deep learning itself: these models learn statistical correlations between pixel intensities and anatomical boundaries that are specific to the structures present in the training set. They do not possess an intrinsic understanding of anatomical shape, topology, or spatial relationships; instead, they memorize patterns. As a result, when confronted with a structure that was not included in training—such as a rare tumor type, a postoperative scar, or an anatomical variant—the model has no basis for inference and produces outputs that are either random, fragmented, or entirely absent. Furthermore, even within a single structure class, such as lung tumors, variations in size, shape, location, texture, and adjacency to other organs can lead to significant performance degradation if not adequately represented in the training data. Attempts to mitigate this issue through data augmentation, transfer learning, or multi-task learning have yielded marginal improvements but have not fundamentally altered the structure-specific nature of these models. Consequently, fully automated segmentation remains impractical for many clinical applications where the target anatomy is variable, rare, or unknown in advance.

### Interactive 2D Image Segmentation Approaches

Interactive segmentation in two dimensions has been extensively studied in both research and commercial settings. Early approaches relied on classical image processing techniques such as region growing, watershed segmentation, and active contours (snakes), which required users to provide seed points or initial contours that the algorithm would then expand or refine based on intensity gradients and edge detection. These methods were computationally efficient but suffered from poor robustness in the presence of noise, low contrast, or heterogeneous tissue. Later developments introduced graph-based methods, such as the GrabCut algorithm, which modeled the segmentation problem as a minimum cut optimization on a graph whose nodes represented pixels and whose edges encoded similarity between neighboring pixels. These methods improved accuracy but still required multiple user interactions to achieve satisfactory results, particularly for complex or irregular shapes. More recently, machine learning has been integrated into interactive 2D segmentation tools. For example, some systems allow users to mark foreground and background regions on a single slice, after which a classifier—often a random forest or support vector machine—is trained on the labeled pixels and applied to segment the entire image. Other approaches employ deep learning models that take as input the image along with a user-provided scribble or mask and output a refined segmentation. While these methods represent a significant advancement over purely manual or classical techniques, they remain confined to two-dimensional analysis. In volumetric imaging, such as CT or MR, the anatomical structure of interest typically extends across multiple slices, and a two-dimensional segmentation must be repeated independently on each slice. This process is time-consuming and prone to inconsistency, as the user must manually re-delineate the structure on each slice without the benefit of three-dimensional context. Moreover, the lack of cross-slice information means that the algorithm cannot exploit the continuity of anatomical structures across depth, resulting in jagged, non-smooth contours that are anatomically implausible. Although some systems attempt to propagate a 2D segmentation across slices using simple interpolation or template matching, these methods are highly susceptible to errors when the structure changes shape or intensity between slices. As a result, interactive 2D segmentation remains a labor-intensive process that does not fully leverage the three-dimensional nature of medical imaging data.

### Interactive 3D Segmentation in Medical Images

Interactive three-dimensional segmentation methods aim to overcome the limitations of 2D approaches by incorporating volumetric context into the segmentation process. One prominent class of methods involves the use of 3D convolutional neural networks that are trained to segment entire volumes from sparse annotations. For example, the 3D U-Net architecture extends the original U-Net to operate on volumetric inputs, allowing it to learn spatial relationships in all three dimensions. When combined with sparse annotation strategies—such as annotating only every fifth slice—the network can be trained to infer the segmentation of unannotated slices using a weighted loss function that penalizes errors more heavily in annotated regions. While this approach reduces the annotation burden, it still requires the network to be trained on a specific structure, and its performance degrades rapidly when applied to structures not included in the training set. Another class of methods employs user-guided propagation techniques, in which a user draws a contour on one slice, and the system automatically propagates that contour to adjacent slices using deformable models or optical flow algorithms. These methods often rely on intensity similarity, edge continuity, or shape priors to guide the propagation. However, they are highly sensitive to image artifacts, partial volume effects, and anatomical discontinuities. For instance, if the structure tapers, bifurcates, or merges with another structure in a subsequent slice, the propagation algorithm may drift, resulting in inaccurate or incomplete segmentations. Furthermore, these methods typically require the user to intervene multiple times during propagation to correct errors, negating much of the time-saving benefit. Some recent approaches have attempted to combine deep learning with interactive propagation by training a neural network to predict the next slice’s segmentation based on the current slice and its contour. However, these models are still trained on specific anatomical structures and do not generalize to unseen entities. They learn to map the relationship between a given structure and its surrounding context, but they lack the ability to abstract that relationship into a generalizable framework applicable to any structure. As a result, these systems remain constrained by their training data and cannot be deployed in clinical workflows where the target anatomy is unknown or variable.

### Interactive Contour Propagation Methods

Contour propagation methods represent a subset of interactive segmentation techniques that focus specifically on transferring a manually drawn contour from one image slice to adjacent slices in a volumetric dataset. These methods have been implemented in commercial software platforms such as 3D Slicer, MIM Software, and Philips IntelliSite, and are widely used in radiotherapy planning workflows. The most common approach involves aligning consecutive slices using rigid or non-rigid registration, then applying a deformation field to the contour from the reference slice to the target slice. Alternatively, some systems use intensity-based matching, where the contour is propagated by finding the region in the next slice that most closely matches the intensity profile of the original contour. While these methods are fast and intuitive, they are fundamentally limited by their reliance on geometric or intensity similarity, which often breaks down in the presence of anatomical variation, partial volume effects, or image artifacts. For example, if a tumor extends into a region of lower contrast or is adjacent to a vessel with similar intensity, the propagation algorithm may incorrectly follow the vessel instead of the tumor. Similarly, if the structure changes shape significantly between slices—such as a lung tumor that narrows as it approaches the pleura—the propagation may fail to capture the true boundary. More sophisticated methods have attempted to incorporate machine learning to improve propagation accuracy. For instance, some systems train a neural network to predict the next contour based on the current contour and the corresponding image intensities. However, these networks are still trained on specific structures, such as the heart or liver, and their performance is contingent upon the presence of those structures in the training data. When applied to a novel structure, such as a metastatic lymph node in the mediastinum, these models produce unreliable results because they have not learned the abstract principles of contour propagation—they have only learned the specific patterns associated with the structures they were trained on. Furthermore, these methods typically require the user to provide a contour on every few slices to correct drift, which undermines the goal of minimizing user interaction. The invention disclosed herein fundamentally departs from these approaches by eliminating the need for structure-specific training. Instead of learning to propagate contours of known structures, the system learns to interpret context—defined as the relationship between a manual contour and the surrounding image intensities—and applies that interpretation to any structure, regardless of its identity or prior exposure during training. This represents a paradigm shift from structure-specific learning to context-driven generalization.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention discloses a novel system and method for interactive three-dimensional segmentation of anatomical structures in medical imaging datasets using a contextual deep learning framework that enables generalization to previously unseen structures based solely on a single user-provided contour. The system operates on volumetric medical image data, such as computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or positron emission tomography (PET) scans, and requires only one manual contour on a single slice to initiate the segmentation process. Unlike prior systems, which rely on pre-trained models specific to known anatomical entities, the invention employs a deep neural network architecture that learns to extract and apply contextual relationships between the provided contour and the image intensities of the target slice, independent of the anatomical identity of the structure. The system does not require prior knowledge of the structure being segmented, nor does it rely on structure-specific training data. Instead, it leverages a diverse training set comprising multiple anatomical structures, enabling it to abstract general principles of spatial continuity, intensity transition, and boundary formation that are applicable across anatomical domains. The invention is implemented as a software module integrated into clinical imaging platforms, capable of running on standard hardware with a single graphics processing unit (GPU), and delivers segmentation results in real-time with an inference time of approximately 0.1 seconds per slice. The method consists of three core components: a multi-channel input representation that encodes contextual information, a modified residual recurrent U-Net with attention gates that processes the input and generates a segmentation probability map, and a training strategy that emphasizes context over structure-specific features through diverse and strategically sampled data augmentation. The input to the system comprises three distinct channels: the first channel contains the intensity values of the target image slice to be segmented; the second channel contains the intensity values of a previously segmented image slice; and the third channel contains a binary mask representing the manual contour drawn on that prior slice. Crucially, the binary mask does not encode any anatomical label or semantic information—it is simply a binary representation of the user’s delineation, with no indication of whether the contour corresponds to a tumor, an organ, a vessel, or any other structure. This deliberate omission of anatomical metadata is central to the invention’s ability to generalize: by removing any explicit reference to the identity of the structure, the network is forced to learn the underlying contextual relationships between the image intensities and the spatial extent of the contour, rather than memorizing the appearance of specific anatomical entities. During training, the system is presented with pairs of image slices and their corresponding contours drawn from a diverse collection of anatomical structures, including but not limited to the heart, lungs, liver, spleen, pancreas, kidneys, spinal cord, prostate, and various tumor types. The training set is constructed such that each structure is represented by hundreds or thousands of image slices, with the previously segmented slice and the target slice selected from a range of interslice distances that vary relative to the size of the structure. For example, for large structures such as the liver, the distance between the prior and target slice may range from 5 to 50 millimeters, whereas for small structures such as lymph nodes, the range may be limited to 1 to 10 millimeters. This adaptive sampling ensures that the network learns to handle both fine-grained and coarse-grained spatial transitions, preventing it from learning trivial solutions such as copying the contour from the adjacent slice. Furthermore, the target slice is not restricted to lie within the volume of the structure; it may lie outside the structure, allowing the network to learn not only where to segment but also where to stop, thereby capturing the full extent of the anatomical boundary. The network architecture is based on a modified U-Net with residual connections, recurrent layers, and attention gates. The residual connections facilitate gradient flow through deep layers, enabling stable training of the network even with complex input configurations. The recurrent layers allow the network to maintain a memory of previous contextual relationships, enhancing its ability to model sequential dependencies across slices. The attention gates, which are inserted between the contracting and expanding paths of the U-Net, dynamically modulate the feature maps by highlighting regions of the image that are most relevant to the segmentation task, based on the context provided by the prior slice and its contour. This mechanism enables the network to focus on salient boundaries and suppress irrelevant background information, even when the structure of interest is poorly defined or embedded in heterogeneous tissue. The final layer of the network applies a sigmoid activation function to produce a probability map, where each pixel value represents the likelihood that the corresponding location belongs to the segmented structure. The network is trained using pixel-wise binary cross-entropy loss, which measures the difference between the predicted probability map and the ground truth segmentation mask. The Adam optimizer is employed with a low initial learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁵ to ensure stable convergence, and a batch size of four is used to balance computational efficiency with gradient stability. To mitigate the variability introduced by random weight initialization, multiple network instances are trained in parallel, and the model with the highest validation performance after a fixed number of epochs is retained for further training. Data augmentation is applied on-the-fly during training to increase the diversity of the training set and improve generalization. Augmentations include random affine transformations such as translation (±10 mm), rotation (±10 degrees), and scaling (±5%), which simulate variations in patient positioning, anatomical orientation, and image resolution. These augmentations are applied independently to each of the three input channels to preserve their spatial correspondence. The training process is designed to emphasize context over structure-specific features by ensuring that the training set includes a wide variety of anatomical structures, none of which dominate the learning process. The inclusion of structures with highly variable appearances—such as irregularly shaped tumors, branching vessels, and complex organ geometries—enables the network to extract abstract, transferable features that are not tied to any single anatomical entity. Experimental results demonstrate that as the diversity of the training set increases, the network’s performance improves not only for structures included in the training set but also for structures that were explicitly excluded. For example, a model trained on the heart, lungs, liver, and pancreas achieves a Dice similarity coefficient of 0.87 for the spleen—a structure never seen during training—when provided with a single user contour on a single slice. This performance is comparable to, and in some cases superior to, fully automated segmentation models trained specifically on spleen data, despite the fact that the invention’s model was never exposed to spleen images during training. This ability to generalize to unseen structures is the defining characteristic of the invention and distinguishes it fundamentally from all prior art. The system supports two modes of segmentation propagation: direct prediction and iterative prediction. In direct prediction, the user provides a single contour on a central slice, and the network generates segmentations for all other slices in the volume simultaneously, using the same prior slice and contour as context for every target slice. This approach is computationally efficient and suitable for structures with relatively uniform morphology across the volume. In iterative prediction, the network segments the adjacent slice to the central slice using the initial contour as context; the resulting segmentation is then used as the new prior contour for the next slice, and the process continues sequentially in both directions until the entire volume is segmented. This mode is particularly effective for structures with complex, non-uniform morphology, such as tumors that change shape along their length or vessels that branch and taper. Experimental results show that iterative prediction outperforms direct prediction for unseen structures, as the cumulative propagation of context allows the network to refine its predictions incrementally, reducing the accumulation of error that occurs when large interslice distances are encountered in direct prediction. The system is designed to be used in a clinical workflow in which a radiologist or radiation oncologist selects a single slice from a volumetric dataset, draws a contour around the structure of interest using a standard graphical interface, and initiates the segmentation process. The system then generates a volumetric segmentation in real time, which the user can review and refine if necessary. If the segmentation deviates from the expected boundary in a particular region, the user may correct the contour on that slice, and the system will automatically propagate the updated contour to adjacent slices, creating a closed-loop interactive system that learns from user corrections in real time. The system is compatible with standard DICOM image formats and can be integrated into existing picture archiving and communication systems (PACS) and radiotherapy planning systems. The invention is not limited to CT or MRI data; it is applicable to any volumetric imaging modality in which anatomical structures can be delineated through intensity-based boundaries. The training data used to develop the system was drawn from publicly available datasets, including The Cancer Imaging Archive and the Medical Segmentation Decathlon, and included a total of 19 distinct anatomical structures spanning multiple organ systems and pathological conditions. The system was evaluated using standard segmentation metrics, including the Dice similarity coefficient, Hausdorff distance, and relative added path length, and demonstrated superior performance compared to both fully automated segmentation methods and traditional interactive tools. Notably, the relative added path length—a metric that measures the amount of manual correction required after automated segmentation—was reduced by more than 50% compared to state-of-the-art commercial tools, indicating a substantial reduction in user effort. The system’s ability to generalize to unseen structures was further validated by training models on subsets of the data and evaluating their performance on structures excluded from training. In every case, the model achieved clinically acceptable segmentation accuracy, even when the target structure had never been encountered during training. This demonstrates that the system has learned a generalizable representation of anatomical context rather than memorizing specific anatomical templates. The invention represents a paradigm shift in medical image segmentation by decoupling segmentation from structure-specific training and anchoring it instead in the abstract, transferable relationships between user-provided contours and image intensities. It eliminates the need for large, structure-specific datasets, enabling rapid, accurate segmentation of any anatomical entity—even those that are rare, novel, or previously undocumented. This capability is particularly valuable in clinical settings where time is critical, training data is scarce, and anatomical variation is high. The invention enables clinicians to perform de novo segmentation with minimal training, reducing the barrier to entry for advanced imaging analysis and democratizing access to high-quality segmentation tools across diverse healthcare environments. The system’s performance, speed, and adaptability make it uniquely suited for integration into clinical workflows, where it can significantly reduce contouring time, improve segmentation consistency, and enhance the accuracy of treatment planning and diagnostic interpretation. The invention is not merely an incremental improvement over existing methods; it is a foundational advancement in the field of medical image analysis, establishing a new paradigm for interactive segmentation that is generalizable, scalable, and clinically practical.
