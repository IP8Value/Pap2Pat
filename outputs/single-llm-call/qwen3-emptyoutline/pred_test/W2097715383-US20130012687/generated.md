# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The purification of recombinant proteins remains a cornerstone of modern molecular biology, structural biology, and biopharmaceutical development. Despite decades of innovation in expression systems, chromatographic resins, and fusion tag technologies, the isolation of pure, functional, and untagged proteins from bacterial hosts continues to present significant technical and economic challenges. The widespread adoption of affinity tags—such as polyhistidine (His6), glutathione S-transferase (GST), and maltose-binding protein (MBP)—has undeniably streamlined the initial capture of target proteins from complex lysates. These tags enable rapid, high-yield purification via immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC), glutathione affinity, or amylose resin binding, respectively. However, the presence of such tags often interferes with downstream applications, including structural studies by X-ray crystallography or cryo-electron microscopy, functional assays requiring native conformation, protein-protein interaction analyses, and therapeutic applications where immunogenicity or altered pharmacokinetics are concerns. Consequently, the removal of these affinity tags has become an indispensable, yet frequently problematic, step in the protein purification workflow.

Traditional methods for tag removal rely on the exogenous addition of site-specific proteases—such as thrombin, factor Xa, TEV protease, or enterokinase—that recognize and cleave engineered peptide sequences positioned between the affinity tag and the target protein. While effective in principle, these approaches are fraught with limitations. First, the proteases themselves are expensive, often requiring stoichiometric or even excess quantities to achieve complete cleavage, particularly when the target protein is expressed at low levels or exhibits poor solubility. Second, many of these proteases suffer from suboptimal activity under standard purification conditions, necessitating extended incubation times—often overnight or longer—which increases the risk of protein degradation, aggregation, or non-specific cleavage. Third, these proteases frequently display residual activity toward non-cognate sites within the target protein, particularly when the target contains internal sequences resembling the protease recognition motif. This promiscuity can result in heterogeneous protein products, reduced yields, and the need for additional purification steps to isolate the desired species. Fourth, the proteases used for cleavage are themselves affinity-tagged for ease of removal, which introduces a secondary purification burden: the protease must be separated from the cleaved target protein, typically requiring a second chromatographic step, such as a second round of IMAC or size exclusion chromatography. This not only increases processing time and material costs but also diminishes overall recovery rates due to losses during each transfer and wash step.

Moreover, the solubility and stability of many recombinant proteins remain major obstacles in their production. A substantial fraction of target proteins, particularly those derived from eukaryotic organisms, membrane-associated domains, or intrinsically disordered regions, are prone to aggregation, misfolding, or degradation when expressed in bacterial systems such as *Escherichia coli*. To mitigate these issues, fusion partners such as NusA, SUMO, or thioredoxin have been employed to enhance solubility and stability. While these strategies improve expression yields, they introduce additional complexity: the fusion partner must be removed without compromising the integrity of the target, and the larger size of these partners can sometimes hinder crystallization or alter functional properties. Furthermore, the use of temperature-sensitive or salt-dependent self-cleaving systems—such as elastin-like polypeptide (ELP) tags or intein-based systems—requires precise control over environmental conditions, including shifts in temperature, pH, or ionic strength, which may not be compatible with sensitive or aggregation-prone proteins. These systems often necessitate multiple buffer exchanges, extended incubations, or specialized equipment, rendering them impractical for high-throughput or automated workflows.

There exists, therefore, a critical unmet need for a protein purification system that integrates affinity capture, site-specific cleavage, and tag removal into a single, robust, and controllable step—without requiring exogenous enzymes, multiple chromatographic steps, or harsh environmental perturbations. Such a system would need to be universally applicable across diverse protein targets, including those that are insoluble, unstable, or difficult to express. It would require high specificity to prevent off-target cleavage, rapid kinetics to minimize processing time, and inducibility to allow precise temporal control over cleavage initiation. Additionally, it would benefit from compatibility with standard laboratory buffers, low-cost inducers, and minimal downstream processing requirements. The ideal system would not only simplify purification but also actively enhance the expression, solubility, and stability of the target protein, thereby addressing the root causes of low yield and poor quality in recombinant protein production.

The discovery and characterization of the Vibrio cholerae MARTX toxin cysteine protease domain (CPD) has provided a unique molecular tool with properties uniquely suited to meet these demands. The CPD is a highly specialized protease that functions as an autoprocessing enzyme, cleaving itself from its own N-terminal fusion partner upon activation by a specific endogenous eukaryotic signaling molecule, inositol hexakisphosphate (InsP6). This molecule is absent in bacterial cytoplasm, allowing the CPD to remain inert during expression and initial purification in *E. coli*. Once the CPD fusion protein is immobilized on a solid support via its C-terminal His6 tag, the addition of InsP6 triggers a rapid, self-contained cleavage event precisely at the junction between the target protein and the CPD. The cleavage occurs exclusively after a leucine residue, which is engineered to occupy the P1 position at the fusion boundary, ensuring that only the intended bond is hydrolyzed. Crucially, the CPD exhibits negligible transcleavage activity toward other leucine residues within the target protein, even when those residues reside within intrinsically disordered regions or repetitive sequences. This exceptional specificity, combined with its inducibility, autoprocessing nature, and stability under a wide range of conditions, positions the CPD as a transformative platform for recombinant protein purification.

The present invention leverages these properties to establish a novel, one-step purification system that eliminates the need for exogenous proteases, reduces processing time from hours to minutes, minimizes protein loss, and enhances the yield and quality of untagged target proteins. Unlike previous systems, this invention does not merely simplify tag removal—it actively improves the biophysical properties of the target protein during expression and purification. The CPD acts not only as a cleavable affinity tag but also as a solubility-enhancing chaperone, stabilizing otherwise unstable domains and preventing proteolytic degradation. This dual functionality—affinity capture and expression enhancement—represents a paradigm shift in recombinant protein production. The system is scalable, compatible with automation, and adaptable to a variety of target proteins, including those previously deemed intractable. Furthermore, the cleavage reaction occurs efficiently at temperatures ranging from 4°C to 37°C, in the presence of common protease inhibitors, and without the need for salt adjustments or buffer exchanges, making it ideally suited for integration into high-throughput pipelines and industrial-scale protein manufacturing. The invention described herein provides a comprehensive, self-contained method for the rapid, high-fidelity, and cost-effective production of untagged recombinant proteins, overcoming longstanding limitations in the field and enabling new possibilities in structural biology, drug discovery, and biotechnology.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention discloses a novel, integrated system for the purification of recombinant proteins that combines affinity-based capture, site-specific proteolytic cleavage, and tag separation into a single, streamlined procedure. This system is based on the use of a fusion protein comprising a target protein of interest covalently linked at its N-terminus to a cysteine protease domain derived from the MARTX toxin of *Vibrio cholerae*, wherein the protease domain is fused to a C-terminal affinity tag capable of binding to a solid support, and wherein the junction between the target protein and the protease domain contains a cleavage site specifically recognized and processed by the protease domain upon exposure to inositol hexakisphosphate. The invention enables the purification of untagged target proteins directly from bacterial lysates without the need for exogenous proteases, secondary chromatographic steps, or environmental perturbations such as temperature shifts or high-salt conditions.

The method comprises the steps of expressing a recombinant fusion protein in a bacterial host, wherein the fusion protein consists of a target protein fused at its N-terminus to a cysteine protease domain from *Vibrio cholerae*, and wherein the cysteine protease domain is fused at its C-terminus to an affinity tag that binds to a metal chelate resin; lysing the bacterial cells to release the fusion protein; contacting the lysate with a solid support functionalized with a metal ion that specifically binds the affinity tag, thereby immobilizing the fusion protein; washing the solid support to remove non-specifically bound contaminants; and inducing cleavage of the fusion protein by contacting the immobilized fusion protein with inositol hexakisphosphate, thereby triggering autoprocessing of the cysteine protease domain at a defined leucine residue located at the junction between the target protein and the protease domain, resulting in the release of the untagged target protein into the supernatant while the C-terminally tagged protease domain remains bound to the solid support. The untagged target protein is then recovered from the supernatant, optionally subjected to further purification if necessary, and characterized for purity, stability, and function.

The cysteine protease domain used in this invention is derived from the MARTX toxin of *Vibrio cholerae* and is characterized by its strict dependence on inositol hexakisphosphate for activation, its exclusive cleavage specificity for leucine residues at the P1 position, and its minimal transcleavage activity toward other leucine-containing sequences within the target protein. The cleavage site is engineered such that the leucine residue at the P1 position is positioned immediately adjacent to the C-terminus of the target protein, ensuring that cleavage liberates the target protein with minimal residual amino acids. The affinity tag is preferably a polyhistidine sequence of six or more residues, although other tags capable of binding to immobilized metal ions, such as nickel or cobalt, may be employed. The solid support is preferably a resin functionalized with nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA) chelated to nickel ions, although other chelating resins or metal-binding matrices may be substituted without departing from the scope of the invention.

The invention further encompasses a suite of expression vectors designed to facilitate the construction of fusion proteins with varying C-terminal extensions on the target protein following cleavage. These vectors are engineered to allow cloning of the target protein into different restriction sites that alter the amino acid sequence immediately following the cleavage site, enabling the addition of zero, one, two, or more residues to the C-terminus of the purified target protein. For example, cloning into the SalI site results in the addition of four residues (Val-Asp-Ala-Leu), cloning into the SacI site results in two residues (Glu-Leu), and cloning into the BamHI site with an added leucine codon results in a single leucine residue. This flexibility allows users to tailor the C-terminal sequence of the purified protein to meet specific requirements, such as avoiding unwanted residues that may interfere with protein function, crystallography, or downstream modifications. In certain embodiments, the vector may be modified to include an epitope tag, such as the HA tag, between the target protein and the protease domain, permitting detection or purification of the fusion protein prior to cleavage.

The invention also provides for the enhanced expression, solubility, and stability of target proteins when fused to the cysteine protease domain. In multiple independent examples, target proteins that were previously expressed at low levels, aggregated into inclusion bodies, or degraded during purification were found to be expressed at significantly higher levels, remained soluble in the cytoplasm, and resisted proteolytic degradation when fused to the CPD domain. This effect is not attributable to the affinity tag alone, as control constructs bearing only the His6 tag failed to exhibit comparable improvements. The CPD domain appears to act as a solubility-enhancing fusion partner, stabilizing the target protein during folding and preventing aggregation, thereby increasing the proportion of functional protein recovered from the soluble fraction. This property renders the invention particularly valuable for the purification of proteins that are traditionally difficult to express, including membrane-associated domains, intrinsically disordered regions, and proteins derived from eukaryotic pathogens such as *Plasmodium falciparum* and *Toxoplasma gondii*.

The cleavage reaction is rapid, typically achieving complete separation of the target protein from the affinity tag within one to two hours at temperatures ranging from 4°C to 37°C, and requires only micromolar concentrations of inositol hexakisphosphate, a small, inexpensive, and biologically abundant molecule. The reaction proceeds efficiently in the presence of standard protease inhibitor cocktails, in the absence of added salts, and under a wide range of pH conditions, allowing users to elute the target protein directly into any desired buffer without the need for desalting or buffer exchange. The system is scalable from microliter-scale laboratory experiments to industrial-scale bioreactor production, and is fully compatible with automated liquid handling systems, making it suitable for high-throughput proteomic, structural genomic, and drug screening applications. The purified target proteins are free of affinity tags, exogenous proteases, and residual fusion partners, exhibiting high purity and homogeneity, as demonstrated by SDS-PAGE, mass spectrometry, and functional assays. In multiple cases, the proteins purified using this method were of sufficient quality to yield diffraction-quality crystals for X-ray structure determination, a feat previously unattainable with conventional purification strategies.

The invention thus provides a transformative platform for recombinant protein production that eliminates the major bottlenecks associated with traditional tag removal methods. It offers a single-step, high-yield, high-fidelity, and cost-effective solution for the purification of untagged proteins, while simultaneously enhancing their expression and stability. The system is broadly applicable across diverse protein targets, including those previously considered intractable, and is readily adaptable to existing laboratory workflows. The invention represents a significant advancement in the field of protein biochemistry, enabling faster, more reliable, and more economical access to pure, functional proteins for research, diagnostic, and therapeutic purposes.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

### TERM DEFINITIONS

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms are defined with specificity to ensure clarity and consistency in the interpretation of the claims and the detailed description. The term “recombinant protein” refers to any polypeptide sequence that is produced through the expression of a genetically engineered nucleic acid construct in a heterologous host organism, including but not limited to bacteria, yeast, insect, or mammalian cells, wherein the polypeptide is not naturally produced in that host in its native form. The term “target protein” denotes the specific polypeptide of interest whose purification is the objective of the method, and which is fused to the cysteine protease domain for the purposes of expression enhancement and affinity purification. The target protein may be derived from any organism, including prokaryotes, eukaryotes, viruses, or synthetic designs, and may encompass full-length proteins, domains, fragments, or engineered variants thereof, provided that the protein does not inherently contain sequences that interfere with the function of the cysteine protease domain.

The term “cysteine protease domain” refers to a discrete, folded polypeptide segment derived from the MARTX toxin of *Vibrio cholerae*, specifically encompassing amino acid residues 3440 to 3650 of the wild-type toxin, or any variant thereof that retains the ability to undergo autoprocessing upon binding to inositol hexakisphosphate and to cleave a peptide bond immediately C-terminal to a leucine residue. This domain may be modified by conservative amino acid substitutions, deletions, or insertions that do not impair its catalytic activity, specificity, or inducibility. The domain is characterized by its structural fold, which includes a catalytic triad composed of cysteine, histidine, and asparagine residues, and a substrate-binding pocket that selectively accommodates a leucine side chain at the P1 position. The domain is inactive in the absence of inositol hexakisphosphate and becomes catalytically competent only upon binding of this molecule, which induces a conformational change that aligns the catalytic residues and positions the scissile bond for hydrolysis.

The term “autoprocessing” refers to the intramolecular cleavage event by which the cysteine protease domain severs its own peptide bond at the junction with the target protein, thereby releasing the target protein as a free, untagged polypeptide. This process is distinct from transcleavage, in which a protease cleaves a separate substrate molecule. Autoprocessing is inherently self-limiting, as the protease domain remains covalently attached to the affinity tag and is immobilized on the solid support, ensuring that only the intended cleavage site is processed. The term “cleavage site” refers to the specific amino acid sequence at the interface between the target protein and the cysteine protease domain, wherein the leucine residue occupies the P1 position, the residue immediately N-terminal to the scissile bond, and wherein the residue immediately C-terminal to the cleavage site is the first residue of the cysteine protease domain. The cleavage site may be engineered to include additional residues upstream of the leucine, such as Val-Asp-Ala-Leu or Glu-Leu, depending on the cloning strategy employed, but the leucine residue must be positioned to serve as the P1 residue for recognition by the protease domain.

The term “inositol hexakisphosphate” or “InsP6” refers to the naturally occurring eukaryotic signaling molecule also known as phytic acid, with the chemical formula C₆H₁₈O₂₄P₆. This molecule is absent in the cytoplasm of bacterial cells and serves as the sole physiological activator of the *Vibrio cholerae* cysteine protease domain. The term encompasses both the free acid form and its salts, including sodium, potassium, or ammonium salts, provided that the molecule retains its ability to bind and activate the protease domain. The concentration of inositol hexakisphosphate required for complete cleavage is typically between 10 and 200 micromolar, with optimal activity observed at 50 to 100 micromolar. The molecule is stable under standard laboratory conditions, inexpensive, and commercially available in high purity, making it ideal for large-scale applications.

The term “affinity tag” refers to a polypeptide sequence or chemical moiety that is fused to the C-terminus of the cysteine protease domain and confers the ability to bind reversibly to a solid support. The preferred affinity tag is a polyhistidine sequence comprising six or more consecutive histidine residues, which binds with high affinity to immobilized nickel or cobalt ions. Alternative affinity tags may include sequences that bind to glutathione, maltose, streptavidin, or other ligands immobilized on resin matrices, provided that the tag does not interfere with the autoprocessing activity of the cysteine protease domain. The term “solid support” refers to any insoluble matrix capable of immobilizing the affinity tag, including but not limited to agarose beads, sepharose resins, magnetic beads, or functionalized silica particles. The solid support is preferably functionalized with a chelating agent such as nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA), iminodiacetic acid (IDA), or tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP), which is in turn complexed with a transition metal ion such as nickel (Ni²⁺), cobalt (Co²⁺), or copper (Cu²⁺), such that the affinity tag binds to the metal ion and thereby anchors the fusion protein to the support.

The term “untagged target protein” refers to the polypeptide of interest that has been liberated from the cysteine protease domain and the affinity tag following the autoprocessing reaction. This protein is free of any exogenous fusion partners, including the cysteine protease domain, the affinity tag, and any linker sequences introduced during cloning. The untagged target protein may retain a small number of residual amino acids at its C-terminus, depending on the cloning strategy, but these residues are not derived from the affinity tag or the protease domain and do not interfere with the protein’s biological function. The term “purification” refers to the process of isolating the untagged target protein from the lysate, the solid support, and any co-purifying contaminants, such that the protein is recovered in a state of high purity, typically greater than 90% as determined by SDS-PAGE or mass spectrometry. The term “high purity” denotes a protein preparation in which the target protein constitutes the predominant species, with minor contaminants present at levels below detectable thresholds by standard analytical methods.

The term “expression vector” refers to a recombinant DNA construct capable of directing the transcription and translation of a fused gene encoding the target protein and the cysteine protease domain in a bacterial host. The vector may be derived from any plasmid backbone commonly used for protein expression, including pET, pGEX, or pMAL systems, and may contain regulatory elements such as a strong promoter (e.g., T7 or lac), a ribosome binding site, and a selectable marker (e.g., ampicillin or kanamycin resistance). The vector may be engineered to contain multiple cloning sites flanking the cysteine protease domain, allowing insertion of the target protein at different restriction sites to generate fusion proteins with varying C-terminal extensions. The term “fusion protein” refers to the polypeptide chain comprising the target protein covalently linked to the cysteine protease domain, which in turn is linked to the affinity tag, forming a single continuous polypeptide. The term “autocleavage” is used synonymously with autoprocessing and refers to the intramolecular cleavage event that occurs upon activation of the protease domain by inositol hexakisphosphate.

The term “transcleavage” refers to the undesired cleavage of a peptide bond within the target protein at a site other than the engineered junction between the target and the protease domain. The cysteine protease domain of the invention exhibits negligible transcleavage activity, even when the target protein contains multiple leucine residues in disordered regions or repetitive motifs. The term “inducible” describes the property of the protease domain whereby its catalytic activity is contingent upon the presence of inositol hexakisphosphate and is otherwise inert during expression and initial purification. The term “solubility-enhancing” refers to the ability of the cysteine protease domain to increase the proportion of the target protein that remains in the soluble fraction of the bacterial lysate, as opposed to aggregating into inclusion bodies. The term “stability-enhancing” refers to the ability of the fusion to protect the target protein from proteolytic degradation during expression, lysis, or purification, as evidenced by the absence of degradation products on SDS-PAGE. The term “high-throughput” refers to the capacity of the method to be automated and scaled to process hundreds or thousands of protein constructs in parallel, using robotic liquid handlers, multi-well plates, and standardized protocols.

### Methods

The method of the invention is implemented through a series of well-defined, reproducible, and scalable steps that integrate protein expression, affinity capture, inducible cleavage, and tag separation into a single, streamlined workflow. The process begins with the construction of an expression vector encoding a fusion protein in which the target protein is fused at its N-terminus to the cysteine protease domain from *Vibrio cholerae*, and the cysteine protease domain is fused at its C-terminus to an affinity tag, preferably a polyhistidine sequence of six or more residues. The fusion gene is cloned into a bacterial expression vector under the control of a strong, inducible promoter, such as the T7 promoter, and the resulting plasmid is transformed into a suitable bacterial host, such as *Escherichia coli* BL21(DE3) or related strains optimized for recombinant protein expression. The choice of host strain may be adjusted based on the characteristics of the target protein, such as its codon usage, propensity for disulfide bond formation, or requirement for chaperone assistance.

Once the transformed bacterial culture is established, the cells are grown in a nutrient-rich medium, such as 2YT or LB broth, supplemented with an appropriate antibiotic to maintain plasmid selection. The culture is incubated at 37°C with vigorous shaking until the optical density at 600 nm reaches approximately 0.6, at which point protein expression is induced by the addition of an inducer, typically isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), at a concentration ranging from 0.1 to 1.0 mM. In certain cases, particularly for proteins that are prone to aggregation or misfolding, induction is performed at lower temperatures, such as 16°C or 20°C, to promote proper folding and reduce inclusion body formation. The duration of induction typically ranges from three to eight hours, depending on the expression kinetics of the target protein and the desired yield. Following induction, the cells are harvested by centrifugation, and the cell pellet is resuspended in a lysis buffer designed to maintain protein stability and prevent degradation. The lysis buffer typically contains 500 mM sodium chloride, 50 mM Tris-HCl at pH 7.5, 15 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol, and may optionally include protease inhibitors such as phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), leupeptin, or a commercial protease inhibitor cocktail. For proteins requiring reducing conditions, such as those with cysteine residues prone to oxidation, the lysis buffer may also contain 1 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP) or dithiothreitol (DTT).

The resuspended cells are lysed by mechanical disruption, most commonly by sonication, though alternative methods such as French press, high-pressure homogenization, or enzymatic lysis with lysozyme may be employed. The lysate is then clarified by centrifugation at high speed, typically 15,000 × g for 30 minutes, to remove cell debris and insoluble material. The resulting supernatant, containing the soluble fusion protein, is collected and incubated with a solid support functionalized with a metal chelate resin, such as Ni-NTA agarose beads, at a volume ratio of approximately 0.5 to 1.0 mL of resin per liter of culture. The binding reaction is carried out with gentle agitation, such as nutation or end-over-end rotation, at 4°C for a period of two to four hours to ensure complete capture of the fusion protein. During this step, the polyhistidine tag on the C-terminus of the cysteine protease domain binds tightly to the nickel ions immobilized on the resin, thereby immobilizing the entire fusion protein while allowing non-specifically bound cellular proteins to remain in the supernatant.

After binding, the resin is collected by low-speed centrifugation, typically at 1,500 × g for one minute, and the supernatant is discarded. The resin is then washed three to four times with fresh lysis buffer to remove residual contaminants, with each wash step involving resuspension of the resin in 3 to 5 mL of buffer, followed by centrifugation and removal of the supernatant. The washed resin, now containing the immobilized fusion protein, is ready for the cleavage step. At this stage, a small aliquot of the resin may be removed and eluted with high imidazole buffer (e.g., 175 mM imidazole) to confirm the presence and integrity of the fusion protein prior to cleavage. The remaining resin is then resuspended in a cleavage buffer, which may be identical to the lysis buffer or may be optimized for the specific target protein. Inositol hexakisphosphate is added to the suspension at a final concentration of 50 to 100 micromolar, and the mixture is incubated with gentle agitation at a temperature between 4°C and 37°C for a period of one to two hours. During this time, the inositol hexakisphosphate diffuses into the resin matrix, binds to the cysteine protease domain, and triggers its autoprocessing activity. The protease cleaves the peptide bond immediately C-terminal to the leucine residue at the fusion junction, releasing the untagged target protein into the surrounding solution while the C-terminally His6-tagged protease domain remains firmly bound to the resin.

Following incubation, the resin is again collected by centrifugation, and the supernatant, now containing the purified untagged target protein, is carefully removed and transferred to a fresh tube. The resin is washed three to four additional times with cleavage buffer to recover any residual target protein, and each wash supernatant is pooled with the initial eluate. The pooled supernatant represents the crude purified target protein and may be subjected to further purification if necessary, such as size exclusion chromatography, ion exchange chromatography, or concentration via ultrafiltration. The resin-bound protease domain may be eluted separately using high imidazole buffer and analyzed to confirm complete cleavage and the absence of residual fusion protein. The purity of the target protein is typically assessed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) followed by Coomassie staining or Western blotting, and its concentration is determined by Bradford assay, absorbance at 280 nm, or other standard protein quantification methods.

In certain embodiments, the method may be adapted to generate target proteins with specific C-terminal extensions. This is achieved by cloning the target protein into different restriction sites within the expression vector that alter the amino acid sequence immediately preceding the cysteine protease domain. For instance, cloning into the SalI site results in the addition of the residues Val-Asp-Ala-Leu to the C-terminus of the target protein, whereas cloning into the SacI site results in the addition of Glu-Leu, and cloning into the BamHI site with an additional leucine codon in the 3′ primer results in a single leucine residue. These variations allow users to tailor the C-terminal sequence of the purified protein to avoid interference with functional domains, crystal packing interfaces, or post-translational modification sites. In some cases, an epitope tag such as the HA tag may be inserted between the target protein and the cysteine protease domain to facilitate detection or purification prior to cleavage. The epitope tag is removed during the autoprocessing step, leaving only the desired C-terminal extension.

The method is highly robust and reproducible across a wide range of target proteins. For example, when applied to green fluorescent protein (GFP), the system achieved complete cleavage within one hour at room temperature, with no detectable degradation products or non-specific cleavage events. When applied to the intracellular domain of gp130—a protein known to be intrinsically disordered and rich in leucine residues—the system cleaved exclusively at the engineered junction, with no cleavage observed at any of the internal leucine residues. Similarly, when applied to the CRAC-activation domain of STIM1, a small, unstable polypeptide that had previously resisted purification despite fusion to GST, the CPD fusion enabled high-yield expression and recovery of a stable, functional protein. In the case of mouse macrophage metalloelastase (MMP12), a protein that had previously required a multi-day refolding protocol from inclusion bodies, the CPD fusion allowed direct purification from the soluble fraction in less than seven hours, with full enzymatic activity retained. The method was also successfully applied to the Plasmodium falciparum SENP1 protease, a parasite-derived enzyme that had previously required thrombin cleavage and multiple chromatographic steps to achieve acceptable purity. Using the CPD system, the protein was purified to homogeneity in a single step, yielding crystals suitable for X-ray structure determination.

The system is compatible with a variety of buffers and conditions. Cleavage proceeds efficiently in the presence of standard protease inhibitor cocktails, in the absence of added salts, and at pH values ranging from 6.5 to 8.5. The reaction is not inhibited by reducing agents, detergents, or glycerol, and may be performed in the presence of up to 1 M urea if required for solubilization. The inositol hexakisphosphate used in the system is stable for years when stored dry at -20°C and is available in high purity from commercial suppliers at low cost. The entire process, from induction to purified protein recovery, can be completed in less than 24 hours, with the cleavage step itself requiring only one to two hours. The method is scalable from microliter volumes in 96-well plates to multi-liter bioreactor cultures, and is fully compatible with automated liquid handling systems, making it ideal for high-throughput protein production pipelines. The purified proteins exhibit high homogeneity, as confirmed by mass spectrometry, and retain full biological activity, as demonstrated by functional assays including fluorescence, enzymatic activity, and binding studies. The invention thus provides a comprehensive, reliable, and universally applicable method for the production of untagged recombinant proteins, overcoming longstanding limitations in the field and enabling new advances in structural biology, drug discovery, and biotechnology.
