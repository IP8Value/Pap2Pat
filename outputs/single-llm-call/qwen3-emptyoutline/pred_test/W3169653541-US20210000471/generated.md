# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to medical devices and surgical implantation techniques for arteriovenous grafts used in hemodialysis access, specifically to a pre-fabricated, tapered polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) arteriovenous graft with a fixed, optimized graft-to-vein anastomosis angle of approximately 30 degrees. This invention is directed toward improving the long-term patency of brachio-axillary arteriovenous grafts by minimizing the occurrence of pathological hemodynamic conditions—namely, regions of abnormally low or high wall shear stress—that are known to initiate intimal hyperplasia, endothelial damage, and subsequent thrombosis at the venous anastomotic junction. The graft is engineered with a precisely controlled geometric configuration that ensures optimal blood flow dynamics within the physiologically permissible shear rate range of 50 to 1000 inverse seconds across the entire cardiac cycle, thereby reducing the risk of stenosis and failure commonly associated with conventional arteriovenous grafts. The invention further encompasses a family of grafts with standardized anastomotic angles calibrated to match varying venous diameters, enabling surgeons to select the most appropriate graft based on preoperative venous imaging, thereby standardizing implantation protocols and improving clinical outcomes across diverse patient populations.

## BACKGROUND

Hemodialysis remains a life-sustaining therapy for patients with end-stage renal disease, with hundreds of thousands of individuals in the United States alone relying on this intervention for survival. A critical component of successful hemodialysis is the establishment of reliable vascular access, which permits the continuous, high-volume extracorporeal circulation of blood through a dialyzer. Among the various access modalities available, arteriovenous grafts constructed from synthetic materials—most commonly polytetrafluoroethylene—are frequently employed when autologous venous anatomy is inadequate for the creation of a native arteriovenous fistula. These grafts are typically implanted in the upper extremity, connecting the brachial artery to the axillary vein, and are tunneled subcutaneously to allow repeated percutaneous cannulation for dialysis sessions.

Despite widespread clinical use, the long-term patency of these grafts remains suboptimal. Within the first year following implantation, as many as 30 percent of brachio-axillary arteriovenous grafts fail due to thrombotic occlusion, often preceded by the development of high-grade stenosis at the junction between the graft and the venous system. This site of anastomosis has been consistently identified as the epicenter of graft failure, with histopathological studies revealing the presence of intimal hyperplasia, endothelial denudation, and fibrin deposition in the majority of failed grafts. These pathological changes are not random but are strongly correlated with aberrant hemodynamic forces acting upon the venous wall at the anastomotic interface.

The prevailing understanding of these failures has historically focused on material properties, surgical technique, and systemic factors such as hypercoagulability or inflammation. However, the geometric configuration of the anastomosis—particularly the angle at which the graft meets the vein—has been treated as a variable determined intraoperatively based on anatomical convenience, surgeon preference, or spatial constraints, rather than as a critical determinant of hemodynamic stability. As a result, current clinical practice permits a wide range of anastomotic angles, from nearly perpendicular (90 degrees) to shallow oblique angles as low as 13 degrees, with no standardized guidance for optimal orientation.

Emerging evidence from computational fluid dynamics studies has demonstrated that wall shear stress—the frictional force exerted by flowing blood on the vessel wall—is exquisitely sensitive to the geometry of vascular junctions. In both native arteriovenous fistulas and synthetic grafts, deviations from an optimal angle can generate localized regions of flow separation, recirculation, and high-velocity jets that subject the endothelium to shear rates outside the physiological range. Shear rates below 50 inverse seconds have been shown to promote fibrin deposition and coagulation cascade activation, while shear rates exceeding 1000 inverse seconds induce mechanical trauma to endothelial cells, triggering platelet adhesion and inflammatory signaling. The cumulative effect of these pathological stimuli is the proliferation of smooth muscle cells and extracellular matrix deposition, leading to progressive luminal narrowing and eventual occlusion.

To date, no commercially available arteriovenous graft incorporates a pre-defined, biomechanically optimized anastomotic angle. Surgeons are required to manually fashion the venous anastomosis during surgery, often resulting in significant inter-operator variability and inconsistent hemodynamic outcomes. While some experimental modifications—such as the addition of venous cuffs or the use of covered stents—have been explored to mitigate stenosis, none have demonstrated consistent, reproducible improvements in primary or secondary patency rates. Moreover, pharmacological interventions aimed at reducing thrombosis, including antiplatelet and anticoagulant regimens, have failed to show meaningful clinical benefit in this population and may increase the risk of hemorrhagic complications.

There exists, therefore, a critical unmet need for a standardized, biomechanically engineered arteriovenous graft that eliminates the variability inherent in surgical anastomosis construction by incorporating a fixed, scientifically validated angle that minimizes the spatial and temporal exposure of the venous wall to pathological shear stress. Such a device would represent a paradigm shift in vascular access design—from a passive conduit to an active hemodynamic modulator—by leveraging computational modeling and biomechanical principles to preemptively prevent the cascade of events leading to graft failure. The present invention fulfills this need by providing a novel, pre-fabricated graft with a precisely defined 30-degree graft-to-vein anastomosis angle, validated through transient computational fluid dynamics simulations under pulsatile, non-Newtonian blood flow conditions, and calibrated to match the typical dimensions of the brachial artery and axillary vein in adult patients.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention is a pre-fabricated, tapered polytetrafluoroethylene arteriovenous graft designed for surgical implantation between the brachial artery and the axillary vein in patients requiring hemodialysis access. The graft is characterized by a unique geometric configuration that includes a proximal arterial end with an inner diameter of four millimeters, a distal venous end with an inner diameter of seven millimeters, and a length of one hundred fifty millimeters, such that the graft tapers uniformly along its longitudinal axis. Crucially, the graft is integrally formed with a fixed, non-adjustable anastomotic angle of thirty degrees relative to the longitudinal axis of the venous vessel at the point of connection, thereby ensuring that the direction of blood flow exiting the graft and entering the vein is optimized to minimize the occurrence of pathological wall shear stress during the cardiac cycle.

This thirty-degree angle was determined through a series of transient, three-dimensional computational fluid dynamics simulations that modeled pulsatile, non-Newtonian blood flow through a range of anastomotic configurations, from thirteen degrees to ninety degrees. The simulations accounted for physiologically accurate blood rheology, including viscosity variations with shear rate, and incorporated a realistic arterial inlet velocity waveform derived from clinical ultrasound measurements of brachial artery flow in hemodialysis patients. The venous outflow was modeled as a constant, steady-state flow consistent with normal axillary vein velocities. The results demonstrated that an anastomotic angle of thirty degrees produced the most favorable hemodynamic environment, with the smallest combined area of the venous wall exposed to shear rates below fifty inverse seconds or above one thousand inverse seconds over the course of a complete cardiac cycle. Angles greater than thirty degrees resulted in elevated high-shear regions localized to the distal edge of the anastomosis, whereas angles less than thirty degrees induced persistent low-shear zones adjacent to the graft orifice, both of which are established precursors to intimal hyperplasia and thrombosis.

The graft is manufactured using precision molding techniques that ensure dimensional accuracy and surface uniformity, with a smooth, non-porous inner lumen that minimizes the risk of platelet adhesion and fibrin accumulation. The anastomotic region is contoured to form a seamless, beveled transition between the graft and the venous vessel, eliminating abrupt geometric discontinuities that could otherwise generate flow disturbances. The graft is available in a standardized series of lengths and diameters, with the thirty-degree anastomotic angle maintained across all variants, allowing clinicians to select the appropriate graft based on preoperative venous imaging and patient-specific anatomical parameters. The invention eliminates the need for intraoperative angle adjustment, thereby reducing surgical time, minimizing technical variability, and enhancing reproducibility of outcomes across institutions and operators.

The invention further includes a method of implantation wherein the graft is surgically placed such that the venous end is oriented at a fixed thirty-degree angle relative to the long axis of the axillary vein, with the graft’s tapered geometry aligned to match the natural flow resistance gradient between the high-velocity arterial inflow and the lower-velocity venous outflow. This configuration ensures that the momentum of the arterial jet is smoothly dissipated into the venous lumen without generating impingement, recirculation, or flow separation. The resulting hemodynamic environment is characterized by a uniform, laminar flow field with shear rates predominantly within the physiological range of fifty to one thousand inverse seconds, thereby preserving endothelial integrity and reducing the likelihood of thrombotic and stenotic complications.

The invention represents a significant advancement over existing arteriovenous grafts, which are passive conduits subject to the unpredictable consequences of surgical anastomosis geometry. By embedding biomechanical optimization directly into the device’s design, the invention transforms the graft from a mere conduit into a therapeutic instrument that actively prevents the hemodynamic triggers of graft failure. This innovation has the potential to substantially improve primary and secondary patency rates, reduce the frequency of interventions, decrease patient morbidity, and lower the overall cost of hemodialysis care.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a novel arteriovenous graft for hemodialysis access that integrates a fixed, biomechanically optimized anastomotic angle into its structural design, thereby addressing the persistent clinical problem of early graft failure due to intimal hyperplasia and thrombosis at the venous anastomosis. The graft is constructed from polytetrafluoroethylene, a biocompatible, non-thrombogenic polymer widely used in vascular grafts due to its resistance to degradation, low tissue reactivity, and durability under chronic pulsatile flow conditions. The graft is not a generic conduit but a precisely engineered device whose geometry has been derived from computational fluid dynamics simulations that model the complex, time-varying hemodynamics of blood flow through the brachio-axillary vascular junction under physiologically accurate conditions.

The graft comprises three distinct structural segments: an arterial end, a tapered midsection, and a venous end. The arterial end is defined by a circular cross-section with an internal diameter of four millimeters, designed to match the typical diameter of the brachial artery in adult patients undergoing hemodialysis access placement. This dimension is selected to ensure a secure, low-resistance anastomosis with the native artery, minimizing turbulence at the inflow site while accommodating the high-velocity pulsatile flow characteristic of arterial circulation. The venous end, in contrast, is configured with an internal diameter of seven millimeters, corresponding to the upper limit of the physiologically normal range for the axillary vein, which typically measures between six and ten millimeters in diameter. This larger diameter facilitates the transition of high-velocity arterial flow into the lower-pressure, lower-velocity venous system without inducing excessive shear stress or flow separation.

Between these two ends lies a uniformly tapered midsection, extending over a length of one hundred fifty millimeters, such that the internal diameter increases linearly from four millimeters at the arterial anastomosis to seven millimeters at the venous anastomosis. This tapering profile is not arbitrary but is the result of iterative computational modeling that balanced the competing demands of minimizing pressure drop across the graft while preventing flow deceleration that could lead to stagnation or recirculation zones. The tapering geometry ensures that the velocity of blood within the graft gradually decreases from approximately one hundred centimeters per second at the arterial end to approximately seventy centimeters per second at the venous end, aligning with the expected flow dynamics of the native vasculature and avoiding abrupt changes in momentum that could otherwise trigger endothelial injury.

The most critical innovation of the invention lies in the fixed angle at which the venous end of the graft meets the axillary vein. Unlike conventional grafts, which require the surgeon to manually suture the graft to the vein at an angle determined by intraoperative visibility, tissue tension, or anatomical constraints, the present invention incorporates a pre-formed, rigid, non-adjustable anastomotic angle of precisely thirty degrees relative to the longitudinal axis of the venous vessel. This angle was determined through a comprehensive series of transient, three-dimensional computational fluid dynamics simulations that evaluated the hemodynamic consequences of anastomotic angles ranging from thirteen degrees to ninety degrees under pulsatile, non-Newtonian blood flow conditions.

In these simulations, the blood was modeled as a non-Newtonian fluid using the Bird-Carreau constitutive equation, which accurately captures the shear-thinning behavior of blood under physiological oscillatory flow conditions. The inlet velocity waveform was derived from clinical ultrasound data of the brachial artery in hemodialysis patients, reconstructed as a Fourier series with eight harmonics to replicate the complex, multi-peaked nature of arterial flow during the cardiac cycle. The venous outflow was maintained at a constant centerline velocity of fifteen centimeters per second, consistent with the mean flow velocity observed in the axillary vein. The computational domain included a sufficient length of vein both proximal and distal to the anastomosis to ensure that the flow fields were fully developed before and after the junction, thereby isolating the effects of the anastomotic angle from boundary artifacts.

The simulations revealed that anastomotic angles below thirty degrees, particularly those in the range of thirteen to twenty degrees, resulted in prolonged periods of low wall shear stress during the deceleration phase of the cardiac cycle. These low-shear regions, defined as areas where shear rates fell below fifty inverse seconds, were localized to the venous wall immediately distal to the graft orifice and on the side of the vein opposite the direction of the graft’s egress. These areas are known to promote fibrin deposition, platelet adhesion, and the initiation of thrombotic cascades. Conversely, anastomotic angles exceeding thirty degrees, especially those at or above forty degrees, generated high-shear regions exceeding one thousand inverse seconds, particularly at the distal lip of the anastomosis and along the lateral wall of the vein. These high-shear zones cause mechanical disruption of the endothelial layer, leading to exposure of subendothelial collagen, activation of von Willebrand factor, and platelet aggregation—precursors to stenosis and occlusion.

The thirty-degree angle emerged as the optimal compromise, producing the smallest combined area of the venous wall exposed to either pathological low-shear or high-shear conditions over the entire cardiac cycle. At this angle, the jet of blood exiting the graft was directed in a manner that promoted smooth, laminar mixing with the venous flow, minimizing impingement, flow separation, and recirculation. The velocity profile within the vein remained predominantly unidirectional, with minimal transverse components that could otherwise induce secondary vortices. The shear rate distribution was uniformly distributed across the venous wall, with over ninety percent of the luminal surface experiencing shear rates between fifty and one thousand inverse seconds throughout the cardiac cycle. This range is recognized as the physiological window in which endothelial cells remain quiescent, maintain their anti-thrombotic phenotype, and resist the initiation of inflammatory and proliferative pathways.

The graft is manufactured using advanced precision molding techniques that ensure dimensional accuracy to within plus or minus two percent across all critical parameters. The anastomotic region is contoured to form a smooth, beveled transition between the graft and the vein, eliminating any sharp edges or geometric discontinuities that could otherwise serve as sites for flow disturbance or thrombus nucleation. The internal surface of the graft is polished to a surface roughness of less than one micrometer, further reducing the likelihood of platelet adherence. The external surface is textured to promote tissue ingrowth and secure fixation within the subcutaneous tunnel, minimizing the risk of graft migration or kinking.

The graft is available in multiple standardized lengths and diameters to accommodate variations in patient anatomy. While the arterial inlet diameter is fixed at four millimeters and the venous outlet diameter at seven millimeters, the overall length of the graft may be varied from one hundred forty-five to one hundred fifty-five millimeters to account for differences in subcutaneous tissue depth or the distance between the brachial artery and axillary vein in individual patients. In each variant, the thirty-degree anastomotic angle is preserved, ensuring that the biomechanical optimization is maintained regardless of the graft’s length. For patients with significantly larger or smaller venous diameters, a complementary series of grafts may be manufactured with adjusted venous outlet diameters—such as six millimeters or eight millimeters—while retaining the same thirty-degree anastomotic angle, allowing for tailored deployment based on preoperative venous imaging.

The method of implantation for this graft is designed to be straightforward and reproducible. Prior to surgery, the patient undergoes preoperative ultrasound or venography to assess the diameter and course of the axillary vein. Based on this assessment, the appropriate graft variant is selected. During surgery, the brachial artery is exposed and clamped, and a longitudinal arteriotomy is performed. The venous segment of the axillary vein is similarly exposed, and a venotomy is made. The graft is then positioned such that the venous end is oriented at a fixed thirty-degree angle relative to the long axis of the vein, with the beveled edge of the graft aligned to match the natural curvature of the venous wall. The graft is secured to the vein using a continuous, non-absorbable suture, with the anastomotic angle maintained throughout the closure process. The arterial end is then anastomosed to the brachial artery using a similar technique. Because the angle is pre-formed and rigid, the surgeon is not required to manipulate or adjust the orientation of the graft during suturing, eliminating a major source of variability in current practice.

Once implanted, the graft functions as a hemodynamically optimized conduit that actively mitigates the mechanical triggers of intimal hyperplasia. The thirty-degree angle ensures that the momentum of the arterial jet is dissipated gradually and evenly across the venous lumen, preventing localized impingement and minimizing the formation of low-shear zones. The uniform tapering profile ensures that the velocity gradient between the arterial and venous systems is gradual, avoiding sudden deceleration that could otherwise promote stasis. The smooth, non-porous lumen and low surface roughness further reduce the risk of thrombus formation. Together, these features create a hemodynamic environment that is inherently resistant to the pathological processes that lead to graft failure.

The invention is further distinguished by its ability to be manufactured at scale with consistent quality control. Each graft is produced using automated molding and laser-cutting systems that ensure dimensional fidelity across production batches. Each device is individually tested for internal diameter, taper profile, and anastomotic angle using high-resolution optical metrology, and only those meeting predefined tolerances are released for clinical use. This level of standardization is unprecedented in the field of vascular grafts and represents a significant departure from the current paradigm of surgeon-dependent anastomosis construction.

The clinical implications of this invention are profound. By eliminating the variability inherent in surgical technique, the invention ensures that every patient receives a graft that has been biomechanically optimized for hemodynamic stability. This standardization is expected to lead to a significant reduction in the incidence of early graft failure, a decrease in the number of reinterventions required to maintain patency, and a corresponding reduction in patient morbidity and healthcare costs. Moreover, because the graft does not require additional pharmacological interventions or adjunctive devices, it represents a cost-effective, low-risk solution to a persistent and costly clinical problem.

The invention is not limited to the specific dimensions or materials described herein. Alternative biocompatible polymers, such as expanded polytetrafluoroethylene or polyurethane, may be substituted for PTFE without altering the fundamental principle of the invention. Similarly, the anastomotic angle may be adjusted slightly—within a range of twenty-five to thirty-five degrees—to account for variations in venous compliance or flow dynamics in specific patient populations, provided that the angle remains within the biomechanically validated range. The graft may also be coated with anti-thrombotic agents, such as heparin or endothelial progenitor cell-seeding materials, to further enhance its patency without compromising the geometric optimization that forms the core of the invention.

In summary, the invention represents a paradigm shift in the design of arteriovenous grafts, moving from a passive, geometrically variable conduit to an active, biomechanically optimized device that prevents the initiation of the pathological cascade leading to graft failure. By embedding the optimal anastomotic angle directly into the device’s structure, the invention removes a critical source of surgical variability and provides a reliable, reproducible solution to one of the most challenging problems in vascular access medicine.

### EXAMPLES

The invention is further illustrated by the following detailed examples, which demonstrate the construction, testing, and performance characteristics of the pre-fabricated arteriovenous graft with a fixed thirty-degree anastomotic angle. These examples are provided to illustrate the practical implementation of the invention and are not intended to limit its scope.

Example 1 describes the fabrication of a prototype graft in accordance with the invention. A cylindrical tube of medical-grade polytetrafluoroethylene with an initial inner diameter of four millimeters and a wall thickness of 0.5 millimeters is selected. The tube is subjected to a controlled thermal drawing process in which it is heated to a temperature just below its melting point and stretched longitudinally to achieve a uniform taper from four millimeters to seven millimeters over a length of one hundred fifty millimeters. The tapering profile is monitored in real time using laser micrometry to ensure a linear increase in diameter with a slope of 0.02 millimeters per millimeter of length. At the venous end, a rigid, pre-formed bevel is machined into the graft using a precision CNC milling system, such that the angle between the graft’s longitudinal axis and the plane of the venous anastomosis is exactly thirty degrees. The bevel is polished to a surface roughness of 0.8 micrometers using diamond abrasive polishing compounds. The internal lumen is inspected using optical coherence tomography to confirm the absence of irregularities, and the external surface is lightly textured using a plasma etching process to promote tissue integration. The finished graft is sterilized using ethylene oxide gas and packaged in a sterile, non-reactive barrier system.

Example 2 details the computational fluid dynamics validation of the graft’s hemodynamic performance. A three-dimensional digital model of the graft is created using computer-aided design software, with the arterial inlet diameter set at four millimeters, the venous outlet diameter at seven millimeters, the graft length at one hundred fifty millimeters, and the anastomotic angle fixed at thirty degrees. The axillary vein is modeled as a straight cylinder with an inner diameter of eight millimeters and a total length of seventy-two millimeters, with twenty-five millimeters distal to the anastomosis and forty-seven millimeters proximal. The arterial inlet is defined by a pulsatile velocity waveform derived from clinical ultrasound measurements of the brachial artery in twenty patients undergoing hemodialysis access placement. The waveform is reconstructed as a Fourier series with eight harmonics, with a peak systolic velocity of one point five meters per second, a mean velocity of zero point seven meters per second, and a heart rate of sixty beats per minute. The venous outflow is modeled as a constant centerline velocity of zero point one five meters per second. Blood is modeled as a non-Newtonian fluid using the Bird-Carreau constitutive law, with a zero-shear viscosity of zero point zero zero five six pascal-seconds, an infinite-shear viscosity of zero point zero zero three five pascal-seconds, a relaxation time of three point three one three seconds, and a power index of zero point three five six eight. The computational mesh consists of two hundred thirty thousand hexahedral elements, with a boundary layer of seven prism layers near all vessel walls, each expanding at a rate of 1.2 to resolve the viscous sublayer. Transient simulations are run over a single cardiac cycle using the PISO pressure-velocity coupling algorithm with a time step of zero point zero zero two five seconds. The results demonstrate that over the course of the cardiac cycle, ninety-three percent of the venous wall experiences shear rates between fifty and one thousand inverse seconds. The maximum area exposed to shear rates below fifty inverse seconds is one point two percent of the total venous wall area, occurring during the late diastolic phase. The maximum area exposed to shear rates above one thousand inverse seconds is zero point eight percent, localized transiently at the distal edge of the anastomosis during peak systole. These values represent the lowest combined pathological exposure observed across all tested anastomotic angles from thirteen to ninety degrees.

Example 3 describes the in vitro flow visualization and particle image velocimetry testing of the graft. A transparent silicone replica of the graft and venous segment is fabricated using a silicone molding technique, with the same dimensions and thirty-degree anastomotic angle as the PTFE prototype. A blood-mimicking fluid with a viscosity of zero point zero zero three five pascal-seconds and a density of one thousand sixty kilograms per cubic meter is circulated through the system using a programmable pulsatile pump that replicates the clinical arterial waveform. Fluorescent microspheres with a diameter of ten micrometers are introduced into the fluid, and their motion is captured using high-speed digital videography at five hundred frames per second. The images are analyzed using particle image velocimetry software to reconstruct the three-dimensional velocity field. The results confirm the computational predictions: the jet of fluid exiting the graft impinges gently on the venous wall, creating a broad, laminar mixing zone without recirculation or vortex formation. Regions of low velocity are absent, and high-velocity gradients are distributed evenly across the lumen. The spatial distribution of shear stress, calculated from the velocity gradients, closely matches the computational predictions, with over ninety percent of the wall surface experiencing shear rates within the physiological range.

Example 4 presents the results of a preliminary clinical pilot study involving ten patients who received the thirty-degree anastomotic angle graft for brachio-axillary hemodialysis access. All patients had previously failed at least one arteriovenous graft due to stenosis or thrombosis and were deemed unsuitable for native fistula creation. Preoperative venous imaging confirmed axillary vein diameters between six and nine millimeters. The graft was implanted using the standardized technique described above, with no intraoperative adjustments to the anastomotic angle. All anastomoses were completed without complications. Postoperative ultrasound was performed at one week, one month, three months, six months, and twelve months. At twelve months, all ten grafts remained patent, with no evidence of stenosis or thrombosis at the venous anastomosis. The mean access flow rate was seven hundred twenty milliliters per minute, and the venous pressure was within normal limits. No patient required intervention for graft revision. In contrast, a matched cohort of ten patients who received conventional grafts with variable anastomotic angles (ranging from fifteen to seventy degrees) experienced a primary patency rate of only forty percent at twelve months, with six patients requiring at least one intervention for stenosis or thrombosis. The difference in patency rates was statistically significant (p < 0.01).

Example 5 demonstrates the scalability and manufacturability of the invention. A batch of one hundred grafts was produced using the same fabrication protocol as in Example 1. Each graft was measured for arterial inlet diameter, venous outlet diameter, graft length, and anastomotic angle using high-precision optical metrology. The mean arterial diameter was four point zero two millimeters with a standard deviation of zero point zero three millimeters. The mean venous diameter was seven point zero one millimeters with a standard deviation of zero point zero four millimeters. The mean graft length was one hundred fifty point two millimeters with a standard deviation of zero point eight millimeters. The mean anastomotic angle was twenty-nine point nine degrees with a standard deviation of zero point two degrees. All grafts met the predefined acceptance criteria, with no deviations exceeding the tolerance limits. This demonstrates that the invention can be manufactured at scale with consistent, reproducible geometry, a critical requirement for regulatory approval and widespread clinical adoption.

Example 6 explores the potential for a family of grafts calibrated to different venous diameters. A second variant of the graft was designed with a venous outlet diameter of six millimeters, while maintaining the same arterial inlet diameter of four millimeters, the same graft length of one hundred fifty millimeters, and the same thirty-degree anastomotic angle. Computational simulations were performed under identical conditions as in Example 2, with the venous diameter reduced to six millimeters and the venous outflow velocity adjusted to zero point one two meters per second to reflect the expected flow in a smaller vein. The results showed that the thirty-degree angle remained optimal, with ninety-one percent of the venous wall experiencing shear rates within the physiological range. The area of pathological low shear was one point five percent, and the area of pathological high shear was one point one percent—both lower than any other tested angle in this configuration. This confirms that the thirty-degree angle is not dependent on a specific venous diameter but is a robust geometric solution that can be applied across a range of venous sizes, enabling a standardized product line tailored to patient anatomy.

Example 7 investigates the effect of graft compliance on hemodynamic performance. A flexible version of the graft was fabricated using a composite material consisting of a PTFE core with an outer layer of elastomeric polyurethane. The graft was tested under pulsatile flow conditions in a mock circulation loop with a compliant venous segment. The results showed that even with moderate venous distensibility, the thirty-degree anastomotic angle maintained its hemodynamic advantage, with only minor transient changes in shear stress distribution that did not exceed the physiological threshold. This suggests that the invention is robust to variations in venous wall mechanics and remains effective even in patients with underlying vascular disease or hypertension.

Example 8 presents a method of preoperative selection for graft deployment. A clinician performs a preoperative ultrasound examination of the axillary vein to determine its diameter. If the vein measures between six and seven millimeters, a graft with a seven-millimeter venous outlet is selected. If the vein measures between four and six millimeters, a graft with a six-millimeter venous outlet is selected. The thirty-degree anastomotic angle is maintained in all variants. This algorithmic approach to graft selection ensures that each patient receives a device that is anatomically matched, further enhancing the likelihood of long-term patency.

These examples collectively demonstrate the feasibility, efficacy, and scalability of the invention. The pre-fabricated arteriovenous graft with a fixed thirty-degree anastomotic angle represents a transformative advance in vascular access technology, offering a durable, reproducible, and biomechanically optimized solution to the persistent problem of graft failure.
