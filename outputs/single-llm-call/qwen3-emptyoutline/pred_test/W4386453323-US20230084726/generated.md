## FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of quantum sensing and quantum information processing, specifically to methods and systems for enhancing the sensitivity and signal-to-noise ratio of solid-state spin-based sensors operating at room temperature. More particularly, the invention pertains to a quantum logic enhanced sensing protocol that leverages the coherent coupling between electronic spin qubits and nearby nuclear spin memory qubits in nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centers embedded within diamond crystals to achieve repeated, high-fidelity readouts of sensor states without requiring additional optical excitation or physical reinitialization of the electronic spin between measurements. This approach enables substantial improvements in the sensitivity of alternating current (AC) magnetic field measurements, as well as other physical quantities detectable through dynamical decoupling sequences, by exploiting the long coherence times of nuclear spins as stable quantum memory elements. The invention is applicable to ensemble-based quantum sensors utilizing nitrogen-vacancy centers in diamond, and is extendable to other solid-state spin platforms featuring hybrid electronic-nuclear qubit architectures, such as silicon carbide defects, rare-earth ion dopants in crystals, or molecular spin systems with coupled spin degrees of freedom. The method is particularly suited for applications requiring high spectral resolution, low-power operation, and extended measurement durations, including nanoscale nuclear magnetic resonance, biomagnetic field detection, materials characterization under stress or pressure, and dark matter search experiments employing spin-based detectors.

## BACKGROUND

Quantum sensors based on nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centers in diamond have emerged as powerful tools for detecting magnetic fields, electric fields, temperature gradients, and strain with nanoscale spatial resolution and high sensitivity at ambient conditions. The electronic spin of the NV center serves as a highly coherent quantum probe, whose state can be initialized, manipulated, and read out optically using microwave and laser pulses. Dynamical decoupling sequences, such as XY8 and DROID, are routinely employed to extend the electronic spin coherence time and isolate the sensor from environmental noise, enabling the detection of weak AC signals at specific frequencies. However, the sensitivity of these sensors is fundamentally limited by the shot noise associated with the optical readout process, which requires repeated initialization and measurement cycles to accumulate sufficient signal statistics. Each optical readout pulse not only consumes time but also induces decoherence in nearby nuclear spins through spin polarization transfer and lattice heating, thereby constraining the number of measurements that can be performed within the lifetime of the quantum memory. Conventional approaches to improving sensitivity rely on increasing the number of measurement repetitions, which is directly limited by the electronic spin coherence time and the rate at which the sensor can be optically reinitialized without degrading the signal. Moreover, the optical readout process itself introduces significant overhead, as each measurement requires a sequence of laser pulses for initialization, state-dependent fluorescence collection, and spin re-polarization, typically lasting several microseconds per cycle. This overhead becomes increasingly prohibitive when attempting to extend the sensing duration beyond the electronic spin coherence limit, as is necessary for detecting low-frequency signals or achieving high spectral resolution. While nuclear spins associated with NV centers possess coherence times orders of magnitude longer than their electronic counterparts, they have historically been treated as passive environmental noise sources rather than active components of the sensing protocol. Efforts to utilize nuclear spins as quantum memory elements have been limited by the difficulty of transferring quantum information between electronic and nuclear spins with high fidelity, the lack of efficient methods for repeated readout without degrading the memory state, and the absence of a systematic framework for optimizing signal accumulation over multiple readout cycles while accounting for the exponential decay of signal amplitude due to nuclear spin relaxation. Furthermore, prior techniques for enhancing readout fidelity have relied on post-processing of ensemble-averaged data without incorporating the temporal decay profile of the memory state into the weighting of individual measurements, resulting in suboptimal signal-to-noise ratios. There exists a critical need for a method that decouples the readout frequency from the optical reinitialization rate, enables multiple high-fidelity measurements of the same quantum state without repeated optical pumping, and systematically enhances sensitivity by exploiting the extended lifetime of nuclear spins as quantum memory while minimizing the total measurement time. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a quantum logic enhanced sensing protocol that integrates coherent quantum gates between electronic and nuclear spins with a weighted accumulation of repeated readouts, thereby achieving unprecedented gains in sensitivity without requiring longer electronic coherence times, higher laser powers, or more complex pulse sequences.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a quantum logic enhanced sensing protocol that dramatically improves the sensitivity of solid-state spin-based sensors by exploiting the long-lived nuclear spin memory associated with nitrogen-vacancy centers in diamond. The method involves the coherent transfer of quantum information from an electronic spin sensor to a nearby nuclear spin memory qubit via a SWAP operation, followed by repeated, non-destructive readouts of the stored information by mapping the nuclear spin state back onto the electronic spin through a series of controlled-NOT (CNOT) operations, each followed by an optical readout pulse. This sequence allows for the acquisition of multiple independent measurements of the same sensor state within the nuclear spin’s relaxation time, thereby multiplying the effective signal-to-noise ratio without increasing the number of optical initialization cycles. The signal amplitude from each readout cycle is weighted according to its expected signal-to-noise contribution, which is determined by the ratio of the measured signal amplitude to the square of the noise standard deviation at that cycle, thereby maximizing the overall signal-to-noise ratio of the accumulated measurement. The protocol is implemented using a sequence of microwave and radiofrequency pulses to execute the SWAP and CNOT gates, combined with precisely timed optical pulses for initialization and readout. The electronic spin is first initialized optically, manipulated using dynamical decoupling sequences to sense an external AC magnetic field, and then its state is transferred to the nuclear spin via a SWAP gate. After this transfer, the electronic spin is reset to its ground state using a brief optical polarization pulse, allowing the same electronic spin to be reused for subsequent readout cycles without reinitializing the nuclear memory. Each readout cycle consists of a CNOT gate that entangles the nuclear spin state with the electronic spin, followed by an optical readout pulse that measures the electronic spin state, thereby indirectly revealing the state of the nuclear memory. This cycle is repeated N times, with N limited only by the nuclear spin’s longitudinal relaxation time T₁. The signal from each of the N readouts is recorded and weighted by the factor Aₙ / σₙ², where Aₙ is the amplitude of the signal in the n-th readout and σₙ is the standard deviation of the noise in that measurement. The weighted sum of all readouts is then used to construct a final measurement result with enhanced signal-to-noise ratio. The protocol is compatible with a wide range of dynamical decoupling sequences, including XY8 and DROID-60, and is particularly effective when the sensing duration exceeds the combined overhead time of the SWAP operation and the readout cycle. The invention achieves a signal-to-noise ratio enhancement of up to thirty-fold compared to conventional single-readout protocols and a sensitivity enhancement exceeding eleven-fold in correlation spectroscopy measurements, with the potential for even greater improvements in systems with longer nuclear spin lifetimes or reduced optical power. The method is fully compatible with existing NV center hardware, requires no modification to the diamond material beyond standard nitrogen doping, and operates under ambient conditions without cryogenic cooling. The invention enables order-of-magnitude improvements in the sensitivity of quantum sensors for applications such as nanoscale nuclear magnetic resonance, biological magnetometry, and dark matter detection, while maintaining high spectral resolution and operational simplicity.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a quantum logic enhanced sensing protocol that fundamentally transforms the way quantum sensors based on nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centers in diamond acquire and process measurement data. Rather than relying on repeated optical initialization and single-shot readouts of the electronic spin, the invention introduces a novel architecture in which the quantum state of the electronic spin sensor is coherently transferred to a nearby nuclear spin memory qubit, enabling multiple high-fidelity readouts of the same sensor state without further optical intervention. This approach decouples the readout rate from the optical reinitialization rate, thereby circumventing the primary bottleneck in conventional NV-based sensing: the slow and noisy optical readout process. The core innovation lies in the integration of quantum logic gates—specifically the SWAP and CNOT operations—between the electronic and nuclear spins, combined with a weighted accumulation of readout signals that accounts for the exponential decay of the nuclear spin polarization over time. The protocol is implemented using a precisely timed sequence of microwave, radiofrequency, and optical pulses, all of which are compatible with existing laboratory setups for NV center magnetometry. The method begins with the initialization of the electronic spin of the NV center using a green laser pulse, which polarizes the spin into its mₛ = 0 ground state with high fidelity. Following initialization, the electronic spin is subjected to a dynamical decoupling sequence designed to sense an external AC magnetic field. This sequence, which may be an XY8, DROID-60, or any other multi-pulse sequence capable of filtering out low-frequency noise and resonantly coupling to the target signal, accumulates a phase shift in the electronic spin state proportional to the integral of the magnetic field over the sensing interval. After the sensing interval, the accumulated quantum state is transferred from the electronic spin to the nuclear spin of a nearby ¹⁵N atom, which is naturally present in the diamond lattice due to nitrogen doping. This transfer is accomplished through a SWAP operation, which is implemented using a tailored microwave and radiofrequency pulse sequence that resonantly drives the flip-flop transition between the electronic and nuclear spins. The SWAP operation is chosen to have a duration of approximately 16.5 microseconds, a value determined by the strength of the hyperfine coupling between the NV electronic spin and the ¹⁵N nuclear spin, and is optimized to maximize the fidelity of the state transfer, which has been experimentally verified to exceed 93%. Following the SWAP, the electronic spin is reset to its ground state using a brief optical polarization pulse, which reinitializes the electronic spin without disturbing the nuclear spin memory. This reset step is critical, as it allows the same electronic spin to serve as a readout interface for multiple subsequent measurements, thereby eliminating the need to reinitialize the entire sensor system for each readout. Once the electronic spin is reset, the nuclear spin state is read out by applying a controlled-NOT (CNOT) gate, which entangles the state of the nuclear spin with that of the electronic spin. The CNOT gate is implemented using a radiofrequency pulse conditioned on the state of the electronic spin, such that the nuclear spin’s state is flipped only if the electronic spin is in a specific state, thereby mapping the nuclear spin’s superposition onto the electronic spin. Immediately following the CNOT gate, an optical readout pulse is applied, which measures the fluorescence intensity of the electronic spin and thereby infers the state of the nuclear memory. This cycle—CNOT followed by optical readout—is repeated N times, where N is limited only by the longitudinal relaxation time T₁ of the nuclear spin, which, under the experimental conditions described herein, is measured to be approximately 3.44 milliseconds at a bias magnetic field of 3700 gauss. Each readout yields a signal amplitude Aₙ, which corresponds to the measured contrast in the NV fluorescence, and a noise standard deviation σₙ, which is determined from the statistical variation of the fluorescence signal over multiple repetitions of the same readout cycle. Crucially, the signal amplitude Aₙ decays exponentially with each successive readout due to the finite T₁ of the nuclear spin, meaning that later readouts carry less information than earlier ones. To account for this decay and maximize the overall signal-to-noise ratio, each readout is weighted by the factor Aₙ / σₙ², which represents the inverse-variance weighting commonly used in statistical estimation theory. This weighting ensures that readouts with higher signal-to-noise contributions are given greater importance in the final measurement, while those with diminished signal due to nuclear spin relaxation are appropriately downweighted. The weighted sum of all N readouts is then computed to produce a final measurement result that exhibits a signal-to-noise ratio significantly higher than that achievable with a single optical readout. In experiments conducted using this protocol, the signal-to-noise ratio enhancement relative to conventional single-readout methods was measured to be 33.3 times for N = 2000 readout cycles, demonstrating the profound advantage of this approach. Importantly, this enhancement is not merely a consequence of averaging more measurements; it is a direct result of the quantum coherence preserved in the nuclear spin memory and the optimal statistical weighting of the readout data. The sensitivity enhancement, which accounts for the total measurement time including the overhead of the SWAP operation and each readout cycle, was found to be up to 11.3 times for correlation spectroscopy measurements with a correlation time of 0.75 milliseconds, and up to 5.6 times for non-correlation spectroscopy measurements using the DROID-60 sequence with a sensing duration of 144 microseconds. These enhancements arise because the overhead time per readout cycle—approximately 3 microseconds for the CNOT gate and optical readout—is negligible compared to the sensing duration, particularly when the sensing time exceeds the SWAP duration of 16.5 microseconds. Under such conditions, the total measurement time for the quantum logic enhanced protocol is dominated by the sensing interval, while the number of readouts is limited only by the nuclear spin lifetime, leading to a dramatic increase in the number of independent measurements per unit time. The protocol is further optimized by selecting the laser power to balance two competing factors: the nuclear spin lifetime and the speed of electronic spin reinitialization. At lower laser powers, the nuclear spin T₁ increases, but the optical reinitialization time also increases, reducing the number of possible readout cycles. At higher laser powers, reinitialization is faster, but the nuclear spin lifetime is shortened due to optical pumping effects. The optimal laser power, determined experimentally to be 130 milliwatts, achieves a compromise wherein approximately 75% of the NV electronic spins are re-polarized within a 3-microsecond optical pulse, while the nuclear spin T₁ remains sufficiently long to support over a thousand readout cycles. This power level is critical to the protocol’s performance, as it enables the maximum number of readouts within the nuclear spin’s coherence window. The invention is compatible with a wide variety of dynamical decoupling sequences, including those designed for sensing magnetic fields, electric fields, temperature, and strain. For example, when applied to the DROID-60 sequence, which decouples NV-NV dipolar interactions and extends the electronic spin coherence time to 144 microseconds, the quantum logic enhanced protocol achieves a sensitivity enhancement of 5.6 times compared to conventional readout, whereas the XY8 sequence, limited by NV-NV interactions to a coherence time of 28 microseconds, yields a smaller enhancement of 2.4 times. This demonstrates that the protocol’s effectiveness scales with the sensing duration, and that its greatest advantages are realized when the sensing interval is long relative to the overhead of the quantum logic operations. The protocol is also applicable to correlation spectroscopy, a technique in which the sensing interval is varied by changing the time delay between two dynamical decoupling sequences. In this context, the invention enables the acquisition of multiple readouts per correlation time, effectively multiplying the signal accumulation rate without increasing the total measurement time. By comparing the quantum logic enhanced measurement to a conventional measurement repeated M times under the constraint that the total acquisition time is identical, the sensitivity enhancement was found to be 11.3 times for correlation times up to 1.5 milliseconds. This result is particularly significant because correlation spectroscopy is widely used in high-resolution AC magnetometry, and the ability to enhance its sensitivity without altering the pulse sequence or increasing the measurement time represents a major advancement. The method is further scalable to other solid-state spin systems that feature coupled electronic and nuclear qubits, such as silicon carbide defects, rare-earth ions in crystals, or molecular spin clusters. The key requirement is the presence of a long-lived nuclear spin memory with a controllable coupling to an optically addressable electronic spin. The protocol does not require cryogenic temperatures, high vacuum, or complex magnetic field shielding, making it suitable for deployment in real-world environments. The invention also enables significant improvements in the sensitivity of nanoscale nuclear magnetic resonance (NV-NMR) experiments, where the goal is to detect the magnetic signals of small numbers of nuclear spins in a sample placed near the diamond surface. By extending the NV ensemble’s coherence time through reduced nitrogen doping—such as lowering the nitrogen concentration from 14 parts per million to 0.8 parts per million—the optimal sensing duration can be increased by a factor of 18, potentially enabling an order-of-magnitude improvement in sensitivity for NV-NMR applications. The protocol is also compatible with techniques for hyperpolarizing nuclear spins in the sample, which would further enhance the signal amplitude and extend the effective measurement window. The invention’s generality extends beyond magnetometry to other physical quantities detectable through dynamical decoupling, including crystal strain, pressure, and temperature gradients. Recent experiments have demonstrated that NV centers can be used to sense these quantities with sensing durations of tens of microseconds or longer, and under such conditions, the quantum logic enhanced protocol would similarly yield substantial sensitivity improvements. The method is also compatible with future extensions involving multiple nuclear spins coupled to a single electronic spin, or with quantum error correction schemes that protect the nuclear memory from decoherence. The invention does not require any new materials, novel fabrication techniques, or exotic physical conditions; it is implemented entirely through the precise control of existing microwave, radiofrequency, and optical pulses, all of which are standard in NV center laboratories. The protocol is robust to variations in the local environment, including fluctuations in the magnetic field, laser power, and microwave amplitude, as long as the timing and sequence of the gates are maintained with sufficient precision. The fidelity of the SWAP and CNOT operations is sufficiently high—greater than 93% for the SWAP and above 90% for the CNOT—to ensure that the accumulated signal-to-noise ratio is not degraded by gate errors. The invention’s performance is further validated by the agreement between the measured nuclear spin T₁ decay and the expected quadratic dependence on the magnetic field, confirming that the dominant relaxation mechanism is the flip-flop transition between the electronic and nuclear spins, which is suppressed at higher fields. The use of a feedback-stabilized electromagnet to maintain a bias field of 3700 gauss ensures that the nuclear spin lifetime remains stable throughout the measurement, which is essential for consistent weighting of the readout cycles. The optical readout system, comprising a 532-nanometer laser, an acousto-optic modulator, and a photodetector with a liquid light guide, is capable of resolving the small fluorescence contrast changes associated with the nuclear spin state, and the arbitrary waveform generator with 83-picosecond temporal resolution ensures that the microwave and radiofrequency pulses are applied with the precision required for high-fidelity quantum gates. The invention’s ability to achieve a thirty-fold signal-to-noise ratio enhancement and an eleven-fold sensitivity enhancement without increasing the measurement time or requiring more sensitive hardware represents a paradigm shift in quantum sensing. It transforms the nuclear spin from a passive noise source into an active quantum memory, and it redefines the relationship between measurement time, signal accumulation, and sensitivity. The protocol is not merely an incremental improvement; it is a fundamental rethinking of how quantum sensors acquire and process information. By decoupling the readout rate from the initialization rate and by optimally weighting the measurements according to their information content, the invention achieves a level of performance that was previously unattainable with conventional methods. This advancement opens new possibilities for detecting weak magnetic signals from biological tissues, characterizing nanoscale materials with unprecedented resolution, and searching for exotic physical phenomena such as dark matter or axion-like particles using spin-based detectors. The method is fully patentable, as it introduces a novel combination of quantum operations, statistical weighting, and temporal control that is not disclosed in the prior art and that provides a substantial and unexpected improvement in sensor performance. The invention is not an obvious extension of existing techniques, as prior work has either ignored the nuclear spin memory entirely or attempted to use it for single-shot readout without the repeated mapping and weighted accumulation that define the present protocol. The inclusion of the SWAP operation to transfer the state, the CNOT operations to enable repeated readout, and the inverse-variance weighting to optimize the final result constitute a unique and non-obvious invention that meets all criteria for patentability under U.S. and international patent law. The invention is therefore claimed in its entirety as a novel method for quantum logic enhanced sensing in solid-state spin systems, and its implementation in NV-diamond sensors represents a transformative advancement in the field of quantum metrology.
