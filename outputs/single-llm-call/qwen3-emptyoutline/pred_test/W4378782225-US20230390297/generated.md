# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The regulation of gene expression in mammalian cells is a highly orchestrated process that depends on the dynamic interplay between chromatin-modifying enzymes, transcriptional cofactors, and the three-dimensional architecture of the genome. Among the most critical regulators of this system are the COMPASS family of histone methyltransferases, which catalyze the methylation of lysine 4 on histone H3, a modification strongly associated with active transcription and enhancer function. Within this family, MLL3 and MLL4—encoded by the KMT2C and KMT2D genes, respectively—have emerged as principal enzymes responsible for mono-methylation of H3K4 at enhancer regions, a modification that serves as a foundational epigenetic mark for the recruitment of transcriptional activators such as p300 and UTX. These enzymes do not function in isolation; rather, they operate as core components of large, multi-subunit complexes that include NCOA6, PTIP, and UTX, forming a specialized branch of the COMPASS family dedicated to enhancer activation. Their activity is indispensable for the proper execution of developmental programs, including myogenesis, adipogenesis, cardiogenesis, and the differentiation of immune cell lineages such as germinal center B cells and macrophages. Loss of MLL3 or MLL4 function leads to profound defects in enhancer activation, resulting in the failure of lineage-specific gene expression programs and, consequently, developmental abnormalities.

In human disease, the significance of MLL3 and MLL4 is underscored by the high frequency of inactivating mutations observed across a broad spectrum of cancers. The Catalogue Of Somatic Mutations In Cancer (COSMIC) has identified KMT2C and KMT2D as among the most frequently mutated genes in human tumors, with recurrent truncating and missense mutations reported in colorectal, breast, bladder, prostate, lung, and hematological malignancies. These mutations are often heterozygous and occur early in tumorigenesis, suggesting that their loss confers a selective advantage to clonal expansion. Notably, the same genes are also implicated in Kabuki syndrome, a congenital disorder characterized by distinctive facial features, intellectual disability, and multisystem anomalies, with approximately 60% of cases caused by heterozygous truncating mutations in KMT2D. Despite the extensive cataloging of these mutations, the molecular mechanisms by which loss of MLL3 or MLL4 function drives oncogenesis or developmental pathology remain poorly understood. While it has been established that these proteins contribute to enhancer activation through both their catalytic activity—mediating H3K4 mono-methylation—and their catalytic-independent scaffolding role in recruiting other chromatin regulators, the downstream consequences of their loss on cellular physiology have not been fully elucidated. In particular, the extent to which the absence of MLL3/4 induces compensatory mechanisms, alters metabolic dependencies, or creates novel therapeutic vulnerabilities has remained an open question.

Recent advances in functional genomics and metabolomics have revealed that epigenetic regulators are not merely passive modifiers of chromatin structure but are deeply integrated into the metabolic networks that sustain cell proliferation and survival. Cancer cells, in particular, exhibit profound rewiring of metabolic pathways to meet the biosynthetic demands of rapid growth, with increased reliance on de novo nucleotide synthesis as a hallmark of transformed cells. The de novo purine and pyrimidine synthesis pathways, which generate the nucleotides required for DNA and RNA replication, are tightly regulated and often upregulated in malignancies. Inhibitors targeting these pathways, such as methotrexate and lometrexol, have been explored clinically for their anti-proliferative effects, yet their utility has been limited by a lack of predictive biomarkers to identify tumors most susceptible to such interventions. The discovery that mutations in chromatin regulators like MLL3 and MLL4 are prevalent across diverse cancers raises the compelling possibility that these mutations may create context-specific metabolic dependencies that can be therapeutically exploited. However, no prior study has demonstrated a direct mechanistic link between the loss of MLL3/4 function and an emergent vulnerability to inhibition of nucleotide metabolism. Furthermore, while compensatory activity among COMPASS family members has been suggested in other contexts, the possibility that loss of MLL3/4 leads to redistribution of shared subunits—such as WDR5, ASH2L, and RBBP5—to other methyltransferases like MLL1 or SET1A, thereby reprogramming transcriptional output and metabolic flux, has not been systematically investigated. This gap in knowledge has hindered the development of precision therapeutic strategies for cancers harboring MLL3/4 mutations, despite their high prevalence. The present invention addresses this critical unmet need by identifying a synthetic lethal interaction between MLL3/4 loss and inhibition of de novo purine synthesis, establishing a novel paradigm for targeted therapy in a wide range of malignancies and developmental disorders characterized by mutations in these genes.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a novel therapeutic strategy for the treatment of diseases associated with loss-of-function mutations in the MLL3 or MLL4 genes, which encode key histone methyltransferases of the COMPASS family. The invention is based on the discovery that cells lacking functional MLL3 or MLL4 exhibit a profound and selective dependency on de novo purine nucleotide synthesis, rendering them exquisitely sensitive to pharmacological inhibition of this pathway. This vulnerability arises not from the loss of catalytic activity per se, but from a compensatory reprogramming of the epigenetic landscape that leads to the aberrant upregulation of purine biosynthesis genes, a phenomenon mediated by the redistribution of shared COMPASS subunits to the MLL1 complex. In the absence of MLL3 and MLL4, the WDR5-RBBP5-ASH2L-DPY30 (WRAD) complex, which is normally shared among multiple COMPASS methyltransferases, becomes preferentially recruited to MLL1, resulting in increased chromatin binding of MLL1 and a concomitant elevation of H3K4 trimethylation at the promoters of genes involved in purine metabolism. This epigenetic rewiring drives increased transcriptional activity and protein expression of key enzymes in the de novo purine synthesis pathway, including GART, PAICS, and MTHFD1, thereby creating a heightened demand for purine precursors that cannot be met by salvage pathways alone. Consequently, inhibition of de novo purine synthesis—whether through direct targeting of GART with lometrexol, inhibition of folate metabolism with methotrexate, or blockade of serine hydroxymethyltransferase with SHIN1—induces catastrophic nucleotide depletion, cell cycle arrest, and apoptosis specifically in MLL3/4-deficient cells, while sparing cells with intact MLL3/4 function. This synthetic lethal interaction is conserved across species, from mouse embryonic stem cells to human cancer cell lines and in vivo xenograft models, and is particularly pronounced in tumors harboring truncating mutations in KMT2D, which result in complete loss of MLL4 protein. The invention further demonstrates that this metabolic vulnerability is independent of the catalytic SET domain of MLL3/4, as cells expressing catalytically inactive MLL3/4 mutants retain normal resistance to purine synthesis inhibitors, whereas complete loss of the proteins triggers sensitivity. This finding establishes that the scaffolding and structural roles of MLL3/4 in maintaining epigenetic equilibrium are paramount to preventing compensatory MLL1 activation. The invention provides a diagnostic and therapeutic framework for identifying patients with MLL3/4-deficient cancers or developmental disorders who are likely to respond to purine synthesis inhibitors, thereby enabling precision medicine approaches for a broad spectrum of malignancies, including colorectal, breast, bladder, and non-Hodgkin lymphoma, as well as Kabuki syndrome. The invention further encompasses the use of small molecule inhibitors that selectively disrupt the interaction between MLL1 and WDR5, such as WDR5-0103, as a therapeutic modality that recapitulates the cytotoxic effects of purine synthesis inhibition in MLL3/4-deficient cells. The invention also includes methods for monitoring therapeutic response through the measurement of purine metabolite flux, H3K4me3 occupancy at purine synthesis gene promoters, or expression of a defined mitotic gene signature—including PLK1, AURKA, CDCA3, and CDC20—that is downregulated upon purine synthesis inhibition in MLL3/4-deficient cells. The invention thus represents a transformative advance in the treatment of MLL3/4-mutant diseases by identifying a druggable metabolic Achilles’ heel that is directly linked to the underlying epigenetic dysfunction.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention relates to a method for treating a disease characterized by loss-of-function mutations in the MLL3 or MLL4 genes through the selective inhibition of de novo purine nucleotide synthesis. The invention is grounded in the discovery that the absence of functional MLL3 or MLL4 proteins triggers a compensatory epigenetic reprogramming that results in the aberrant upregulation of purine biosynthesis genes, thereby creating a synthetic lethal dependency on this metabolic pathway. This dependency is not observed in cells with intact MLL3/4 function and is not attributable to the catalytic methyltransferase activity of MLL3/4, but rather to the structural and scaffolding roles these proteins play in maintaining the balance of COMPASS complex assembly and chromatin occupancy. In the absence of MLL3 and MLL4, the shared WRAD subcomplex—composed of WDR5, RBBP5, ASH2L, and DPY30—is redistributed from the MLL3/4-containing COMPASS complexes to the MLL1-containing COMPASS complex. This redistribution leads to a significant increase in the chromatin binding of MLL1, particularly at the transcriptional start sites of genes encoding enzymes involved in de novo purine synthesis, such as GART, PAICS, MTHFD1, and PPAT. The increased occupancy of MLL1 at these loci is accompanied by a corresponding elevation in H3K4 trimethylation, a mark associated with active transcription, which in turn drives increased mRNA and protein expression of these purine biosynthesis enzymes. As a result, MLL3/4-deficient cells become reliant on de novo purine synthesis to maintain adequate nucleotide pools necessary for DNA replication, RNA transcription, and cellular proliferation. Inhibition of this pathway—whether through direct targeting of GART with lometrexol, inhibition of dihydrofolate reductase with methotrexate, blockade of serine hydroxymethyltransferase with SHIN1, or disruption of the MLL1-WDR5 interaction with WDR5-0103—induces severe depletion of purine nucleotides, leading to cell cycle arrest, mitotic defects, and apoptosis exclusively in MLL3/4-deficient cells. Cells with intact MLL3/4 function are unaffected by these inhibitors, as their COMPASS complexes remain properly assembled, MLL1 chromatin occupancy is not elevated, and purine synthesis genes are not aberrantly upregulated. This selective vulnerability provides a powerful therapeutic window for the treatment of diseases associated with MLL3/4 loss-of-function mutations.

The invention encompasses the use of any compound that inhibits de novo purine synthesis as a therapeutic agent for MLL3/4-deficient conditions. Such compounds include, but are not limited to, lometrexol, which directly inhibits glycinamide ribonucleotide formyltransferase (GART); methotrexate, which inhibits dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) and thereby depletes tetrahydrofolate pools required for purine ring formation; SHIN1, which inhibits serine hydroxymethyltransferase 1 and 2 (SHMT1/2), preventing the transfer of one-carbon units from serine to tetrahydrofolate; and mycophenolic acid, which inhibits inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase (IMPDH), a key enzyme in the conversion of IMP to GMP. The invention further includes the use of inhibitors that target upstream components of the one-carbon metabolism pathway, such as pemetrexed, which inhibits multiple folate-dependent enzymes, and trimethoprim, which inhibits bacterial dihydrofolate reductase and may be repurposed for human applications in combination with other agents. The invention also encompasses the use of nucleoside analogs that interfere with purine metabolism, such as azathioprine and 6-mercaptopurine, although their mechanism of action may differ from direct de novo synthesis inhibition. The therapeutic efficacy of these compounds is demonstrated in a variety of cellular and animal models, including mouse embryonic stem cells, human colorectal cancer cell lines such as HCT116 and RKO, breast cancer cell lines such as CAL51, and xenograft models derived from these cell lines. In all cases, MLL3/4-deficient cells exhibit significantly greater sensitivity to purine synthesis inhibition than their MLL3/4-wild-type counterparts, as measured by reduced cell viability, decreased colony formation, induction of apoptosis, and suppression of tumor growth in vivo. Importantly, this sensitivity is rescued by supplementation with hypoxanthine or inosine, which can be salvaged to regenerate purine nucleotides, confirming that the cytotoxic effect is due to purine depletion and not off-target toxicity. The invention further includes the use of combinations of purine synthesis inhibitors with other agents, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, given that MLL3/4 loss has been shown to sensitize tumors to immunotherapy, or with inhibitors of mitochondrial metabolism, although the latter have been shown to lack selectivity in this context.

The invention further provides a method for identifying patients who are candidates for treatment with purine synthesis inhibitors based on the presence of loss-of-function mutations in KMT2C or KMT2D. Such mutations include truncating mutations, frameshift mutations, nonsense mutations, and splice-site mutations that result in the absence of full-length MLL3 or MLL4 protein, as well as missense mutations in critical domains such as the PHD fingers or the SET domain that impair protein stability or complex assembly. The invention encompasses the use of next-generation sequencing panels, whole-exome sequencing, or targeted sequencing assays to detect these mutations in tumor biopsies, circulating tumor DNA, or other clinical samples. The invention further includes the use of immunohistochemistry to assess MLL3 or MLL4 protein expression in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections, with loss of staining serving as a surrogate marker for functional inactivation. The invention further encompasses the use of epigenetic biomarkers, such as increased H3K4me3 occupancy at the promoters of purine synthesis genes, as determined by chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing or quantitative PCR, to confirm the presence of the compensatory epigenetic reprogramming that underlies the synthetic lethal vulnerability. The invention further includes the use of transcriptomic signatures, such as the upregulation of a defined set of purine metabolism genes—including GART, PAICS, MTHFD1, PPAT, and AK3—as a predictive biomarker for therapeutic response. The invention further encompasses the use of proteomic profiling to detect elevated levels of purine synthesis enzymes in tumor lysates, as well as metabolomic profiling to measure increased flux through de novo purine synthesis using stable isotope tracing with 15N-glutamine or 13C-hypoxanthine. The invention further provides a method for monitoring therapeutic response by measuring changes in the expression of a mitotic gene signature—including PLK1, AURKA, CDCA3, CDC20, SFPQ, POLA1, PSRC1, KIF20A, FAM83D, and DLGAP5—that is selectively downregulated in MLL3/4-deficient cells following purine synthesis inhibition. The invention further includes the use of circulating tumor DNA to track the emergence of resistance mutations, such as mutations in GART or MTHFD1 that confer resistance to inhibitor binding, as a means of guiding adaptive therapy.

The invention further provides a method for treating MLL3/4-deficient diseases using inhibitors that specifically disrupt the interaction between MLL1 and WDR5, such as WDR5-0103, which binds to the WDR5 pocket and prevents its association with MLL1. This approach recapitulates the cytotoxic effects of purine synthesis inhibition in MLL3/4-deficient cells by preventing the compensatory recruitment of the WRAD complex to MLL1, thereby reversing the aberrant upregulation of purine metabolism genes and restoring nucleotide homeostasis. The invention further encompasses the use of other small molecules that target the MLL1-WDR5 interface, including OICR-9429, MI-503, and MI-463, although these compounds may have broader effects on other MLL family members. The invention further includes the use of CRISPR-based synthetic lethal screens to identify additional genetic vulnerabilities that may be co-targeted with purine synthesis inhibition, such as mutations in TP53, RB1, or BRCA1/2, which may enhance the therapeutic effect. The invention further encompasses the use of nanoparticle-based delivery systems to deliver purine synthesis inhibitors or MLL1-WDR5 disruptors specifically to tumor tissues, thereby minimizing systemic toxicity and improving therapeutic index.

## EXAMPLES

The invention is further illustrated by the following detailed examples, which demonstrate the synthetic lethal interaction between MLL3/4 deficiency and purine synthesis inhibition in multiple experimental systems, including mouse embryonic stem cells, human cancer cell lines, and in vivo xenograft models. These examples provide conclusive evidence that the therapeutic strategy described herein is robust, reproducible, and translatable to human disease.

Example 1: Generation and Validation of MLL3/4-Deficient Mouse Embryonic Stem Cells

To establish a model system for studying the consequences of MLL3/4 loss, mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) were genetically engineered to harbor biallelic deletions of exons 8 and 9 of KMT2C (MLL3) and exons 16–22 of KMT2D (MLL4) using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing. Two guide RNAs targeting each locus were electroporated into V6.5 mESCs, and clones were selected with puromycin and expanded for further analysis. Western blot analysis confirmed the complete absence of full-length MLL3 and MLL4 proteins in the double-knockout (DKO) line, as detected by antibodies against both N-terminal and C-terminal epitopes. The integrity of the COMPASS complex was assessed by probing for associated subunits, including RBBP5, NCOA6, UTX, and PTIP, all of which were markedly reduced in the DKO cells, consistent with the destabilization of the MLL3/4-containing COMPASS complex. In contrast, the levels of MLL1, MLL2, and SET1A remained unchanged, indicating that the loss of MLL3/4 did not globally affect the expression of other COMPASS members. ChIP-seq analysis of H3K4me1 and H3K27ac revealed a dramatic reduction in these enhancer-associated marks at a subset of MLL4-bound enhancers, particularly those associated with genes involved in cytoskeletal organization and cell junction formation, while pluripotency markers such as Pou5f1 and Nanog remained unchanged, confirming that the DKO cells retained their undifferentiated state. Cell proliferation rates were comparable between wild-type and DKO cells under standard culture conditions, indicating that MLL3/4 loss does not impair baseline growth in mESCs. These findings established a genetically defined, isogenic model system in which the functional consequences of MLL3/4 deficiency could be studied without confounding genetic or epigenetic variability.

Example 2: Genome-Wide CRISPR Screening Identifies Purine Synthesis Genes as Synthetic Lethal Dependencies

To identify cellular vulnerabilities arising from MLL3/4 loss, a genome-wide CRISPR knockout screen was performed using the Brie pooled sgRNA library in wild-type and MLL3/4-DKO mESCs. Cells were infected at low MOI to ensure single sgRNA integration, and selection was maintained for 21 days to allow for the outgrowth of cells resistant to gene knockout. Genomic DNA was harvested at day 3 (baseline) and day 21 (final), and sgRNA abundance was quantified by next-generation sequencing. Analysis using the MAGeCK RRA algorithm revealed that 12 genes involved in de novo purine and pyrimidine synthesis were significantly depleted in the DKO cells relative to wild-type controls, with the strongest negative selection observed for enzymes such as Ppat, Mthfd1, Gart, Paics, and Umps. The β scores calculated using the MLE algorithm confirmed that these genes were essential in DKO cells but dispensable in wild-type cells, with no significant effect on viability in the presence of intact MLL3/4. Validation by individual sgRNA knockdown in a mixed fluorescent reporter system (GFP-wild-type, mCherry-DKO) demonstrated that depletion of these genes resulted in preferential loss of the DKO population over time, confirming the synthetic lethal interaction. These findings established that MLL3/4-deficient cells acquire a unique dependency on de novo nucleotide synthesis, a vulnerability not shared by their wild-type counterparts.

Example 3: Metabolomic Profiling Reveals Increased Flux Through De Novo Purine Synthesis

To determine whether the genetic dependency observed in the CRISPR screen translated into altered metabolic flux, steady-state metabolite profiling was performed using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) in wild-type and DKO mESCs. Significant alterations were detected in metabolites related to methionine metabolism, urea cycle intermediates, and nucleotide biosynthesis, including elevated levels of UMP, CMP, AICAR, inosine, and hypoxanthine in DKO cells. To distinguish between increased synthesis and decreased catabolism, isotope tracing experiments were conducted using 15N-labeled glutamine, a key nitrogen donor for purine ring formation. The incorporation of 15N into purine intermediates such as AICAR and IMP was significantly higher in DKO cells compared to wild-type, indicating increased flux through de novo purine synthesis. In contrast, 13C-labeled hypoxanthine tracing revealed only a modest increase in salvage pathway activity, confirming that the primary driver of purine nucleotide accumulation was de novo synthesis. These findings demonstrated that MLL3/4 loss reprograms cellular metabolism to increase the demand for purine nucleotides through enhanced de novo biosynthesis, providing a mechanistic basis for the synthetic lethal interaction observed in the CRISPR screen.

Example 4: Pharmacological Inhibition of Purine Synthesis Selectively Kills MLL3/4-Deficient Cells

To test whether the increased purine synthesis dependency could be therapeutically exploited, wild-type and DKO mESCs were treated with lometrexol (LTX), a potent inhibitor of glycinamide ribonucleotide formyltransferase (GART), a key enzyme in the de novo purine pathway. LTX treatment resulted in a dose-dependent reduction in cell viability in DKO cells, with an IC50 approximately tenfold lower than in wild-type cells. The cytotoxic effect of LTX was completely rescued by supplementation with hypoxanthine, which can be salvaged to regenerate purine nucleotides, confirming that the effect was due to purine depletion. Similar results were obtained with methotrexate and SHIN1, inhibitors of folate metabolism and serine hydroxymethyltransferase, respectively, both of which restrict the availability of one-carbon units required for purine synthesis. In contrast, inhibition of pyrimidine synthesis with brequinar had no differential effect, confirming the specificity for purine metabolism. These findings demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition of de novo purine synthesis selectively kills MLL3/4-deficient cells, establishing a viable therapeutic strategy.

Example 5: Catalytic Activity of MLL3/4 Is Dispensable for Resistance to Purine Synthesis Inhibition

To determine whether the catalytic methyltransferase activity of MLL3/4 was responsible for conferring resistance to purine synthesis inhibition, a catalytically inactive mutant of MLL3/4 was generated by deleting the SET domain. Western blot analysis confirmed that the SET domain deletion did not affect the stability of MLL3/4 or its associated subunits, and H3K4me1 levels were reduced to the same extent as in the complete knockout. However, unlike the DKO cells, the SET domain-deleted cells showed no increased sensitivity to LTX, and their viability was indistinguishable from wild-type cells under purine synthesis inhibition. This finding demonstrated that the synthetic lethal interaction is not due to the loss of H3K4 methylation activity but rather to the structural loss of the MLL3/4 protein, which leads to the redistribution of COMPASS subunits and compensatory MLL1 activation. This distinction is critical for therapeutic development, as it indicates that small molecules targeting the catalytic site of MLL3/4 would not recapitulate the desired therapeutic effect.

Example 6: Tandem Mass Tag Proteomics Confirms Upregulation of Purine Metabolism Proteins

To complement transcriptomic analyses, tandem mass tag (TMT) proteomics was performed on wild-type and DKO mESCs. A total of 7,096 proteins were quantified, and 343 were significantly upregulated in DKO cells. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed a striking enrichment of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I components and, more specifically, enzymes involved in purine nucleotide metabolism. Fifty-nine purine metabolism proteins were differentially abundant, including GART, PAICS, MTHFD1, and PPAT, all of which were elevated at the protein level despite no corresponding increase in mRNA expression for some, suggesting post-transcriptional regulation. The upregulation of these proteins was validated by Western blot and immunofluorescence, confirming that MLL3/4 loss leads to a global increase in the abundance of purine biosynthesis enzymes, thereby creating a metabolic bottleneck that can be exploited therapeutically.

Example 7: Epigenetic Compensation by MLL1 Drives Purine Gene Upregulation

To identify the mechanism underlying purine gene upregulation, ChIP-seq was performed for H3K4me3, MLL1, MLL2, and Menin in wild-type and DKO cells. A significant increase in MLL1 binding was observed at the promoters of upregulated purine synthesis genes in DKO cells, accompanied by elevated H3K4me3. In contrast, MLL2 and SET1A binding was unchanged or decreased. Genome-wide analysis revealed that over 80% of the purine metabolism genes upregulated in DKO cells exhibited increased MLL1 occupancy, and approximately 83% of these MLL1 peaks were de novo, meaning they were absent in wild-type cells. Treatment with WDR5-0103, a small molecule that disrupts the MLL1-WDR5 interaction, reversed the upregulation of purine genes and reduced viability of DKO cells, confirming that MLL1 compensation is necessary and sufficient for the synthetic lethal phenotype. These findings established that the redistribution of the WRAD complex to MLL1 is the central mechanism driving the metabolic dependency.

Example 8: MLL4-Mutant Colorectal Cancer Cells Are Selectively Sensitive to LTX

To extend the findings to human cancer, a panel of colorectal cancer cell lines was analyzed for MLL4 mutation status. Cell lines with truncating mutations in KMT2D (RKO, HCT116, DLD1, SW480) were significantly more sensitive to LTX than wild-type lines (Caco2, HT55, SW1417). Isotope tracing confirmed increased de novo purine flux in mutant lines, and shRNA-mediated knockdown of GART or PAICS selectively inhibited growth and colony formation in mutant but not wild-type cells. These findings demonstrated that the synthetic lethal interaction is conserved in human cancers and that MLL4 mutation status can serve as a predictive biomarker for response to purine synthesis inhibitors.

Example 9: In Vivo Efficacy of LTX in MLL4-Mutant Xenografts

To evaluate therapeutic efficacy in vivo, HCT116 (MLL4-mutant) and HT55 (MLL4-wild-type) cells were implanted subcutaneously into athymic mice. Once tumors reached 100 mm³, mice were treated with LTX (25 mg/kg) or vehicle control. LTX treatment resulted in a profound suppression of tumor growth in HCT116 xenografts, with a 75% reduction in tumor volume compared to controls, while HT55 tumors were completely resistant. Tumor tissue analysis revealed decreased proliferation (Ki67), increased apoptosis (cleaved caspase-3), and downregulation of mitotic genes such as PLK1 and AURKA, consistent with the in vitro findings. Survival was significantly extended in the LTX-treated HCT116 group. At a lower dose (15 mg/kg), HCT116 tumors remained sensitive, while HT55 tumors showed no response, confirming the specificity of the therapeutic effect. These findings established that purine synthesis inhibition is a viable therapeutic strategy for MLL4-mutant cancers in vivo.

Example 10: Development of a Diagnostic Biomarker Panel for Patient Stratification

Based on the findings above, a diagnostic biomarker panel was developed for clinical use. The panel includes: (1) sequencing of KMT2C and KMT2D to detect loss-of-function mutations; (2) immunohistochemistry for MLL3 and MLL4 protein expression in tumor tissue; (3) measurement of H3K4me3 enrichment at the promoters of GART, PAICS, and MTHFD1 by ChIP-qPCR; (4) quantification of purine metabolite flux using 15N-glutamine tracing in tumor biopsies; and (5) assessment of the mitotic gene signature (PLK1, AURKA, CDCA3, CDC20) by RNA expression profiling. A patient is deemed eligible for treatment if two or more of these criteria are met. This panel enables precise patient selection, ensuring that only those with the underlying biological vulnerability receive the therapy, thereby maximizing clinical benefit and minimizing unnecessary exposure to toxic agents.
