# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to computational systems and methods for the allelic decomposition of highly polymorphic genes that exhibit structural variations, including partial or complete gene duplications, deletions, and hybridizations with homologous pseudogenes, using high-throughput sequencing (HTS) data. Specifically, the invention provides a novel framework for accurately determining the complete sequence content and copy number of each allele of a target gene in a biological sample, even when the gene resides in a genomic region characterized by extensive sequence homology, repetitive elements, and structural rearrangements that render conventional genotyping approaches inadequate. The invention is particularly applicable to pharmacogenomic genes such as CYP2D6, CYP2A6, CYP2C19, and DPYD, where allelic variation directly influences drug metabolism, therapeutic efficacy, and adverse drug reactions. The system is designed to operate on standard HTS data formats, including BAM and SAM files generated from whole-genome sequencing or targeted capture sequencing platforms such as PGRNseq, without requiring prior variant calling or specialized data preprocessing. The invention enables precise, comprehensive, and clinically actionable genotyping by integrating structural variant detection, copy number estimation, mutation phasing, and star-allele assignment into a unified combinatorial optimization framework that minimizes the number of novel mutations required to explain observed sequencing patterns, thereby ensuring biological plausibility and diagnostic accuracy.

## BACKGROUND

The accurate determination of genetic variation in functionally significant genes has become a cornerstone of precision medicine, particularly in the field of pharmacogenomics, where an individual’s genetic makeup dictates their response to commonly prescribed medications. Many of these genes, including CYP2D6, CYP2A6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and DPYD, are located in genomic regions that are rich in segmental duplications and highly homologous pseudogenes, which frequently participate in non-allelic homologous recombination events. These events lead to the formation of chimeric gene structures, partial deletions, and duplications that alter gene dosage and protein function in ways that are not captured by traditional single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) arrays or short-read sequencing pipelines. Existing genotyping tools, such as SNP microarrays and PCR-based assays, are limited to predefined variant sets and are incapable of detecting novel or rare alleles that deviate from known reference patterns. Even next-generation sequencing-based tools, which theoretically offer comprehensive variant detection, fail to resolve the complex allelic architecture of these genes due to their reliance on reference-based alignment and variant calling methods that assume uniform coverage, unique mappability, and the absence of structural rearrangements.

Current computational approaches for genotyping rely on the assumption that reads can be uniquely mapped to a single genomic locus, an assumption that breaks down in regions of high sequence similarity. Tools such as Cypiripi and Astrolabe, which are specifically designed for CYP2D6 genotyping, operate under the constraint that the gene is present in a single-copy, non-rearranged configuration and can only detect variants that are explicitly encoded in their reference allele database. These tools are unable to interpret reads that originate from hybrid gene structures formed between CYP2D6 and its pseudogene CYP2D7, nor can they distinguish between partial and complete gene duplications or deletions. Furthermore, they require variant calls in VCF format as input, which themselves are generated using pipelines that discard reads mapping to repetitive or ambiguous regions, thereby losing critical information necessary for reconstructing the true allelic composition. As a result, these tools misclassify up to 50% of samples with structural variants, often reporting false homozygous or heterozygous calls that do not reflect the underlying biological reality.

The problem is further exacerbated by the widespread use of targeted capture sequencing panels, such as PGRNseq, which are designed to enrich for exonic and flanking regions of pharmacogenes but produce highly non-uniform coverage profiles due to differences in probe efficiency, GC content, and hybridization bias. Conventional copy number estimation methods, which rely on normalized read depth across uniformly mappable regions, are rendered ineffective when applied to such data because they cannot distinguish between true copy number alterations and technical artifacts arising from uneven capture efficiency. Additionally, existing structural variant detection algorithms, including Delly, Pindel, and BreakDancer, are optimized for identifying large-scale genomic rearrangements in uniquely mappable regions of the genome and lack the sensitivity or specificity to detect breakpoints within highly homologous gene-pseudogene clusters. These tools also do not reconstruct the sequence content of fusion alleles or determine which exons or introns originate from which parental sequence, rendering them incapable of assigning star-allele nomenclature to hybrid structures.

Moreover, the star-allele nomenclature system used in pharmacogenomics, which classifies alleles based on combinations of gene-disrupting and neutral mutations, requires not only the identification of individual variants but also their precise phasing and assignment to specific gene copies. Current pipelines treat variants as independent entities and lack the capability to resolve which mutations co-occur on the same haplotype, especially when multiple gene copies are present. This leads to ambiguous genotype calls, particularly in cases where a single gene-disrupting mutation is shared across multiple star-alleles, and distinguishing between them requires the presence of additional neutral variants that are often absent or undetected. The inability to accurately phase mutations across gene copies results in the misassignment of functional phenotypes, which can have serious clinical consequences, such as incorrect dosing of drugs metabolized by CYP2D6, including codeine, tamoxifen, and antidepressants.

The absence of a unified computational framework capable of simultaneously resolving copy number, structural variation, mutation phasing, and star-allele assignment has created a critical gap in clinical genomics. While whole-genome sequencing offers the theoretical potential for comprehensive variant detection, its practical utility for pharmacogenomic applications has been severely limited by the lack of algorithms that can interpret the resulting data in the context of complex, structurally altered genes. The invention described herein addresses this gap by introducing a novel, end-to-end computational framework that integrates read mapping, coverage normalization, structural variant inference, and combinatorial optimization to reconstruct the complete allelic composition of any gene of interest, regardless of its structural complexity or the uniformity of sequencing coverage. This framework enables the accurate detection of novel star-alleles, the identification of previously unrecognized hybrid gene structures, and the precise assignment of minor star-allele extensions based on neutral mutations, thereby transforming HTS data from a source of ambiguous signals into a reliable foundation for clinical decision-making.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a novel computational system and method for the allelic decomposition of genetically complex genes using high-throughput sequencing data, wherein the system performs a comprehensive reconstruction of the sequence content, copy number, and structural configuration of each allele present in a biological sample. The system operates on standard BAM or SAM files generated from whole-genome or targeted capture sequencing and does not require preprocessed variant calls or external genotyping data. The method comprises a sequence of interdependent computational steps that collectively resolve the ambiguity inherent in reads originating from highly homologous gene-pseudogene regions, partial gene duplications or deletions, and hybrid gene structures. The first step involves the alignment of sequencing reads to a reference genome and the identification of all single-nucleotide variants and short insertions or deletions within the target gene region. The second step employs a coverage normalization procedure that accounts for non-uniform sequencing depth and biases introduced by targeted capture protocols, enabling the accurate estimation of the aggregate copy number of each exon and intron in the gene and its associated pseudogenes. The third step formulates the problem of structural variant detection as an integer linear programming optimization task, wherein the system identifies the minimal set of known structural configurations—such as full or partial gene duplications, deletions, and gene fusions—that collectively explain the observed copy number profile across all genomic regions. The fourth step determines the major star-alleles present in the sample by matching the observed gene-disrupting mutations to known allele definitions, while enforcing constraints that ensure the total copy number of each mutation is consistent with the inferred structural configuration. The fifth and final step refines the genotype by assigning neutral mutations to specific major star-alleles to form minor star-alleles, using a quadratic integer programming model that minimizes the number of novel mutations required to explain the observed data, thereby preserving biological plausibility and enabling the discovery of previously unreported alleles. The system outputs a complete genotype for each gene copy, including the star-allele designation, copy number, and structural configuration, and reports all equally likely configurations when ambiguity cannot be resolved. The invention is capable of processing high-coverage data from targeted panels such as PGRNseq in under ten seconds on a standard laptop, with memory usage below 100 megabytes, making it suitable for real-time clinical deployment. The system has been validated across multiple independent cohorts, demonstrating superior accuracy compared to existing tools, identifying novel star-alleles, and resolving genotypes that were previously uninterpretable by conventional methods. The invention enables the first fully automated, comprehensive, and clinically actionable genotyping of structurally complex pharmacogenes directly from raw sequencing data, eliminating the need for confirmatory assays and significantly improving the precision of pharmacogenomic-guided therapy.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### CYP2D6

The gene CYP2D6 serves as the primary exemplar for the invention due to its central role in the metabolism of approximately 25% of clinically prescribed drugs, including antidepressants, antipsychotics, beta-blockers, and opioids such as codeine and tramadol. Its allelic diversity is among the most complex in the human genome, with over 100 documented star-alleles classified under the star-allele nomenclature system, each defined by a unique combination of gene-disrupting and neutral mutations. The gene resides in a genomic region on chromosome 22q13.1 that is flanked by two highly homologous pseudogenes, CYP2D7 and CYP2D8, which share over 95% sequence identity with CYP2D6 over large stretches of their exonic and intronic regions. This extreme sequence similarity facilitates non-allelic homologous recombination events that result in gene fusions, partial deletions, and duplications, leading to the formation of hybrid alleles that are indistinguishable from canonical CYP2D6 sequences using conventional alignment and variant calling methods. For instance, the CYP2D6*35 allele, which contains a fusion between the 5’ end of CYP2D6 and the 3’ end of CYP2D7, is often misclassified as CYP2D6*2 by standard genotyping panels due to the absence of distinguishing SNPs in the assay design. Similarly, the CYP2D6*4 allele, which is defined by a splicing-disrupting SNP, is frequently confused with CYP2D6*5, a complete gene deletion, when coverage-based methods are used without structural context. The presence of multiple gene copies, including triplications and quadruplications, further complicates genotype interpretation, as the total number of functional copies determines whether an individual is classified as a poor, intermediate, extensive, or ultrarapid metabolizer. The invention overcomes these challenges by treating CYP2D6 not as a single locus but as a dynamic genomic region composed of multiple, potentially rearranged gene copies, each of which may originate from CYP2D6, CYP2D7, or a hybrid of the two. The system leverages the known structural architecture of this region, including the precise boundaries of exons and introns, the locations of known fusion breakpoints, and the sequence differences between CYP2D6 and its pseudogenes, to construct a comprehensive model of all possible allelic configurations. By integrating read mapping, coverage normalization, and combinatorial optimization, the system is able to distinguish between a true CYP2D6 duplication and a CYP2D6/CYP2D7 fusion, to identify which exons in a hybrid allele are derived from which parental sequence, and to assign the correct star-allele designation based on the full complement of mutations present on each copy. This level of resolution is not achievable by any existing tool and represents a fundamental advancement in the ability to interpret pharmacogenomic data with clinical precision.

### HTS Data

The invention is designed to operate on high-throughput sequencing (HTS) data generated by any platform capable of producing paired-end or single-end reads in standard SAM or BAM format, including Illumina HiSeq, NovaSeq, and NextSeq instruments. The data may be derived from whole-genome sequencing or targeted capture sequencing, such as the PGRNseq panel, which selectively enriches for exonic and flanking regions of 84 pharmacogenes, including CYP2D6, CYP2A6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, DPYD, and TPMT. The system does not require prior variant calling, filtering, or normalization of the input data; instead, it accepts raw alignment files as generated by standard read mappers such as BWA-MEM or Bowtie2, even when those alignments contain reads that map to multiple genomic locations due to sequence homology. The system is specifically optimized for high-coverage data, with demonstrated performance on samples sequenced to an average depth exceeding 500×, as is typical for targeted capture panels. The ability to process such high-depth data without degradation in performance or increase in computational burden is a key innovation, as most existing tools either fail to scale with coverage or require significant computational resources that preclude clinical deployment. The system is also capable of handling data with variable insert sizes, ranging from 200 to 500 base pairs, and tolerates a wide range of error rates inherent to Illumina sequencing technology. Importantly, the system does not rely on the assumption that all reads are uniquely mappable; instead, it explicitly models the ambiguity inherent in reads that originate from homologous regions and uses this ambiguity as a source of information for structural variant inference. For example, reads that span the boundary between a gene and its pseudogene, or that map equally well to multiple exons across different copies, are not discarded but are instead incorporated into the combinatorial optimization model to constrain the possible configurations of gene copies. This approach transforms what is typically considered noise in conventional pipelines into a powerful signal for detecting complex structural rearrangements. The system is also agnostic to the specific sequencing protocol used, whether it be PCR-based enrichment, hybridization capture, or amplicon sequencing, as long as the resulting data conform to the standard BAM/SAM format and contain sufficient coverage to detect variants with statistical confidence.

### Alignment/Read Mapping

The system accepts any valid SAM or BAM file as input, regardless of the read mapper or alignment parameters used to generate it. The alignment process is not modified or re-performed by the system; instead, it leverages the existing alignments to extract information about read coverage, mapping quality, and read-pair orientation. The system identifies all reads that overlap the target gene region and its associated pseudogenes, as defined by the gene database, and classifies them according to their mapping location and orientation. Reads that map to multiple locations with equal quality are flagged as ambiguous and are retained for downstream analysis rather than being discarded, as is common in conventional pipelines. The system then constructs a coverage profile across the entire gene region by aggregating the number of reads overlapping each base pair, while accounting for the strand-specificity of the sequencing protocol. This coverage profile is used as the primary input for the subsequent copy number estimation step. The system does not perform local realignment or indel correction, as these steps are not required for its operation; however, it is compatible with data that has been processed through standard best-practice workflows, such as those recommended by the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK), including base quality score recalibration and local indel realignment. The system’s robustness to variations in alignment quality stems from its reliance on aggregate coverage patterns and combinatorial modeling rather than individual variant calls. By preserving ambiguous mappings and incorporating them into the optimization framework, the system is able to infer structural configurations that would otherwise be invisible to tools that filter out multi-mapping reads. This is particularly critical in regions such as the intronic segment between exon 6 and exon 9 of CYP2D6 and CYP2D7, where the sequence identity exceeds 99% over a span longer than the typical read length, rendering any attempt at unambiguous assignment impossible. Rather than masking these regions entirely, the system uses their high ambiguity to constrain the possible combinations of gene copies, ensuring that any inferred configuration must be consistent with the observed distribution of ambiguous reads. This innovative use of alignment ambiguity as a source of structural information represents a paradigm shift from traditional genotyping approaches and is a key enabler of the system’s ability to resolve complex allelic structures.

### Sequence Variant Calling

Unlike conventional genotyping pipelines, the invention does not perform independent variant calling using tools such as GATK HaplotypeCaller or FreeBayes. Instead, it identifies all single-nucleotide variants and short insertions or deletions directly from the alignment data by examining the base calls and read support at each position within the target gene region. The system considers a variant to be present at a given position if a sufficient number of reads support an alternative allele, taking into account the base quality scores, mapping quality, and strand bias. The threshold for variant detection is determined empirically based on the sequencing depth and error profile of the input data, ensuring sensitivity even in low-coverage regions while maintaining specificity in high-coverage areas. The system does not apply population-based filters or remove variants that are common in public databases such as dbSNP; instead, it retains all detected variants, regardless of their frequency, and defers their functional interpretation to later stages of the analysis. This approach ensures that rare, novel, or population-specific variants are not prematurely excluded from consideration. The system also distinguishes between variants that occur within coding regions and those that occur in intronic or regulatory regions, as the former are more likely to impact protein function and are therefore classified as gene-disrupting mutations, while the latter are classified as neutral mutations. The classification of mutations as gene-disrupting or neutral is determined by reference to a curated database that contains the known functional consequences of each variant for the target gene, including its effect on splicing, codon usage, and protein stability. This database is pre-populated with information from authoritative sources such as the Human Cytochrome P450 Allele Nomenclature Database, PharmGKB, and dbSNP, and can be updated as new functional annotations become available. The system does not attempt to predict the functional impact of novel variants; instead, it records their presence and defers their classification to the genotype refinement step, where they may be assigned to a novel minor star-allele if they consistently co-occur with a known major star-allele. This separation of variant detection from functional interpretation allows the system to remain agnostic to the evolving understanding of variant pathogenicity and ensures that its predictions are grounded in the most current biological knowledge.

### Detecting Structural Variants

The detection of structural variants is the cornerstone of the invention and represents a fundamental departure from existing approaches. Rather than identifying structural variants as discrete events such as deletions, duplications, or translocations, the system models them as configurations of gene copies, each of which may be composed of segments derived from the target gene, its pseudogenes, or a combination of both. The system begins by segmenting the target gene region into contiguous units corresponding to exons and introns, as defined by the gene database. For CYP2D6, this results in 19 exons and 18 introns, each of which is treated as an independent unit that can be inherited from CYP2D6, CYP2D7, or CYP2D8. The system then estimates the aggregate copy number of each unit by normalizing the observed read coverage against a reference sample, as described in the coverage normalization section. This results in a vector of copy numbers, one for each exon and intron, that reflects the total number of copies of each genomic segment present in the sample. The system then formulates the problem of structural variant detection as an integer linear programming problem, in which the goal is to find the minimal combination of known structural configurations—each represented as a binary vector indicating the origin of each exon and intron—that can explain the observed copy number profile. For example, a configuration in which the first 10 exons are derived from CYP2D6 and the remaining 9 from CYP2D7 represents a known fusion allele, while a configuration in which all exons are duplicated represents a full gene duplication. The system considers only those configurations that are documented in the gene database, which includes all known hybrid alleles, partial deletions, and partial duplications reported in the literature. The optimization problem is solved using state-of-the-art integer programming solvers such as Gurobi or SCIP, which efficiently identify the set of configurations that minimizes the difference between the observed and predicted copy number profiles. The system then selects the most parsimonious solution, defined as the one that requires the fewest number of distinct gene copies to explain the data. This approach ensures that the inferred genotypes are biologically plausible and avoid overfitting to noise or technical artifacts. The system is capable of detecting complex combinations of structural variants, such as a sample containing both a full gene duplication and a partial deletion, and can resolve these configurations even when they involve multiple pseudogenes or overlapping rearrangements. The ability to detect and quantify these complex structural variants directly from sequencing data, without requiring specialized assays or long-read technologies, represents a major advancement in the field of pharmacogenomics.

### Coverage Normalization

The accurate estimation of copy number from high-throughput sequencing data is complicated by the non-uniform coverage that arises from targeted capture protocols, differences in GC content, and biases in probe hybridization efficiency. Conventional methods for copy number estimation, which rely on the assumption of uniform coverage across the genome, are therefore inadequate for this application. The invention introduces a novel coverage normalization procedure that corrects for these biases by comparing the observed coverage profile of the target gene region in the sample to a reference coverage profile derived from a set of control samples sequenced using the same protocol. The reference profile is constructed by aggregating coverage data from multiple samples that are known to have a standard diploid copy number and no structural variants, and is stored as a baseline for each sequencing platform and capture panel. The system computes the normalized coverage for each base pair in the target region by dividing the observed coverage by the expected coverage from the reference profile, resulting in a continuous function that reflects the relative copy number at each position. This normalized coverage function is then averaged over each exon and intron to produce a discrete copy number estimate for each genomic unit. The system further refines this estimate by excluding regions with high sequence ambiguity, such as the identical segments between CYP2D6 and CYP2D7, which could distort the coverage profile due to misaligned reads. The normalization procedure is robust to variations in sequencing depth and is capable of accurately estimating copy numbers even in regions with low coverage, as long as the total number of reads mapping to the region is sufficient to establish a statistically significant signal. The system also accounts for the fact that some regions may be partially or completely deleted, in which case the normalized coverage will be close to zero, or duplicated, in which case it will be elevated. The resulting normalized copy number vector serves as the primary input for the structural variant detection step, and its accuracy is critical to the success of the entire analysis. By decoupling copy number estimation from variant calling and using a data-driven, sample-specific normalization approach, the system overcomes the limitations of reference-based methods and achieves high precision in the detection of both partial and complete gene copy number alterations.

### Major Star-Allele Identification

Once the structural configuration of the gene copies has been determined, the system proceeds to identify the major star-alleles present in each copy. A major star-allele is defined as a gene sequence that differs from the wild-type reference by one or more gene-disrupting mutations, which are mutations that alter the amino acid sequence, disrupt splicing, or otherwise affect protein function. The system begins by compiling a list of all gene-disrupting mutations detected in the sample and then filters the set of known star-alleles to retain only those whose defining mutations are fully contained within the detected set. For example, if a sample contains the gene-disrupting mutations associated with CYP2D6*2 and CYP2D6*10, but not those associated with CYP2D6*4, then only *2 and *10 are considered as candidate alleles. The system then formulates the problem of major star-allele identification as an integer linear programming task, in which the goal is to assign a copy number to each candidate allele such that the total number of copies of each detected mutation matches the estimated copy number derived from the coverage normalization step. This is achieved by introducing a binary variable for each candidate allele, representing the number of copies of that allele present in the sample, and constraining the sum of the variables for each mutation to equal its estimated copy number. The system minimizes the total deviation between the observed and predicted mutation copy numbers, ensuring that the final genotype is the most consistent with the data. The system also incorporates a phasing constraint that requires any mutation that is spanned by a single read to be present on the same allele, thereby eliminating configurations in which mutations that are physically linked on the same DNA molecule are assigned to different gene copies. This constraint is particularly important in resolving ambiguous cases where multiple star-alleles share a common mutation, as it allows the system to distinguish between, for example, a sample containing two copies of *2 and one copy of *10 versus a sample containing one copy of *2 and one copy of *10 with an additional copy of *2. The system is capable of identifying novel major star-alleles when no combination of known alleles can explain the observed mutation profile, in which case it reports the presence of an unclassified allele and defers its characterization to the genotype refinement step. The major star-allele identification step is thus a critical bridge between the structural configuration of the gene and its functional interpretation, ensuring that the final genotype accurately reflects both the physical arrangement and the mutational content of each gene copy.

### Genotype Refining

The final step in the genotyping process is genotype refinement, which assigns neutral mutations to the major star-alleles identified in the previous step to form minor star-alleles. A minor star-allele is defined as a major star-allele extended by one or more neutral mutations, which are variants that do not alter the protein product but may serve as markers for distinguishing between closely related alleles. The system begins by compiling a list of all neutral mutations detected in the sample and then considers each known minor star-allele as a potential extension of each major star-allele. For each possible combination of a major star-allele and a minor star-allele, the system calculates a score based on the agreement between the observed copy number of each neutral mutation and the expected copy number if that minor star-allele were present. The score penalizes the inclusion of novel mutations that are not present in the database, as well as the omission of known mutations that are expected to be present. The system uses a quadratic integer programming model to simultaneously optimize the assignment of minor star-alleles to major star-alleles and the selection of novel mutations to form new minor star-alleles, with the goal of minimizing the total penalty score. The system assigns a high penalty to the inclusion of gene-disrupting mutations in minor star-alleles, ensuring that such mutations are only used to define new major star-alleles if no combination of existing alleles can explain their presence. The system also enforces constraints that ensure each gene copy is assigned exactly one minor star-allele and that the total copy number of each mutation is consistent with the inferred genotype. The result is a refined genotype that includes not only the major star-allele designation for each gene copy but also the specific minor star-allele extension, such as *2A, *4B, or *10X. In cases where multiple combinations of minor star-alleles yield the same minimal penalty score, the system reports all equally likely genotypes, acknowledging the inherent ambiguity in the data. The genotype refinement step is the key innovation that enables the discovery of novel minor star-alleles and the resolution of previously ambiguous genotype calls, such as those involving the CYP2D6*4 family, where the presence of the c.843 T>G mutation was previously unassociated with *4A but is now recognized as a defining feature of a new sub-allele. By integrating neutral mutations into the genotyping process in a principled, data-driven manner, the system transforms the star-allele nomenclature from a static catalog of known variants into a dynamic, evolving framework capable of adapting to new biological discoveries.

## Complexity

The computational complexity of the invention arises from the combinatorial nature of the problems it solves, particularly the copy number estimation problem and the major star-allele identification problem, both of which are formulated as integer linear programming tasks. While specific instances of these problems may be solvable in polynomial time under certain constraints, such as when the gene has no structural variants or when the configuration vectors form a unimodular matrix, the general case is NP-hard. This is demonstrated through reductions from the Closest Vector Problem, a well-established NP-hard problem in lattice theory, which shows that even approximating the optimal solution within a constant factor is computationally intractable in the worst case. The system does not attempt to circumvent this theoretical limitation; instead, it leverages state-of-the-art integer programming solvers such as Gurobi and SCIP, which are designed to handle hard combinatorial problems efficiently in practice. These solvers employ a combination of branch-and-bound algorithms, cutting plane methods, and heuristics to rapidly converge on optimal or near-optimal solutions, even for large and complex instances. The system’s practical efficiency is further enhanced by the fact that the number of possible structural configurations is constrained by the gene database, which contains only a limited set of known variants, and by the fact that the number of candidate major star-alleles is typically small, often fewer than 20 for most pharmacogenes. As a result, the system is able to process high-coverage BAM files from targeted capture panels in under ten seconds on a standard laptop computer, with memory usage below 100 megabytes, making it suitable for real-time clinical deployment. The system’s performance is not significantly affected by increases in sequencing depth or the number of samples, as the underlying optimization problems are solved independently for each sample. The computational complexity is therefore not a barrier to scalability but rather a reflection of the biological complexity of the problem being solved. The system’s ability to solve these complex problems efficiently represents a triumph of algorithmic engineering over theoretical limitations and enables the first practical application of combinatorial optimization to clinical genomics.

## Systems

The invention is implemented as a software system comprising a suite of interconnected modules, each responsible for a distinct phase of the genotyping pipeline. The system is written in a high-performance programming language such as C++ or Rust and is designed to run on standard Linux or macOS operating systems. The system accepts as input a BAM or SAM file containing aligned sequencing reads and a gene database in a structured format such as JSON or SQLite, which contains the genomic coordinates of the target gene and its pseudogenes, the list of known major and minor star-alleles, the sequence of each allele, the classification of each mutation as gene-disrupting or neutral, and the structural configurations of all known hybrid alleles. The system first loads the gene database and validates its integrity, ensuring that all required fields are present and that the allele definitions are consistent with the reference genome. It then reads the BAM file and extracts the coverage profile for the target gene region, applying the coverage normalization procedure to correct for sequencing biases. The system then invokes the integer linear programming solver to solve the copy number estimation problem, identifying the set of structural configurations that best explain the observed coverage. It then uses the resulting configuration to filter the set of candidate major star-alleles and solves the major star-allele identification problem to determine the copy number of each allele. Finally, it solves the quadratic integer programming problem for genotype refinement, assigning neutral mutations to form minor star-alleles. The system outputs a structured report in JSON or CSV format, containing the genotype for each gene copy, including the star-allele designation, copy number, structural configuration, and the list of mutations that define each allele. The system also includes a command-line interface that allows users to specify input and output paths, select the gene to be genotyped, and adjust optimization parameters such as penalty weights. The system is designed to be modular and extensible, allowing new genes to be added by providing a new database file without requiring changes to the core code. The system also includes a built-in validation module that compares its predictions against known genotypes from reference samples and generates quality control metrics such as concordance rates and Mendelian consistency scores. The system is distributed as a standalone executable with no external dependencies, ensuring ease of deployment in clinical laboratories and research settings.

## Examples

The invention has been applied to multiple independent cohorts of samples, including 96 Coriell cell line samples sequenced using the PGRNseq v.2 protocol, 137 GeT-RM samples sequenced using PGRNseq v.1, and 25 whole-genome sequencing samples from the Platinum Genome and 1000 Genomes Projects. In all cases, the system was able to accurately genotype CYP2D6 and other pharmacogenes with a concordance rate exceeding 99% compared to validated genotypes from PCR-based panels. The system identified all known structural variants, including full and partial deletions, duplications, and hybrid alleles, and correctly assigned star-allele designations in cases where existing tools such as Cypiripi and Astrolabe failed. The system also discovered novel major star-alleles, including a CYP2D6*10-like allele with an additional c.77 G>A mutation and a novel DPYD allele formed by the combination of mutations from *5 and *9. In samples where the genotyping panel was unable to detect key SNPs, such as those distinguishing CYP2D6*35 from *2, the system correctly identified the hybrid structure and assigned the appropriate allele. The system also resolved ambiguous cases where multiple minor star-alleles shared the same gene-disrupting mutations, such as the CYP2D6*4 family, by assigning novel neutral mutations to form new sub-alleles. The system’s performance was consistent across different sequencing platforms and depths, demonstrating its robustness and generalizability. The system was also applied to other pharmacogenes, including CYP2A6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and DPYD, with similarly high accuracy, confirming its applicability beyond CYP2D6. The system’s ability to process data in under ten seconds and with minimal memory usage makes it suitable for integration into clinical workflows, where rapid turnaround times are essential for timely drug prescribing decisions.

### DISCUSSION

The invention represents a paradigm shift in the field of pharmacogenomic genotyping by addressing the fundamental limitations of existing methods that rely on variant calling and reference-based alignment. By treating the gene as a dynamic, multi-copy structure rather than a single locus, the system is able to resolve the complex allelic architectures that have long confounded clinical interpretation. The integration of structural variant detection, copy number estimation, and combinatorial optimization into a unified framework enables the first fully automated, comprehensive, and clinically actionable genotyping of structurally complex genes directly from raw sequencing data. The system’s ability to identify novel star-alleles and assign minor allele extensions based on neutral mutations transforms the star-allele nomenclature from a static catalog into a living, evolving framework that can adapt to new biological discoveries. The system’s speed, accuracy, and low resource requirements make it ideal for clinical deployment, where rapid, reliable genotyping is essential for personalized medicine. The invention also has broader implications for the analysis of other highly polymorphic gene families, such as the HLA genes, the KIR genes, and the amylase gene cluster, which share similar challenges of sequence homology and structural variation. By providing a generalizable framework for allelic decomposition, the invention lays the foundation for a new generation of genomic diagnostics that are not limited by the constraints of traditional genotyping technologies. The system’s success demonstrates that the complexity of the human genome can be tamed not by longer reads or higher coverage, but by smarter algorithms that respect the biological reality of gene structure and evolution.
