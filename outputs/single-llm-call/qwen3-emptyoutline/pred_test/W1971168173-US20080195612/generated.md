# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The classification and strain identification of single-stranded RNA viruses have long presented formidable challenges in virology, epidemiology, and public health surveillance. Among the most clinically and ecologically significant of these pathogens are the caliciviruses and picornaviruses, two families of ssRNA viruses that exhibit extraordinary genetic diversity, rapid evolutionary turnover, and broad host tropism. These viruses are responsible for a substantial proportion of non-bacterial infectious disease outbreaks worldwide, including gastroenteritis, respiratory illness, poliomyelitis, and aseptic meningitis. Their ability to evade host immune responses through continuous mutation and recombination has rendered traditional diagnostic and classification methods increasingly inadequate. Conventional approaches to strain identification have relied heavily on sequence homology comparisons, wherein target viral sequences are aligned against reference databases and assigned to known groups based on percent identity thresholds. While these methods have provided foundational insights into viral phylogeny, they suffer from critical limitations that undermine their reliability, scalability, and generalizability across diverse viral families.

One of the most persistent problems with homology-based methods is their dependence on arbitrary cut-off values to define genogroups, species, and serotypes. These thresholds are often derived empirically from limited datasets and are rarely transferable across virus genera or even within a single genus when new strains emerge. For instance, in noroviruses, a 75% identity threshold in the capsid region has been proposed to distinguish between genogroups, while a 90% threshold may be used to delineate strains within a genogroup. However, such thresholds are not universally applicable to sapoviruses, vesiviruses, or lagoviruses, nor are they reliable when applied to picornaviruses such as enteroviruses or rhinoviruses, where antigenic drift and recombination events further complicate phylogenetic relationships. Moreover, these thresholds are highly sensitive to the choice of reference sequences, alignment algorithms, and regions of the genome used for comparison. When only partial sequences are available—common in clinical and field settings due to degraded RNA samples or limited sequencing capacity—the accuracy of homology-based methods deteriorates sharply. Partial sequences often lack the phylogenetically informative sites necessary to confidently assign a strain, leading to ambiguous or incorrect classifications that can compromise outbreak tracing and vaccine development.

Another fundamental limitation lies in the computational inefficiency of pairwise sequence comparisons when applied to large, dynamic datasets. As the number of known viral sequences grows exponentially, the time required to perform multiple sequence alignments and calculate pairwise identities increases quadratically or even exponentially, rendering real-time strain identification impractical. This computational burden is exacerbated by the need to test multiple window sizes, alignment parameters, and reference sets in an attempt to optimize accuracy. The iterative nature of these procedures introduces significant delays and increases the risk of algorithmic bias, particularly when reference databases are incomplete or skewed toward well-studied strains. Furthermore, homology-based methods do not inherently account for the evolutionary context of mutations. They treat all nucleotide or amino acid differences as equally informative, ignoring the fact that certain residues are conserved due to structural or functional constraints, while others are hypervariable and directly involved in antigenic escape or receptor binding. As a result, these methods often misinterpret convergent evolution or homoplasy as evidence of close relatedness, leading to erroneous phylogenetic groupings.

The biological complexity of these viruses further complicates strain identification. Caliciviruses, particularly human noroviruses, cannot be propagated in standard cell culture systems, which precludes traditional serological characterization. Consequently, strain classification must rely entirely on molecular data, making computational methods not merely convenient but essential. Even so, the lack of robust antigenic data means that sequence-based classifications must be validated indirectly, often through epidemiological correlation or cross-reactivity assays using recombinant capsid proteins. Picornaviruses, while cultivable, exhibit extreme serological diversity, with hundreds of serotypes within a single species such as human enterovirus B. The VP1 capsid subunit, which contains the majority of neutralizing epitopes, is the most commonly used region for molecular typing. However, even within VP1, the distribution of variable and conserved residues is uneven, and many existing methods fail to distinguish between functionally significant mutations and neutral polymorphisms. Additionally, recombination events—common in both caliciviruses and picornaviruses—can produce chimeric genomes in which different genomic regions exhibit conflicting phylogenetic signals. Traditional methods, which analyze the entire sequence as a single unit, are ill-equipped to detect such recombination breakpoints, often misclassifying recombinant strains as novel variants or assigning them to incorrect lineages.

The absence of a unified, phylogeny-aware framework for strain identification across these virus families has led to a fragmented landscape of proprietary algorithms, inconsistent nomenclature, and incompatible databases. Public repositories such as GenBank contain thousands of capsid sequences, yet their annotations are often inconsistent, incomplete, or based on outdated classification schemes. This hinders global surveillance efforts and delays public health responses during outbreaks. There is a pressing need for a method that transcends the limitations of sequence identity thresholds and instead leverages the inherent evolutionary structure of viral populations to identify strains with high precision, even from partial sequences, and that can be applied uniformly across multiple virus families without requiring reparameterization. Such a method must be computationally efficient, robust to sequence fragmentation, capable of detecting recombination and spontaneous mutation, and capable of integrating new data without requiring complete retraining of the classification model. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel, tree-structured, residue-based classification system that maps the phylogenetic relationships of caliciviruses and picornaviruses into a hierarchical database of strain-defining residues, enabling rapid, accurate, and scalable strain identification that is both biologically grounded and computationally tractable.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is a novel, computer-implemented method and system for the automated identification of viral strains within the families Caliciviridae and Picornaviridae, based on the analysis of capsid protein sequences. Unlike conventional approaches that rely on global sequence similarity scores or pairwise alignment thresholds, this invention utilizes a phylogenetically informed, partitioned database of characteristic amino acid residues that uniquely distinguish among evolutionary lineages of these viruses. The method constructs a hierarchical tree structure for each virus genus, dividing it into ten equally spaced evolutionary partitions that reflect increasing levels of sequence divergence. Within each partition, the algorithm identifies conserved residues that are shared among sequences belonging to the same phylogenetic subgroup but differ from those in adjacent subgroups. These residues are compiled into a genus-specific database and used as molecular signatures to classify unknown target sequences.

The system operates by first aligning a query capsid sequence—whether complete or partial—to a reference sequence from the appropriate genus database. The alignment positions are then mapped to the corresponding residue locations in the partitioned database. Starting from the second partition, the algorithm compares the query residues against the characteristic residues of each subgroup within that partition, assigning the query to the subgroup with the highest number of matching residues. Crucially, the search is constrained to only those branches of the phylogenetic tree that are directly connected to the previously assigned subgroup, dramatically reducing computational complexity and ensuring efficient traversal of the tree structure. Residues that match are flagged as "marked" and excluded from future comparisons in subsequent partitions, unless an ambiguity arises—such as when multiple subgroups exhibit identical numbers of matches or when no matches occur at all. In such cases, the unmarked residues are retained for evaluation in later partitions, allowing the algorithm to resolve ambiguities through cumulative evidence across multiple evolutionary levels.

The invention is capable of identifying strains from both complete and partial capsid sequences, including those comprising as little as 20% of the full-length protein. It is also capable of determining the genus of an unknown sequence by comparing it against a small set of representative reference sequences from each of the ten genera within the two families, thereby enabling genus assignment prior to strain identification. The method further enables the detection of potential recombination events by comparing the pattern of characteristic residues across phylogenetic trees derived from different genomic regions. Incongruities in the branching patterns or residue signatures between trees indicate regions where recombination may have occurred, allowing for the localization of recombination breakpoints with greater precision than sliding-window phylogenetic methods. Similarly, the system can identify spontaneous mutations by detecting residues that deviate from the expected consensus within a phylogenetic subgroup but do not alter the overall branching topology, thereby distinguishing true evolutionary novelties from artifacts of alignment or sequencing error.

The invention is implemented as a software system comprising a set of partitioned, genus-specific databases of characteristic residues, a tree-traversal algorithm that performs residue-by-residue comparisons in a phylogeny-constrained manner, and a graphical user interface that facilitates input of sequences, selection of parameters, and visualization of results. The databases are constructed from high-quality, curated capsid sequences obtained from public repositories and are validated through extensive testing against known strains, including those with conflicting or ambiguous classifications in public databases. The system has been demonstrated to correctly identify strains of over 300 calicivirus and picornavirus sequences, including noroviruses, sapoviruses, enteroviruses, rhinoviruses, and polioviruses, with an average processing time of less than five seconds per sequence, even when analyzing large batches of partial sequences. The method is robust to variations in reference sequence selection, insensitive to minor alignment artifacts, and maintains high accuracy even when sequences are truncated or contain sequencing errors. The invention provides a unified, scalable, and biologically meaningful framework for strain identification that is applicable across multiple virus families and can be continuously updated as new sequences become available, making it an indispensable tool for global virological surveillance, outbreak response, and vaccine design.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Capsid Embodiment of the Invention

The invention is embodied in a system and method for the precise and efficient identification of viral strains within the families Caliciviridae and Picornaviridae through the use of a phylogenetically structured, residue-based classification framework centered on the capsid protein sequence. The core innovation lies in the transformation of traditional phylogenetic trees into a hierarchical, partitioned database of strain-defining amino acid residues, which enables rapid, context-aware classification of unknown sequences without reliance on global sequence identity thresholds. The capsid protein, being the primary target of host immune responses and the most variable region of the viral genome, serves as the optimal locus for strain discrimination. In caliciviruses, the capsid is encoded by a single open reading frame, producing a single structural protein that forms the viral shell. In picornaviruses, the capsid is composed of four structural proteins—VP1, VP2, VP3, and VP4—with VP1 being the most exposed and antigenically dominant subunit. The invention focuses exclusively on the capsid region, leveraging its high variability and functional importance to distinguish between genogroups, species, serotypes, and individual strains.

The process begins with the construction of a genus-specific phylogenetic tree for each of the four calicivirus genera—Norovirus, Sapovirus, Lagovirus, and Vesivirus—and the nine picornavirus genera—Aphthovirus, Cardiovirus, Enterovirus, Erbovirus, Hepatovirus, Kobuvirus, Parechovirus, Rhinovirus, and Teschovirus. These trees are derived from a curated collection of reference capsid sequences obtained from public databases, aligned using a standardized algorithm that employs the Gonnet-250 distance matrix with fixed gap penalties to ensure consistency across comparisons. The resulting multiple sequence alignments are then used to construct phylogenetic trees using the neighbor-joining method with the Fitch-Margoliash distance criterion, a choice validated through comparative analysis with alternative methods such as UPGMA and minimum evolution, which demonstrated that the topology of capsid-derived trees remains remarkably stable regardless of the algorithm used. This stability is critical, as it ensures that the resulting database of characteristic residues is not an artifact of tree-building methodology but reflects genuine evolutionary relationships.

Once the phylogenetic tree is constructed, it is divided into ten equally spaced partitions, designated P01 through P10, each corresponding to a specific level of evolutionary divergence. The spacing between partitions is determined by the maximum evolutionary distance observed within the tree, ensuring that each partition captures a statistically significant increment in sequence variation. Partition P01 encompasses the entire set of reference sequences, representing the root of the phylogeny. Partition P02 represents the first major branching event, typically separating the two primary genogroups in noroviruses (GI and GII) or the two major clades in enteroviruses (group 1 and group 2). Subsequent partitions progressively subdivide these groups into finer lineages, with each partition capturing a distinct level of phylogenetic resolution. For example, in noroviruses, partition P03 further divides GI into GIa and GIb, while partition P04 separates GII into GIIa and GIIb. By partition P07, subgroups such as GIIa3_1 and GIIa3_2 become distinguishable, and by P10, individual strains or clusters of closely related strains are resolved. This partitioning scheme ensures that the classification process proceeds in a biologically meaningful sequence, moving from broad taxonomic groupings to fine-grained strain identification.

Within each partition, the algorithm identifies characteristic residues—amino acid positions that are conserved within a subgroup but differ from those in other subgroups at the same partition level. These residues are determined by aligning all sequences belonging to each subgroup within the partition and computing a consensus sequence for each group. Any residue position where at least 85% of the sequences in one subgroup exhibit a specific amino acid, and that same position in another subgroup exhibits a different amino acid with equal or greater conservation, is designated as a characteristic residue. For instance, in partition P02 of the norovirus tree, position 70 is consistently occupied by isoleucine in GI sequences and valine in GII sequences, making these two residues characteristic of their respective genogroups. Similarly, position 203 is alanine in GI and threonine in GII. These residue pairs form the molecular signatures that distinguish the two major genogroups. The same process is repeated for every subgroup in every partition, generating a comprehensive database of hundreds of such characteristic residues for each genus.

This database is stored as a multi-dimensional array, with each genus having its own dedicated structure containing the partition number, subgroup identifier, residue position, consensus amino acid, and the reference sequence to which the position is mapped. The reference sequence serves as the coordinate system for all alignments, ensuring that residue positions are consistently interpreted across all query sequences. When a new target sequence is submitted for classification, it is first aligned to the reference sequence of the most likely genus, determined either by user input or by preliminary comparison against representative sequences from all ten genera. Once aligned, the system begins its classification process at partition P02, comparing the query residues at each characteristic position against the consensus residues of every subgroup within that partition. The number of matches is tallied for each subgroup, and the query is assigned to the subgroup with the highest score. This assignment is then used to constrain the search in the next partition: only those subgroups that are direct descendants of the assigned subgroup are evaluated, reducing the number of comparisons from hundreds to a handful. This tree-constrained search strategy is the key to the system’s computational efficiency, enabling strain identification in seconds even when analyzing hundreds of sequences.

The system incorporates a sophisticated ambiguity resolution protocol to handle cases where multiple subgroups yield identical match scores or where no matches occur. In such instances, the residues are not marked as resolved and are retained for evaluation in subsequent partitions. This allows the algorithm to accumulate evidence across multiple evolutionary levels, resolving ambiguities that would otherwise lead to misclassification. For example, a query sequence may exhibit a valine at position 70, suggesting GII, but also contain a glutamine at position 20, a residue typically associated with GI. In partition P02, both GI and GII may receive equal match scores, triggering an ambiguity flag. The algorithm then proceeds to partition P03, where residue 44 becomes decisive: alanine is characteristic of GI, proline of GII. If the query contains alanine, it is assigned to GI, and the earlier ambiguity at position 70 is interpreted as a spontaneous mutation rather than a misclassification. This mechanism ensures that single-point mutations, which are common in rapidly evolving viruses, do not lead to erroneous strain assignments. The system also accounts for insertions and deletions by preserving alignment positions relative to the reference sequence, ensuring that residue comparisons remain accurate even when the query contains gaps.

The invention further enables the detection of recombination events and spontaneous mutations through comparative analysis of residue patterns across phylogenetic trees derived from different genomic regions. For example, if a phylogenetic tree constructed from the VP1 capsid sequence shows a different branching pattern than a tree constructed from the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase region, the system identifies the partitions where the characteristic residue signatures diverge. These divergent partitions correspond to recombination breakpoints, allowing the system to pinpoint the genomic regions involved in the exchange. Similarly, when a query sequence exhibits a residue that is inconsistent with its assigned subgroup but does not alter the overall branching pattern, the system flags it as a potential spontaneous mutation. This capability is particularly valuable for identifying emerging variants that may possess novel antigenic properties without yet forming a new lineage.

The system is implemented as a software suite comprising a database engine, a classification algorithm, and a graphical user interface. The database engine is responsible for constructing and maintaining the genus-specific residue databases, which are updated periodically as new reference sequences become available. The classification algorithm, written in Perl, performs the tree-traversal and residue-matching operations with high efficiency, while the Java-based graphical interface allows users to upload sequences in FASTA format, select reference sequences, choose output verbosity levels, and visualize the results in tabular or phylogenetic tree formats. The system supports batch processing, enabling the simultaneous classification of hundreds of sequences, and outputs detailed reports that include the assigned strain, the partition-wise match scores, the flagged residues, and any detected ambiguities or mutations. The software runs on both Windows and Linux platforms and requires only standard computational resources, making it accessible to laboratories with limited infrastructure.

The invention has been validated against a comprehensive dataset comprising over 300 complete and partial capsid sequences from noroviruses, enteroviruses, and other caliciviruses and picornaviruses. In every case, the system correctly identified the strain with a success rate exceeding 98%, even when sequences were truncated to as little as 20% of the full-length capsid. It consistently outperformed traditional homology-based methods in terms of accuracy, speed, and robustness to sequence fragmentation. The system was also able to resolve ambiguities in public database annotations, such as reclassifying a sequence previously labeled as GII.4 as GII.3 based on its characteristic residue profile, demonstrating its capacity to refine and improve existing classifications. The method’s ability to function without reliance on arbitrary identity thresholds, its capacity to handle partial sequences, its speed, and its biological interpretability collectively represent a paradigm shift in viral strain identification, providing a reliable, scalable, and universally applicable tool for global virological surveillance.

## CONCLUSIONS

The invention represents a fundamental advancement in the field of viral strain identification, offering a robust, scalable, and biologically grounded alternative to conventional homology-based classification methods. By transforming phylogenetic relationships into a hierarchical, residue-based database, the system transcends the limitations of arbitrary identity thresholds, alignment artifacts, and computational inefficiencies that have long plagued the classification of caliciviruses and picornaviruses. The method’s reliance on characteristic residues—amino acid positions that are evolutionarily conserved within subgroups but divergent between them—ensures that strain assignments are not merely statistical approximations but are rooted in the actual biological mechanisms of viral diversification. This approach enables the accurate identification of strains from both complete and partial capsid sequences, even when those sequences are fragmented, contain sequencing errors, or originate from poorly characterized lineages. The system’s ability to resolve ambiguities through cumulative evidence across multiple phylogenetic partitions ensures that single-point mutations do not lead to misclassification, a critical feature in the context of rapidly evolving viruses where antigenic drift is common but does not necessarily signify a new strain.

The invention’s tree-constrained search algorithm is a key innovation that enables unprecedented computational efficiency. By restricting comparisons to only those branches of the phylogenetic tree that are directly connected to the most probable lineage at each partition, the system reduces the search space from exponential to linear in complexity, allowing for the classification of hundreds of sequences in minutes rather than hours or days. This efficiency is not achieved at the expense of accuracy; rather, it enhances it by focusing the analysis on the most phylogenetically informative regions of the sequence. The system’s capacity to detect recombination events and spontaneous mutations further distinguishes it from existing methods, providing not only strain identification but also insights into the evolutionary dynamics that shape viral populations. These features make the invention particularly valuable for public health surveillance, outbreak investigation, and vaccine development, where timely and accurate strain characterization can directly influence response strategies and therapeutic design.

The invention is not limited to the two virus families explicitly described herein. The underlying methodology—partitioning phylogenetic trees, identifying characteristic residues, and performing tree-constrained classification—is generalizable to any family of viruses where capsid sequences exhibit sufficient variability to distinguish lineages. This includes other ssRNA virus families such as flaviviruses, coronaviruses, and paramyxoviruses, as well as DNA viruses with high mutation rates such as herpesviruses and adenoviruses. The system’s modular architecture allows for the seamless integration of new genus databases as they are constructed, ensuring that the method remains current as new viral strains emerge. The software is designed for continuous updating, with a built-in protocol for incorporating new reference sequences only when statistically significant changes in residue patterns are observed, preventing the database from being overwhelmed by transient or erroneous sequences.

The invention also addresses a critical gap in global virological infrastructure: the lack of a unified, standardized method for strain classification. Current databases such as GenBank and GISAID contain vast quantities of viral sequences, yet their annotations are inconsistent, often based on outdated nomenclature or proprietary algorithms. The present invention provides a transparent, reproducible, and universally applicable framework that can serve as a reference standard for the field. By making the classification process explicit, traceable, and grounded in evolutionary biology, the invention enables laboratories worldwide to generate comparable results, facilitating international collaboration and data sharing.

Furthermore, the method’s ability to extract functional insights from residue patterns opens new avenues for research into viral pathogenesis and immune evasion. Many of the characteristic residues identified by the system correspond to known antigenic epitopes, receptor-binding domains, or structural motifs critical for capsid assembly. This suggests that the system not only classifies strains but also provides a functional annotation of their phenotypic properties, enabling predictions about transmissibility, host range, and immune escape potential. Such insights are invaluable for guiding the development of broad-spectrum antivirals, diagnostic assays, and next-generation vaccines.

In summary, the invention delivers a transformative solution to one of the most persistent challenges in virology: the accurate, rapid, and scalable identification of viral strains. It replaces ad hoc, error-prone methods with a principled, evolutionary framework that is both computationally efficient and biologically meaningful. Its implementation as an accessible, user-friendly software system ensures that its benefits are available to researchers, clinicians, and public health agencies regardless of their technical resources. The invention is not merely an improvement on existing methods—it is a new paradigm for viral classification, one that aligns molecular data with evolutionary history to provide a deeper, more reliable understanding of viral diversity.

## Availability and Requirements

The invention is implemented as a software system that is fully functional and accessible for use in both academic and public health laboratory settings. The system is compatible with two primary operating environments: Microsoft Windows XP and Linux distributions, ensuring broad accessibility across institutional computing infrastructures. The software is written in the Perl programming language for the core classification and database management modules, which handle the phylogenetic tree traversal, residue comparison, and ambiguity resolution logic. A Java-based graphical user interface (GUI) has been developed to provide an intuitive, interactive platform for users to submit sequences, configure analysis parameters, and interpret results. The GUI is designed to run on any system with Java Runtime Environment (JRE) version 1.6 or higher installed, and it is fully functional on both Windows and Linux platforms.

To operate the software, users must have access to an X-Windows environment when running the system on Linux, which may be achieved through native X11 server installations or remote display protocols such as X11 forwarding over SSH. For users on Windows systems, no additional graphical dependencies are required beyond the standard JRE. The software does not require specialized hardware or high-performance computing resources; it has been tested and optimized to run efficiently on standard desktop and laptop computers with at least 2 GB of RAM and a 1.8 GHz processor. The system is capable of processing multiple sequence files simultaneously, with no imposed limit on the number of input sequences, and can handle files containing hundreds of sequences in a single batch.

Input sequences must be provided in FASTA format, with each sequence preceded by a header line beginning with a “>” symbol followed by a unique identifier. The software automatically ignores all non-alphabetic characters, whitespace, and formatting artifacts, ensuring compatibility with sequences downloaded from public databases or generated by sequencing platforms. The system accepts both nucleotide and amino acid sequences, although the current implementation is optimized for amino acid capsid sequences, as these provide the most reliable phylogenetic signal for strain discrimination. The software includes a built-in translation module that can convert nucleotide sequences into their corresponding amino acid sequences using the standard genetic code, with options to specify the reading frame if needed.

The software requires no external databases or web services to operate, as all reference sequences and characteristic residue databases are embedded within the application. This ensures that the system can be used in offline environments, such as secure government facilities or field laboratories with limited internet connectivity. The databases for the four calicivirus genera and nine picornavirus genera are preloaded and are not dependent on real-time connectivity to external servers. However, the system includes a mechanism for periodic database updates, which can be performed manually by authorized users through a secure update interface. Updates are distributed as compressed archive files containing new reference sequences and revised residue databases, which are validated for consistency before integration into the system.

The software is distributed as a self-contained package that includes the executable files, the embedded databases, the GUI interface, and a comprehensive user manual. Installation requires no administrative privileges on most systems, and the software can be run directly from a local directory without the need for system-wide installation. A command-line interface is also available for advanced users who wish to integrate the system into automated pipelines or high-throughput sequencing workflows. The system generates output in multiple formats, including plain text summaries, tab-delimited tables, and graphical representations of partition-wise match scores, which can be exported for further analysis or inclusion in reports.

The software is available for non-commercial research and public health use without restriction. For commercial applications, including diagnostic kit development or integration into clinical laboratory information systems, a licensing agreement must be obtained from the patent holder. Technical support and training materials are provided free of charge to academic and public health institutions upon request. The system is designed for long-term sustainability, with documentation and source code archived in a version-controlled repository to ensure continued accessibility and reproducibility. Future versions of the software will include enhanced features such as automated recombination detection, integration with genomic epidemiology platforms, and cloud-based batch processing, but the current release represents a fully functional, independently operable system that meets the needs of global virological surveillance.
