# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to photothermal energy conversion systems and, more specifically, to a novel class of carbon-based membranes engineered for highly efficient solar-driven interfacial evaporation and thermal management. The invention encompasses a composite photothermal membrane structure comprising a porous graphitized foam substrate functionalized with a fluorinated silane surface layer and coated with a precisely controlled mixture of graphite particles and phenolic resin, wherein the resulting architecture exhibits exceptional solar absorption, minimal thermal loss, and superior hydrophobicity to enable sustained, high-efficiency water evaporation under ambient solar irradiation. The membrane is designed for use in desalination, wastewater purification, humidity control, and renewable energy harvesting applications where low-cost, scalable, and energy-efficient vapor generation is required. The invention further includes methods for the fabrication, surface modification, and operational deployment of such membranes under natural or simulated solar conditions without the need for external heating, electrical input, or pressurized systems. The photothermal membrane is distinguished by its multi-scale structural design, wherein the underlying graphitized foam provides a macroporous scaffold for capillary water transport, while the surface-modified graphite-phenolic coating serves as a broadband solar absorber and a water-repellent barrier that confines the evaporation interface to the topmost layer, thereby minimizing conductive and convective heat losses to the bulk liquid. The combination of these features results in a system that achieves solar-to-vapor conversion efficiencies exceeding 90% under one-sun illumination, surpassing the performance of conventional evaporative membranes and competing photothermal materials by a significant margin.

The invention is particularly suited for deployment in off-grid, remote, or resource-constrained environments where access to electricity or advanced infrastructure is limited. Its mechanical robustness, chemical stability under prolonged exposure to saline or contaminated water, and compatibility with low-temperature thermal processing make it ideal for integration into portable, modular, or large-scale water purification units. Furthermore, the materials used in the fabrication are commercially available, non-toxic, and cost-effective, enabling mass production without reliance on rare or exotic components. The method of fabrication avoids high-vacuum deposition, lithographic patterning, or complex chemical synthesis, relying instead on simple immersion, coating, and thermal curing steps that are readily scalable using industrial manufacturing techniques. The resulting membrane is not only thermally and optically optimized but also structurally self-supporting, eliminating the need for secondary substrates, supports, or encapsulation layers commonly required in prior art systems. This self-contained architecture significantly reduces system complexity, weight, and cost while enhancing durability and operational lifetime. The invention thus represents a fundamental advancement in the field of solar-driven interfacial evaporation, offering a practical, commercially viable solution to global challenges in clean water access and sustainable energy utilization.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The global demand for clean freshwater continues to outpace available supply, particularly in arid regions, coastal communities, and developing nations where conventional desalination and purification technologies are either economically prohibitive or energetically unsustainable. Reverse osmosis, multi-effect distillation, and electrodialysis—while effective—require substantial energy inputs, high-pressure infrastructure, and frequent maintenance, rendering them unsuitable for decentralized or off-grid applications. In recent years, solar-driven interfacial evaporation has emerged as a promising alternative, leveraging the natural abundance of sunlight to generate water vapor at the air-liquid interface without heating the entire bulk volume. This approach exploits the principle of localized heating, wherein a photothermal material absorbs solar radiation and converts it into thermal energy concentrated at the surface, promoting evaporation while minimizing heat dissipation into the underlying water. The theoretical maximum efficiency of such systems is governed by the balance between solar absorption, thermal confinement, and evaporative kinetics, with the most successful designs achieving efficiencies above 80% under one-sun illumination.

Prior art in this domain has primarily focused on two classes of materials: polymer-based composites infused with carbon nanotubes, graphene oxide, or plasmonic nanoparticles, and porous substrates coated with thin films of metallic or semiconductor absorbers. While these systems have demonstrated promising laboratory results, they suffer from significant limitations that hinder practical deployment. Polymer-based composites often exhibit poor mechanical stability under prolonged water immersion, degrade under UV exposure, and lack the thermal conductivity necessary for rapid heat redistribution. Moreover, the incorporation of nanoscale carbon materials such as graphene or carbon nanotubes introduces high fabrication costs, scalability challenges, and potential environmental concerns related to nanoparticle leaching. Metallic and semiconductor thin films, on the other hand, are typically deposited via vacuum-based techniques such as sputtering or chemical vapor deposition, which are incompatible with large-area, low-cost manufacturing. These films also tend to be brittle, prone to delamination under thermal cycling, and susceptible to fouling by organic or ionic contaminants present in real-world water sources.

Another critical shortcoming of existing photothermal membranes is their inability to simultaneously achieve high solar absorption, low thermal conductivity, and strong hydrophobicity. Many systems rely on hydrophilic surfaces to facilitate capillary water transport, but this leads to excessive heat conduction into the bulk liquid, reducing overall efficiency. Others employ superhydrophobic coatings to confine evaporation to the surface, yet these coatings often compromise water uptake, leading to insufficient feed rates and localized drying. The challenge, therefore, lies in designing a material architecture that decouples water transport from heat transfer—allowing rapid capillary replenishment from below while preventing thermal loss through the substrate and minimizing evaporation from non-irradiated regions. Furthermore, the material must remain stable under prolonged exposure to saline, acidic, or alkaline environments, resist biofouling, and maintain performance over thousands of operational cycles without degradation.

Previous attempts to address these issues have included the use of aerogels, wood-derived foams, and electrospun fibers as substrates, each with inherent drawbacks. Aerogels, while highly porous and thermally insulating, are mechanically fragile and difficult to handle at scale. Wood-derived foams offer natural capillary channels but suffer from low thermal conductivity and poor photothermal conversion efficiency due to their lignocellulosic composition. Electrospun fibers provide high surface area but lack structural integrity and require complex fabrication processes. None of these materials have successfully integrated the triad of properties required for high-efficiency, durable, and scalable interfacial evaporation: a macroporous, graphitized scaffold for efficient water transport; a broadband solar absorber with near-unity absorption across the solar spectrum; and a chemically stable, low-surface-energy surface layer that repels water while permitting vapor escape.

Additionally, prior art has largely overlooked the importance of surface chemistry in long-term performance. Many photothermal membranes are coated with hydrophobic agents such as fluorinated alkyl silanes or polytetrafluoroethylene, but these coatings are often applied as thin, discontinuous layers that degrade under UV exposure, mechanical abrasion, or thermal cycling. The present invention overcomes this limitation by introducing a novel surface functionalization protocol that ensures covalent bonding of fluorosilane molecules to the graphitized foam substrate, resulting in a durable, chemically anchored hydrophobic layer that withstands prolonged immersion, thermal stress, and fouling without loss of performance. The invention further introduces a unique composite coating of graphite particles and phenolic resin, applied via a simple, scalable coating method that forms a dense, conformal, and thermally conductive layer with precisely controlled porosity and optical properties. This coating is not merely a passive absorber but an active participant in the evaporation process, serving as both the primary solar absorber and the structural matrix that defines the evaporation interface.

The present invention thus represents a paradigm shift in photothermal membrane design. It is not an incremental improvement over existing materials but a fundamentally new architecture that integrates material selection, surface engineering, and structural design into a single, synergistic system. By combining a graphitized foam substrate with a fluorinated silane surface layer and a graphite-phenolic composite coating, the invention achieves a level of performance, durability, and manufacturability that has not been previously demonstrated. It eliminates the need for expensive nanomaterials, complex fabrication processes, or external energy inputs, while providing a system that is robust, scalable, and capable of sustained operation under real-world conditions. The invention therefore fills a critical gap in the field of solar-driven water purification, offering a practical, economically viable, and environmentally sustainable solution to one of the most pressing challenges of the 21st century.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a photothermal membrane for solar-driven interfacial evaporation comprising a graphitized foam substrate, a fluorinated silane surface layer covalently bonded to the substrate, and a composite coating formed from a mixture of graphite particles and phenolic resin, wherein the composite coating is disposed on at least a portion of the outer surface of the graphitized foam substrate and exhibits a solar absorption efficiency of greater than 95% across the solar spectrum from 330 to 2500 nanometers. The graphitized foam substrate is characterized by a macroporous structure with interconnected pores ranging from 50 to 500 micrometers in diameter, providing a continuous pathway for capillary-driven water transport from a liquid reservoir to the evaporation interface. The fluorinated silane surface layer is formed by immersion of the graphitized foam substrate in a solution containing (heptadecafluoro-1,1,2,2-tetrahydrodecyl) trichlorosilane at a concentration of 0.1 weight percent in hexane, followed by thermal curing at 100 degrees Celsius for one hour, resulting in a surface with a water contact angle exceeding 140 degrees and a surface energy below 10 millinewtons per meter. The composite coating is applied by dispersing graphite particles and phenolic resin in a weight ratio of 2:1 in an aqueous slurry, coating the surface of the graphitized foam substrate with the slurry using a non-metallic applicator, and subsequently curing the coated substrate at 300 degrees Celsius for one hour to induce cross-linking of the phenolic resin and densification of the graphite network. The resulting membrane exhibits a total thickness of 3 to 8 millimeters, with the composite coating forming a continuous, conformal layer no thicker than 500 micrometers on the upper surface of the substrate, thereby confining the photothermal heating to the topmost region and minimizing conductive heat loss to the underlying liquid.

The invention further includes a method for fabricating the photothermal membrane, comprising the steps of: machining a graphitized foam substrate into a desired geometry using a mechanical cutting tool; immersing the machined substrate in a solution of (heptadecafluoro-1,1,2,2-tetrahydrodecyl) trichlorosilane dissolved in hexane at a concentration of 0.1 weight percent for a period of at least 12 hours; rinsing the substrate with deionized water and ethanol to remove unreacted silane molecules; drying the substrate in an oven at 100 degrees Celsius for one hour; preparing a slurry by mixing graphite particles with a particle size distribution of 225 to 275 square meters per gram and phenolic resin in a weight ratio of 2:1; applying the slurry to the upper surface of the dried substrate using a wooden stick knife or similar non-abrasive applicator to form a uniform coating; and thermally curing the coated substrate at 300 degrees Celsius for one hour in an inert or ambient atmosphere to form a dense, carbonized composite layer. The method further includes the step of calibrating the solar irradiation source to an intensity of 100 milliwatts per square centimeter using a NIST-traceable silicon reference cell, wherein the membrane is placed in contact with a liquid source and exposed to the calibrated solar irradiation to achieve a water evaporation rate of at least 1.8 kilograms per square meter per hour under standard AM 1.5 G solar spectrum conditions.

The photothermal membrane of the invention is characterized by its ability to achieve a solar-to-vapor conversion efficiency of greater than 90% under one-sun illumination, as determined by measuring the mass loss of water over time under controlled environmental conditions and comparing it to the incident solar energy flux. The membrane exhibits negligible transmission of solar radiation due to its thickness exceeding 3 millimeters and the high optical density of the composite coating, resulting in a transmission efficiency of less than 1%. The reflectance efficiency is maintained below 5% across the entire solar spectrum, contributing to the high absorption efficiency. The surface chemistry of the membrane, as confirmed by time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry, reveals the presence of fluorinated carbon chains covalently bonded to the graphitized surface, with no detectable residual silane or hydroxyl groups. The composite coating, as observed via scanning electron microscopy, exhibits a uniform morphology with no visible cracks, delamination, or phase separation, and the graphite particles are fully embedded within the carbonized phenolic matrix, forming a continuous conductive network that facilitates rapid lateral heat distribution.

The invention further encompasses the use of the photothermal membrane in a desalination system wherein the membrane is positioned at the interface between a saline water reservoir and an ambient air environment, wherein the membrane absorbs solar radiation and generates water vapor that condenses on a cooled surface above, resulting in the production of freshwater with a salt rejection rate exceeding 99.9%. The membrane is capable of sustained operation for over 1000 hours without measurable degradation in evaporation rate or absorption efficiency, even when exposed to seawater, brackish water, or wastewater containing suspended solids, organic matter, or ionic contaminants. The fluorinated silane layer provides resistance to biofouling and prevents the accumulation of salts or minerals on the evaporation surface, thereby eliminating the need for periodic cleaning or chemical regeneration. The graphitized foam substrate remains structurally intact under thermal cycling and mechanical stress, and the composite coating demonstrates no signs of carbonization, oxidation, or erosion after prolonged exposure to solar irradiation and water contact.

The invention further includes a modular water purification unit comprising multiple photothermal membranes arranged in parallel or stacked configuration, each membrane independently operable and connected to a condensation chamber, wherein the system is capable of producing freshwater at a rate exceeding 10 liters per square meter per day under natural sunlight conditions. The unit requires no external power source, pump, or pressure system, and is constructed entirely from non-toxic, recyclable materials. The photothermal membrane may be integrated into portable devices, rooftop collectors, or large-scale solar farms for water production, and is compatible with existing condensation and collection technologies. The invention thus provides a complete, self-contained, and scalable solution for solar-driven water purification that is economically viable, environmentally sustainable, and operationally robust under real-world conditions.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The photothermal membrane of the present invention is a meticulously engineered composite structure designed to maximize solar energy absorption while minimizing thermal losses and maximizing evaporation efficiency through a synergistic integration of material selection, surface chemistry, and structural architecture. The foundation of the invention is a graphitized foam substrate, which serves as the primary mechanical scaffold and capillary transport medium. This substrate is fabricated from heat-treated pitch-based mesophase carbon, a material selected for its exceptional thermal conductivity, chemical inertness, and structural stability under prolonged exposure to elevated temperatures and aqueous environments. The foam is produced through a controlled pyrolysis and graphitization process that results in a three-dimensional network of interconnected macropores, with pore diameters ranging from 50 to 500 micrometers. This pore size range is critical: pores smaller than 50 micrometers impede rapid capillary water transport, while pores larger than 500 micrometers reduce the surface area available for water-vapor interfacial contact and compromise mechanical integrity. The graphitized foam is obtained in pre-formed blocks with a density of approximately 0.15 grams per cubic centimeter and a thickness of 5 millimeters, which is subsequently machined into the desired geometry using precision electrical saws and drills. The machining process is performed under ambient conditions without the use of coolants or lubricants to prevent contamination of the foam surface, ensuring that the intrinsic hydrophobicity and surface chemistry of the graphitized material remain unaltered prior to functionalization.

Following machining, the graphitized foam substrate undergoes a surface modification procedure to render its outer surfaces hydrophobic. This is accomplished through immersion in a dilute solution of (heptadecafluoro-1,1,2,2-tetrahydrodecyl) trichlorosilane at a concentration of 0.1 weight percent in anhydrous hexane. The choice of this specific fluorosilane molecule is not arbitrary; its long perfluorinated alkyl chain provides an exceptionally low surface energy, while its trichlorosilane head group enables covalent bonding to residual hydroxyl and silanol groups present on the graphitized surface. The immersion is carried out for a minimum of 12 hours to ensure complete diffusion of the silane molecules into the porous network and to allow sufficient time for surface reactions to reach equilibrium. During this period, the silane molecules adsorb onto the graphitized surface and undergo hydrolysis and condensation reactions, forming a dense, monolayer-like coating of fluorinated siloxane bonds. The use of hexane as the solvent is essential, as it is non-polar and does not interfere with the silane reaction kinetics, nor does it introduce water prematurely that could lead to uncontrolled polymerization. After immersion, the substrate is removed from the solution and rinsed thoroughly with deionized water to remove any unreacted silane or hydrolysis byproducts, followed by a second rinse with absolute ethanol to displace residual water and accelerate drying. The rinsing process is performed with copious volumes of solvent—typically at least 100 times the volume of the substrate—to ensure complete removal of non-covalently bound species, which, if left behind, could compromise hydrophobicity and lead to premature fouling.

The rinsed substrate is then placed in an oven and dried at 100 degrees Celsius for one hour. This thermal treatment serves a dual purpose: it drives off any residual solvent and promotes the final condensation of silanol groups into a stable siloxane network. The temperature is carefully chosen to be below the decomposition threshold of the graphitized foam (which begins to oxidize above 400 degrees Celsius in air) while being sufficient to complete the silane cross-linking. The resulting surface exhibits a water contact angle greater than 140 degrees, as measured by optical tensiometry, indicating a superhydrophobic state. The surface energy, calculated using the Owens-Wendt method, is reduced to less than 10 millinewtons per meter, which is among the lowest values reported for carbon-based surfaces. This extreme hydrophobicity is critical to the function of the invention, as it prevents water from penetrating into the bulk of the substrate and forces the evaporation interface to remain confined to the very top layer of the membrane. This spatial confinement is the key to minimizing thermal losses, as heat generated by solar absorption is not conducted downward into the water reservoir but is instead retained at the interface where it is most effective.

The next critical step in the fabrication process is the application of the photothermal composite coating. This coating is prepared by mixing graphite particles, grade 4827, with a specific surface area of 225 to 275 square meters per gram, and a phenolic resin, Durite SC1008, in a precise weight ratio of 2:1. The graphite particles are selected for their high intrinsic absorption of solar radiation across the visible and near-infrared spectrum, their excellent thermal conductivity, and their chemical stability under ambient and elevated temperatures. The phenolic resin, a thermosetting polymer derived from phenol and formaldehyde, serves as a binder that encapsulates the graphite particles and forms a continuous, carbonizable matrix upon thermal curing. The mixture is dispersed in deionized water to form a slurry with a viscosity suitable for uniform coating. The slurry is then applied to the upper surface of the hydrophobized graphitized foam substrate using a wooden stick knife, a non-metallic tool chosen to avoid introducing metallic contaminants that could catalyze unwanted side reactions or interfere with the optical properties of the coating. The application is performed manually with consistent pressure and stroke length to ensure a uniform thickness of approximately 200 to 500 micrometers across the entire surface. The coating is allowed to dry overnight at ambient temperature, during which time the water evaporates, leaving behind a porous network of graphite particles embedded in the uncured phenolic resin.

Following drying, the coated substrate is subjected to a thermal curing process at 300 degrees Celsius for one hour in an ambient atmosphere. This step initiates the cross-linking and carbonization of the phenolic resin, transforming it from a soft, thermoplastic polymer into a rigid, carbon-rich matrix that firmly binds the graphite particles together. The temperature is selected to be above the glass transition and decomposition temperature of the resin but below the onset of graphite oxidation, ensuring complete curing without degradation of the underlying substrate. The resulting composite coating is dense, continuous, and highly conductive, with the graphite particles forming a percolating network that facilitates rapid lateral heat distribution. This lateral conduction is essential to prevent localized hot spots and to ensure uniform evaporation across the entire surface area of the membrane. Scanning electron microscopy confirms that the coating exhibits no visible cracks, voids, or phase separation, and that the graphite particles are uniformly distributed and fully encapsulated by the carbonized resin. The thickness of the coating is deliberately kept below 500 micrometers to ensure that the majority of solar absorption occurs within the topmost layer, thereby maximizing the thermal confinement effect. Any thicker coating would increase thermal mass and reduce the rate of heat transfer to the surface, while any thinner coating would compromise absorption efficiency and mechanical durability.

The combined structure—the graphitized foam substrate, the fluorinated silane layer, and the graphite-phenolic composite coating—forms a tri-layered photothermal membrane that operates as a self-contained, solar-powered evaporation device. When exposed to solar irradiation, the composite coating absorbs over 95% of the incident photons across the solar spectrum from 330 to 2500 nanometers, as confirmed by portable reflectometry measurements. The transmission of light through the membrane is negligible due to its total thickness exceeding 3 millimeters, and the reflectance is kept below 5% due to the broadband absorption properties of the graphite-carbon matrix. The absorption efficiency, calculated as A = 1 − R − T, where R is reflectance and T is transmission, consistently exceeds 95% under standard AM 1.5 G solar spectrum conditions. The hydrophobic surface layer prevents water from wetting the coating, ensuring that evaporation occurs only at the vapor-liquid interface just above the coating, while the underlying graphitized foam continuously replenishes water via capillary action. The thermal conductivity of the graphitized foam is sufficient to conduct heat laterally across the surface but insufficient to conduct it downward into the bulk water, thereby minimizing conductive losses. The result is a system in which nearly all of the absorbed solar energy is utilized for phase change, with less than 5% lost to conduction, convection, or radiation.

The performance of the photothermal membrane is evaluated under controlled laboratory conditions using a solar simulator calibrated to 100 milliwatts per square centimeter, equivalent to one-sun illumination. The membrane is placed in contact with a reservoir of deionized water, seawater, or simulated wastewater, and the mass loss of water over time is recorded using a high-precision balance. The evaporation rate is calculated by dividing the mass loss by the surface area and exposure time, and the solar-to-vapor conversion efficiency is determined by comparing the latent heat of vaporization of the evaporated water to the total incident solar energy. Under these conditions, the membrane achieves an evaporation rate of 1.8 kilograms per square meter per hour, corresponding to a solar-to-vapor conversion efficiency of 92.3%. This efficiency is maintained over 1000 consecutive hours of continuous operation, with no measurable decline in performance. The membrane is then subjected to accelerated aging tests, including exposure to UV radiation, thermal cycling between 5 and 60 degrees Celsius, and immersion in saline solutions with salinities up to 100 grams per liter. In all cases, the membrane retains its hydrophobicity, absorption efficiency, and evaporation rate, demonstrating exceptional durability and resistance to fouling.

The surface chemistry of the membrane is analyzed using time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry, which reveals the presence of characteristic fluorinated fragments such as CF2, CF3, and C2F5, confirming the successful covalent attachment of the fluorosilane molecules. No signals corresponding to unreacted silane, hydroxyl groups, or organic contaminants are detected, indicating a clean, fully reacted surface. The composite coating is further analyzed using Raman spectroscopy, which confirms the graphitic nature of the carbon structure with a low I_D/I_G ratio, indicating high crystallinity and minimal defects. The thermal stability of the coating is confirmed by thermogravimetric analysis, which shows no weight loss below 400 degrees Celsius, well above the operational temperature range of the device.

The invention further encompasses the deployment of the photothermal membrane in practical water purification systems. In one embodiment, multiple membranes are arranged in parallel within a modular housing, each membrane connected to a condensation surface maintained at a temperature below the dew point of the evaporated vapor. The vapor condenses on the cooled surface and is collected as freshwater, while the remaining salt and contaminants are left behind on the membrane surface. Due to the superhydrophobic nature of the coating, salts do not accumulate on the evaporation interface but are instead pushed downward by the capillary flow and remain in the reservoir. This self-cleaning mechanism eliminates the need for periodic descaling or chemical cleaning, a major limitation of existing desalination technologies. In another embodiment, the membrane is integrated into a portable, foldable unit designed for emergency or field use, where it can be deployed in a shallow basin of seawater and left under sunlight to produce potable water without any external power source. The entire system weighs less than 500 grams and can produce up to 1.5 liters of freshwater per day under typical solar conditions.

The fabrication method of the invention is entirely scalable and compatible with existing industrial manufacturing processes. The graphitized foam substrate is commercially available in large sheets, and the fluorosilane immersion and coating application steps can be automated using conveyor-based systems. The thermal curing steps can be performed in batch ovens or continuous ribbon furnaces, making mass production economically feasible. The materials used—graphite, phenolic resin, and fluorosilane—are all low-cost, non-toxic, and widely available, with no reliance on rare earth elements, noble metals, or complex nanomaterials. The entire process requires no cleanroom conditions, high-vacuum equipment, or hazardous chemicals, and generates minimal waste. The resulting product is not only highly efficient but also environmentally benign, recyclable, and safe for use in drinking water applications.

In summary, the photothermal membrane of the present invention represents a revolutionary advance in solar-driven interfacial evaporation technology. By integrating a macroporous graphitized foam substrate with a covalently bonded fluorinated silane surface layer and a precisely engineered graphite-phenolic composite coating, the invention achieves an unprecedented combination of high solar absorption, minimal thermal loss, superior hydrophobicity, and exceptional durability. The membrane operates without external power, requires no maintenance, and produces freshwater at efficiencies and rates that surpass all prior art systems. It is a practical, scalable, and sustainable solution to the global water crisis, and its design principles open new avenues for the development of next-generation photothermal materials for energy, environmental, and industrial applications.
