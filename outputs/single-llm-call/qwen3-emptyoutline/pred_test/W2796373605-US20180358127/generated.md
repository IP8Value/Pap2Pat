# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE DISCLOSURE

Autism spectrum disorder is a complex neurodevelopmental condition characterized by persistent impairments in social communication and interaction, alongside restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities. The disorder manifests with considerable heterogeneity in symptom severity, cognitive ability, and functional outcomes, making diagnosis and intervention challenging. Over the past two decades, the estimated prevalence of autism spectrum disorder has increased substantially, with recent surveillance data indicating that approximately one in forty-four children in the United States receives a diagnosis by the age of eight. This rise has prompted heightened scientific and clinical interest in identifying objective, biologically grounded indicators that could support earlier detection, improve diagnostic accuracy, and inform targeted therapeutic strategies. Despite extensive research into potential neurobiological correlates, no validated biochemical, genetic, or molecular biomarker has yet been adopted into routine clinical practice for the diagnosis or stratification of autism spectrum disorder.

Historically, diagnosis has relied entirely on behavioral observation and standardized psychometric assessments, such as the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule and the Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised. While these tools remain the gold standard, they are subject to inter-rater variability, require specialized training, and often cannot be reliably applied until a child is at least two to three years of age—delaying the initiation of early intervention services that have been shown to significantly improve long-term outcomes. Consequently, there is an urgent need for objective, quantifiable, and accessible biological markers that can complement behavioral assessments and enable earlier, more precise identification of individuals along the autism spectrum.

Numerous lines of inquiry have explored the possibility that alterations in lipid metabolism, particularly in polyunsaturated fatty acids, may serve as such biomarkers. Polyunsaturated fatty acids, including omega-3 and omega-6 derivatives such as docosahexaenoic acid, eicosapentaenoic acid, and arachidonic acid, are critical structural components of neuronal membranes and play essential roles in synaptic plasticity, neuroinflammation modulation, and signal transduction. Given their abundance in the central nervous system and their dynamic regulation by dietary intake and enzymatic conversion, these fatty acids have been hypothesized to reflect underlying neurodevelopmental perturbations associated with autism spectrum disorder. Several early studies reported statistically significant differences in plasma or erythrocyte membrane concentrations of these fatty acids between individuals diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder and neurotypical controls. These findings generated considerable optimism that fatty acid profiles might serve as reliable, non-invasive biomarkers for autism spectrum disorder.

However, many of these studies were limited by small sample sizes, inconsistent methodologies for sample collection and fatty acid quantification, and a lack of rigorous statistical validation at the individual level. In particular, a critical flaw in much of the prior literature is the reliance on group-level comparisons of mean or median concentrations, without evaluating whether these differences are sufficient to distinguish one individual from another. Statistical significance at the population level does not equate to diagnostic utility at the individual level. For example, a group may exhibit a small but statistically significant reduction in mean docosahexaenoic acid concentration, yet the distributions of values between the autism spectrum disorder and neurotypical groups may overlap extensively, rendering the measure useless for classification purposes. This distinction is fundamental: a biomarker must be able to correctly assign a novel individual to one of two or more categories with high accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity—not merely demonstrate a difference in central tendency across cohorts.

Recent meta-analyses have attempted to synthesize findings across multiple studies, but they too have been constrained by the heterogeneity of methods and the absence of individual-level data. Many published studies report only summary statistics—means, standard deviations, p-values—without releasing raw data, making independent validation impossible. In some cases, researchers have attempted to reconstruct individual data points from published figures, a process fraught with error and subject to significant measurement uncertainty. Even when raw data are available, the statistical frameworks employed often fail to incorporate modern machine learning or classification techniques that are standard in diagnostic biomarker development. Instead, many studies rely on simple t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests to compare group means, without ever assessing the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, the C-statistic, or other metrics that quantify the actual ability of a biomarker to classify individuals.

Furthermore, confounding variables such as dietary intake, particularly seafood consumption, which is the primary dietary source of long-chain omega-3 fatty acids, have not been adequately controlled for in many studies. Differences in dietary patterns between autism spectrum disorder and neurotypical populations may contribute to observed fatty acid differences, thereby obscuring any true neurobiological signal. In some cases, caregivers of children with autism spectrum disorder may alter dietary habits in response to perceived nutritional deficiencies or anecdotal reports of benefit, further complicating interpretation. Without accounting for these environmental influences, any observed differences in fatty acid concentrations may reflect behavioral or socioeconomic factors rather than intrinsic neurodevelopmental pathology.

This background underscores a critical gap in the field: while numerous studies have reported statistically significant differences in fatty acid profiles between individuals with autism spectrum disorder and neurotypical controls, none have demonstrated that these differences are sufficient to reliably classify individuals with diagnostic accuracy. The failure to transition from group-level statistical significance to individual-level diagnostic utility has led to persistent ambiguity in the literature, with conflicting reports, irreproducible findings, and a general lack of clinical translation. The absence of a validated biomarker has also hindered the development of precision medicine approaches for autism spectrum disorder, including personalized nutritional interventions, pharmacological strategies targeting lipid metabolism, or stratification of patients for clinical trials based on biological subtypes.

In light of these challenges, a rigorous, large-scale investigation was undertaken to comprehensively evaluate the diagnostic potential of erythrocyte membrane fatty acid profiles in autism spectrum disorder. This study employed a well-characterized cohort of individuals with autism spectrum disorder and neurotypical controls, matched for key demographic variables, with standardized sample collection, processing, and quantification procedures. All fatty acid measurements were normalized to total fatty acid content to account for variations in overall lipid concentration. Crucially, the analysis did not stop at group-level comparisons but extended to a thorough evaluation of classification performance using receiver operating characteristic analysis, multivariate discriminant modeling, and correlation with behavioral severity metrics. The results of this investigation revealed that, despite minor group-level differences in certain fatty acids, the individual-level distributions of all measured fatty acids—including docosahexaenoic acid, eicosapentaenoic acid, arachidonic acid, and their ratios—exhibited substantial overlap between the autism spectrum disorder and neurotypical groups. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve for every individual fatty acid and every composite ratio was consistently near or at 0.5, indicating no discriminatory power beyond chance. Even when multivariate methods such as Fisher discriminant analysis were applied to combinations of all measured fatty acids, the resulting classification accuracy remained insufficient for diagnostic purposes, with a C-statistic of only 0.76, a value that falls well below the threshold required for clinical implementation.

These findings challenge the prevailing assumption that fatty acid profiles are viable biomarkers for autism spectrum disorder. They demonstrate that the statistical significance observed in prior studies, while real, is not clinically meaningful. The absence of a robust, reproducible, and diagnostically useful signal suggests that alterations in erythrocyte membrane fatty acids are either not a primary feature of autism spectrum disorder pathophysiology or are too heterogeneous and context-dependent to serve as a reliable indicator. This conclusion has profound implications for future research directions. It indicates that efforts to develop diagnostic tools based on fatty acid profiling should be redirected toward other biological domains, such as metabolomic signatures, epigenetic markers, neuroimaging patterns, or immune profiling. It also suggests that previous clinical trials investigating omega-3 supplementation as a therapeutic intervention for autism spectrum disorder may have been based on a flawed premise—that individuals with autism spectrum disorder have a quantifiable deficiency in these fatty acids. If no consistent difference exists between groups, then supplementation cannot be rationally targeted to correct a deficiency; any observed effects in such trials must be attributed to other mechanisms, such as general anti-inflammatory properties or placebo effects.

Moreover, this study highlights a broader methodological issue in biomarker research: the overreliance on group-level statistics and the underappreciation of classification performance metrics. The field of autism spectrum disorder research would benefit from adopting standards common in oncology, cardiology, and neurology, where diagnostic biomarkers are evaluated not by p-values but by sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values, and cross-validated accuracy. The absence of publicly accessible, individual-level datasets has further impeded progress, as it prevents independent validation and meta-analytic synthesis. This study contributes such a dataset, and its findings serve as a cautionary example: a statistically significant group difference does not constitute a biomarker. Only when a measure can accurately classify individuals with high confidence, consistently across diverse populations and settings, does it rise to the level of clinical utility.

In sum, the background of this disclosure is rooted in a decade of conflicting literature on fatty acid biomarkers in autism spectrum disorder, a field that has yet to deliver a single validated diagnostic tool despite considerable investment and enthusiasm. The present disclosure resolves this ambiguity by demonstrating, through comprehensive, individual-level analysis of a large, well-controlled cohort, that erythrocyte membrane fatty acid profiles do not possess the discriminatory power necessary to serve as a biomarker for autism spectrum disorder. This conclusion is not merely negative; it is constructive. It redirects the field away from an unproductive line of inquiry and toward more promising avenues of biological investigation, while simultaneously establishing a methodological benchmark for future biomarker studies: diagnostic utility must be measured by classification accuracy, not group mean differences.

## SUMMARY OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present disclosure provides a comprehensive and definitive assessment of the diagnostic potential of erythrocyte membrane fatty acid profiles in autism spectrum disorder, concluding that such profiles are not suitable for use as a biomarker to distinguish individuals with autism spectrum disorder from neurotypical controls. This conclusion is based on an extensive analysis of a large, well-characterized cohort of participants, employing rigorous statistical methods that prioritize individual-level classification performance over group-level mean comparisons. The disclosure demonstrates that while minor differences in the average concentrations of certain fatty acids may exist between groups, these differences are not sufficient to enable accurate, reliable, or clinically meaningful classification of any individual based on their fatty acid profile.

The core finding of this disclosure is that the distributions of all measured fatty acids—including arachidonic acid, docosahexaenoic acid, eicosapentaenoic acid, dihomo-gamma-linolenic acid, linoleic acid, oleic acid, palmitic acid, stearic acid, and their respective ratios such as DHA/AA, EPA/AA, and n-3/n-6—exhibit substantial overlap between individuals with autism spectrum disorder and neurotypical controls. This overlap is quantified using the C-statistic, a metric that measures the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve and reflects the probability that a randomly selected individual from one group will have a higher biomarker value than a randomly selected individual from the other group. For every individual fatty acid and every composite ratio, the C-statistic was found to be no greater than 0.62, with the majority of values clustering near 0.50, which corresponds to random chance. This indicates that no single fatty acid or combination of fatty acids can be used to reliably classify an individual as having autism spectrum disorder or being neurotypical.

The disclosure further demonstrates that multivariate modeling techniques, including Fisher discriminant analysis, applied to all measured fatty acids simultaneously, do not improve classification performance to a clinically acceptable level. The resulting multivariate classifier achieved a C-statistic of 0.76, which, while statistically higher than univariate measures, remains insufficient for diagnostic use. A C-statistic of 0.76 corresponds to a sensitivity and specificity profile that would result in an unacceptably high rate of false positives and false negatives in a real-world clinical setting. Moreover, the disclosure explicitly notes that no cross-validation or external validation was performed, as the fitting performance was already so poor that any attempt at validation would inevitably yield inferior results. This underscores the conclusion that the fatty acid profiles under investigation lack the intrinsic discriminatory power required for diagnostic biomarker development.

The disclosure also evaluates the relationship between fatty acid concentrations and dietary intake, particularly seafood consumption, which is the primary source of long-chain omega-3 fatty acids in the Western diet. Regression analyses revealed that seafood intake was modestly correlated with increased levels of docosahexaenoic acid and eicosapentaenoic acid in both autism spectrum disorder and neurotypical groups, confirming that dietary factors significantly influence fatty acid profiles. However, these correlations were not stronger in one group than the other, and no interaction was observed between diagnosis and dietary intake that would suggest a pathological disruption in fatty acid metabolism specific to autism spectrum disorder. This finding further supports the conclusion that observed differences in fatty acid concentrations are more likely attributable to environmental or behavioral factors than to an intrinsic neurobiological abnormality associated with autism spectrum disorder.

Additionally, the disclosure examines the relationship between fatty acid profiles and measures of autism severity, including standardized behavioral assessments of social communication, repetitive behaviors, and overall functioning. Using partial least squares regression and its nonlinear extension, kernel partial least squares, the disclosure finds that no combination of fatty acids shows a statistically significant or clinically meaningful correlation with severity scores. The R² values for all models were uniformly low, indicating that fatty acid concentrations explain negligible variance in behavioral outcomes. This reinforces the conclusion that fatty acid profiles are not reflective of the underlying neurodevelopmental pathology of autism spectrum disorder, nor do they serve as a proxy for symptom intensity or functional impairment.

The disclosure further addresses the methodological shortcomings of prior studies by emphasizing the critical distinction between statistical significance at the group level and diagnostic utility at the individual level. Many previous reports have claimed that fatty acid profiles are promising biomarkers based on p-values derived from group mean comparisons. However, the present disclosure demonstrates that even when such p-values are statistically significant—such as the p-value of 0.03 observed for dihomo-gamma-linolenic acid—the resulting C-statistic remains too low to be useful for classification. This discrepancy arises because group mean differences can be driven by small shifts in central tendency, even when the distributions are highly overlapping. The disclosure establishes that such findings, while mathematically valid, are biologically irrelevant for diagnostic purposes.

The disclosure also contributes a publicly available dataset of individual-level fatty acid measurements, including demographic information, dietary intake records, and behavioral assessments, which can serve as a reference for future research. By making this data available, the disclosure enables independent validation, meta-analysis, and the development of alternative analytical approaches. This transparency is presented as a model for future biomarker research in autism spectrum disorder, where the culture of publishing only positive results and withholding raw data has historically impeded progress.

In sum, the present disclosure definitively establishes that erythrocyte membrane fatty acid profiles, as measured by standard clinical laboratory methods, are not a viable biomarker for autism spectrum disorder. The disclosure does not claim that fatty acids are unimportant in neurodevelopment or that dietary interventions are without potential benefit; rather, it asserts that the specific profiles of these fatty acids in red blood cell membranes do not differ in a manner that can be used to diagnose autism spectrum disorder. This conclusion has significant implications for research funding, clinical trial design, and public health policy, as it redirects attention away from an unproductive line of inquiry and toward more promising biological domains. The disclosure provides a clear, evidence-based endpoint to a decade of conflicting literature and establishes a new standard for biomarker validation: classification accuracy, not group mean differences, must be the criterion for diagnostic utility.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present disclosure provides a detailed and comprehensive characterization of the fatty acid composition of erythrocyte membranes in individuals with autism spectrum disorder and neurotypical controls, with the explicit purpose of evaluating whether these profiles can serve as a diagnostic biomarker. The methodology employed in this disclosure is designed to eliminate the methodological limitations that have plagued prior studies, ensuring that conclusions are grounded in individual-level classification performance rather than group-level statistical significance. The disclosure describes the study population, the laboratory procedures for fatty acid quantification, the statistical framework for analysis, and the interpretation of results in a manner that is fully reproducible and scientifically rigorous.

The study population consisted of 63 individuals diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder and 49 neurotypical controls, all recruited as part of a larger 12-month dietary intervention study. Participants were selected based on strict inclusion and exclusion criteria designed to minimize confounding variables. All participants had not consumed any nutritional supplements, including fish oil, flaxseed, or other fatty acid-containing products, for at least two months prior to sample collection. Additionally, participants were excluded if they were on any restrictive diets, such as gluten-free, casein-free, or ketogenic diets, that could significantly alter lipid metabolism. The age range of participants spanned from 3 to 18 years, with a median age of 9.7 years in the autism spectrum disorder group and 10.0 years in the neurotypical group. Gender distribution was balanced between groups, with no statistically significant difference in sex ratio. All participants were recruited from the same geographic region, minimizing regional dietary variation. Caregivers provided detailed dietary logs, including the number of seafood meals consumed per month, which was used as a covariate in subsequent analyses.

Erythrocyte samples were collected via venipuncture into EDTA-containing tubes and processed within four hours of collection to prevent degradation. Red blood cells were isolated by centrifugation, washed three times with isotonic saline, and then frozen at −80°C until analysis. Fatty acid quantification was performed by a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments-certified commercial laboratory using standardized protocols. Red blood cell membranes were lysed, and lipids were extracted using a modified Folch method. Fatty acids were then converted to their methyl ester derivatives via base-catalyzed transesterification and analyzed by gas chromatography with flame ionization detection. All fatty acid concentrations were normalized to the total fatty acid content of the sample to account for variations in overall lipid concentration, ensuring that reported values reflect relative membrane composition rather than absolute abundance. The following fatty acids were quantified and included in the analysis: arachidonic acid (20:4n-6), dihomo-gamma-linolenic acid (20:3n-6), docosahexaenoic acid (22:6n-3), eicosapentaenoic acid (20:5n-3), linoleic acid (18:2n-6), oleic acid (18:1n-9), palmitic acid (16:0), stearic acid (18:0), palmitoleic acid (16:1n-7), palmitelaidic acid (16:1n-9 trans), elaidic acid (18:1n-9 trans), and the composite ratios DHA/AA, EPA/AA, n-3/n-6, and total polyunsaturated fatty acids.

Statistical analysis was conducted in two distinct phases: univariate and multivariate. In the univariate phase, the normality of each fatty acid distribution was assessed using the Anderson-Darling test, with a significance threshold of 0.05. Distributions that passed the normality test were compared using the F-test for equal variances, followed by either a Student’s t-test or Welch’s t-test depending on variance homogeneity. Distributions that failed the normality test were evaluated using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test to determine whether the shapes of the distributions were statistically equivalent. When distribution shapes were not significantly different, the Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare medians; when shapes differed, Welch’s t-test was applied to compare means. All tests were performed using MATLAB, and p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate at 10%. The probability density functions of each fatty acid were visualized using kernel density estimation, with bandwidth selected via Silverman’s rule of thumb to ensure consistent smoothing across all variables.

Classification performance for each fatty acid was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis. The C-statistic, defined as the area under the ROC curve, was calculated for each fatty acid and each composite ratio. A C-statistic of 0.50 indicates no discriminatory power, equivalent to random guessing, while a value of 1.00 indicates perfect classification. The disclosure reports that no fatty acid achieved a C-statistic greater than 0.62, with the majority of values falling between 0.50 and 0.55. For example, arachidonic acid exhibited a C-statistic of 0.51, docosahexaenoic acid 0.50, eicosapentaenoic acid 0.51, and dihomo-gamma-linolenic acid 0.62—the highest observed value. The 95% confidence intervals for all C-statistics included 0.50, confirming that none of the individual fatty acids provided statistically significant classification ability beyond chance.

In the multivariate phase, Fisher discriminant analysis was employed to determine whether a linear combination of all measured fatty acids could improve classification performance. All 14 variables—12 individual fatty acids and 2 composite ratios—were included as input features. The analysis was performed using the entire dataset without dimensionality reduction, regularization, or feature selection, to avoid biasing the results toward overfitting. The resulting discriminant scores were then used to generate probability density functions for each group, and the C-statistic for the multivariate model was computed. The resulting C-statistic was 0.76, indicating a modest improvement over univariate measures. However, this value was interpreted in the context of clinical applicability: a C-statistic of 0.76 corresponds to a sensitivity of approximately 65% and a specificity of approximately 70% at the optimal threshold, which would result in one in three individuals being misclassified. In a diagnostic setting, such performance is unacceptable, particularly for a condition as heterogeneous and high-stakes as autism spectrum disorder, where false negatives delay critical early intervention and false positives lead to unnecessary anxiety, testing, and potential misallocation of resources.

To further evaluate the biological relevance of the findings, the disclosure performed regression analyses between fatty acid concentrations and measures of autism severity. Twelve standardized behavioral assessments were used, including the Social Communication Questionnaire, the Repetitive Behavior Scale, the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales, and the Autism Treatment Evaluation Checklist. Partial least squares regression and kernel partial least squares were applied to identify combinations of fatty acids that might predict severity scores. The R² values for all models ranged from 0.01 to 0.12, with none reaching statistical significance after correction for multiple comparisons. This indicates that fatty acid profiles do not correlate with the intensity of core symptoms or overall functional impairment in autism spectrum disorder.

The disclosure also conducted regression analyses to examine the influence of seafood consumption on fatty acid levels. The number of seafood meals per month was recorded for each participant and treated as a continuous variable. Correlation coefficients were calculated for each fatty acid in the autism spectrum disorder group, the neurotypical group, and the combined cohort. Significant positive correlations were observed between seafood intake and docosahexaenoic acid (r = 0.347 in autism spectrum disorder, r = 0.477 in neurotypical), eicosapentaenoic acid (r = 0.400 and 0.320, respectively), and the DHA/AA and EPA/AA ratios. Stearic acid showed a negative correlation with seafood intake in the neurotypical group (r = −0.498), suggesting a possible dietary substitution effect. However, these correlations were not significantly different between the two groups, indicating that the relationship between diet and fatty acid composition is similar in individuals with and without autism spectrum disorder. No interaction effect was detected between diagnosis and seafood intake, further supporting the conclusion that fatty acid profiles are shaped by environmental factors rather than intrinsic neurodevelopmental pathology.

The disclosure includes a direct comparison with prior literature, using digitized data extracted from published figures in two major studies. These data were obtained by manually identifying marker positions in high-resolution scans of published scatter plots and density plots, followed by cross-correlation-based center detection. The extracted data were then analyzed using identical methods as the current study. The results showed that even when prior studies reported statistically significant group differences—for example, lower arachidonic acid in autism spectrum disorder—the C-statistic for classification remained below 0.75, and the distributions exhibited extensive overlap. This demonstrates that the apparent discrepancies in the literature are not due to methodological differences in sample collection or quantification, but rather to the misinterpretation of group-level statistics as diagnostic indicators.

The disclosure further emphasizes that no attempt was made to identify a subset of fatty acids that might yield better classification performance, as such an approach would constitute data dredging and would be inherently prone to overfitting. The disclosure explicitly states that if a classifier using all available variables performs poorly, then a classifier using a subset of those variables will perform no better, and likely worse, due to the loss of information and increased vulnerability to noise. This principle is grounded in the statistical theory of generalization and is a cornerstone of modern machine learning practice.

Finally, the disclosure acknowledges the limitations of the study, including the age range of participants, the lack of longitudinal data, and the absence of genetic or epigenetic profiling. However, it asserts that these limitations do not undermine the central conclusion: that erythrocyte membrane fatty acid profiles, as currently measured, lack the discriminatory power necessary to serve as a diagnostic biomarker for autism spectrum disorder. The disclosure does not preclude the possibility that other lipid species, such as sphingolipids, phospholipid subclasses, or oxidized fatty acid derivatives, may hold diagnostic potential. Nor does it rule out the possibility that fatty acids may play a role in symptom modulation or therapeutic response. It simply concludes that the specific profiles of these fatty acids in red blood cell membranes do not differ in a manner that can be used to diagnose autism spectrum disorder.

## EXAMPLES

The following examples are provided to illustrate the practical application of the methods and findings described in the present disclosure, demonstrating how the conclusions are derived from empirical data and statistical analysis. These examples are not intended to limit the scope of the disclosure but to provide concrete, reproducible instances of the analytical framework that supports the central assertion: that erythrocyte membrane fatty acid profiles cannot serve as a diagnostic biomarker for autism spectrum disorder.

Example 1: Univariate Classification of Arachidonic Acid  
In a cohort of 63 individuals with autism spectrum disorder and 49 neurotypical controls, the concentration of arachidonic acid in erythrocyte membranes was measured and normalized to total fatty acid content. The mean concentration in the autism spectrum disorder group was 20.05% (95% confidence interval: 17.04% to 22.76%), while in the neurotypical group it was 20.16% (18.12% to 22.03%). A Welch’s t-test yielded a p-value of 0.68, indicating no statistically significant difference in mean concentrations between groups. The kernel density estimate of the distribution revealed near-perfect overlap between the two groups, with the peak of each distribution occurring at approximately the same value and the tails extending similarly across the range. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, or C-statistic, was calculated to be 0.51. This value indicates that the probability of correctly classifying a randomly selected individual from the autism spectrum disorder group as having a higher arachidonic acid concentration than a randomly selected neurotypical individual is only 51%, which is indistinguishable from random chance. A diagnostic test with a C-statistic of 0.51 would be clinically useless, as it provides no more information than flipping a coin.

Example 2: Univariate Classification of Docosahexaenoic Acid  
The concentration of docosahexaenoic acid was similarly measured across the same cohort. The median concentration in the autism spectrum disorder group was 3.707% (2.05% to 7.11%), and in the neurotypical group it was 3.816% (2.721% to 5.347%). The distributions were non-normally distributed, as confirmed by the Anderson-Darling test, and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test indicated that the shapes of the distributions were not significantly different. Therefore, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied, yielding a p-value of 1.00, indicating no difference in median concentrations. The kernel density estimate showed complete overlap between the two groups, with no discernible separation in the tails or peaks. The C-statistic for docosahexaenoic acid was 0.50, confirming that this fatty acid provides no discriminatory power whatsoever for distinguishing autism spectrum disorder from neurotypical status. Even in studies that reported statistically significant lower levels of docosahexaenoic acid in autism spectrum disorder, the individual-level distributions, when reconstructed from published figures, showed C-statistics no higher than 0.75, still insufficient for diagnostic use.

Example 3: Multivariate Classification Using Fisher Discriminant Analysis  
All 14 fatty acid variables—12 individual fatty acids and 2 composite ratios—were included in a Fisher discriminant analysis to determine whether a linear combination could improve classification. The analysis produced a single discriminant score for each participant, representing a weighted sum of all fatty acid concentrations. The probability density functions of these scores were then plotted for the two groups. The peak of the autism spectrum disorder distribution was slightly shifted to the left of the neurotypical distribution, but the overlap remained extensive. The C-statistic for this multivariate model was 0.76, corresponding to an estimated sensitivity of 65% and specificity of 70% at the optimal threshold. To put this into clinical context, if this model were applied to a population with a 1 in 44 prevalence of autism spectrum disorder, the positive predictive value would be less than 10%, meaning that 9 out of 10 individuals flagged as positive would be false positives. Such a high rate of false alarms renders the model unsuitable for clinical deployment, regardless of its statistical significance.

Example 4: Correlation Between Seafood Intake and Fatty Acid Levels  
Caregivers reported the number of seafood meals consumed per month by each participant. For individuals with autism spectrum disorder, the median number of meals per month was 2.1, and for neurotypical controls it was 2.3. The distribution of seafood intake was not significantly different between groups. Regression analysis showed that for each additional seafood meal per month, docosahexaenoic acid increased by 0.08% in the autism spectrum disorder group and by 0.11% in the neurotypical group. The correlation coefficient was 0.347 in the autism spectrum disorder group and 0.477 in the neurotypical group, both statistically significant (p < 0.01). However, the difference in slopes between the two groups was not significant (p = 0.23), indicating that the relationship between dietary intake and fatty acid concentration is similar in both populations. This suggests that any observed differences in fatty acid levels between groups are more likely attributable to dietary habits than to a pathological disruption in metabolism.

Example 5: Lack of Correlation with Autism Severity  
Twelve standardized behavioral assessments were used to quantify autism severity. The total score from the Social Communication Questionnaire was regressed against the concentration of eicosapentaenoic acid, docosahexaenoic acid, and their ratio. The resulting R² value was 0.04, indicating that less than 5% of the variance in social communication deficits could be explained by these fatty acid levels. Similar results were obtained for repetitive behaviors, adaptive functioning, and overall autism severity scores. Kernel partial least squares, a nonlinear extension of the model, yielded no improvement in predictive power. The maximum R² achieved across all models was 0.12, which is not clinically meaningful. This confirms that fatty acid profiles do not reflect the intensity of core symptoms and cannot be used to stratify individuals by severity.

Example 6: Comparison with Prior Literature  
Data from two prior studies, Brigandi et al. and Yui et al., were extracted from published figures using image digitization and marker detection algorithms. The arachidonic acid distributions from these studies were reanalyzed using the same methods as the current disclosure. In Brigandi et al., the mean arachidonic acid concentration was reported as significantly lower in autism spectrum disorder (p = 0.02), yet the reconstructed individual-level data yielded a C-statistic of 0.62. In Yui et al., the mean difference was also statistically significant (p = 0.01), but the C-statistic was 0.75. Both values fall below the threshold for clinical utility. This demonstrates that even when prior studies report statistically significant group differences, the individual-level classification performance remains inadequate. The disclosure concludes that these studies, while methodologically sound in their group comparisons, misinterpret their findings as diagnostic indicators.

Example 7: Absence of Diagnostic Threshold  
No cutoff value for any fatty acid or ratio was found to yield a sensitivity and specificity combination acceptable for clinical use. For example, the highest sensitivity achieved for dihomo-gamma-linolenic acid was 68% at a specificity of 55%, resulting in a false positive rate of 45%. No combination of thresholds across multiple fatty acids improved this performance. Receiver operating characteristic curves for all variables were consistently close to the diagonal line, confirming the absence of a diagnostic threshold. This is in stark contrast to validated biomarkers in other fields, such as troponin for myocardial infarction or HbA1c for diabetes, where clear thresholds with high sensitivity and specificity are established and routinely used in clinical decision-making.

Example 8: Implications for Clinical Trials  
The disclosure notes that numerous clinical trials have tested omega-3 fatty acid supplementation as a treatment for autism spectrum disorder, often based on the assumption that individuals with autism spectrum disorder have a deficiency in these fatty acids. The findings of this disclosure directly challenge that assumption. If no consistent difference exists between individuals with autism spectrum disorder and neurotypical controls, then supplementation cannot be rationally targeted to correct a deficiency. Any observed benefits in such trials must be attributed to nonspecific effects, such as anti-inflammatory properties, placebo responses, or changes in gut microbiota, rather than to the correction of a biomarker-defined deficit. This disclosure provides a scientific basis for reevaluating the rationale behind such trials and for redirecting resources toward more promising biological targets.

Example 9: Contribution to Open Science  
The complete dataset used in this disclosure—including individual fatty acid measurements, demographic information, dietary logs, and behavioral scores—is made publicly available as supplementary material. This allows independent researchers to reproduce the analyses, test alternative hypotheses, or apply novel machine learning techniques. The disclosure emphasizes that the lack of access to raw data has been a major barrier to progress in autism biomarker research. By releasing this dataset, the disclosure sets a precedent for transparency and reproducibility in the field.

Example 10: Methodological Standard for Future Biomarker Research  
The disclosure proposes a new standard for evaluating potential biomarkers in autism spectrum disorder: classification performance, as measured by the C-statistic, must be the primary criterion for clinical utility. Group mean differences, p-values, and effect sizes are secondary considerations. Any future study claiming a biomarker must report the C-statistic, the confidence interval around it, and the sensitivity and specificity at the optimal threshold. Without these metrics, a claim of biomarker discovery is scientifically incomplete and clinically misleading. This disclosure provides a template for how such claims should be evaluated, ensuring that future research moves beyond statistical significance toward diagnostic validity.
