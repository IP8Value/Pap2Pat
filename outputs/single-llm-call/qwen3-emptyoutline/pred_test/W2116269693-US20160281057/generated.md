# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to a method for enhancing sperm motility in human males suffering from asthenozoospermia, particularly in the context of assisted reproductive technologies such as in vitro fertilization (IVF) and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI). More specifically, the invention pertains to the use of molecular hydrogen (H₂) as a therapeutic agent to improve the progressive motility of sperm cells by mitigating oxidative stress-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby restoring cellular energy production without altering sperm viability, morphology, or swimming velocity. The method is applicable to both freshly collected and cryopreserved sperm suspensions, and is particularly effective in cases where sperm motility is severely compromised due to prolonged exposure to ambient oxidative conditions, cryoinjury, or underlying metabolic deficiencies. The invention further encompasses the preparation and use of hydrogen-saturated media for the treatment of sperm prior to assisted reproduction procedures, enabling clinical implementation in fertility clinics without requiring complex equipment or prolonged incubation times. The therapeutic effect is achieved through the selective scavenging of cytotoxic hydroxyl radicals by hydrogen gas, which diffuses rapidly across biological membranes and accumulates within mitochondria, thereby preserving mitochondrial membrane potential and sustaining adenosine triphosphate synthesis without interfering with physiologically essential reactive oxygen species such as nitric oxide or superoxide. This approach represents a novel, non-invasive, and biologically safe intervention for male infertility that circumvents the limitations of conventional antioxidant therapies, which often lack specificity, exhibit poor bioavailability, or induce unintended metabolic perturbations.

## BACKGROUND ART

Male infertility due to asthenozoospermia, defined as a reduction in the percentage of sperm exhibiting progressive motility below the threshold established by the World Health Organization, remains one of the most prevalent and clinically challenging conditions in reproductive medicine. Despite extensive research into the etiological factors underlying this phenotype, therapeutic interventions have remained largely empirical and ineffective in reversing the underlying cellular dysfunction. Numerous studies have identified a spectrum of contributing factors, including genetic abnormalities in DNA methylation enzymes such as DNMT3B and MTHFR, mitochondrial DNA haplogroup variations, systemic disorders like polycystic kidney disease, and environmental stressors that induce oxidative damage. Spermatozoa are uniquely vulnerable to oxidative stress due to their high content of polyunsaturated fatty acids in the plasma membrane, their limited capacity for de novo synthesis of antioxidant enzymes, and their minimal ability to repair oxidative DNA lesions. These characteristics render sperm particularly susceptible to lipid peroxidation, protein carbonylation, and mitochondrial membrane depolarization, all of which directly impair flagellar motility and compromise the ability of sperm to undergo capacitation, acrosome reaction, and oocyte fusion.

Historically, attempts to ameliorate asthenozoospermia have focused on the supplementation of exogenous antioxidants, including zinc, aspartic acid, coenzyme Q10, myoinositol, and various xanthine derivatives. While some of these agents have demonstrated modest improvements in semen parameters in clinical trials, their efficacy is often inconsistent, and their mechanisms of action remain poorly understood. Many of these compounds act through broad-spectrum radical scavenging or metal chelation, which may inadvertently disrupt redox signaling pathways essential for normal sperm function. For instance, while coenzyme Q10 supports mitochondrial electron transport, its oral bioavailability is low, and its effects are delayed, requiring weeks of administration to yield measurable outcomes. Similarly, myoinositol has been shown to improve sperm motility in some cohorts, but its mechanism is linked to insulin signaling and phosphatidylinositol metabolism, making its utility limited to patients with metabolic comorbidities. Furthermore, none of these interventions have demonstrated consistent efficacy in the context of cryopreserved sperm, which suffers from additional structural and metabolic insults during freezing and thawing, including ice crystal formation, osmotic shock, and mitochondrial membrane destabilization.

The role of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in sperm physiology presents a paradox: while excessive ROS leads to oxidative damage and motility loss, physiological levels of ROS are indispensable for the regulation of key sperm functions, including capacitation, hyperactivation, and acrosomal exocytosis. Consequently, non-selective antioxidant therapies that indiscriminately neutralize all ROS have often failed to improve fertility outcomes and, in some cases, have even suppressed sperm function. This has led to a growing recognition that an ideal therapeutic agent for asthenozoospermia must selectively target only the most cytotoxic ROS species while leaving physiologically relevant signaling molecules intact. Molecular hydrogen (H₂) has emerged in recent years as a unique candidate for such selective modulation. Unlike conventional antioxidants, hydrogen is the smallest molecule in the universe and possesses exceptional membrane permeability, allowing it to rapidly penetrate cellular and subcellular compartments, including the mitochondrial matrix and the nucleus. Importantly, hydrogen has been shown in multiple preclinical models to react selectively with the hydroxyl radical (•OH), one of the most damaging ROS species, while exhibiting no reactivity toward hydrogen peroxide, superoxide, nitric oxide, or other signaling molecules critical for sperm physiology. This selectivity distinguishes hydrogen from all previously studied antioxidants and positions it as a potentially transformative agent in reproductive medicine.

Prior studies have demonstrated the protective effects of hydrogen in a variety of ischemic and inflammatory conditions, including cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury, myocardial infarction, hepatic toxicity, retinal degeneration, and radiation-induced lung injury. In these contexts, hydrogen has been shown to preserve mitochondrial integrity, reduce apoptosis, and maintain ATP levels without altering cellular metabolism or inducing compensatory stress responses. Notably, hydrogen has also been shown to prevent the decline in mitochondrial ATP synthesis under oxidative stress conditions, suggesting a direct link between its radical-scavenging properties and energy metabolism. However, despite this compelling body of evidence, no prior study has investigated the application of hydrogen to human sperm in the context of assisted reproduction, nor has any prior art described a method for the controlled delivery of hydrogen to sperm suspensions in a clinically viable format. The use of hydrogen gas in gaseous atmospheres for sperm treatment, the preparation of hydrogen-saturated culture media, and the demonstration of sustained motility enhancement following transient hydrogen exposure represent entirely novel approaches that have not been disclosed, suggested, or anticipated in any prior patent or scientific publication.

Moreover, the clinical translation of hydrogen therapy has been hindered by a lack of standardized protocols for its application to reproductive cells. While hydrogen-rich water and hydrogen-dissolved saline have been explored in other medical contexts, their efficacy in sperm preservation is unproven due to the rapid diffusion and volatility of hydrogen in aqueous media. The present invention overcomes these limitations by establishing a reproducible, scalable, and clinically applicable method for exposing sperm to hydrogen gas under controlled atmospheric conditions, as well as by defining the optimal concentration and duration of exposure necessary to elicit a robust and lasting improvement in motility. The invention further introduces the concept of repeated hydrogen exposure to sustain motility enhancement over extended periods, a strategy that has no precedent in the field of andrology or assisted reproduction. The absence of any prior disclosure of hydrogen as a therapeutic agent for sperm motility, the lack of any method for its controlled delivery to sperm suspensions, and the failure of existing antioxidants to provide consistent, rapid, and durable improvements in motility collectively establish the novelty and non-obviousness of the present invention.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

### Object to be Solved by the Invention

The primary object of the present invention is to provide a safe, effective, and clinically practical method for enhancing the progressive motility of human spermatozoa in patients diagnosed with asthenozoospermia, particularly in cases where conventional therapeutic interventions have failed or are unsuitable. A further object is to overcome the limitations of existing antioxidant therapies by selectively neutralizing only the most cytotoxic reactive oxygen species without disrupting the physiological redox signaling necessary for sperm capacitation, hyperactivation, and oocyte fusion. Another object is to restore mitochondrial membrane potential and sustain intracellular ATP production in sperm cells that have been compromised by prolonged oxidative stress, cryopreservation, or metabolic dysfunction, thereby improving their functional competence for use in assisted reproductive techniques. A further object is to provide a method that can be implemented in standard fertility clinic settings without requiring specialized equipment, prolonged incubation periods, or complex handling procedures. An additional object is to enable the repeated application of hydrogen treatment to maintain motility enhancement over extended durations, thereby facilitating multiple procedures such as sequential ICSI cycles or the use of sperm stored for future attempts. Finally, an object of the invention is to provide a treatment modality that does not alter sperm viability, morphology, or swimming velocity, ensuring that the improvement in motility is attributable to enhanced energy metabolism rather than artificial stimulation or hyperactivation, thus preserving the biological integrity of the sperm for optimal fertilization potential.

### Means to Solve the Object

The object of the invention is solved by a method comprising the exposure of human sperm suspensions to a gaseous atmosphere containing molecular hydrogen at a concentration sufficient to saturate the aqueous medium surrounding the sperm, wherein the hydrogen selectively scavenges cytotoxic hydroxyl radicals without affecting physiologically important reactive oxygen species such as nitric oxide or superoxide. The method involves placing a sperm suspension in a sealed chamber and introducing a gas mixture comprising between 20% and 75% molecular hydrogen, with the remainder comprising nitrogen, carbon dioxide, and oxygen in proportions that maintain physiological pH and osmolarity. The sperm are exposed to this atmosphere for a duration of at least 30 minutes, during which time hydrogen diffuses into the sperm cells, accumulates within mitochondria, and reduces oxidative damage to the electron transport chain, thereby preserving mitochondrial membrane potential and sustaining ATP synthesis. Alternatively, the method may involve the preparation of a sperm-wash medium saturated with molecular hydrogen at concentrations ranging from 50% to 100% of its solubility limit in aqueous solution, followed by the incubation of frozen-thawed or freshly processed sperm in this medium for a period of 5 to 30 minutes prior to use in IVF or ICSI procedures. The hydrogen-saturated medium is prepared by bubbling hydrogen gas through the medium under controlled pressure and temperature conditions until equilibrium is achieved, and the medium is then used immediately to wash and resuspend sperm prior to insemination. The method further encompasses the repeated application of hydrogen treatment to sperm suspensions that have been stored for extended periods, wherein a second exposure to hydrogen gas or hydrogen-saturated medium is performed after 24 hours to restore motility to levels approaching those observed after the initial treatment. The invention further provides for the use of hydrogen treatment in combination with standard sperm processing techniques, including density gradient centrifugation, washing in plasma protein fraction-supplemented media, and cryopreservation, without interfering with the efficacy of these procedures. Crucially, the method does not require the addition of exogenous enzymes, cofactors, or metabolic substrates, and does not alter the intrinsic swimming velocity of sperm, ensuring that the improvement in motility reflects enhanced energy availability rather than artificial activation.

### Effect of the Invention

The effect of the invention is the significant and reproducible enhancement of progressive sperm motility in both experimentally damaged and clinically relevant frozen-thawed sperm suspensions, without compromising sperm viability, morphology, or swimming speed. In sperm suspensions that had been left at room temperature for more than five days—conditions that induce severe oxidative stress and reduce motility to less than 5%—treatment with hydrogen gas increased the percentage of progressively motile sperm to over 16%, representing a more than fourfold improvement. This effect was not observed in control suspensions exposed to nitrogen-rich atmospheres, confirming that the improvement is specifically attributable to hydrogen and not to mechanical agitation or changes in gas composition unrelated to hydrogen. In sperm suspensions derived from patients with asthenozoospermia, treatment with hydrogen-saturated wash media increased motility by up to 50% in samples with initially low motility, while having no adverse effect on samples with normal motility. Importantly, the enhancement of motility was sustained for at least two and a half hours after the removal of hydrogen, indicating that the treatment induces a lasting metabolic change rather than a transient physical effect. The improvement in motility was directly correlated with an increase in mitochondrial membrane potential, as evidenced by elevated fluorescence of the potential-sensitive dye TMRM, and was not accompanied by changes in sperm viability, as confirmed by propidium iodide and Hoechst staining. Furthermore, the treatment did not alter sperm swimming velocity, demonstrating that the effect is not due to hyperactivation or artificial stimulation, but rather to the restoration of endogenous energy production. The method is applicable to both fresh and cryopreserved sperm, making it suitable for routine use in fertility clinics, and can be repeated without diminishing efficacy or inducing cellular toxicity. The invention thus provides a novel, non-invasive, and biologically safe intervention that directly addresses the root cause of asthenozoospermia—mitochondrial dysfunction due to oxidative stress—without the drawbacks of conventional antioxidant therapies. The ability to enhance motility rapidly, sustainably, and selectively renders this method uniquely suited for integration into IVF and ICSI workflows, where the availability of motile sperm is a critical determinant of success.

## MODE OF CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION

### EXAMPLES

In the following examples, the method of the invention is described in detail, including the preparation of sperm suspensions, the exposure to hydrogen gas, the preparation of hydrogen-saturated media, the assessment of motility and mitochondrial function, and the application of repeated treatments. All procedures were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions in accordance with ethical guidelines and institutional review board approvals.

Example 1: Preparation of Experimentally Damaged Sperm Suspensions  
Sperm suspensions were obtained from patients undergoing in vitro fertilization or intracytoplasmic sperm injection at the Yamashita Shonan Yume Clinic. Semen samples were collected after a period of 3 to 5 days of ejaculatory abstinence, and sperm parameters were assessed according to World Health Organization 2010 criteria. Samples exhibiting oligozoospermia, hypospermia, or asthenozoospermia were selected for the study. Semen was washed twice in SAGE cleavage medium supplemented with 10% plasma protein fraction to remove seminal plasma, followed by centrifugation through a two-layer Percoll® density gradient at 600 × g for 15 minutes. The resulting pellet was resuspended in cleavage medium with 10% plasma protein fraction to a concentration of approximately 10 million sperm per milliliter. These suspensions were then incubated in standard culture dishes at room temperature in ambient air for more than five days to induce severe oxidative damage. After this period, the forward motility rate of the sperm was measured and found to be consistently below 5%, with an average of 3.6% across 35 independent suspensions. These suspensions were designated as experimentally damaged and were used for subsequent hydrogen treatment experiments.

Example 2: Exposure of Damaged Sperm to Hydrogen Gas  
Each experimentally damaged sperm suspension was divided into three groups: untreated control, hydrogen-treated, and nitrogen-treated. Each group consisted of 100 microliters of sperm suspension placed in separate 35-mm culture dishes. The dishes were then sealed within a custom-built exposure chamber with a volume of approximately five liters. The chamber was flushed with a gas mixture containing 5% carbon dioxide, 20% oxygen, 50% hydrogen, and 25% nitrogen at a flow rate of one liter per minute for five minutes to achieve saturation. A second group of suspensions was exposed to a control gas mixture containing 5% carbon dioxide, 20% oxygen, and 75% nitrogen under identical conditions. Gas concentrations within the chamber were continuously monitored using needle-type sensors for hydrogen and oxygen (Unisense, Aarhus N, Denmark) to confirm that equilibrium was reached and maintained throughout the exposure period. After 40 minutes of exposure, the sperm suspensions were removed from the chamber, gently mixed by pipetting, and a 5–10 microliter aliquot was placed in a counting chamber for visual assessment of motility. Forward motility, non-forward motility, and immobility rates were determined by manual counting of at least 200 sperm per sample, with each measurement repeated three times. The results showed a statistically significant increase in forward motility from 3.6% to 16.8% in the hydrogen-treated group, whereas the nitrogen-treated group showed no significant change (7.7%). The pH of all suspensions remained stable at 7.3 ± 0.1, confirming that the effect was not due to acid-base shifts.

Example 3: Optimization of Exposure Duration  
To determine the minimum effective exposure time, a separate set of 12 sperm suspensions with moderately reduced motility (average forward motility of 12.1%) was exposed to the hydrogen-rich gas mixture for durations of 5, 30, and 60 minutes. After each exposure, motility was assessed as described above. The 5-minute exposure resulted in no significant change in motility (12.4% vs. 12.1%), while the 30-minute exposure increased motility to 21.3%, and the 60-minute exposure yielded a similar increase (20.8%). These results demonstrated that 30 minutes of exposure is sufficient to achieve maximal motility enhancement, and that longer exposures do not confer additional benefit. The 30-minute duration was therefore adopted as the standard protocol for all subsequent experiments.

Example 4: Effect of Sperm Viability on Hydrogen Treatment Response  
To assess whether the efficacy of hydrogen treatment depends on sperm viability, 30 additional sperm suspensions were stained with propidium iodide and Hoechst 33342 to quantify viable (PI-negative) sperm. The suspensions were divided into two groups based on viability: high viability (>20% viable sperm, n = 17) and low viability (<20% viable sperm, n = 13). In the high-viability group, hydrogen treatment increased forward motility from an average of 3.0% to 11.0%, with 16 out of 17 suspensions showing a significant improvement. In contrast, the low-viability group exhibited no measurable improvement in motility, with the majority of sperm remaining immotile. However, in three of the 13 suspensions, a very sluggish, pendular movement of the sperm head was observed following hydrogen exposure, suggesting that even in severely compromised sperm, hydrogen may partially restore minimal motility in a subset of cells. These findings indicate that hydrogen treatment is most effective in sperm populations retaining a baseline level of viability, but may still exert a subtle beneficial effect even in nearly necrotic suspensions.

Example 5: Assessment of Sperm Swimming Velocity  
To determine whether hydrogen treatment induced hyperactivation or altered flagellar beating patterns, time-lapse microscopy was performed on 10 randomly selected sperm suspensions from the high-viability group. Sperm were recorded for 10 seconds using an Olympus microscope, and the trajectories were analyzed using ImageJ with the CASA plugin. The average swimming velocity, measured as micrometers per second, was calculated for each sample before and after hydrogen exposure. No statistically significant difference was observed between control and hydrogen-treated groups, with mean velocities remaining constant at approximately 42 micrometers per second. This confirmed that the improvement in motility was not due to increased speed or altered flagellar dynamics, but rather to an increase in the proportion of sperm exhibiting progressive movement, consistent with restored energy metabolism rather than artificial stimulation.

Example 6: Duration of Motility Enhancement  
To evaluate the persistence of the hydrogen-induced motility enhancement, 12 sperm suspensions treated with hydrogen gas for 30 minutes were transferred to 25°C ambient air and monitored for motility over time. Motility was measured at 0, 30, 60, 120, and 150 minutes post-treatment. The enhanced motility remained significantly elevated for up to 2.5 hours, at which point it began to decline toward baseline levels. To assess longer-term retention, one suspension was stored at 25°C for 24 hours, during which motility dropped to 36% of its peak value. A second hydrogen treatment was then administered for 30 minutes, resulting in a further increase in motility to 67% of the initial peak, demonstrating that the effect is reversible and repeatable without loss of efficacy. This finding supports the clinical utility of repeated hydrogen treatments in multi-cycle IVF or ICSI protocols.

Example 7: Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Analysis  
To investigate the mechanism underlying the motility enhancement, six sperm suspensions were stained with MitoTracker Green and tetramethylrhodamine methyl ester (TMRM) following 30 minutes of hydrogen or nitrogen exposure. MitoTracker Green labels mitochondria independently of membrane potential, while TMRM fluorescence intensity is proportional to mitochondrial membrane potential. Confocal microscopy revealed that hydrogen-treated sperm exhibited significantly higher TMRM fluorescence compared to untreated or nitrogen-treated controls, with no change in MitoTracker Green signal, indicating that mitochondrial mass was unchanged but membrane potential was enhanced. Quantitative analysis using ImageJ confirmed a 2.3-fold increase in TMRM fluorescence intensity in hydrogen-treated sperm (p < 0.001). These results strongly suggest that hydrogen preserves mitochondrial function by reducing oxidative damage to the electron transport chain, thereby maintaining the proton gradient necessary for ATP synthesis.

Example 8: Treatment of Frozen-Thawed Sperm with Hydrogen-Saturated Medium  
Frozen-thawed sperm suspensions were prepared from 21 patients undergoing cryopreservation for future IVF or ICSI cycles. Sperm were mixed in equal volumes with TEST-yolk buffer and cryopreserved using standard protocols involving nitrogen vapor exposure and storage in liquid nitrogen. Upon thawing at 37°C for five minutes, suspensions were divided into four aliquots and washed for five minutes in sperm-wash media containing 0%, 50%, 75%, or 100% hydrogen saturation. Hydrogen-saturated media were prepared by bubbling pure hydrogen gas through cleavage medium equilibrated with 5% carbon dioxide for 30 minutes under constant stirring, followed by immediate use. Motility was assessed before and after washing. In the group with low baseline motility (<50%), treatment with 50% hydrogen-saturated medium increased forward motility from 27.5% to 41.2% (p < 0.05), while 75% saturation yielded a similar improvement (40.8%), and 100% saturation produced no significant benefit. In the high-motility group (≥50%), 75% hydrogen saturation significantly improved motility from 61.3% to 70.1%, whereas 50% and 100% saturation had no effect. These results indicate that there is an optimal concentration window for hydrogen efficacy, with excessive saturation potentially reducing oxygen availability and impairing sperm function. The findings further demonstrate that hydrogen treatment is effective in cryopreserved sperm, a population notoriously resistant to conventional therapies.

Example 9: Clinical Application Protocol  
A clinical protocol was developed for the routine use of hydrogen treatment in IVF and ICSI procedures. Upon collection of a semen sample, sperm are processed using standard density gradient centrifugation and washed in cleavage medium with plasma protein fraction. If the sample exhibits asthenozoospermia (progressive motility <32%), the sperm suspension is transferred to a sealed chamber and exposed to a gas mixture of 50% hydrogen, 20% oxygen, 5% carbon dioxide, and 25% nitrogen for 30 minutes. Following exposure, the sperm are washed once in standard medium and prepared for insemination. Alternatively, for cryopreserved sperm, the thawed suspension is washed in hydrogen-saturated medium (75% saturation) for five minutes prior to ICSI. In cases where sperm are stored for multiple cycles, a second hydrogen treatment is administered after 24 hours of storage at 4°C to restore motility. No adverse effects on fertilization rates, embryo development, or pregnancy outcomes have been observed in preliminary clinical trials, and no changes in sperm DNA fragmentation or chromatin integrity have been detected following hydrogen exposure.

Example 10: Reproducibility and Safety Assessment  
To assess the reproducibility of the method, 50 independent sperm suspensions from different patients were treated using the standardized protocol described above. In 47 of the 50 suspensions, a statistically significant increase in progressive motility was observed, with an average improvement of 14.2%. In the remaining three suspensions, no change was detected, and these were found to contain sperm with near-complete necrosis and absent mitochondrial staining. No cytotoxic effects, morphological abnormalities, or changes in DNA integrity were observed in any of the treated samples. Furthermore, hydrogen treatment did not interfere with the ability of sperm to undergo capacitation or acrosome reaction in vitro, and no increase in reactive oxygen species was detected following treatment. These findings confirm that the method is safe, reproducible, and broadly applicable across diverse patient populations.

## INDUSTRIAL APPLICABILITY

The method described herein has substantial industrial applicability in the field of assisted reproductive technology, particularly within fertility clinics, cryobanks, and reproductive research laboratories. The invention provides a simple, cost-effective, and scalable intervention that can be readily integrated into existing clinical workflows for in vitro fertilization and intracytoplasmic sperm injection. The equipment required for hydrogen gas exposure—such as gas mixing chambers, flow controllers, and gas sensors—is commercially available and commonly used in cell culture and embryology laboratories. The preparation of hydrogen-saturated media requires only a hydrogen gas cylinder, a gas diffusion apparatus, and standard laboratory media, all of which are routinely available in clinical settings. The treatment protocol requires no specialized training beyond standard sperm handling procedures and can be completed in less than one hour, making it suitable for high-throughput application. The invention eliminates the need for expensive and inconsistent antioxidant supplements, which often require prolonged administration and exhibit variable bioavailability. Furthermore, the method is applicable to both fresh and cryopreserved sperm, expanding its utility to sperm banking, donor sperm programs, and fertility preservation for cancer patients. The ability to repeat hydrogen treatment without diminishing efficacy or inducing cellular damage enables its use in multi-cycle IVF protocols, where sperm quality may decline over time in storage. The safety profile of hydrogen, which has been demonstrated in numerous human clinical trials for other indications, further supports its translation into routine clinical practice. The invention therefore represents a commercially viable, patentable, and industrially scalable solution to a major unmet need in male infertility treatment.

## EXPLANATION OF LETTERS OR NUMERALS

In the context of this patent application, the following letters and numerals are used to denote specific components, parameters, or conditions relevant to the method of the invention. The term “H₂” refers to molecular hydrogen, the active therapeutic agent used to enhance sperm motility. The term “•OH” denotes the hydroxyl radical, the cytotoxic reactive oxygen species selectively scavenged by hydrogen. The term “TMRM” refers to tetramethylrhodamine methyl ester, a fluorescent dye used to measure mitochondrial membrane potential. The term “MTG” refers to MitoTracker Green, a mitochondrial marker that stains mitochondrial mass independently of membrane potential. The term “PI” denotes propidium iodide, a membrane-impermeant dye used to identify non-viable sperm. The term “CASA” refers to computer-assisted sperm analysis, a software-based system used to quantify sperm motility parameters. The term “ICSI” denotes intracytoplasmic sperm injection, a clinical procedure in which a single sperm is injected directly into an oocyte. The term “IVF” refers to in vitro fertilization, the process by which sperm and oocytes are combined in a culture dish to achieve fertilization. The term “PPF” refers to plasma protein fraction, a supplement used in sperm washing media to stabilize sperm membranes. The term “Percoll®” denotes a density gradient medium used for sperm purification. The term “SAGE cleavage medium” refers to a commercial culture medium specifically formulated for human embryo and sperm culture. The term “TEST-yolk buffer” refers to a cryopreservation medium containing egg yolk and other cryoprotectants. The term “% H₂” denotes the concentration of hydrogen gas in a gaseous mixture, expressed as a percentage by volume. The term “% saturation” refers to the concentration of dissolved hydrogen in an aqueous medium relative to its maximum solubility under given temperature and pressure conditions. The term “n” refers to the number of independent sperm suspensions or patient samples used in an experiment. The term “CI” refers to confidence interval, used to express the precision of motility measurements. The term “SD” denotes standard deviation, a measure of variability in experimental data. The term “ANOVA” refers to analysis of variance, a statistical method used to compare means across multiple groups. The term “p < 0.05” indicates statistical significance at the 5% level. All other letters and numerals used in the description are standard scientific notation consistent with established biological and chemical conventions.
