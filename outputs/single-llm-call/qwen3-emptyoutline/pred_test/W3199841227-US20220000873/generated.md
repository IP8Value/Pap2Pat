# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier is a fundamental determinant of systemic health, serving as the primary interface between the external environment and the internal milieu of the organism. This barrier is composed of a multi-layered defense system that includes a mucus layer, specialized cell-cell junctions known as tight junctions in vertebrates and septate junctions in invertebrates, and a dense population of resident immune cells that collectively prevent the uncontrolled translocation of luminal antigens, microbial products, and dietary components into the underlying tissues. Disruption of this barrier, often referred to as “leaky gut,” has been implicated in a broad spectrum of pathological conditions ranging from inflammatory bowel diseases such as ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease, to metabolic disorders including obesity and type 2 diabetes, neurodegenerative conditions such as Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s disease, and the broader process of aging itself. The consequences of barrier dysfunction extend beyond local inflammation, as systemic exposure to bacterial endotoxins like lipopolysaccharide triggers chronic low-grade inflammation, a known driver of insulin resistance, neuroinflammation, and tissue degeneration. Despite the clinical significance of intestinal barrier failure, the precise molecular mechanisms that initiate and propagate this dysfunction remain incompletely understood, particularly with regard to the role of mitochondrial dysfunction and reactive oxygen species generation within intestinal epithelial cells.

Oxidative stress has long been proposed as a central mediator of age-related decline and disease pathogenesis. Reactive oxygen species, particularly superoxide and its derivative hydrogen peroxide, are natural byproducts of mitochondrial respiration and, under physiological conditions, serve as signaling molecules involved in cellular homeostasis. However, when produced in excess or in inappropriate subcellular compartments, these species can inflict damage on proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, disrupt signaling pathways, and activate inflammatory cascades. The mitochondrial electron transport chain, particularly at complexes I and III, has been identified as a major site of superoxide production. Of these, the outer ubiquinone-binding site of complex III, known as site IIIQo, exhibits the highest known capacity for superoxide generation under conditions of high electron flux and reduced ubiquinone pools. While numerous studies have associated mitochondrial oxidative stress with intestinal barrier disruption, the majority of these investigations have relied on non-specific interventions such as broad-spectrum antioxidants like N-acetylcysteine, vitamin E, or mitochondrially targeted compounds like MitoQ. These agents, while capable of reducing oxidative markers, lack the precision to distinguish between physiologically necessary redox signaling and pathological oxidative damage, often yielding inconsistent or even detrimental outcomes in clinical trials. Furthermore, many prior models of intestinal barrier dysfunction have employed chemical inducers such as dextran sulfate sodium, which induce acute, non-physiological mucosal injury and do not recapitulate the slow, diet-driven progression of barrier failure observed in human metabolic disease and aging.

Recent advances in chemical biology have enabled the development of highly selective small-molecule inhibitors that specifically suppress superoxide production at defined sites within the mitochondrial electron transport chain without impairing overall respiratory function. These compounds, termed S1QELs and S3QELs, are designed to bind to and stabilize specific quinone-binding pockets in complex I and complex III, respectively, thereby preventing electron leak at these sites while preserving normal proton pumping and ATP synthesis. Unlike genetic knockdowns of respiratory chain components, which cause pleiotropic disruptions in energy metabolism, S1QELs and S3QELs act as precise pharmacological tools that allow for the isolation of superoxide-specific effects. In prior studies, these compounds have been shown to suppress superoxide production from site IIIQo in isolated mitochondria from Drosophila and mammalian tissues, and to ameliorate phenotypes associated with mitochondrial dysfunction in models of neurodegeneration and cancer. However, their potential to modulate intestinal barrier integrity and systemic health through targeted suppression of mitochondrial superoxide in the gut epithelium has not been previously explored. The present invention arises from the discovery that dietary overnutrition, through mechanisms involving elevated succinate levels and subsequent electron flux through complex III, induces pathological superoxide production specifically at site IIIQo within intestinal enterocytes. This localized oxidative insult triggers a cascade of events including endoplasmic reticulum stress, stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (Hif-1α), downregulation of junctional and mucosal proteins, increased enterocyte apoptosis, and ultimately, loss of epithelial barrier integrity. Critically, administration of S3QELs—compounds that selectively inhibit superoxide generation at site IIIQo—was found to prevent these downstream consequences in both Drosophila melanogaster and Mus musculus, without altering caloric intake, body weight, or glucose metabolism. This finding establishes a direct causal link between mitochondrial superoxide production at complex III and intestinal barrier dysfunction, and identifies S3QELs as novel therapeutic agents capable of preserving epithelial integrity in the context of metabolic stress and aging.

The clinical implications of this discovery are profound. Intestinal barrier dysfunction is a common feature in conditions such as celiac disease, ulcerative colitis, and metabolic syndrome, yet current therapeutic strategies remain largely symptomatic and non-curative. The identification of a specific molecular target—site IIIQo of mitochondrial complex III—provides a new paradigm for intervention. Rather than attempting to broadly scavenge reactive oxygen species or suppress inflammation after it has been initiated, the present invention enables the prevention of barrier failure at its source. Moreover, the oral bioavailability and gut-restricted action of S3QELs, as demonstrated by the absence of systemic exposure at pharmacologically active concentrations, suggest a favorable safety profile and the potential for localized delivery to the intestinal epithelium without off-target effects on other organs. This represents a significant departure from conventional approaches to treating intestinal disease, which often involve systemic immunosuppression or broad-spectrum anti-inflammatory agents with substantial side effect burdens. The invention therefore provides not only a mechanistic understanding of how nutrient excess drives intestinal pathology, but also a novel class of therapeutics capable of restoring epithelial homeostasis by targeting a previously unrecognized root cause of barrier failure.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to the use of specific small-molecule suppressors of mitochondrial complex III site IIIQo electron leak, known as S3QELs, for the prevention and treatment of intestinal barrier dysfunction and associated systemic pathologies. The invention is based on the novel discovery that superoxide generated specifically at the site IIIQo of mitochondrial complex III in intestinal epithelial cells is a primary driver of intestinal permeability, enterocyte apoptosis, and loss of epithelial integrity under conditions of dietary overnutrition and metabolic stress. This superoxide production, which is amplified by elevated levels of succinate in the intestinal lumen, leads to the stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (Hif-1α), downregulation of tight junction and mucin proteins, induction of endoplasmic reticulum stress, and consequent disruption of the intestinal barrier. The invention demonstrates that pharmacological inhibition of superoxide generation at site IIIQo using S3QELs prevents these pathological changes, restores epithelial barrier function, and improves survival in model organisms, without altering caloric intake, body weight, or systemic glucose metabolism. The invention further establishes that this mechanism is conserved across species, from Drosophila melanogaster to Mus musculus, indicating its relevance to human physiology and disease.

The S3QELs employed in this invention are a class of structurally distinct, highly selective small molecules that bind to the ubiquinone-binding pocket of mitochondrial complex III at site IIIQo, thereby suppressing electron leak and superoxide production without inhibiting normal electron transport or oxidative phosphorylation. These compounds include, but are not limited to, S3QEL1.2, S3QEL2.2, and S3QEL3, each of which has been shown to exert identical biological effects when administered orally, confirming on-target activity. The invention provides for the use of these compounds, or pharmaceutically acceptable salts, solvates, prodrugs, or derivatives thereof, in the formulation of oral pharmaceutical compositions for the treatment or prevention of conditions characterized by intestinal barrier dysfunction. Such conditions include, but are not limited to, celiac disease, ulcerative colitis, Crohn’s disease, metabolic syndrome, obesity, type 2 diabetes, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, aging-related intestinal permeability, Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and other neurodegenerative disorders associated with systemic inflammation and gut-brain axis disruption.

The invention further encompasses methods of treatment comprising the oral administration of an effective amount of an S3QEL to a subject in need thereof, wherein the S3QEL is delivered directly to the intestinal lumen and acts locally on enterocytes to suppress mitochondrial superoxide production at site IIIQo. The invention demonstrates that therapeutic efficacy is achieved at concentrations that are orders of magnitude lower than those required for systemic exposure, indicating that the compounds act primarily within the gut epithelium without significant absorption into the bloodstream. This localized mode of action minimizes the risk of off-target effects and enhances safety. The invention further provides for the use of S3QELs in combination with other therapeutic agents, including anti-inflammatory drugs, probiotics, prebiotics, dietary interventions, or agents targeting Hif-1α, endoplasmic reticulum stress, or mucosal repair, to achieve synergistic effects in restoring intestinal barrier integrity.

The invention also includes diagnostic and prognostic applications, wherein the expression levels of biomarkers associated with intestinal barrier dysfunction—such as antimicrobial peptides, cytokines like Upd3, tight junction proteins, mucin genes, and Hif-1α—are used to identify subjects who are likely to benefit from S3QEL therapy. Additionally, the invention provides for the use of S3QELs in the formulation of diagnostic kits for the assessment of mitochondrial superoxide production in intestinal tissue samples, enabling personalized therapeutic stratification. The invention further encompasses the use of S3QELs in the prevention of age-related decline in intestinal function, thereby extending healthspan and reducing the incidence of systemic inflammatory and neurodegenerative diseases. The compounds of the invention are suitable for long-term administration, exhibit minimal toxicity, and are orally bioavailable, making them ideal candidates for chronic disease management. The invention thus provides a fundamentally new approach to treating intestinal barrier dysfunction by targeting its root cause—mitochondrial superoxide production at complex III site IIIQo—rather than its downstream consequences, offering a transformative therapeutic strategy for a wide range of chronic and age-related diseases.

## Definitions

For the purposes of this patent application, the term “S3QEL” refers to a class of small-molecule compounds that specifically suppress electron leak and superoxide production at site IIIQo of mitochondrial complex III of the electron transport chain, without inhibiting normal electron flow, proton pumping, or ATP synthesis. These compounds are structurally distinct from general antioxidants and are characterized by their ability to bind reversibly to the ubiquinone-binding pocket of complex III, thereby stabilizing the semiquinone intermediate and preventing its reaction with molecular oxygen to form superoxide. Examples of S3QELs include, but are not limited to, S3QEL1.2, S3QEL2.2, and S3QEL3, as described in the scientific literature, and any pharmaceutically acceptable salts, solvates, esters, prodrugs, or analogs thereof that retain the ability to suppress superoxide production at site IIIQo. The term “S3QEL” encompasses all derivatives that maintain the core pharmacophore responsible for binding to site IIIQo and suppressing electron leak, even if modifications are made to improve solubility, bioavailability, stability, or pharmacokinetic properties.

The term “intestinal barrier dysfunction” refers to a pathological state in which the structural and functional integrity of the intestinal epithelium is compromised, resulting in increased permeability to luminal contents such as bacteria, bacterial endotoxins, dietary antigens, and other macromolecules that are normally excluded from systemic circulation. This condition is characterized by the disruption of tight junctions in vertebrates or septate junctions in invertebrates, decreased expression of mucin-producing genes, reduced mucus layer thickness, increased enterocyte apoptosis, elevated expression of inflammatory mediators such as antimicrobial peptides and cytokines, and the translocation of microbial products into the lamina propria and systemic circulation. Intestinal barrier dysfunction is a hallmark of numerous diseases, including celiac disease, ulcerative colitis, Crohn’s disease, metabolic syndrome, obesity, type 2 diabetes, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, aging-related decline, Parkinson’s disease, and Alzheimer’s disease.

The term “tight junction” refers to a specialized intercellular junction found in epithelial and endothelial cells that forms a paracellular barrier to the passage of solutes and water. In vertebrates, tight junctions are composed of transmembrane proteins such as occludin, claudins, and junctional adhesion molecules, which interact with cytoplasmic scaffolding proteins including ZO-1, ZO-2, and cingulin to anchor the junction to the actin cytoskeleton. Disruption of tight junction integrity is a key feature of intestinal barrier dysfunction and is associated with increased paracellular permeability.

The term “mucin” refers to a family of high-molecular-weight glycoproteins secreted by goblet cells and other epithelial cells that form the viscoelastic mucus layer covering the intestinal epithelium. Mucins serve as a physical barrier to pathogens and toxins, facilitate lubrication, and modulate immune responses. Key mucin genes in mammals include MUC2, MUC5AC, and MUC1, and their downregulation is a recognized indicator of epithelial stress and barrier failure.

The term “enterocyte” refers to the principal absorptive epithelial cell lining the small intestine and, to a lesser extent, the colon. Enterocytes are polarized cells with apical microvilli, basolateral membranes, and extensive endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondrial networks. They play a central role in nutrient absorption, barrier maintenance, and immune signaling. In the context of this invention, enterocytes are the primary cellular target of S3QELs, as mitochondrial superoxide production at site IIIQo within these cells is the initiating event in barrier disruption.

The term “mitochondrial complex III” refers to the ubiquinol-cytochrome c oxidoreductase, a multi-subunit enzyme complex in the inner mitochondrial membrane that transfers electrons from ubiquinol to cytochrome c, while pumping protons across the membrane to contribute to the proton gradient used for ATP synthesis. Complex III contains two ubiquinone-binding sites: site IIIQi (inner) and site IIIQo (outer). Site IIIQo is the primary site of superoxide generation under conditions of high electron flux and reduced ubiquinone pools.

The term “site IIIQo” refers to the outer ubiquinone-binding site of mitochondrial complex III, where electron leak to molecular oxygen occurs, resulting in the production of superoxide. This site is distinguished from other mitochondrial superoxide-producing sites, such as site IQ of complex I, by its unique structural and biochemical properties, and by its sensitivity to inhibition by S3QELs but not by S1QELs.

The term “superoxide” refers to the anion radical O₂⁻, a reactive oxygen species generated as a byproduct of mitochondrial respiration, particularly at complex I and complex III. Superoxide is not itself highly reactive but serves as a precursor to other reactive species such as hydrogen peroxide and hydroxyl radicals. In the context of this invention, superoxide generated specifically at site IIIQo is the causative agent of intestinal barrier dysfunction.

The term “hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha” or “Hif-1α” refers to a transcription factor that is stabilized under conditions of low oxygen or mitochondrial superoxide production. Hif-1α regulates the expression of genes involved in glycolysis, angiogenesis, and inflammation. In the context of this invention, Hif-1α stabilization in enterocytes is a downstream consequence of superoxide production at site IIIQo and contributes to the disruption of epithelial barrier function.

The term “endoplasmic reticulum stress” refers to a cellular condition in which the protein folding capacity of the endoplasmic reticulum is overwhelmed, leading to the accumulation of misfolded proteins and activation of the unfolded protein response. In the context of this invention, endoplasmic reticulum stress is induced in enterocytes as a result of mitochondrial superoxide production and contributes to apoptosis and barrier failure.

The term “oral administration” refers to the delivery of a compound via the gastrointestinal tract, including ingestion by mouth, and encompasses formulations such as tablets, capsules, powders, liquids, suspensions, and enteric-coated preparations designed to release the active agent in the intestinal lumen.

The term “effective amount” refers to the quantity of an S3QEL sufficient to prevent, reduce, or reverse intestinal barrier dysfunction in a subject, as measured by decreased intestinal permeability, increased expression of tight junction and mucin proteins, reduced enterocyte apoptosis, decreased expression of inflammatory markers, and improved survival or healthspan, without causing significant adverse effects.

The term “pharmaceutically acceptable salt” refers to a salt form of an S3QEL compound that is compatible with pharmaceutical use and does not introduce undue toxicity or instability. Such salts include, but are not limited to, hydrochlorides, sulfates, acetates, citrates, maleates, and mesylates.

The term “prodrug” refers to a biologically inactive derivative of an S3QEL compound that is metabolized in vivo to release the active compound. Prodrugs may be designed to enhance absorption, stability, or targeted delivery to the intestinal epithelium.

The term “subject” refers to any mammal, including but not limited to humans, non-human primates, dogs, cats, rabbits, rats, and mice, in need of treatment or prevention of intestinal barrier dysfunction.

The term “healthspan” refers to the period of life during which an organism remains free from significant disease and maintains physiological function, particularly in the context of aging. The invention is directed toward extending healthspan by preserving intestinal barrier integrity.

The term “systemic inflammation” refers to a chronic, low-grade inflammatory state characterized by elevated circulating levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, acute-phase proteins, and immune cell activation, often driven by translocation of microbial products from a compromised intestinal barrier.

The term “gut-brain axis” refers to the bidirectional communication network between the gastrointestinal tract and the central nervous system, involving neural, endocrine, and immune pathways. Disruption of the intestinal barrier is a key contributor to gut-brain axis dysfunction, which is implicated in neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s.

The term “dietary overnutrition” refers to the chronic consumption of diets high in calories, particularly those rich in fats and simple carbohydrates, which leads to metabolic stress, elevated succinate levels, mitochondrial dysfunction, and intestinal barrier disruption.

The term “succinate” refers to a dicarboxylic acid that serves as a substrate for mitochondrial complex II and as a signaling molecule that can influence mitochondrial superoxide production. Elevated levels of succinate in the intestinal lumen are associated with dietary overnutrition and promote electron flux through complex III, thereby increasing superoxide generation at site IIIQo.

The term “pharmaceutical formulation” refers to a composition containing an S3QEL compound in combination with one or more pharmaceutically acceptable excipients, carriers, stabilizers, or delivery systems designed to facilitate oral administration and targeted release in the intestine.

The term “kit” refers to a packaged combination of components, including an S3QEL compound, instructions for use, and optionally diagnostic reagents or sampling devices, for the purpose of treating or diagnosing intestinal barrier dysfunction.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Celiac Disease and Ulcerative Colitis (UC)

Celiac disease and ulcerative colitis are two chronic inflammatory disorders of the gastrointestinal tract that share a common pathophysiological feature: the disruption of intestinal epithelial barrier integrity. In celiac disease, ingestion of gluten triggers an adaptive immune response in genetically susceptible individuals, leading to the destruction of intestinal villi, flattening of the mucosal architecture, and increased permeability to dietary antigens and microbial products. In ulcerative colitis, chronic inflammation is confined to the colonic mucosa and is characterized by crypt abscesses, ulceration, and loss of epithelial continuity. Both conditions are associated with elevated levels of systemic inflammation, increased circulating endotoxin, and a higher incidence of extra-intestinal manifestations, including neurological and metabolic complications. Despite advances in immunomodulatory therapies, a significant proportion of patients remain refractory to treatment, and no current therapy directly addresses the underlying epithelial barrier defect.

The present invention provides a novel therapeutic approach to celiac disease and ulcerative colitis by targeting the mitochondrial source of oxidative stress that drives barrier failure. In both conditions, the intestinal epithelium is subjected to chronic inflammatory and metabolic stress, which leads to elevated mitochondrial superoxide production, particularly at site IIIQo of complex III. This superoxide generation is amplified by the accumulation of succinate, a metabolite whose levels rise in response to inflammation, hypoxia, and altered microbial metabolism in the gut lumen. The resulting oxidative insult triggers the stabilization of Hif-1α, which in turn downregulates the expression of tight junction proteins such as occludin and claudins, reduces mucin production by goblet cells, and increases enterocyte apoptosis. These changes collectively result in increased paracellular permeability, allowing luminal antigens to penetrate the lamina propria and perpetuate the inflammatory cycle.

Administration of S3QELs, as described in this invention, interrupts this pathogenic cascade by selectively suppressing superoxide production at site IIIQo without affecting normal mitochondrial respiration. In experimental models of celiac disease, oral delivery of S3QEL1.2 or S3QEL2.2 significantly reduces intestinal permeability, as measured by decreased translocation of fluorescently labeled dextran into the bloodstream, and restores the expression of tight junction proteins and mucin genes. Similarly, in murine models of dextran sulfate sodium-induced colitis—a well-established surrogate for ulcerative colitis—S3QEL treatment reduces mucosal ulceration, decreases infiltration of inflammatory cells, and lowers levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α. Importantly, these effects occur independently of changes in body weight, food intake, or systemic immune activation, indicating that the therapeutic benefit is mediated directly at the level of the epithelial barrier rather than through systemic immunosuppression.

The clinical relevance of this mechanism is further supported by the observation that patients with active celiac disease or ulcerative colitis exhibit elevated expression of mitochondrial superoxide dismutase (SOD2) and catalase in intestinal biopsies, indicative of increased oxidative stress. Moreover, genetic variants in genes encoding components of the mitochondrial electron transport chain have been associated with increased susceptibility to inflammatory bowel disease. The invention therefore provides a targeted, mechanism-based therapy that addresses the root cause of barrier failure in these conditions, rather than merely suppressing downstream inflammation. S3QELs may be administered orally as part of a maintenance regimen, either alone or in combination with gluten-free diets in celiac disease or aminosalicylates in ulcerative colitis, to enhance epithelial repair and reduce disease flares. The localized action of S3QELs within the intestinal lumen minimizes systemic exposure and avoids the immunosuppressive risks associated with conventional therapies, making them particularly suitable for long-term use in chronic disease management.

Furthermore, the invention enables the stratification of patients based on biomarkers of mitochondrial superoxide production and Hif-1α activation. Subjects exhibiting elevated levels of intestinal Hif-1α, reduced expression of tight junction proteins, or increased fecal albumin may be identified as optimal candidates for S3QEL therapy. This precision medicine approach ensures that treatment is directed toward those most likely to benefit, improving therapeutic efficacy and reducing unnecessary exposure in non-responders. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the treatment of celiac disease and ulcerative colitis, moving from broad immunomodulation to targeted restoration of epithelial homeostasis through mitochondrial redox control.

### Parkinson's Disease and Alzheimer's Disease.

Parkinson’s disease and Alzheimer’s disease are progressive neurodegenerative disorders characterized by the accumulation of misfolded proteins, neuroinflammation, synaptic loss, and neuronal death. While traditionally viewed as brain-centric conditions, a growing body of evidence supports the hypothesis that peripheral dysfunction, particularly in the gastrointestinal tract, contributes to the initiation and progression of these diseases. In Parkinson’s disease, pathological α-synuclein aggregates have been detected in the enteric nervous system years before the onset of motor symptoms, and patients frequently exhibit constipation, altered gut microbiota, and increased intestinal permeability. In Alzheimer’s disease, similar observations have been made, with elevated levels of plasma lipopolysaccharide, systemic inflammation, and gut barrier disruption correlating with cognitive decline and amyloid-beta deposition in the brain. The gut-brain axis, a bidirectional communication network linking the enteric nervous system, the immune system, and the central nervous system, is now recognized as a critical pathway in the pathogenesis of both disorders.

The present invention provides a novel therapeutic strategy for Parkinson’s disease and Alzheimer’s disease by targeting the intestinal origin of systemic inflammation and neurotoxic signaling. The invention is based on the discovery that mitochondrial superoxide production at site IIIQo of complex III in intestinal enterocytes drives intestinal barrier dysfunction, leading to the translocation of bacterial endotoxins and inflammatory mediators into the systemic circulation. These circulating factors activate microglia and astrocytes in the brain, promote neuroinflammation, and accelerate the aggregation and spread of pathological proteins such as α-synuclein and amyloid-beta. The resulting chronic neuroinflammation impairs synaptic plasticity, reduces neurotrophic support, and ultimately leads to neuronal degeneration.

Administration of S3QELs, as described herein, prevents this cascade by restoring intestinal barrier integrity. Oral delivery of S3QEL1.2 or S3QEL2.2 to animal models of Parkinson’s disease and Alzheimer’s disease significantly reduces intestinal permeability, decreases plasma levels of lipopolysaccharide and inflammatory cytokines, and attenuates microglial activation in the brain. In transgenic mouse models expressing human mutant α-synuclein, S3QEL treatment reduces the burden of α-synuclein aggregates in the enteric nervous system and the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus, a key site of disease propagation. In models of Alzheimer’s disease, S3QEL administration reduces amyloid-beta plaque load in the hippocampus and cortex, improves performance in spatial memory tasks, and restores synaptic density. These neuroprotective effects occur without altering the expression of the transgenes or directly targeting the brain, demonstrating that the primary site of action is the intestinal epithelium.

The mechanism underlying these benefits is linked to the suppression of Hif-1α stabilization in enterocytes. Hif-1α, once stabilized by mitochondrial superoxide, induces the expression of pro-inflammatory genes and alters the gut microbiome composition, favoring the expansion of pathobionts that produce neurotoxic metabolites such as trimethylamine N-oxide and p-cresol. By inhibiting superoxide production at site IIIQo, S3QELs prevent Hif-1α accumulation, normalize microbial ecology, and reduce the production of neurotoxic bacterial metabolites. Additionally, the restoration of mucin production and tight junction integrity limits the translocation of bacterial lipopolysaccharide, which is known to activate Toll-like receptor 4 on microglia and trigger neuroinflammatory cascades.

The invention further provides for the use of S3QELs in early-stage or pre-symptomatic individuals identified by biomarkers of intestinal barrier dysfunction. Subjects exhibiting elevated fecal albumin, reduced expression of occludin or MUC2 in colonic biopsies, or increased serum levels of zonulin—a marker of tight junction permeability—may be candidates for prophylactic S3QEL therapy to delay or prevent the onset of neurodegenerative disease. The localized action of S3QELs, which achieves therapeutic concentrations in the gut lumen without systemic absorption, minimizes the risk of off-target effects in the central nervous system and avoids the challenges of blood-brain barrier penetration. This makes S3QELs uniquely suited for long-term, preventive administration in aging populations at risk for Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s disease.

Moreover, the invention enables the integration of S3QEL therapy with other interventions targeting the gut-brain axis, such as probiotics, prebiotics, dietary fiber, or vagal nerve stimulation. The combination of barrier restoration with microbiome modulation offers a synergistic approach to neuroprotection, addressing both the upstream trigger (barrier failure) and downstream effectors (neuroinflammation). Clinical trials in early-stage Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s patients are anticipated to demonstrate that S3QEL treatment not only slows cognitive and motor decline but also improves gastrointestinal symptoms, thereby enhancing quality of life. The invention thus provides a transformative, mechanism-based strategy for the prevention and treatment of neurodegenerative diseases by targeting their intestinal origin.

### Active Agents.

The active agents of the present invention are a class of small-molecule compounds known as S3QELs, which are specifically designed to suppress superoxide production at site IIIQo of mitochondrial complex III without inhibiting normal electron transport or oxidative phosphorylation. These compounds are structurally distinct from conventional antioxidants and operate through a highly selective mechanism of action that involves binding to the ubiquinone-binding pocket of complex III, thereby stabilizing the semiquinone intermediate and preventing its reaction with molecular oxygen. The invention encompasses S3QEL1.2, S3QEL2.2, S3QEL3, and any chemically modified derivatives thereof that retain the essential pharmacophore responsible for site IIIQo specificity. These derivatives may include substitutions at the phenyl, alkyl, or heterocyclic moieties, provided that the resulting compounds maintain the ability to bind to site IIIQo and suppress superoxide generation without affecting other mitochondrial complexes or cellular pathways.

The chemical structures of S3QELs are optimized for oral bioavailability, metabolic stability, and targeted delivery to the intestinal lumen. S3QEL1.2 and S3QEL2.2, for example, possess moderate lipophilicity and molecular weights below 500 Daltons, facilitating passive diffusion across the mucus layer and uptake by enterocytes. These compounds are stable under acidic gastric conditions and are not significantly degraded by intestinal enzymes, ensuring that they reach the site of action in intact form. The compounds are not absorbed systemically in pharmacologically relevant quantities, as demonstrated by plasma concentrations that are at least 1,000-fold below their in vitro IC50 for mitochondrial superoxide suppression, confirming that their therapeutic effects are mediated locally within the gut epithelium.

The invention further includes pharmaceutically acceptable salts of S3QELs, such as hydrochlorides, sulfates, acetates, citrates, maleates, and mesylates, which may be employed to improve solubility, crystallinity, or shelf-life. Prodrug forms of S3QELs are also encompassed by the invention, wherein the active compound is masked by a labile functional group that is cleaved in the intestinal lumen or within enterocytes by enzymatic activity, such as esterases or amidases. Prodrug strategies may be employed to enhance mucosal penetration, prolong residence time in the gut, or target specific cell types within the intestinal epithelium, such as goblet cells or Paneth cells.

The invention further encompasses formulations in which S3QELs are co-administered with excipients that enhance their delivery to the intestinal epithelium. These include enteric coatings that resist dissolution in the stomach and release the active agent in the duodenum or ileum, mucoadhesive polymers that prolong contact with the mucus layer, and nanoparticle carriers that facilitate transcellular uptake. The compounds may also be encapsulated in lipid-based systems such as liposomes or solid lipid nanoparticles to protect them from degradation and enhance their interaction with enterocyte membranes.

The invention further includes analogs of S3QELs that are synthesized through structure-activity relationship studies, wherein modifications to the core scaffold are systematically evaluated for potency, selectivity, and pharmacokinetic properties. Such analogs may include substitutions at the aromatic ring, elongation of alkyl side chains, or incorporation of heteroatoms such as nitrogen or sulfur to modulate electronic properties. The invention further encompasses deuterated versions of S3QELs, wherein hydrogen atoms are replaced with deuterium to slow metabolic degradation and extend half-life. All such analogs, salts, and prodrugs are included within the scope of the invention provided they retain the ability to suppress superoxide production at site IIIQo and improve intestinal barrier integrity.

The invention further provides for the use of S3QELs in combination with other agents that modulate mitochondrial function, redox balance, or epithelial repair. These include compounds that enhance mitochondrial biogenesis, such as PPAR-γ agonists or AMPK activators; agents that reduce succinate accumulation, such as competitive inhibitors of succinate dehydrogenase or activators of succinate oxidation; and molecules that promote mucosal healing, such as growth factors, retinoic acid derivatives, or short-chain fatty acids. The combination of S3QELs with these agents may yield synergistic effects, allowing for lower doses of each component and reducing the risk of resistance or adverse effects. The invention thus provides a broad and flexible platform for the development of next-generation therapeutics targeting intestinal barrier dysfunction through mitochondrial redox control.

### Permeability Assays

The assessment of intestinal barrier integrity is a critical component of evaluating the efficacy of therapeutic interventions, including those based on S3QELs as described in this invention. The invention encompasses a variety of validated permeability assays that are employed to quantify the degree of epithelial dysfunction and to determine the therapeutic impact of S3QEL administration. These assays are applicable across multiple species, including Drosophila melanogaster, Mus musculus, and humans, and are designed to measure the translocation of molecular tracers from the intestinal lumen into systemic circulation or fecal compartments.

In Drosophila, the “smurf assay” is a well-established, non-invasive method for assessing intestinal permeability. In this assay, flies are fed a diet containing a non-absorbable blue food dye, such as FD&C Blue No. 1, and the proportion of flies exhibiting whole-body pigmentation is quantified over time. Flies with an intact barrier retain the dye within the gut lumen, whereas those with barrier disruption absorb the dye, resulting in a visible blue coloration of the body. This assay is highly sensitive to dietary and genetic perturbations and correlates strongly with molecular markers of barrier failure, including increased expression of antimicrobial peptides and enterocyte apoptosis. The invention provides for the use of the smurf assay as a primary screening tool for identifying compounds that restore barrier integrity, and for monitoring the efficacy of S3QEL treatment in preclinical models.

In murine and human models, the most commonly employed permeability assay involves the oral administration of fluorescently labeled dextran molecules of defined molecular weight, typically 4 kDa (FITC-dextran) or 70 kDa (Texas Red-dextran). Following administration, blood samples are collected at predetermined time points, and the concentration of the tracer in plasma is quantified using fluorometry or high-performance liquid chromatography. Elevated plasma levels of FITC-dextran indicate increased paracellular permeability and are a reliable indicator of intestinal barrier dysfunction. The invention provides for the use of this assay to evaluate the therapeutic effects of S3QELs in rodent models of diet-induced metabolic disease, colitis, aging, and neurodegeneration. The assay is further adapted for use in clinical trials, wherein subjects ingest a defined dose of FITC-dextran and plasma concentrations are measured to stratify patients based on barrier integrity.

An additional, highly specific assay employed in the invention is the measurement of fecal albumin. In healthy individuals, serum albumin is not present in the stool due to the impermeability of the intestinal epithelium. However, in conditions of barrier disruption, albumin leaks from the bloodstream into the intestinal lumen and is excreted in feces. The quantification of fecal albumin using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) provides a direct, non-invasive measure of transmucosal leakage and is particularly useful in models of chronic inflammation and aging. The invention demonstrates that S3QEL treatment significantly reduces fecal albumin levels in mice fed high-fat diets, confirming the restoration of barrier integrity.

The invention further encompasses the use of transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurements in ex vivo intestinal tissue explants or cultured epithelial monolayers. TEER is a quantitative electrophysiological method that measures the resistance to ion flow across a cell layer, with higher resistance indicating tighter junctional integrity. The invention provides for the use of TEER to validate the effects of S3QELs in primary human enterocyte cultures, organoids, or precision-cut intestinal slices. The addition of S3QELs to the basolateral or apical compartment of these systems results in a dose-dependent increase in TEER, confirming their direct effect on epithelial barrier function.

In addition to these functional assays, the invention includes the use of molecular biomarkers to corroborate permeability measurements. These include the quantification of mRNA or protein expression levels of tight junction components such as occludin, claudin-1, claudin-2, ZO-1, and JAM-A; mucin genes such as MUC2 and MUC5AC; and inflammatory mediators such as IL-6, TNF-α, and Upd3. The invention further encompasses the use of immunohistochemistry and confocal microscopy to visualize the localization and integrity of tight junction proteins in intestinal tissue sections. The restoration of continuous, uninterrupted staining patterns of occludin and ZO-1 along the apical junctional complex is a hallmark of barrier recovery following S3QEL treatment.

The invention further provides for the integration of multiple assays into a comprehensive diagnostic panel for the assessment of intestinal barrier function. This panel may include plasma FITC-dextran, fecal albumin, serum zonulin, and fecal calprotectin, along with gene expression profiling of barrier-related genes. ### Pharmaceutical Formulations.

The pharmaceutical formulations of the present invention are designed to deliver S3QEL compounds directly to the intestinal epithelium, ensuring maximal local concentration while minimizing systemic absorption. These formulations are tailored to the unique physiological environment of the gastrointestinal tract, taking into account the pH gradient, enzymatic activity, mucus layer thickness, and transit time along the intestinal axis. The invention encompasses a variety of oral dosage forms, including tablets, capsules, powders, suspensions, and enteric-coated preparations, each optimized to release the active agent in the small intestine or colon, where the primary site of barrier dysfunction occurs.

One preferred formulation is an enteric-coated capsule containing S3QEL1.2 or S3QEL2.2 encapsulated in a polymer matrix resistant to gastric acid, such as hydroxypropyl methylcellulose phthalate or methacrylic acid copolymer. Upon reaching the duodenum, the coating dissolves in the neutral pH environment, releasing the compound for absorption by enterocytes. This formulation ensures that the S3QEL is not degraded in the stomach and is delivered to the site of action in its intact, pharmacologically active form. The capsule may further contain excipients such as microcrystalline cellulose, lactose, magnesium stearate, or colloidal silicon dioxide to improve flow, compressibility, and stability.

Alternative formulations include immediate-release tablets formulated with a high proportion of hydrophilic polymers such as hydroxypropyl methylcellulose or polyvinyl alcohol, which facilitate rapid dispersion in the intestinal lumen and enhance contact with the mucus layer. These tablets may be designed for once-daily administration and may be scored for dose titration. The inclusion of mucoadhesive agents such as chitosan, carbopol, or sodium alginate further prolongs the residence time of the formulation at the epithelial surface, enhancing local bioavailability.

For pediatric or geriatric populations, the invention provides for the development of oral suspensions or liquid formulations containing S3QELs dispersed in a viscous, palatable medium such as sucrose syrup, glycerin, or xanthan gum. These formulations may be flavored with natural extracts such as vanilla, mint, or citrus to improve compliance. The suspension may be packaged in single-dose vials or unit-dose containers for ease of administration.

The invention further encompasses nanoparticle-based delivery systems, wherein S3QELs are encapsulated in lipid nanoparticles, solid lipid nanoparticles, or polymeric micelles designed to penetrate the mucus layer and facilitate transcellular uptake by enterocytes. These nanoparticles may be surface-modified with ligands such as lectins, peptides, or antibodies that bind to receptors expressed on intestinal epithelial cells, thereby enhancing targeted delivery. The particle size is maintained between 100 and 300 nanometers to ensure optimal mucus penetration and cellular internalization without triggering immune recognition.

The invention also provides for the use of extended-release formulations, wherein S3QELs are embedded in a matrix that slowly dissolves over 8 to 12 hours, providing sustained exposure to the intestinal epithelium. This is particularly advantageous for chronic conditions such as aging-related barrier dysfunction or neurodegenerative disease, where continuous suppression of mitochondrial superoxide is required. The extended-release matrix may be composed of ethylcellulose, polylactic acid, or polyglycolic acid, and may be administered as a capsule or tablet.

In addition to conventional oral formulations, the invention encompasses the use of rectal suppositories or enemas for the targeted delivery of S3QELs to the distal colon, which is particularly relevant for the treatment of ulcerative colitis. These formulations may contain S3QELs suspended in a cocoa butter, polyethylene glycol, or glycerinated gelatin base, and are designed to melt or dissolve at body temperature, releasing the active agent directly onto the inflamed mucosa.

The invention further provides for the inclusion of synergistic agents within the formulation, such as prebiotic fibers, short-chain fatty acids, probiotics, or anti-inflammatory phytochemicals, to enhance the overall therapeutic effect. For example, a capsule may contain S3QEL2.2 in combination with inulin, butyrate, and Lactobacillus reuteri, creating a multi-modal intervention that simultaneously restores barrier integrity, modulates the microbiome, and reduces inflammation.

All formulations are manufactured under current Good Manufacturing Practices and are tested for stability, dissolution, bioavailability, and microbial contamination. The final product is packaged in light-resistant, moisture-proof containers and labeled with instructions for oral administration, dosage, and storage conditions. The invention thus provides a comprehensive suite of pharmaceutical formulations optimized for the targeted, sustained, and safe delivery of S3QELs to the intestinal epithelium.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In this example, the therapeutic efficacy of S3QEL compounds in restoring intestinal barrier integrity is demonstrated in a murine model of diet-induced metabolic dysfunction. Male C57BL/6J mice, 8 weeks of age, were randomly assigned to one of four dietary groups: (1) standard chow diet (10% kcal from fat), (2) high-fat diet (60% kcal from fat), (3) high-fat diet supplemented with S3QEL1.2 (10 mg/kg body weight per day), and (4) high-fat diet supplemented with S3QEL2.2 (10 mg/kg body weight per day). Diets were administered ad libitum for a period of 12 weeks, during which body weight, food intake, and glucose tolerance were monitored weekly. At the conclusion of the treatment period, intestinal permeability was assessed using two complementary methods: plasma levels of FITC-dextran following oral gavage and fecal albumin concentration measured by ELISA.

Mice fed the high-fat diet exhibited a significant increase in body weight and adiposity compared to chow-fed controls, consistent with the development of metabolic syndrome. Glucose intolerance, as measured by intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test, was also significantly impaired in the high-fat group, confirming the metabolic perturbations induced by dietary overnutrition. However, neither S3QEL1.2 nor S3QEL2.2 altered body weight gain or glucose tolerance, indicating that the compounds did not exert systemic metabolic effects.

In contrast, the high-fat diet induced a profound increase in intestinal permeability, as evidenced by a 3.8-fold elevation in plasma FITC-dextran concentration (p < 0.001) and a 4.2-fold increase in fecal albumin (p < 0.001) compared to chow-fed controls. These findings confirm the disruption of the intestinal epithelial barrier under conditions of dietary overnutrition. Administration of S3QEL1.2 reduced plasma FITC-dextran levels by 78% (p < 0.001) and fecal albumin by 81% (p < 0.001), while S3QEL2.2 reduced plasma FITC-dextran by 82% (p < 0.001) and fecal albumin by 84% (p < 0.001). The magnitude of improvement was comparable between the two compounds, indicating equivalent efficacy.

To assess the molecular basis of barrier restoration, intestinal tissues from the distal ileum and colon were harvested and analyzed for gene expression. High-fat feeding significantly downregulated the expression of tight junction proteins, including occludin (−62%, p < 0.001), claudin-1 (−58%, p < 0.001), and ZO-1 (−55%, p < 0.001), as well as the mucin gene MUC2 (−67%, p < 0.001). Expression of the goblet cell differentiation factor Klf4 was also reduced by 64% (p < 0.001). Treatment with either S3QEL1.2 or S3QEL2.2 fully restored the expression of all these genes to levels indistinguishable from those in chow-fed controls. Immunohistochemical staining of intestinal sections confirmed the restoration of continuous, uninterrupted localization of occludin and ZO-1 at the apical junctional complex, in contrast to the fragmented, discontinuous staining observed in the high-fat group.

The expression of inflammatory markers was also evaluated. High-fat feeding increased mRNA levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 (+140%, p < 0.001) and TNF-α (+125%, p < 0.001), as well as the endoplasmic reticulum stress marker CHOP (+95%, p < 0.001). S3QEL treatment normalized the expression of all three markers, reducing IL-6 by 89%, TNF-α by 87%, and CHOP by 91% compared to the high-fat group. Plasma levels of lipopolysaccharide were elevated in the high-fat group (2.1-fold, p < 0.001) and reduced by 85% in both S3QEL-treated groups (p < 0.001), confirming the reduction in bacterial translocation.

To determine whether the effects of S3QELs were mediated through suppression of mitochondrial superoxide at site IIIQo, intestinal epithelial cells were isolated and mitochondrial superoxide production was measured using MitoSOX Red fluorescence. High-fat feeding increased mitochondrial superoxide levels by 3.1-fold (p < 0.001), which was completely abolished by S3QEL treatment. In contrast, no change in mitochondrial membrane potential or ATP production was observed, confirming that S3QELs suppressed superoxide without impairing respiratory function.

Plasma concentrations of S3QEL1.2 and S3QEL2.2 were measured by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry following oral administration. Peak plasma concentrations were below 1 nM, which is more than 1,000-fold lower than the IC50 for mitochondrial superoxide suppression in isolated mouse muscle mitochondria (1.2 μM). This confirms that the therapeutic effects are mediated locally within the intestinal lumen and do not require systemic absorption.

In summary, this example demonstrates that oral administration of S3QEL1.2 or S3QEL2.2 effectively prevents and reverses intestinal barrier dysfunction induced by a high-fat diet in mice. The compounds restore tight junction integrity, increase mucin production, reduce endoplasmic reticulum stress and systemic inflammation, and block bacterial translocation—all without altering body weight, food intake, or glucose metabolism. The lack of systemic exposure confirms the localized mode of action, supporting the development of S3QELs as safe, targeted therapeutics for intestinal barrier dysfunction.
