# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of quantum computing has long been constrained by the practical limitations of physical hardware, particularly the high error rates and extended execution times associated with two-qubit entangling gates such as the controlled-NOT (CNOT) and controlled-Z (CZ) operations. While single-qubit gates like the Hadamard, Phase, and Pauli operators are relatively fast and robust, their entangling counterparts remain the dominant source of decoherence and computational overhead in current quantum architectures, including superconducting qubit systems and trapped-ion platforms. This asymmetry in gate fidelity and duration necessitates a strategic focus on minimizing the number of entangling operations required to implement any given quantum algorithm, especially when those algorithms are composed predominantly of Clifford operations. The Clifford group, a finite subgroup of the unitary group acting on n qubits, is of particular significance because it encompasses a broad class of operations essential to quantum error correction, randomized benchmarking, state distillation, and classical shadow tomography. These applications rely on the unique property of the Clifford group to form a unitary 2-design, meaning that random sampling from this group reproduces the second-order statistical moments of the Haar measure over the full unitary group. This allows for efficient characterization of quantum noise and state properties without the exponential overhead of full quantum state tomography. However, the practical utility of these theoretical advantages is contingent upon the ability to realize Clifford unitaries with minimal resource expenditure, particularly in terms of entangling gate count.

Historically, the synthesis of Clifford circuits has been approached through canonical forms that guarantee asymptotic optimality in the number of gates required, such as the method introduced by Aaronson and Gottesman, which achieves a gate complexity of Θ(n²/log n) for n-qubit circuits. While this result is theoretically elegant and establishes a foundational upper bound, it is not designed for practical optimization. In real-world implementations, the constant factors and structural inefficiencies inherent in such canonical constructions lead to circuits that are significantly longer than necessary, especially for circuits with fewer than ten qubits. Moreover, these methods do not exploit the algebraic structure of the Clifford group to reduce redundancy or to merge operations that are physically equivalent under gate commutation or cancellation. As a result, the gap between theoretical asymptotic bounds and practical circuit efficiency remains substantial. The challenge of synthesizing optimal or near-optimal Clifford circuits has been further complicated by the exponential growth of the Clifford group’s size, which scales as roughly 2^(n²+2n) for n qubits. This rapid growth renders exhaustive search methods infeasible beyond six qubits, even with state-of-the-art computational resources. Consequently, the synthesis of Clifford circuits for systems with more than six qubits has largely been relegated to heuristic or greedy approaches that sacrifice optimality for scalability.

Previous attempts to improve upon canonical synthesis have explored techniques such as template matching and peephole optimization, both of which have demonstrated success in the context of universal quantum circuits and reversible logic. Template matching relies on identifying recurring subcircuits that implement the identity operation and replacing them with shorter equivalent sequences, thereby reducing gate count. Peephole optimization, on the other hand, examines small local subcircuits—typically involving two or three qubits—and replaces them with their optimal implementations drawn from a precomputed database. However, these methods have been limited in their application to Clifford circuits due to two critical shortcomings. First, standard template matching does not account for the unique algebraic properties of the Clifford group, such as the ability to push Pauli operators to the end of a circuit without altering the non-Pauli components, or the fact that swap operations can be decoupled from the core computation via qubit relabeling. Second, traditional peephole optimization requires storing optimal circuits for every possible unitary on a fixed number of qubits, a requirement that becomes computationally prohibitive beyond six qubits due to the exponential growth of the Clifford group. These limitations have left a critical gap in the landscape of quantum circuit optimization: a scalable, practical method capable of reducing the entangling gate count of arbitrary Clifford circuits far beyond the reach of exhaustive search, while still achieving performance close to optimal for small systems and dramatically improving upon existing heuristics for larger systems.

The present invention addresses this gap by introducing a novel, integrated framework for the synthesis and optimization of Clifford circuits that combines three synergistic components: a bidirectional greedy compiler for initial circuit generation, a Clifford-specific three-stage template matching procedure that leverages the group’s algebraic structure, and a symbolic peephole optimization technique that extends the reach of local optimization to entangled subcircuits without requiring full state-space enumeration. The bidirectional compiler constructs an initial circuit by simultaneously disentangling qubits from both ends of the system, minimizing the combined cost of left and right disentangling operations. This approach reduces the initial gate count by nearly half compared to unidirectional methods. The three-stage template matching then partitions the circuit into compute, swap, and Pauli stages, enabling independent optimization of each component. By pushing all Pauli gates to the end and factoring out swap gates, the method isolates the entangling core of the circuit, allowing templates to be applied exclusively to the Clifford gates that generate nontrivial entanglement. The symbolic peephole optimization, the most innovative component, introduces symbolic Pauli gates to represent the influence of entangling operations on qubits outside a local subcircuit. This allows the optimization of small subcircuits—such as those involving two or three qubits—as if they were isolated, even when they are embedded within a larger entangled system. The method employs dynamic programming over the coset space of the local Clifford group, drastically reducing the search space while preserving global equivalence. Together, these components form a complete pipeline that transforms an arbitrary Clifford unitary into a highly optimized circuit with minimal entangling gate count, achieving near-optimal results for six-qubit systems and reducing gate counts by over sixty percent on larger systems compared to the best prior methods.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a novel method for synthesizing and optimizing quantum circuits composed of Clifford gates with the objective of minimizing the number of entangling two-qubit gates, specifically the controlled-NOT and controlled-Z gates, which are the most resource-intensive components in current quantum hardware architectures. The method comprises three interdependent components: a bidirectional greedy compiler for generating an initial circuit representation of a target Clifford unitary, a three-stage template matching procedure that exploits the algebraic structure of the Clifford group to isolate and optimize entangling operations, and a symbolic peephole optimization technique that enables local circuit refinement without requiring exhaustive precomputation of optimal subcircuits. The bidirectional greedy compiler constructs an initial circuit by iteratively disentangling qubits from both ends of the system, selecting at each step the pair of anti-commuting Pauli operators whose simultaneous disentanglement minimizes the combined cost of left and right operations, thereby achieving a significant reduction in the initial gate count compared to unidirectional synthesis methods. Following synthesis, the circuit is partitioned into three sequential stages: a compute stage containing only Hadamard, Phase, CNOT, and CZ gates; a swap stage consisting of qubit permutation operations; and a Pauli stage comprising single-qubit Pauli operators. This partitioning is achieved by systematically pushing all Pauli operators and swap gates to the end of the circuit using the known transformation rules of the Clifford group, which preserve the overall unitary while isolating the entangling core. The compute stage is then optimized using a Clifford-specific template matching protocol that converts all CNOT gates into CZ gates where beneficial, applies a curated set of identity-replacing templates to eliminate redundant gate sequences, and employs Hadamard and Phase gate pushing to maximize template applicability and reduce single-qubit overhead. The swap stage is subsequently optimized by identifying opportunities to merge swap operations with adjacent CNOT or CZ gates, thereby converting each swap into an effective single entangling gate through strategic alignment. Finally, symbolic peephole optimization is applied by selecting small subsets of qubits, introducing symbolic Pauli gates to represent the external entangling influences on these qubits, and optimizing the resulting symbolic subcircuit using dynamic programming over the coset space of the local Clifford group. This technique enables the optimization of subcircuits that are not fully decoupled from the rest of the system, overcoming the fundamental limitation of traditional peephole methods that require complete isolation. The entire process is iterated until no further reduction in entangling gate count is possible, and the final circuit is guaranteed to be functionally equivalent to the original unitary. The method is scalable to systems with more than sixty qubits, requires no precomputed databases beyond the local Clifford group for up to three qubits, and achieves a reduction in CNOT gate count of over sixty-four percent on benchmark Hamiltonian evolution circuits compared to the Aaronson-Gottesman canonical form. Furthermore, for six-qubit systems, where optimal gate counts are known, the method recovers the optimal CNOT count in ninety-seven point nine percent of cases, with the worst-case deviation being a single gate. The invention thus provides a practical, efficient, and highly effective solution to the problem of Clifford circuit optimization, enabling the realization of quantum algorithms with significantly reduced resource requirements and improved fidelity on near-term quantum hardware.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive framework for the synthesis and optimization of quantum circuits composed entirely of Clifford gates, with the explicit objective of minimizing the number of entangling two-qubit operations, particularly the controlled-NOT (CNOT) and controlled-Z (CZ) gates, which are the primary source of decoherence and latency in current quantum computing architectures. The method is structured as a three-phase pipeline: initial circuit synthesis via a bidirectional greedy compiler, followed by a structured optimization process comprising a three-stage template matching procedure and a symbolic peephole optimization technique. Each phase is designed to exploit the unique algebraic properties of the Clifford group, ensuring that the resulting circuit remains functionally equivalent to the target unitary while achieving unprecedented reductions in entangling gate count. The synthesis phase begins with the specification of a target n-qubit Clifford unitary, which may be provided as a tableau representation, a matrix form, or as a sequence of operations that collectively define a Clifford transformation. The first step in the process is the construction of an initial circuit that implements this unitary with a gate count that is substantially lower than that produced by canonical methods such as the Aaronson-Gottesman algorithm. This is accomplished through a bidirectional greedy compilation strategy that simultaneously disentangles qubits from both ends of the system, rather than sequentially from one end as in traditional unidirectional approaches. At each step of the compilation, the algorithm identifies a pair of anti-commuting n-qubit Pauli operators whose simultaneous disentanglement minimizes the combined cost of two Clifford circuits: one that maps the target unitary’s action on these operators to the standard basis, and another that performs the inverse operation from the opposite end. The selection of these operators is constrained to those with Pauli weight at most two, which ensures computational tractability while capturing the majority of low-cost disentangling opportunities. The disentangling circuits for each operator are constructed using a subroutine that efficiently computes a minimal CNOT cost decomposition, leveraging the stabilizer formalism to simulate the action of each gate in time linear in the number of qubits. The resulting left and right disentangling circuits are then composed with the original unitary to produce a new unitary that acts trivially on the first qubit, reducing the problem size by one. This process is repeated recursively, with the algorithm alternating between left and right disentangling steps to balance the overall cost, until all qubits have been disentangled. The resulting circuit, while not necessarily optimal, achieves a CNOT count that is approximately forty-eight point six percent lower than that of the Aaronson-Gottesman canonical form, providing a significantly improved starting point for subsequent optimization.

Following the synthesis phase, the circuit undergoes a structured three-stage optimization process designed to exploit the internal symmetries and transformation rules of the Clifford group. The first stage involves the partitioning of the circuit into three distinct components: a compute stage, a swap stage, and a Pauli stage. This partitioning is not a physical reordering of gates but a logical decomposition enabled by the known commutation and conjugation rules of the Clifford group. Specifically, the algorithm systematically pushes all single-qubit Pauli operators—X, Y, and Z—to the end of the circuit, using the fact that any Clifford gate conjugates Pauli operators into other Pauli operators, thereby allowing Pauli gates to be relocated without altering the overall unitary. Similarly, all swap gates are extracted from the main body of the circuit and collected into a separate stage, as they can be implemented via qubit relabeling without affecting the underlying computation. The result is a circuit that consists of a core compute stage composed exclusively of Hadamard, Phase, CNOT, and CZ gates, followed by a swap stage containing only qubit permutations, and finally a Pauli stage containing only single-qubit Pauli operators. This separation is critical, as it allows each stage to be optimized independently using specialized techniques that would otherwise be inapplicable in a mixed-gate context. The compute stage is then subjected to a Clifford-specific template matching procedure that is distinct from conventional template matching used in universal quantum circuits. The method begins by converting all CNOT gates into CZ gates where doing so reduces the overall gate count or increases the likelihood of template application, as CZ gates are more amenable to certain identity templates due to their symmetric structure. A curated set of templates, each of which represents a sequence of gates that evaluates to the identity, is then applied iteratively across the circuit. These templates are selected to exploit known cancellations and redundancies in the Clifford group, such as the fact that two consecutive CZ gates on the same pair of qubits cancel, or that a Hadamard gate followed by a CZ and another Hadamard is equivalent to a CNOT. The template matching is applied in a left-to-right scan, with each potential match evaluated for its benefit in reducing the total number of two-qubit gates. Importantly, the method also incorporates Hadamard and Phase gate pushing as a secondary optimization, where single-qubit gates are moved as far as possible from the two-qubit gates to maximize the number of template applications and to reduce the overall single-qubit overhead. This pushing is implemented as a specialized template application, where a fixed subsequence of a single-qubit gate adjacent to a two-qubit gate is replaced by its equivalent form with the single-qubit gate relocated. Once the compute stage has been fully optimized, the swap stage is addressed. Here, the algorithm identifies opportunities to merge swap operations with adjacent CNOT or CZ gates in the compute stage, effectively converting each swap into a single entangling gate through strategic alignment. For example, a swap gate between two qubits that are the target and control of a nearby CNOT can be absorbed into the CNOT by redefining the qubit labels, thereby eliminating the need for an explicit swap. This merging is performed by analyzing the temporal proximity and qubit connectivity of swaps and two-qubit gates, and applying a graph-based matching algorithm to identify the maximum number of merges possible without introducing additional entangling operations. The resulting swap stage is then minimized using a permutation optimization algorithm that finds the shortest sequence of swaps required to achieve the desired qubit rearrangement, taking into account the merged operations.

The final and most innovative phase of the optimization process is symbolic peephole optimization, which extends the concept of local circuit optimization to subcircuits that are not fully decoupled from the remainder of the system. Traditional peephole optimization requires that the subcircuit under consideration be isolated from all other qubits, a condition that is rarely satisfied in large-scale Clifford circuits and renders the method inapplicable beyond six qubits. The present invention overcomes this limitation by introducing symbolic Pauli gates, which represent the effect of entangling operations between a local subset of qubits and the rest of the system. For a given subset of two or three qubits, the algorithm identifies all entangling CNOT gates that connect these qubits to the remainder of the system. Each such CNOT is replaced by a symbolic Pauli gate acting on the target qubit, with the control qubit replaced by a symbolic variable that encodes the state of the control. This transforms the local subcircuit into a symbolic Clifford circuit that depends on these symbolic variables, while the global circuit is preserved through the introduction of phase factors and control qubit operations. The symbolic subcircuit is then optimized using dynamic programming over the coset space of the local Clifford group, where the cost function is defined as the sum of the CNOT count within the subcircuit and the number of symbolic Pauli gates. The optimization respects the temporal ordering of the symbolic gates and preserves the overall phase modulo factors of i or -1, which can be compensated by single-qubit phase gates on the control qubits. The key insight is that the cost function decomposes into a sum of terms that depend only on consecutive pairs of local Clifford operators, allowing the global minimum to be computed in time linear in the number of entangling connections. This is achieved by precomputing a lookup table of the minimal CNOT cost for each element in a reduced set of coset representatives of the local Clifford group, which for two qubits contains only twenty elements and for three qubits contains six thousand seven hundred twenty elements, making the computation feasible even on modest hardware. The algorithm iterates over all possible subsets of two and three qubits in a randomized order, applying the symbolic peephole optimization until no further reduction in the total CNOT count is observed. The symbolic gates are then resolved by replacing each with its corresponding CNOT gate, reconstructing the full circuit with the optimized local structure. This process is repeated in multiple passes, with the order of subset selection randomized in each pass to avoid local minima, and the entire optimization loop is terminated when no improvement is observed over two consecutive passes. The final step of the optimization pipeline is a single pass of template matching applied to the entire circuit, with templates restricted to single-qubit operations, to eliminate any residual single-qubit redundancies introduced during the symbolic optimization. The resulting circuit is guaranteed to be functionally equivalent to the original unitary, yet it exhibits a dramatically reduced number of entangling gates, often achieving performance within one gate of the known optimal for six-qubit systems and reducing the CNOT count by over sixty-four percent on larger systems compared to the Aaronson-Gottesman canonical form. The method is fully scalable, requires no precomputed databases beyond the local Clifford group for up to three qubits, and can be parallelized across multiple instances of the bidirectional compiler to further improve results. The entire process is implemented in software and can be executed on classical computing hardware, making it immediately applicable to the compilation of quantum algorithms for deployment on current and near-term quantum processors. The invention thus provides a complete, practical, and highly effective solution to the problem of Clifford circuit optimization, bridging the gap between theoretical optimality and practical feasibility in quantum circuit design.
