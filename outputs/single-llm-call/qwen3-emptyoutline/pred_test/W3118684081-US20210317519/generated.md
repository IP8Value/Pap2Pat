# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of epigenetic analysis and molecular diagnostics, specifically to novel chemical and enzymatic methods for the direct, quantitative, and subtraction-free detection of cytosine modifications in genomic DNA at single-base resolution. The invention encompasses a suite of sequencing methodologies that leverage pyridine borane-based reductive chemistry, in combination with selective enzymatic blocking or chemical oxidation steps, to distinguish between 5-methylcytosine (5mC), 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), 5-formylcytosine (5fC), and 5-carboxylcytosine (5caC) without reliance on bisulfite conversion or indirect inference through differential comparison of multiple assays. These methods enable the precise mapping of epigenetic marks across the entire genome with minimal DNA degradation, high sequence fidelity, and enhanced coverage, thereby providing a transformative platform for the study of DNA methylation dynamics in development, disease, and cellular differentiation. The invention further includes optimized protocols for library preparation, reagent formulation, and data analysis that collectively overcome the longstanding limitations of prior art methods, including low mapping efficiency, high background noise, sequence bias, and the introduction of artifactual signals through subtraction-based calculations. The methods described herein are applicable to a broad range of biological samples, including but not limited to mammalian tissues, stem cells, cancer biopsies, and clinical specimens, and are compatible with standard high-throughput sequencing platforms such as Illumina NovaSeq and HiSeq systems.

## BACKGROUND

The genetic code of life is encoded in the linear sequence of four nucleotide bases—adenine, thymine, guanine, and cytosine—yet the functional interpretation of this sequence is profoundly modulated by chemical alterations to the bases themselves. Among these modifications, cytosine methylation has emerged as one of the most critical epigenetic regulators, influencing gene expression, chromatin architecture, genomic stability, and cellular identity. The canonical form, 5-methylcytosine (5mC), has long been recognized as a stable mark associated with transcriptional repression, particularly in promoter regions and repetitive elements. However, subsequent discoveries revealed that 5mC is not an endpoint but rather an intermediate in a dynamic demethylation pathway initiated by the ten-eleven translocation (TET) family of dioxygenases. These enzymes oxidize 5mC to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), which in turn can be further oxidized to 5-formylcytosine (5fC) and 5-carboxylcytosine (5caC). While 5fC and 5caC are present at extremely low abundance, they are now understood to serve not merely as transient intermediates but as potential regulatory signals in their own right, particularly in the context of active gene enhancers and pluripotency networks. The biological significance of these modifications is underscored by their tissue-specific distributions: 5hmC is highly enriched in neuronal tissues, while 5fC and 5caC are preferentially localized at transcriptionally active regions during early embryogenesis and cellular reprogramming.

For decades, bisulfite sequencing (BS) has served as the gold standard for detecting DNA methylation at single-base resolution. This method exploits the differential reactivity of cytosine and its derivatives under harsh acidic and oxidative conditions: unmodified cytosine is deaminated to uracil, which is subsequently amplified as thymine during PCR, while methylated cytosines resist conversion and remain as cytosine. However, bisulfite treatment inflicts catastrophic damage to DNA, degrading up to 99% of the starting material, fragmenting strands, and reducing sequence complexity by converting nearly all native cytosines into thymines. This results in severely compromised library complexity, low mapping rates, elevated sequencing errors, and an inability to distinguish between 5mC and 5hmC, as both resist bisulfite conversion. To address this limitation, several modified bisulfite-based methods have been developed, including oxidative bisulfite sequencing (oxBS-seq), which uses chemical oxidation to convert 5hmC to 5fC, thereby enabling its distinction from 5mC through subtraction of oxBS-seq signals from standard BS data. Similarly, TET-assisted bisulfite sequencing (TAB-seq) employs β-glucosyltransferase to protect 5hmC from TET-mediated oxidation, allowing it to be read as cytosine while 5mC is converted to uracil. Despite these advances, all bisulfite-based approaches suffer from the same fundamental flaws: irreversible DNA damage, loss of sequence information, and the necessity of performing two separate experiments followed by arithmetic subtraction to infer the true abundance of each modification. This subtraction introduces significant statistical noise, particularly when one modification is present at low abundance, leading to negative values, inflated variance, and unreliable quantification. Moreover, the requirement for high sequencing depth to overcome these artifacts renders these methods costly, inefficient, and unsuitable for clinical applications where sample material is limited.

In response to these challenges, bisulfite-free alternatives have emerged, including enzymatic approaches such as APOBEC-coupled epigenetic sequencing (ACE-seq) and Enzymatic Methyl-seq (EM-seq). These methods replace the harsh chemical deamination of bisulfite with enzymatic deamination using APOBEC3A or TET-based oxidation followed by enzymatic conversion. While these methods preserve DNA integrity and improve mapping efficiency, they still rely on indirect detection: unmodified cytosines are converted to thymine, rendering the resulting sequence a transformed version of the original genome. This transformation continues to reduce sequence complexity and introduces bias in read alignment, particularly in CpG-rich regions. Furthermore, ACE-seq and EM-seq remain incapable of distinguishing 5mC from 5hmC in a single assay; their resolution still requires the performance of two complementary experiments and subsequent subtraction, perpetuating the same statistical and technical limitations as bisulfite-based methods. The field has thus been in urgent need of a paradigm shift: a method that enables direct, simultaneous, and subtraction-free detection of all four cytosine modifications in a single sequencing run, without compromising DNA integrity or introducing systematic bias.

The present invention fulfills this unmet need by introducing a family of novel chemical methodologies grounded in the reductive decarboxylation and deamination chemistry of pyridine borane. This chemistry, previously demonstrated in a limited context for model DNA substrates, is here fully optimized and scaled to whole-genome applications. The core innovation lies in the strategic use of pyridine borane—a mild, aqueous-compatible reducing agent—to selectively convert oxidized cytosine derivatives into dihydrouracil (DHU), which is then amplified and sequenced as thymine. Crucially, unmodified cytosine and 5-methylcytosine are inert under these conditions, allowing for selective targeting of specific modifications through prior chemical or enzymatic manipulation. By combining this chemistry with β-glucosyltransferase-mediated blocking of 5hmC, we achieve TAPSβ, the first method capable of directly and quantitatively mapping 5mC without interference from 5hmC. By employing potassium ruthenate to oxidize 5hmC to 5fC, followed by pyridine borane reduction, we achieve CAPS, the first bisulfite-free method that directly reads 5hmC as thymine while leaving 5mC untouched. Furthermore, the direct application of pyridine borane to genomic DNA enables PS, a method that detects both 5fC and 5caC as thymine, and when combined with selective blocking of 5fC using O-ethylhydroxylamine, yields PS-c, a method that exclusively detects 5caC. Together, these four methods—TAPSβ, CAPS, PS, and PS-c—constitute a unified, chemically coherent platform for the complete epigenetic profiling of cytosine modifications, eliminating the need for subtraction, minimizing DNA damage, maximizing sequence fidelity, and enabling the accurate quantification of all four modifications in a single experiment. The invention represents a fundamental advancement in epigenomic technology, providing a robust, scalable, and clinically translatable framework for the next generation of DNA methylation analysis.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and comprehensive suite of methods for the direct, quantitative, and subtraction-free detection of cytosine modifications in genomic DNA at single-base resolution. The invention comprises four distinct but chemically related sequencing protocols—TAPSβ, CAPS, PS, and PS-c—each designed to specifically and selectively convert one or more cytosine derivatives into dihydrouracil, which is subsequently amplified and sequenced as thymine, thereby enabling unambiguous identification of the original modification state. The core innovation lies in the application of pyridine borane-based reductive chemistry to genomic DNA following targeted chemical or enzymatic pre-treatment, which allows for the direct readout of 5-methylcytosine, 5-hydroxymethylcytosine, 5-formylcytosine, and 5-carboxylcytosine without the need for bisulfite treatment or differential subtraction between multiple assays. Unlike prior art methods, which rely on destructive chemical conversion, indirect inference, or combinatorial subtraction, the methods of the invention preserve the integrity of the DNA molecule, maintain high sequence complexity, and deliver high-fidelity, base-resolution data with minimal background noise and maximal coverage.

In a first embodiment, the invention provides TAPSβ, a method for the specific detection of 5-methylcytosine. This method involves the enzymatic glucosylation of 5-hydroxymethylcytosine using β-glucosyltransferase, which renders 5hmC resistant to subsequent oxidation by TET enzymes. Following this blocking step, the genomic DNA is subjected to TET-mediated oxidation, which converts 5-methylcytosine to 5-carboxylcytosine, and then to dihydrouracil via reduction with pyridine borane. Unmodified cytosine and glucosylated 5hmC remain unchanged and are not converted to thymine, allowing for the exclusive detection of 5mC as a C-to-T transition during sequencing. This method achieves a conversion efficiency of greater than 97% for 5mC, with a false-positive rate below 0.3%, and demonstrates superior sequencing quality, mapping rate, and coverage compared to all existing bisulfite-based and bisulfite-free methods for 5mC detection.

In a second embodiment, the invention provides CAPS, a method for the specific detection of 5-hydroxymethylcytosine. This method employs a two-step chemical strategy: first, 5-hydroxymethylcytosine is selectively oxidized to 5-formylcytosine using potassium ruthenate under optimized conditions that minimize DNA damage; second, the resulting 5fC is reduced to dihydrouracil using 2-methylpyridine borane (pic-borane), which is uniquely effective on single-stranded DNA and achieves a conversion rate exceeding 83% with a false-positive rate below 0.8%. Unmodified cytosine and 5-methylcytosine remain unaffected, enabling the direct and exclusive detection of 5hmC as a C-to-T transition. CAPS outperforms all prior methods for 5hmC detection—including TAB-seq and ACE-seq—in terms of sequencing quality, mapping efficiency, and signal-to-noise ratio, and eliminates the need for subtraction-based estimation, thereby providing a more accurate and reliable profile of 5hmC distribution across the genome.

In a third embodiment, the invention provides PS, a method for the direct detection of both 5-formylcytosine and 5-carboxylcytosine. This method applies pyridine borane reduction directly to genomic DNA without prior modification, resulting in the conversion of both 5fC and 5caC to dihydrouracil and their subsequent detection as thymine during sequencing. Unmodified cytosine, 5mC, and 5hmC are not affected, allowing for the specific identification of these two oxidation intermediates. PS achieves a conversion efficiency of over 75% for 5fC and over 90% for 5caC, with a false-positive rate below 0.3%, making it uniquely suited for the detection of these low-abundance modifications in regulatory regions such as enhancers and promoters.

In a fourth embodiment, the invention provides PS-c, a method for the specific detection of 5-carboxylcytosine. This method incorporates a selective blocking step in which 5-formylcytosine is chemically capped using O-ethylhydroxylamine prior to pyridine borane reduction. This modification prevents 5fC from being reduced to dihydrouracil, while 5caC remains fully susceptible to reduction. As a result, only 5caC is converted to thymine during sequencing, enabling its exclusive detection. PS-c achieves a conversion efficiency of over 95% for 5caC and a false-positive rate below 0.3%, with minimal interference from 5fC, thereby providing the first method capable of directly and specifically profiling 5caC at single-base resolution.

Each of these methods is compatible with standard next-generation sequencing library preparation workflows, including end-repair, A-tailing, adaptor ligation, and PCR amplification using uracil-tolerant polymerases. The invention further includes optimized protocols for DNA fragmentation, purification, and quality control, as well as specialized reagent formulations, including the use of uracil-containing loop-structured adaptors and USER enzyme treatment to protect ligated ends from oxidative damage during CAPS. The methods are validated across multiple biological systems, including mouse embryonic stem cells, and demonstrate exceptional correlation with published datasets while delivering superior data quality metrics. The invention further provides computational pipelines for data preprocessing, base calling, and statistical analysis, including maximum likelihood estimation of modification levels and enrichment analysis across genomic features. Collectively, the methods of the invention provide a complete, integrated, and subtraction-free platform for the comprehensive profiling of the mammalian epigenome, enabling unprecedented insights into the dynamics of DNA methylation and demethylation in health and disease.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a set of novel, chemically distinct, and biologically validated methods for the direct, quantitative, and subtraction-free detection of cytosine modifications in genomic DNA. These methods are based on the selective application of pyridine borane-based reductive chemistry to convert specific cytosine derivatives into dihydrouracil, which is then amplified and sequenced as thymine, thereby enabling the unambiguous identification of the original modification state without the need for bisulfite treatment or differential subtraction between multiple assays. The invention encompasses four distinct protocols—TAPSβ, CAPS, PS, and PS-c—each tailored to detect a specific subset of cytosine modifications with high specificity, efficiency, and fidelity. The underlying principle of all four methods is the differential reactivity of cytosine derivatives toward pyridine borane under controlled chemical and enzymatic conditions, which allows for the selective conversion of one modification while leaving others intact. This principle is enabled by the unique chemical properties of pyridine borane, which acts as a mild, water-soluble reducing agent capable of cleaving the formyl and carboxyl groups from 5-formylcytosine and 5-carboxylcytosine, respectively, to yield dihydrouracil, a structure that base-pairs with adenine during PCR amplification and is therefore read as thymine in sequencing data. Unmodified cytosine and 5-methylcytosine are chemically inert under these conditions, and 5-hydroxymethylcytosine can be selectively modified through prior enzymatic or chemical steps to either block or enable its conversion.

The first method, referred to as TAPSβ, is designed for the specific detection of 5-methylcytosine. This method begins with the enzymatic glucosylation of 5-hydroxymethylcytosine using β-glucosyltransferase, an enzyme that transfers a glucose moiety from UDP-glucose to the hydroxyl group of 5hmC, forming β-glucosyl-5-hydroxymethylcytosine. This modification sterically and chemically blocks the ability of TET enzymes to oxidize 5hmC to 5fC, thereby preserving it in its native state. Following this blocking step, the DNA is subjected to a two-round TET oxidation reaction, in which the TET1 catalytic domain is used in the presence of α-ketoglutarate, iron(II), and ascorbic acid to oxidize 5-methylcytosine to 5-carboxylcytosine. This oxidation step is performed under optimized conditions to ensure complete conversion, with the second round of oxidation serving to eliminate any residual unoxidized 5mC. The resulting 5-carboxylcytosine is then reduced to dihydrouracil using pyridine borane in an aqueous buffer at pH 4.3 and 37°C for 16 hours. During this reduction, the carboxyl group is removed, and the ring structure is saturated, yielding dihydrouracil, which is not recognized as cytosine during PCR amplification and is instead read as thymine. Unmodified cytosine and glucosylated 5-hydroxymethylcytosine remain unchanged, and thus retain their original C signal in sequencing reads. The result is a sequencing profile in which only 5mC sites appear as C-to-T transitions, while all other cytosines remain as C, enabling direct, quantitative, and subtraction-free mapping of 5mC across the genome. The method achieves a conversion efficiency of 97.6% for 5mC in spike-in controls, with a false-positive rate of only 0.24% at unmodified cytosine sites, and demonstrates a mapping rate of 90.7% in whole-genome sequencing of mouse embryonic stem cells, significantly outperforming all existing bisulfite-based and bisulfite-free methods for 5mC detection.

The second method, referred to as CAPS, is designed for the specific detection of 5-hydroxymethylcytosine. This method employs a chemical oxidation step to convert 5hmC to 5fC, followed by pyridine borane reduction to convert 5fC to dihydrouracil. The key innovation in CAPS lies in the selection of potassium ruthenate (K₂RuO₄) as the oxidizing agent, which has been shown to be more efficient and less damaging to DNA than the previously used potassium perruthenate (KRuO₄). To further enhance the efficiency and specificity of this oxidation, the protocol incorporates several critical optimizations. First, double-stranded DNA library preparation is employed instead of single-stranded protocols, which improves ligation efficiency and reduces bias. Second, a uracil-containing loop-structured adaptor is used during ligation, which is subsequently cleaved by USER enzyme (a mixture of uracil-DNA glycosylase and endonuclease VIII), leaving 5′ and 3′ phosphate ends that protect the DNA from oxidative damage during the K₂RuO₄ treatment. Third, a two-step oxidation protocol is implemented, in which the initial oxidation is followed by a second addition of oxidant, increasing the conversion rate of 5hmC to 5fC from 82.8% to 97.2% as measured by HPLC-MS/MS. A critical discovery in this method is the identification of 2-methylpyridine borane (pic-borane) as the optimal reducing agent for single-stranded DNA. While pyridine borane, optimized for double-stranded DNA, shows poor conversion efficiency (65.8%) and elevated false-positive rates (1.3%) on single-stranded DNA, pic-borane achieves a conversion rate of 83.1% for 5hmC with a false-positive rate of only 0.72%. This makes pic-borane uniquely suited for the CAPS workflow, where DNA is denatured prior to oxidation. The resulting dihydrouracil is then amplified and sequenced as thymine, while unmodified cytosine and 5-methylcytosine remain unchanged. The result is a sequencing profile in which only 5hmC sites appear as C-to-T transitions, enabling direct, quantitative, and subtraction-free mapping of 5hmC. CAPS detects over 1.7 million 5hmC sites in mouse embryonic stem cells, with a mapping rate of 89.4%, a base quality score exceeding 35 at cytosine positions, and a correlation coefficient of 0.79 with TAB-seq and 0.67 with ACE-seq, all of which are superior to the performance of those methods. Importantly, the subtraction of TAPS from TAPSβ, which has been used in prior studies to estimate 5hmC, yields a correlation of only 0.54 with TAB-seq, demonstrating that the subtraction-free approach of CAPS provides significantly more accurate and reliable data.

The third method, referred to as PS, is designed for the direct detection of both 5-formylcytosine and 5-carboxylcytosine. This method applies pyridine borane reduction directly to genomic DNA without any prior enzymatic or chemical modification. Under the same conditions used in TAPSβ and CAPS, pyridine borane efficiently reduces both 5fC and 5caC to dihydrouracil, which is then sequenced as thymine. Unmodified cytosine, 5mC, and 5hmC are unaffected, allowing for the specific identification of these two oxidation intermediates. The method achieves a conversion efficiency of 76.8% for 5fC and 93.8% for 5caC in spike-in controls, with a false-positive rate of only 0.27%. This low background noise is critical for detecting these low-abundance modifications, which are present at less than 0.002% of total cytosines in most mammalian genomes. The method is particularly effective in regulatory regions, where 5fC and 5caC are enriched, and enables the detection of modification signals at key pluripotency genes such as Nanog, which were previously only detectable through enrichment-based methods. The simplicity and directness of PS make it an ideal tool for studying the dynamics of active DNA demethylation, as it captures both intermediates in a single assay without requiring separate experiments or complex bioinformatic subtraction.

The fourth method, referred to as PS-c, is designed for the specific detection of 5-carboxylcytosine. This method builds upon PS by incorporating a selective blocking step that prevents 5-formylcytosine from being reduced. In this step, genomic DNA is treated with O-ethylhydroxylamine in MES buffer at pH 5.0 for four hours, which forms a stable oxime adduct with the formyl group of 5fC, rendering it chemically inert to pyridine borane reduction. 5-Carboxylcytosine, however, remains fully reactive under these conditions and is subsequently reduced to dihydrouracil by pyridine borane. The result is a sequencing profile in which only 5caC sites appear as C-to-T transitions, while 5fC sites remain as cytosine. PS-c achieves a conversion efficiency of 95.3% for 5caC and a false-positive rate of 0.22%, with a conversion rate of only 15.2% for 5fC, demonstrating exceptional specificity. This method provides the first reliable means of mapping 5caC at single-base resolution, enabling the study of its role in transcriptional regulation, enhancer activation, and the final steps of active demethylation pathways.

Each of these methods is integrated into a standardized workflow that begins with the extraction of high-quality genomic DNA, followed by fragmentation using a Covaris M220 instrument to generate fragments of 200–400 base pairs for CAPS and 300–500 base pairs for the other methods. Fragmented DNA is then subjected to end-repair and A-tailing using the KAPA Hyper Prep Kit, followed by ligation of NEBNext adaptors containing a uracil-containing loop structure. In the CAPS protocol, the USER enzyme is added directly to the ligation reaction to cleave the loop and generate phosphorylated ends, which are protected from oxidative damage. The DNA is then purified using Ampure XP beads, with a critical modification in CAPS: 80% acetonitrile:H₂O is used instead of ethanol to wash the beads, which improves the recovery of oxidized DNA. For TAPSβ, the DNA is incubated with β-glucosyltransferase and UDP-glucose for one hour at 37°C, followed by two rounds of TET oxidation and pyridine borane reduction. For CAPS, the DNA is denatured with sodium hydroxide, oxidized with K₂RuO₄ in two steps, and then reduced with pic-borane. For PS and PS-c, the DNA is directly treated with pyridine borane, with PS-c including the prior O-ethylhydroxylamine blocking step. All samples are purified using Zymo-IC columns and amplified using KAPA HiFi HotStart Uracil+ ReadyMix PCR for four cycles, with dual-indexed Illumina primers. Libraries are sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platforms using 150-bp paired-end reads, with no PhiX spike-in required due to the high sequence complexity preserved by these methods.

The data analysis pipeline is optimized for each method and includes trimming of adaptor sequences with Trim Galore!, alignment to a combined reference genome containing both the host genome and spike-in sequences using BWA mem, removal of PCR duplicates with Picard, and filtering of low-quality reads with MAPQ < 10. Modified bases are called using asTair v3.3.1, which calculates the ratio of C to C+T at each cytosine position. Regions prone to mapping artifacts, such as ENCODE blacklists and known SNPs, are excluded from analysis. For quantitative estimation of modification levels, maximum likelihood methylation level (MLML) estimation is applied to sites with a minimum coverage of five reads, and modification levels are tiled into 1-kb bins for genome-wide visualization. Statistical significance for modification calling is determined using a binomial test with the false-positive rate as the probability parameter, and Benjamini–Hochberg correction is applied to control for multiple testing. Enrichment analysis is performed using bedtools intersect to annotate modifications relative to genomic features such as promoters, enhancers, and CTCF-binding sites, with background enrichment calculated using ten random samplings of CG sites. The methods are validated across multiple biological replicates and show exceptional reproducibility, with Pearson correlation coefficients exceeding 0.95 between technical replicates and high concordance with published datasets from RRoxBS-seq, TAB-seq, and ACE-seq, while delivering superior data quality metrics in every category.

## EXAMPLES

To demonstrate the efficacy, specificity, and practical utility of the methods described herein, a series of comprehensive experiments were conducted using mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) as a model system. These experiments were designed to validate each of the four novel methods—TAPSβ, CAPS, PS, and PS-c—against established benchmarks, evaluate their performance across multiple metrics, and assess their ability to detect biologically relevant patterns of cytosine modification. The experimental design incorporated the use of synthetic spike-in controls with known modification states, including CpG-methylated lambda DNA, 2-kb-unmodified DNA, and chemically synthesized oligonucleotides containing 5mC, 5hmC, 5fC, and 5caC at defined positions. These spike-ins allowed for the precise quantification of conversion efficiency and false-positive rates under controlled conditions, independent of biological variability. In addition, whole-genome sequencing was performed on mESC genomic DNA, and the resulting data were compared against publicly available datasets from RRoxBS-seq, oxBS-seq, TAB-seq, and ACE-seq to establish concordance and superiority in data quality.

In the first set of experiments, TAPSβ was applied to mESC genomic DNA spiked with 0.5% CpG-methylated lambda DNA, 0.025% 2-kb-unmodified DNA, and 0.025% 5caC spike-in. Following β-glucosyltransferase treatment, TET oxidation, and pyridine borane reduction, sequencing revealed a conversion rate of 97.6% at known 5mC sites in lambda DNA, with a false-positive rate of only 0.24% at unmodified cytosine sites, as determined by analysis of the 2-kb-unmodified control. At 5hmC sites, the conversion rate was reduced to 1.9%, demonstrating effective blocking by glucosylation. The sequencing quality scores at cytosine positions were consistently high, with Phred scores exceeding 35 across the entire read length, and the mapping rate reached 90.7%, significantly higher than the 66.2–68.2% observed in RRoxBS-seq and the 21.4–26.1% in oxBS-seq. Correlation analysis with published RRoxBS-seq and oxBS-seq datasets showed Pearson’s r values of 0.77 and 0.72, respectively, indicating strong agreement with established methods, while the TAPSβ data exhibited markedly improved coverage and reduced noise. When compared to TAPS data generated from the same biological sample, the correlation coefficient was 0.98, confirming that the glucosylation step did not introduce systematic bias and that TAPSβ faithfully reports 5mC levels.

In the second set of experiments, CAPS was applied to mESC genomic DNA using the optimized K₂RuO₄ oxidation and pic-borane reduction protocol. After double oxidation and pic-borane treatment, sequencing revealed a conversion rate of 97.2% at known 5hmC sites in synthetic spike-ins, with a false-positive rate of 0.72% at unmodified cytosines. The conversion rate at 5mC sites was negligible, at 0.8%, confirming the specificity of the method. The mapping rate for CAPS was 89.4%, compared to 61.3% for ACE-seq and 78.9% for TAB-seq, and the base quality scores at cytosine positions were significantly higher than those obtained with ACE-seq, which exhibited a pronounced drop in quality due to the depletion of cytosines in its sequencing library. When compared to published TAB-seq and ACE-seq datasets, CAPS showed Pearson’s r values of 0.79 and 0.67, respectively, in 10-kb bins, demonstrating high concordance. In contrast, the subtraction of TAPS from TAPSβ yielded a Pearson’s r of only 0.54 with TAB-seq and 0.40 with ACE-seq, indicating that the subtraction-based approach introduces substantial noise and inaccuracies, particularly for low-abundance modifications. Genome-wide analysis revealed 1,762,287 high-confidence 5hmC sites in mESCs, with enrichment at enhancers, insulators, and CTCF-binding sites, consistent with prior literature. The distribution of 5hmC across regulatory elements was more sharply defined in CAPS than in ACE-seq or TAB-seq, and the signal-to-noise ratio was significantly improved, enabling the detection of subtle, biologically relevant patterns of 5hmC enrichment that were obscured in the subtraction-based methods.

In the third set of experiments, PS was applied to mESC genomic DNA without any pre-treatment, and sequencing revealed a conversion rate of 76.8% at 5fC spike-ins and 93.8% at 5caC spike-ins, with a false-positive rate of 0.27% at unmodified cytosines. The low background noise enabled the detection of 5fC and 5caC signals even in samples with minimal levels of these modifications. When the data were analyzed across genomic features, 5fC and 5caC signals were significantly enriched at H3K4me1- and H3K4me3-marked regions, promoters, and enhancers, with peak signals centered at the transcription start sites of pluripotency genes such as Nanog, Sox2, and Oct4. These findings corroborated previous enrichment-based studies and demonstrated that PS can detect these low-abundance modifications at single-base resolution without the need for antibody pulldown or other enrichment techniques. The method’s sensitivity was further validated by the detection of 5fC/5caC signals in regions previously reported to be enriched in active demethylation, such as the enhancers of lineage-specific genes during early differentiation.

In the fourth set of experiments, PS-c was applied to mESC genomic DNA following O-ethylhydroxylamine blocking of 5fC. Sequencing revealed a conversion rate of 95.3% at 5caC spike-ins and only 15.2% at 5fC spike-ins, demonstrating that the blocking step effectively prevented 5fC reduction while preserving full reactivity of 5caC. The false-positive rate remained at 0.22%, confirming the specificity of the method. When applied to whole-genome data, PS-c detected 5caC sites that were highly enriched at enhancers and promoters, with a distribution pattern that overlapped with, but was distinct from, the broader 5fC/5caC signal detected by PS. This distinction allowed for the clear separation of 5caC from 5fC, providing the first direct evidence of their differential genomic localization. The method enabled the identification of 5caC peaks at key regulatory elements, including the enhancers of genes involved in DNA repair and chromatin remodeling, suggesting a potential role for 5caC beyond its function as a demethylation intermediate.

To further validate the robustness of the methods, replicate experiments were performed across three independent biological samples of mESCs, and technical replicates were conducted for each method. The Pearson correlation coefficients between replicates ranged from 0.93 to 0.98, demonstrating exceptional reproducibility. The methods were also tested on human cancer cell lines, including HeLa and HCT116, and showed consistent performance, with conversion efficiencies and false-positive rates comparable to those observed in mESCs. The scalability of the methods was confirmed by successful library preparation from as little as 10 ng of input DNA, and the compatibility with clinical samples was demonstrated using formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue extracts, where the preservation of DNA integrity enabled reliable detection of 5mC and 5hmC despite prior degradation. Finally, the computational pipeline was validated using multiple alignment and calling algorithms, and the results were consistent across platforms, confirming the generalizability of the methods. Together, these examples demonstrate that the invention provides a complete, reliable, and transformative platform for the direct, quantitative, and subtraction-free profiling of all four cytosine modifications in genomic DNA, with unparalleled accuracy, sensitivity, and applicability across diverse biological and clinical contexts.
