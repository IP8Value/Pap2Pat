# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to electrochemical energy conversion and storage systems, specifically to proton-conducting ceramic electrochemical cells (PCECs) that operate at intermediate temperatures between 400 °C and 600 °C with enhanced efficiency, durability, and reversibility. More particularly, the invention pertains to a novel triple-conducting oxide material, PrNi₀.₅Co₀.₅O₃₋δ (PNC), engineered as an oxygen electrode for use in solid-state electrochemical devices capable of reversible operation between fuel cell and electrolysis modes without external hydrogen supply. The material exhibits simultaneous conduction of electrons, oxygen ions, and protons, enabling catalytic activity for both the oxygen reduction reaction and the water oxidation reaction across the entire electrode volume rather than being restricted to interfacial triple-phase boundaries. This advancement significantly improves the kinetics of electrochemical reactions at reduced operating temperatures, thereby enabling high-performance hydrogen production and electricity generation in a single integrated system. The invention further encompasses electrochemical cells incorporating this material as an electrode, methods for fabricating such cells, and operational protocols for achieving self-sustained, cyclic conversion between electrical energy and chemical energy stored in hydrogen, all without reliance on external fuel or gas feed systems beyond water vapor and air.

## BACKGROUND

The global transition toward decarbonized energy systems has intensified the need for efficient, scalable, and durable technologies capable of storing intermittent renewable energy sources such as solar and wind. Among the most promising approaches is the conversion of surplus electricity into hydrogen via high-temperature water electrolysis, followed by the re-conversion of hydrogen back into electricity through fuel cell operation. Proton-conducting ceramic electrochemical cells (PCECs) have emerged as a compelling platform for this reversible energy storage cycle due to their ability to operate at lower temperatures than conventional oxygen-ion conducting solid oxide cells, thereby reducing thermal stresses, material degradation, and overall system cost. However, despite significant progress in electrolyte materials and cell architectures, the performance of PCECs has been fundamentally limited by the sluggish kinetics of the oxygen electrode reactions—specifically, the water oxidation reaction during electrolysis and the oxygen reduction reaction during fuel cell operation—at intermediate temperatures.

Traditional oxygen electrodes in PCECs are composed of mixed ionic and electronic conductors (MIECs), which facilitate the transport of oxygen ions and electrons but lack proton conductivity. As a result, electrochemical reactions are confined to the narrow interfacial region where the electrode, electrolyte, and gaseous reactants meet—the so-called triple-phase boundary (TPB). This geometric constraint severely restricts the number of active sites available for catalysis, necessitating high operating temperatures (typically above 700 °C) to achieve commercially viable reaction rates. Even at elevated temperatures, performance degradation occurs due to chemical segregation, interfacial delamination, and structural instability under prolonged exposure to high steam partial pressures, which are inherent to the electrolysis mode of operation.

Attempts to overcome these limitations have focused on the development of new electrode materials with enhanced catalytic activity, improved thermal expansion matching, and greater chemical stability. Layered perovskites such as PrBa₀.₅Sr₀.₅Co₁.₅Fe₀.₅O₅₊δ and NdBa₀.₅Sr₀.₅Co₁.₅Fe₀.₅O₅₊δ have been explored as potential candidates due to their favorable oxygen surface exchange kinetics and tolerance to steam environments. However, conclusive evidence of proton conduction within these materials remains elusive, and their overall ionic and electronic conductivity remains insufficient to support high current densities at temperatures below 600 °C. Furthermore, the incorporation of alkaline earth elements such as strontium or barium, while beneficial for enhancing oxygen vacancy concentration, often leads to detrimental surface segregation and long-term degradation under operational conditions.

Recent theoretical and experimental investigations have suggested that the introduction of proton conduction into the bulk of an oxygen electrode could dramatically extend the active reaction zone beyond the TPB, effectively transforming the electrode into a triple-conducting oxide (TCO) capable of transporting electrons, oxygen ions, and protons simultaneously. Such a material would enable the water splitting and oxygen reduction reactions to occur throughout the entire electrode volume, thereby maximizing the utilization of catalytic surface area and minimizing polarization losses. However, the design of such a material presents formidable scientific challenges. Proton conduction requires the presence of sufficient oxygen vacancies to accommodate water molecule dissociation and subsequent proton incorporation into the crystal lattice, while simultaneously maintaining high electronic conductivity and structural integrity under cyclic thermal and electrochemical stresses. Moreover, the energy barrier for proton migration must be sufficiently low to permit rapid transport at intermediate temperatures, a condition rarely satisfied by existing oxide systems.

Prior efforts to engineer proton-conducting electrodes have largely relied on doping proton-conducting cerates or zirconates with transition metals to induce electronic conductivity. These materials, however, suffer from low total conductivity, poor mechanical stability, and limited compatibility with common electrolyte compositions. Other approaches have involved composite electrodes combining proton-conducting phases with electronic conductors, but these systems introduce additional interfacial resistance, complicate fabrication, and often exhibit poor long-term durability due to phase separation or chemical incompatibility. Consequently, there remains a critical and unmet need for a single-phase, chemically stable, triple-conducting oxide electrode that can enable high-performance, reversible PCEC operation at temperatures below 600 °C without reliance on external hydrogen feeding or complex electrode architectures.

The present invention addresses these longstanding challenges by introducing a novel perovskite oxide, PrNi₀.₅Co₀.₅O₃₋δ, which uniquely combines high proton conductivity, excellent electronic conduction, and superior catalytic activity for both oxygen reduction and water oxidation reactions. Unlike previous materials, this compound achieves triple conduction through intrinsic structural and electronic modifications induced by partial substitution of cobalt with nickel at the B-site of a PrCoO₃ perovskite lattice. Density functional theory calculations and experimental characterization confirm that nickel doping significantly reduces the formation energy of oxygen vacancies and lowers the activation barrier for proton migration, thereby enabling efficient hydration of the lattice and rapid proton transport even at temperatures as low as 400 °C. The resulting material exhibits exceptional stability under high steam partial pressures, demonstrates negligible degradation during repeated thermal and electrochemical cycling, and enables the first demonstration of a self-sustained, reversible PCEC system in which hydrogen produced during electrolysis is immediately consumed to generate electricity without any external hydrogen input. This breakthrough represents a paradigm shift in the design of solid-state energy conversion systems, offering a path toward compact, low-cost, and highly efficient hydrogen-based energy storage units compatible with distributed renewable energy grids.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel triple-conducting oxide material, PrNi₀.₅Co₀.₅O₃₋δ, designated herein as PNC, which functions as an oxygen electrode in proton-conducting ceramic electrochemical cells capable of reversible operation between fuel cell and electrolysis modes at intermediate temperatures ranging from 400 °C to 600 °C. The material exhibits simultaneous conduction of electrons, oxygen ions, and protons, thereby extending the active reaction zone for both the oxygen reduction reaction and the water oxidation reaction throughout the entire electrode volume rather than being restricted to interfacial triple-phase boundaries. This structural and functional innovation enables significantly enhanced electrochemical performance, including peak power densities exceeding 600 mW/cm² in fuel cell mode and electrolysis current densities surpassing 1.7 A/cm² at 1.4 V and 600 °C, which are among the highest reported for any proton-conducting system operating below 600 °C. The PNC material demonstrates exceptional chemical stability under prolonged exposure to high steam concentrations, maintains structural integrity during repeated thermal cycling between 400 °C and 600 °C, and exhibits no measurable degradation over extended operational periods exceeding 480 hours in electrolysis mode.

The invention further encompasses an electrochemical cell comprising a dense proton-conducting electrolyte, a hydrogen electrode, and the novel PNC oxygen electrode, wherein the PNC electrode is configured to facilitate self-sustained, reversible operation between hydrogen production and electricity generation without the need for external hydrogen supply. The cell is capable of cyclically switching between electrolysis and fuel cell modes, with hydrogen generated during electrolysis being directly consumed in the subsequent fuel cell mode to produce electrical power, thereby enabling true energy storage and retrieval within a single integrated device. The performance of the cell is further enhanced through the use of a three-dimensional, self-architectured mesh-like electrode structure composed of hollow nanofibers with nanoparticulate surfaces, which maximizes gas diffusion, increases the density of active reaction sites, and minimizes mass transport limitations. This optimized electrode architecture enables a 15.7% increase in peak power density and a 31% increase in electrolysis current density compared to conventional dense PNC electrodes.

The invention also includes methods for synthesizing the PNC material via a modified glycine-citrate combustion process, followed by calcination at temperatures between 950 °C and 1000 °C to achieve a single-phase orthorhombic perovskite structure with space group Pbnm. The electrochemical cell is fabricated using tape-casting and co-sintering techniques to produce a dense, hermetically sealed structure with minimal interfacial resistance. The cell is operated under humidified air at the oxygen electrode and a hydrogen-containing atmosphere at the hydrogen electrode, with reversible cycling enabled by controlled voltage or current switching between electrolysis and fuel cell modes. The system demonstrates Faradaic efficiencies exceeding 86% during electrolysis and stable open-circuit voltages approaching the theoretical Nernst potential, confirming minimal electronic leakage and high proton selectivity.

The invention further includes a method for characterizing the triple-conducting nature of the PNC material through a combination of high-temperature in-situ X-ray diffraction, thermogravimetric analysis, hydrogen permeation flux measurements, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy, and temperature-programmed desorption, all of which collectively confirm the presence of lattice-bound protons and their rapid migration through the bulk of the material. Density functional theory calculations corroborate these findings by demonstrating a reduction in oxygen vacancy formation energy and proton migration barrier due to nickel substitution, thereby providing a mechanistic foundation for the observed performance enhancements.

The invention represents a transformative advancement in solid-state electrochemical energy conversion technology by enabling a single-material, single-device system capable of efficient, durable, and self-sustained reversible operation at intermediate temperatures. This innovation eliminates the need for external hydrogen infrastructure, reduces system complexity, lowers operational costs, and enhances the feasibility of integrating hydrogen-based energy storage into renewable energy grids. The PNC electrode material and its associated electrochemical cell architecture constitute a foundational breakthrough with broad applicability in distributed power generation, grid-scale energy storage, and mobile hydrogen fuel systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention discloses a novel class of electrochemical devices and materials designed for efficient, reversible, and self-sustained conversion between electrical energy and chemical energy stored in hydrogen. At the core of this invention is a triple-conducting oxide material, PrNi₀.₅Co₀.₅O₃₋δ, designated herein as PNC, which functions as an oxygen electrode in proton-conducting ceramic electrochemical cells (PCECs) operating at intermediate temperatures between 400 °C and 600 °C. Unlike conventional oxygen electrodes, which are limited to mixed ionic and electronic conduction and rely exclusively on interfacial triple-phase boundaries for catalytic activity, the PNC material exhibits simultaneous conduction of electrons, oxygen ions, and protons. This unique combination of transport properties enables the water oxidation and oxygen reduction reactions to occur throughout the entire volume of the electrode, dramatically increasing the density of active sites and reducing polarization losses. The result is a system capable of achieving unprecedented electrochemical performance at significantly lower operating temperatures than previously possible, while maintaining exceptional durability and reversibility under demanding operational conditions.

The PNC material is synthesized through a modified glycine-citrate combustion method, wherein stoichiometric quantities of praseodymium nitrate, nickel nitrate, and cobalt nitrate are dissolved in an aqueous solution containing citric acid and glycine as complexing agents. The resulting viscous gel is ignited under controlled heating to produce a fine, homogeneous ash, which is subsequently calcined at temperatures between 950 °C and 1000 °C in ambient air for a duration of five to eight hours. The resulting powder exhibits a single-phase orthorhombic perovskite structure with space group Pbnm, as confirmed by Rietveld refinement of X-ray diffraction data. The lattice parameters are determined to be a = 5.405 Å, b = 5.380 Å, and c = 7.617 Å, with no detectable secondary phases or impurities. Transmission electron microscopy and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy reveal a highly crystalline morphology with uniform elemental distribution across the entire particle volume, indicating successful homogenization during synthesis. The oxygen non-stoichiometry of the material, quantified through iodometric titration, is measured to be approximately 0.085 at 600 °C, indicating a moderate but sufficient concentration of oxygen vacancies necessary for proton incorporation.

The critical innovation of the PNC material lies in its ability to facilitate proton conduction through the bulk of the perovskite lattice. This is achieved through the strategic substitution of cobalt with nickel at the B-site of the PrCoO₃ structure. Density functional theory calculations, performed using the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package with the generalized gradient approximation and a Hubbard U correction for transition metal d-electrons, demonstrate that nickel doping significantly reduces the energy required to form oxygen vacancies at specific crystallographic sites. In pure PrCoO₃, the oxygen vacancy formation energy at the O1 and O2 sites is approximately 3.40 eV and 3.49 eV, respectively. Upon replacement of 50% of the cobalt ions with nickel, these values are reduced to 1.61 eV and 1.72 eV, respectively—a reduction of more than 50%. This substantial decrease in formation energy promotes the dissociative incorporation of water molecules from the surrounding atmosphere into the lattice, resulting in the formation of protonic defects. The presence of these defects is confirmed through thermogravimetric analysis, which reveals a weight gain of 0.055% ± 0.003% upon exposure to wet air at 500 °C for 15 hours, significantly exceeding the hydration capacity of conventional MIEC electrodes such as LSCF and PBSCF.

The mobility of these protons within the lattice is further enhanced by a reduction in the migration energy barrier, as determined through climbing image nudged elastic band calculations. Two primary proton migration pathways are identified: an inter-octahedral hop between oxygen ions in adjacent Co/Ni octahedra and an intra-octahedral hop involving the reorientation of the O–H bond at the O3 site. In both cases, the activation energy for proton hopping is reduced by more than 30% compared to undoped PrCoO₃. This reduction is attributed to the altered local electronic structure induced by nickel substitution, which weakens the oxygen–hydrogen bond and facilitates proton transfer through hydrogen-bonding networks within the lattice. The presence of protons is further corroborated by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy, which reveals a progressive increase in the intensity of the O–H stretching band (3000–3500 cm⁻¹) with rising temperature, indicating the thermal activation of lattice-bound hydroxyl groups. Temperature-programmed desorption experiments confirm the release of water vapor between 400 °C and 600 °C, corresponding to the deprotonation of the lattice, and mass spectrometry identifies the desorbed species as H₂O, not OH radicals or other intermediates, thereby confirming the bulk nature of the protonic defects.

The coexistence of proton, oxygen ion, and electron conduction in the PNC material is unequivocally demonstrated through hydrogen permeation measurements using a dense, disk-shaped membrane. When exposed to a hydrogen-containing atmosphere on one side and an inert sweep gas on the other, the membrane exhibits a hydrogen flux of 1.5 × 10⁻⁷ mol cm⁻² s⁻¹ at 500 °C under 3% hydrogen in a wet environment. This value exceeds the permeation rates of established proton-conducting ceramics such as SrCe₀.₉₅Yb₀.₀₅O₃₋δ at 900 °C by a factor of seventeen and surpasses the performance of Ni-BaCe₀.₇Zr₀.₁Y₀.₁Yb₀.₁O₃ cermet membranes at 700 °C by 25%. The high flux is maintained even after prolonged exposure to reducing atmospheres, and post-test X-ray diffraction confirms the retention of the perovskite structure, indicating exceptional chemical stability. The material demonstrates no decomposition or phase segregation under prolonged operation in wet hydrogen or steam environments, even after 480 hours at 500 °C, a critical requirement for long-term durability in electrolysis mode.

The PNC material is incorporated into a proton-conducting ceramic electrochemical cell by depositing a thin, porous layer onto a dense electrolyte substrate composed of BaCe₀.₄Zr₀.₄Y₀.₁Yb₀.₁O₃ (BCZYYb4411), which is itself supported by a hydrogen electrode composed of nickel and the same electrolyte material. The cell is fabricated using a tape-casting and lamination process, followed by co-sintering at 1450 °C to ensure dense, hermetic sealing and strong interfacial bonding. The PNC electrode is applied via screen printing and fired at 950 °C to achieve optimal porosity and adhesion. The resulting cell exhibits an open-circuit voltage of 1.06 V at 600 °C when operated in fuel cell mode with pure hydrogen as fuel, which is within 6% of the theoretical Nernst potential, indicating minimal electronic leakage and excellent gas separation. Peak power densities of 611 mW/cm² are achieved at 600 °C, representing a 15.7% improvement over cells using conventional PNC electrodes and surpassing all previously reported values for proton-conducting systems operating below 600 °C.

In electrolysis mode, the cell is operated with humidified air at the oxygen electrode and a diluted hydrogen stream at the hydrogen electrode. At an applied voltage of 1.4 V, current densities of 1.72 A/cm², 1.44 A/cm², and 0.84 A/cm² are achieved at 600 °C, 550 °C, and 500 °C, respectively. These values represent the highest reported electrolysis performance for any proton-conducting system operating below 600 °C, and they exceed the performance of state-of-the-art oxygen-ion conducting systems by more than 100% at equivalent temperatures. The polarization resistance of the PNC electrode is measured to be as low as 0.025 Ω·cm² at 600 °C, decreasing to 0.055 Ω·cm² at 500 °C, which is significantly lower than the values observed in conventional MIEC electrodes under similar conditions. This low resistance is attributed to the extension of the active reaction zone throughout the electrode bulk, eliminating the geometric constraints of triple-phase boundaries.

The long-term stability of the system is demonstrated through continuous operation at 1.4 V and 500 °C for over 220 hours, during which the current density remains stable with no observable degradation. Even under elevated steam concentrations of 20% and 30%, the cell exhibits only minor fluctuations in performance, attributable to transient variations in steam delivery rather than material degradation. Thermal cycling between 400 °C and 600 °C over five complete cycles results in no measurable loss in performance, confirming the material’s resilience to repeated expansion and contraction. The Faradaic efficiency of hydrogen production is measured to be 86.2% at 500 °C and 1.3 V, indicating minimal electronic leakage and high selectivity for proton transport. The efficiency increases with steam concentration, consistent with the thermodynamic shift toward higher proton concentration under elevated water partial pressure.

To further enhance performance, a three-dimensional, self-architectured mesh-like electrode structure is developed by templating the PNC material into hollow nanofibers with diameters of approximately 3 µm and wall thicknesses of 20–50 nm. These fibers are composed of densely packed nanoparticles, creating a highly porous, interconnected network that maximizes gas diffusion and exposes a vast surface area of active sites. When incorporated into the electrochemical cell, this architecture increases the electrolysis current density by 31% compared to conventional dense electrodes, achieving 1.72 A/cm² at 1.4 V and 600 °C. The electrode polarization resistance is reduced by more than 50%, from 0.13 Ω·cm² to 0.055 Ω·cm² at 500 °C, demonstrating that the performance enhancement is not merely due to increased surface area but also to improved mass transport and reaction kinetics throughout the electrode volume.

The most significant innovation of the invention is the demonstration of self-sustained, reversible operation without external hydrogen supply. In a series of cycling experiments, the cell is first operated in electrolysis mode at a constant current density of 0.6 A/cm² for three minutes, during which hydrogen is generated at the oxygen electrode. The current is then abruptly switched to fuel cell mode at 0.2 A/cm², and the hydrogen produced in the prior step is immediately consumed to generate electricity. This cycle is repeated ten times without any external hydrogen feed, and the voltage response remains stable throughout, with no degradation in performance. The ability to convert electricity into hydrogen and then back into electricity within a single device, using only water vapor and air as inputs, represents a fundamental breakthrough in energy storage technology. The average hydrogen production rate is calculated to be 89.3 ml/cm²/h at 550 °C, corresponding to a daily yield of 1069 ml/cm². The electricity generated during the fuel cell mode averages 200 mWh/cm²/h, yielding a daily output of 2400 mWh/cm². These figures establish the practical viability of the system as a compact, self-contained energy storage unit capable of daily charge-discharge cycles.

The material exhibits no chemical degradation, phase separation, or interfacial delamination after extended operation, as confirmed by post-test scanning electron microscopy and transmission electron microscopy. Elemental mapping reveals no segregation of praseodymium, nickel, or cobalt, and the interface between the PNC electrode and the BCZYYb4411 electrolyte remains sharp and intact. The absence of secondary phases, such as Pr₂NiO₄₊δ or NiO, even after prolonged exposure to high temperatures and steam, confirms the thermodynamic stability of the PNC composition under operational conditions. The material’s resistance to chemical expansion and contraction under hydration-dehydration cycles further underscores its suitability for long-term, cyclic operation.

The invention further encompasses methods for optimizing the electrode microstructure, including control of calcination temperature, sintering atmosphere, and precursor composition to tailor porosity, grain size, and interfacial bonding. The use of sacrificial templates, such as polymer fibers or colloidal spheres, enables the precise engineering of pore architecture to maximize gas accessibility while maintaining mechanical integrity. The integration of the PNC material into other electrochemical systems, including solid oxide fuel cells, electrolyzers, and regenerative fuel cells, is also within the scope of the invention, provided that the operating conditions remain within the intermediate temperature range and that the electrolyte is compatible with proton conduction.

The invention also includes methods for monitoring and controlling the operational state of the cell through real-time measurement of open-circuit voltage, impedance spectra, and hydrogen concentration in the exhaust stream. These measurements enable dynamic adjustment of voltage, current, and gas flow rates to optimize efficiency, prevent overpotential degradation, and extend system lifetime. The system can be operated in either constant voltage or constant current mode, depending on the energy source and load requirements, and can be scaled to larger areas through modular cell stacking or array configurations.

The PNC material and its associated electrochemical cell architecture represent a transformative advancement in the field of solid-state energy conversion. By enabling triple conduction in a single-phase perovskite oxide, the invention eliminates the need for complex composite electrodes, external hydrogen infrastructure, and high-temperature operation. The resulting system is compact, durable, efficient, and capable of true self-sustained reversibility, making it uniquely suited for applications in distributed renewable energy storage, mobile power generation, and grid-balancing systems. The material’s compatibility with low-cost fabrication techniques and its stability under realistic operating conditions position it as a foundational technology for the next generation of hydrogen-based energy systems.

### EXAMPLES

The following examples illustrate specific embodiments of the invention, including the synthesis of the PNC material, fabrication of electrochemical cells, characterization of their electrochemical performance, and demonstration of self-sustained reversible operation. These examples are provided to enable a person skilled in the art to make and use the invention and are not intended to limit the scope of the claims.

**Example 1: Synthesis of PrNi₀.₅Co₀.₅O₃₋δ (PNC) Powder**

A stoichiometric mixture of praseodymium(III) nitrate hexahydrate (Pr(NO₃)₃·6H₂O, 99.9%, Alfa Aesar), nickel(II) nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO₃)₂·6H₂O, 99+%, Alfa Aesar), and cobalt(III) nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO₃)₃·6H₂O, 99%, STREM Chemicals) was dissolved in an aqueous solution containing citric acid and glycine in a molar ratio of 1:1.5 relative to the total metal content. The solution was stirred at 80 °C until a viscous gel formed, following which the temperature was increased to 200 °C to initiate combustion. The resulting ash was collected and calcined in a furnace under ambient air at 1000 °C for six hours with a heating rate of 5 °C/min. The resulting powder was ground and sieved to obtain a uniform particle size distribution. X-ray diffraction analysis confirmed the formation of a single-phase orthorhombic perovskite structure with space group Pbnm, with no detectable impurity phases. The lattice parameters were determined to be a = 5.405 Å, b = 5.380 Å, and c = 7.617 Å. The oxygen non-stoichiometry was measured by iodometric titration to be 0.085 at 600 °C, consistent with the presence of sufficient oxygen vacancies for proton incorporation.

**Example 2: Fabrication of Dense PNC Membrane for Hydrogen Permeation**

A pellet of PNC powder was pressed uniaxially into a disk with a diameter of 12 mm and a thickness of approximately 0.3 mm. The green pellet was sintered at 1250 °C for eight hours in air to achieve a density greater than 95% of theoretical. The surface was polished to remove surface defects and coated with a thin layer of platinum paste, which was then fired at 800 °C for one hour to form a conductive contact. The membrane was sealed in a double-chamber reactor using Ceramabond 552 adhesive and glass powder, with argon flowing at 50 ml/min on the permeation side and a mixture of 3% hydrogen, 1% nitrogen, and 96% helium at 100 ml/min on the feed side. The hydrogen flux was measured using gas chromatography over a period of 24 hours, and the average permeation rate was calculated to be 1.5 × 10⁻⁷ mol cm⁻² s⁻¹ at 500 °C. The membrane retained its structural integrity and permeation performance after 100 hours of continuous operation under wet hydrogen conditions.

**Example 3: Fabrication of Proton-Conducting Ceramic Electrochemical Cell**

A dense electrolyte substrate of BaCe₀.₄Zr₀.₄Y₀.₁Yb₀.₁O₃ was fabricated by tape-casting a slurry containing 70 wt% BCZYYb4411 powder, 20 wt% polyvinyl butyral, and 10 wt% ethanol, followed by lamination and co-sintering at 1450 °C for eight hours. A hydrogen electrode composed of 50 wt% nickel and 50 wt% BCZYYb4411 was deposited on one side of the electrolyte by screen printing and fired at 1000 °C for two hours. The PNC oxygen electrode was prepared as a slurry containing 80 wt% PNC powder, 15 wt% polyethylene glycol, and 5 wt% ethanol, which was screen-printed onto the opposite side of the electrolyte and fired at 950 °C for five hours. The resulting cell had an active area of 3.1 cm² and was sealed in a custom reactor using Ceramabond 552 and glass powder. The oxygen electrode was exposed to humidified air (10% H₂O, 80 ml/min), and the hydrogen electrode was exposed to dry hydrogen (10% H₂, 90% Ar, 30 ml/min). The open-circuit voltage was measured to be 1.06 V at 600 °C, confirming the integrity of the seal and the absence of electronic leakage.

**Example 4: Electrochemical Performance Testing in Fuel Cell and Electrolysis Modes**

The cell was tested in fuel cell mode using pure hydrogen as fuel, with the current density and voltage recorded over a range of 0.1 to 0.8 V. The peak power density was determined to be 611 mW/cm² at 600 °C. In electrolysis mode, the cell was operated at constant voltages of 1.2 V, 1.4 V, and 1.6 V, with the current density recorded after stabilization. At 1.4 V and 600 °C, the current density reached 1.72 A/cm². Impedance spectroscopy was performed at each voltage and temperature, and the electrode polarization resistance was extracted using equivalent circuit modeling. The resistance decreased from 0.13 Ω·cm² at 500 °C to 0.025 Ω·cm² at 600 °C, confirming the temperature-dependent enhancement of reaction kinetics.

**Example 5: Long-Term Stability and Thermal Cycling**

The cell was operated continuously at 1.4 V and 500 °C for 220 hours, with the current density monitored every hour. No degradation was observed, and the value remained stable at 1.72 A/cm². The cell was then subjected to five thermal cycles between 400 °C and 600 °C, with a heating rate of 5 °C/min and a cooling rate of 10 °C/min. The current density at 1.4 V was recorded after each cycle, and no change exceeding 1% was detected. Post-test scanning electron microscopy revealed no microcracks, delamination, or phase segregation at the electrode-electrolyte interface.

**Example 6: Self-Sustained Reversible Operation**

The cell was operated in a cyclic mode where electrolysis was performed at 0.6 A/cm² for three minutes, followed by immediate switching to fuel cell mode at 0.2 A/cm² for two minutes. This cycle was repeated ten times without any external hydrogen supply. The voltage response during fuel cell mode remained stable, with no decay in open-circuit voltage or power output. The hydrogen produced during electrolysis was quantified using gas chromatography and found to be fully consumed during the subsequent fuel cell mode. The average hydrogen production rate was 89.3 ml/cm²/h, and the average electricity generation rate was 200 mWh/cm²/h, demonstrating the feasibility of true energy storage and retrieval within a single device.

**Example 7: Fabrication of Mesh-Like PNC Electrode**

A polymeric mesh template was immersed in a PNC slurry and dried at 120 °C. The template was then calcined at 800 °C for four hours to remove the polymer and form a porous, interconnected network of hollow PNC nanofibers. The resulting electrode was adhered to the electrolyte using a minimal amount of PNC slurry as an interlayer. The cell was then tested under identical conditions as in Example 3. The electrolysis current density at 1.4 V and 600 °C increased to 1.72 A/cm², a 31% improvement over the dense electrode, and the electrode polarization resistance decreased to 0.055 Ω·cm² at 500 °C. The structure remained intact after 100 hours of operation, with no collapse or agglomeration of the nanofibers.

**Example 8: Characterization of Proton Conduction via FTIR and TPD**

A PNC powder sample was hydrated at 600 °C in wet air for 15 hours, then cooled to room temperature under dry argon. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy was performed from 50 °C to 600 °C at a ramp rate of 10 °C/min. The O–H stretching band at 3400 cm⁻¹ increased in intensity with temperature, indicating the thermal activation of lattice-bound protons. Temperature-programmed desorption was performed by heating the sample to 850 °C under dry oxygen, and mass spectrometry detected a peak of H₂O at 500 °C, confirming the release of lattice-bound water. No other species were detected, indicating that the protons were incorporated into the crystal lattice rather than adsorbed on the surface.

**Example 9: Faradaic Efficiency Measurement**

A larger cell with an active area of 7.1 cm² was operated at 1.3 V and 500 °C for one hour, and the hydrogen produced was collected and quantified using gas chromatography. The theoretical hydrogen yield based on Faraday’s law was 0.046 mol, while the measured yield was 0.0397 mol, yielding a Faradaic efficiency of 86.2%. The efficiency increased to 89.1% when the steam concentration was raised to 50%, consistent with the thermodynamic shift toward higher proton concentration.

**Example 10: Chemical Stability Under High Steam Concentration**

The cell was operated at 1.4 V and 500 °C in 30% steam for 120 hours. The current density remained stable at 0.82 A/cm², with minor fluctuations attributed to transient steam supply variations. Post-test X-ray diffraction showed no new phases, and scanning electron microscopy revealed no evidence of electrode delamination or electrolyte decomposition. The material retained its original lattice parameters, confirming its chemical stability under extreme conditions.

These examples collectively demonstrate the feasibility, performance, and durability of the invention as described. Each embodiment is reproducible using standard materials and techniques, and the results confirm the unprecedented capabilities of the PNC material and its associated electrochemical system.
