# DESCRIPTION

## INTRODUCTION

The biological machinery responsible for protein synthesis has evolved with extraordinary precision to ensure the faithful translation of genetic information into functional polypeptides. This fidelity is maintained through a multi-layered system involving the selective recognition of amino acids by aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, the accurate codon-anticodon pairing between messenger RNA and transfer RNA, and subsequent proofreading mechanisms that correct mischarged tRNAs before they enter the ribosome. Despite these safeguards, the system is not infallible, particularly when structurally similar non-canonical molecules are present in sufficient concentration to compete with the canonical amino acids. Such molecular mimics can exploit the inherent flexibility of the translation apparatus, leading to their erroneous incorporation into nascent polypeptide chains. This phenomenon, known as misincorporation, results in the production of aberrant proteins that often misfold, aggregate, and disrupt cellular homeostasis. The consequences of such errors are particularly severe in post-mitotic cells such as neurons, where the accumulation of damaged proteins cannot be diluted through cell division and must be managed by limited proteostatic mechanisms. The identification of environmental agents capable of inducing such errors has therefore become a critical area of investigation in the study of neurodegenerative disease.

One such agent is β-N-methylamino-l-alanine, a non-protein amino acid that has been detected in cyanobacteria, aquatic food chains, and human brain tissues associated with neurodegenerative conditions. Prior studies have suggested that BMAA may be taken up by cells and accumulate in protein fractions, but the mechanistic basis for its association with proteins remained ambiguous. Some hypotheses proposed that BMAA might bind non-covalently to proteins through electrostatic or hydrophobic interactions, while others speculated that it could be incorporated during translation. However, definitive evidence demonstrating that BMAA is covalently linked into the peptide backbone of proteins via the ribosomal machinery had not been established. The present invention resolves this uncertainty by demonstrating, through multiple orthogonal lines of experimental evidence, that BMAA is actively and specifically misincorporated into human proteins during de novo protein synthesis. This incorporation occurs in a manner that mirrors the natural process of amino acid selection, is dependent on active translation, and is competitively inhibited by the canonical amino acid l-serine. Furthermore, the misincorporation of BMAA leads to the formation of structurally abnormal proteins that exhibit hallmark features of pathological aggregation, including resistance to denaturing agents, induction of cellular autofluorescence, and triggering of apoptotic pathways. These findings establish a direct causal link between environmental exposure to BMAA and the initiation of protein misfolding events that underlie neurodegeneration. The invention further discloses a therapeutic strategy based on the competitive inhibition of BMAA misincorporation by exogenous l-serine, thereby preventing the formation of toxic protein variants and mitigating downstream cellular dysfunction. This represents a novel, mechanism-based intervention that targets the root cause of BMAA-induced toxicity rather than its downstream consequences.

The significance of this discovery extends beyond the specific context of BMAA. It establishes a new paradigm for understanding how environmental toxins can induce neurodegenerative disease not merely through direct neurotoxicity or oxidative stress, but through the subversion of the fundamental process of protein synthesis itself. This mechanism may apply to other non-protein amino acids and structurally analogous compounds that share similar physicochemical properties with canonical amino acids. The invention provides not only a diagnostic framework for identifying misincorporation events in human tissues but also a therapeutic platform for preventing such events through targeted amino acid supplementation. By demonstrating that the ribosome, the central engine of protein production, can be hijacked by environmental molecules to generate endogenous toxins, this invention redefines the boundaries between environmental exposure and genetic vulnerability in the etiology of neurodegenerative disorders. It further opens the door to the development of precision interventions that modulate the translational fidelity of specific amino acid sites, offering a new class of neuroprotective therapies grounded in the biochemistry of translation.

## SUMMARY

The invention relates to a method for preventing the misincorporation of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine into human proteins during de novo protein synthesis by administering an effective amount of l-serine to a subject exposed to or at risk of exposure to β-N-methylamino-l-alanine. This method is based on the discovery that β-N-methylamino-l-alanine is actively incorporated into human proteins in place of l-serine through the canonical protein synthesis machinery, resulting in the formation of aberrant polypeptides that misfold, aggregate, and induce cellular toxicity. The misincorporation event is dependent on active translation, as demonstrated by its complete inhibition in the presence of cycloheximide, a known inhibitor of ribosomal elongation. Furthermore, the incorporation of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine into proteins is competitively inhibited by l-serine in a concentration-dependent manner, while the non-protein isomer d-serine, which is not utilized by mammalian translational machinery, has no inhibitory effect. This specificity confirms that the inhibition is mediated through competition at the level of aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase recognition and tRNA charging, rather than through non-specific cellular effects.

The invention further encompasses a method for detecting the presence of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine incorporated into proteins in biological samples, comprising the steps of isolating proteins from a tissue or cell sample, subjecting the proteins to acid hydrolysis or proteolytic digestion to release amino acid residues, and quantifying the liberated β-N-methylamino-l-alanine using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry with selective reaction monitoring. The method utilizes unique fragmentation patterns and retention time profiles that distinguish β-N-methylamino-l-alanine from its structural isomers, including 2,4-diaminobutyric acid and N-(2-aminoethyl)glycine, thereby enabling unambiguous identification of protein-incorporated β-N-methylamino-l-alanine. This detection method provides a diagnostic tool for identifying individuals exposed to β-N-methylamino-l-alanine who have undergone misincorporation events, thereby enabling early intervention before the onset of clinical neurodegeneration.

The invention additionally includes a method for assessing the risk of neurodegenerative disease in a subject by measuring the levels of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine incorporated into proteins in a biological sample, wherein elevated levels correlate with increased risk of developing amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Parkinsonism dementia complex, or Alzheimer’s disease. The invention further provides a composition for therapeutic use comprising l-serine in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, wherein the composition is administered orally, intravenously, or intrathecally to a subject to prevent or reduce the misincorporation of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine into proteins. The dosage of l-serine is selected to achieve a plasma or cerebrospinal fluid concentration sufficient to outcompete β-N-methylamino-l-alanine for aminoacylation by seryl-tRNA synthetase, thereby restoring translational fidelity. The invention further encompasses a kit for the detection and prevention of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine misincorporation, comprising a first component containing l-serine in a therapeutically effective amount and a second component comprising reagents for the extraction, hydrolysis, and liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry-based quantification of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine from biological samples.

The invention further demonstrates that the misincorporation of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine into proteins induces protein aggregation, as evidenced by the development of autofluorescent inclusions in human fibroblast and neuroblastoma cell lines, which are abrogated by co-administration of l-serine. These aggregates are resistant to denaturation by reducing agents and detergents, confirming their covalent nature as peptide-bonded adducts rather than non-specific binding events. The formation of these aggregates is accompanied by activation of apoptotic pathways, as demonstrated by increased Annexin V binding and caspase activation, which is also prevented by l-serine supplementation. The invention thus establishes a causal chain linking environmental exposure to β-N-methylamino-l-alanine, its misincorporation into proteins, the generation of misfolded and aggregated protein species, and the induction of neuronal cell death. By interrupting this chain at its earliest molecular step—misincorporation—the invention provides a novel, mechanism-based therapeutic strategy for preventing neurodegenerative disease in individuals exposed to cyanobacterial toxins or other environmental sources of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention is directed to a novel and unexpected discovery that β-N-methylamino-l-alanine, a non-protein amino acid found in cyanobacteria and certain dietary sources, is misincorporated into human proteins during translation in place of the canonical amino acid l-serine. This misincorporation occurs through the normal machinery of protein synthesis, specifically via the seryl-tRNA synthetase, which erroneously activates β-N-methylamino-l-alanine and charges it onto tRNA^Ser, thereby enabling its delivery to the ribosome and subsequent incorporation into growing polypeptide chains. The structural similarity between β-N-methylamino-l-alanine and l-serine—both possessing a small, polar side chain with a hydroxyl group and a primary amine—allows β-N-methylamino-l-alanine to mimic l-serine sufficiently to evade the proofreading mechanisms of the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase. This results in the production of proteins containing β-N-methylamino-l-alanine at positions normally occupied by serine, leading to conformational instability, loss of function, and aggregation. The invention demonstrates that this process is not a passive binding event but an active, ribosome-dependent incorporation that requires ongoing protein synthesis, as confirmed by the complete inhibition of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine incorporation when translation is blocked by cycloheximide.

The misincorporation of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine into proteins was first observed in human cell lines derived from diverse tissues, including lung fibroblasts, neuroblastoma cells, and endothelial cells, all of which exhibited time-dependent accumulation of radiolabeled β-N-methylamino-l-alanine in the protein fraction following exposure to tritiated β-N-methylamino-l-alanine. The incorporation was not observed in non-protein fractions, and the radiolabel remained tightly associated with the protein pellet even after extensive washing with detergents and reducing agents, indicating a covalent linkage rather than non-specific adsorption. The persistence of the radiolabel after acid hydrolysis and proteolytic digestion confirmed that β-N-methylamino-l-alanine was integrated into the peptide backbone as a constituent amino acid residue. These findings were corroborated by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry, which identified β-N-methylamino-l-alanine in hydrolyzed protein samples with high specificity and sensitivity, using multiple diagnostic fragmentation transitions and retention time matching to synthetic standards. The detection limits of the method were established at 48 femtomoles for the limit of detection and 0.48 picomoles for the limit of quantification, enabling the detection of trace levels of misincorporation in clinically relevant samples.

The competitive inhibition of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine incorporation by l-serine was a pivotal discovery underlying the therapeutic strategy of the invention. When human cells were exposed to β-N-methylamino-l-alanine in the presence of increasing concentrations of l-serine, the amount of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine incorporated into proteins decreased in a dose-dependent manner. This inhibition was specific to l-serine, as none of the other 19 canonical amino acids produced a comparable reduction in misincorporation. Furthermore, d-serine, the non-protein isomer of l-serine that is not recognized by mammalian aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, had no effect on β-N-methylamino-l-alanine incorporation, confirming that the inhibition was mediated through the canonical translational machinery rather than through non-specific biochemical interactions. The ability of l-serine to outcompete β-N-methylamino-l-alanine for aminoacylation by seryl-tRNA synthetase suggests that the enzyme’s active site discriminates between the two molecules based on subtle differences in stereochemistry and side chain geometry, but that under conditions of elevated β-N-methylamino-l-alanine concentration, the enzyme’s fidelity is compromised. This provides a biochemical rationale for the therapeutic use of l-serine supplementation to restore translational fidelity in individuals exposed to β-N-methylamino-l-alanine.

The biological consequences of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine misincorporation were profound and consistent across multiple cell types. Cells exposed to β-N-methylamino-l-alanine developed intracellular autofluorescent aggregates that were localized to the perinuclear region and cytoplasm, a hallmark of protein misfolding and aggregation. These aggregates were resistant to solubilization by sodium dodecyl sulfate and dithiothreitol, confirming their covalent nature and distinguishing them from non-specific protein aggregates induced by oxidative stress or metal toxicity. The formation of these aggregates correlated with the induction of apoptosis, as evidenced by increased binding of Annexin V to phosphatidylserine externalized on the plasma membrane, without concomitant release of lactate dehydrogenase, indicating that cell death occurred via programmed rather than necrotic pathways. Crucially, co-administration of l-serine completely abrogated both the formation of autofluorescent aggregates and the induction of apoptosis, demonstrating that the toxicity was directly attributable to misincorporation rather than to off-target effects of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine. These findings establish a direct causal relationship between β-N-methylamino-l-alanine misincorporation and the initiation of neurodegenerative pathology.

The invention further encompasses a method for diagnosing individuals at risk of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine-induced neurodegeneration by quantifying the levels of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine incorporated into proteins in accessible biological samples such as peripheral blood mononuclear cells, cerebrospinal fluid, or post-mortem brain tissue. The method involves the isolation of total protein from the sample, followed by acid hydrolysis under controlled conditions to release all amino acid residues without degradation of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine. The hydrolysate is then derivatized with 6-aminoquinolyl-N-hydroxysuccinimidyl carbamate to enhance detection sensitivity and analyzed by ultra-high-pressure liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry. The mass spectrometer is operated in selective reaction monitoring mode, monitoring multiple fragmentation transitions from the precursor ion m/z 459 to product ions at m/z 119, 171, 188, 214, 258, and 289, each of which has been empirically validated as unique to β-N-methylamino-l-alanine and distinct from its isomers. The retention time of the analyte is compared to a synthetic standard, and the ratios of the fragment ion intensities are used to confirm identity and exclude false positives. This method has been validated for intra-day and inter-day precision, with relative standard deviations below 6.5% for peak area and below 0.3% for retention time, and demonstrates linearity over a concentration range spanning five orders of magnitude, with a correlation coefficient greater than 0.99.

The invention further provides a pharmaceutical composition comprising l-serine in a therapeutically effective amount, formulated for oral, intravenous, or intrathecal administration. The composition may be administered as a free amino acid, a salt, a prodrug, or in combination with other amino acids or cofactors that enhance absorption or stability. The dosage is selected to achieve a plasma concentration of l-serine sufficient to saturate the aminoacylation capacity of seryl-tRNA synthetase in the presence of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine, thereby preventing mischarging. Based on in vitro data, a plasma concentration of l-serine exceeding 250 µM is required to achieve significant inhibition of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine incorporation, and therapeutic regimens are designed to maintain plasma levels above this threshold. In clinical settings, this may require daily oral dosing of 1 to 10 grams of l-serine, depending on body weight, metabolic rate, and level of exposure. For individuals with compromised blood-brain barrier integrity or advanced neurodegeneration, intrathecal administration may be employed to achieve higher cerebrospinal fluid concentrations. The composition may be formulated as capsules, tablets, powders, or liquid suspensions, and may be combined with other neuroprotective agents such as antioxidants, chaperone inducers, or anti-inflammatory compounds to provide synergistic protection against downstream consequences of protein misfolding.

The invention also encompasses a diagnostic and therapeutic kit designed for use in clinical and research settings. The kit includes a first component comprising a therapeutically effective amount of l-serine in a stable, sterile formulation, and a second component comprising reagents for the extraction, hydrolysis, derivatization, and quantitative analysis of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine from biological samples. The second component includes acid hydrolysis tubes, derivatization reagents, internal standards, calibration curves, chromatographic columns, and instructions for sample preparation and mass spectrometric analysis. The kit enables clinicians to screen individuals for evidence of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine misincorporation and to initiate l-serine therapy promptly upon detection, thereby preventing the progression of protein aggregation and neuronal loss. The kit may be used in populations at high risk of exposure, including residents of regions with endemic cyanobacterial blooms, consumers of contaminated seafood, individuals with occupational exposure to cyanobacteria, and patients with unexplained neurodegenerative symptoms.

The invention further provides a method for preventing neurodegenerative disease in a subject exposed to β-N-methylamino-l-alanine by administering l-serine prior to or concurrently with exposure. This prophylactic approach is particularly relevant for populations living in geographic areas where cyanobacterial blooms are recurrent, such as the Great Lakes region, Chesapeake Bay, or Guam. In such settings, l-serine supplementation may be implemented as a public health intervention to reduce the incidence of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Parkinsonism dementia complex, and Alzheimer’s disease. The invention further provides a method for monitoring the efficacy of l-serine therapy by periodically quantifying β-N-methylamino-l-alanine levels in protein samples from the subject, allowing for dose adjustment based on individual pharmacokinetics and exposure levels. The invention thus bridges the gap between environmental toxicology and molecular medicine by providing a mechanism-based, biomarker-guided intervention that targets the root cause of toxin-induced neurodegeneration.

## EXAMPLES

In the first example, human MRC-5 lung fibroblasts were cultured in amino acid-depleted Hank’s buffered salt solution supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and exposed to 31.25 nM of tritiated β-N-methylamino-l-alanine for periods of 2, 4, and 16 hours. At each time point, cells were washed extensively with phosphate-buffered saline containing 10% fetal bovine serum to remove unincorporated radiolabel, lysed by three cycles of freeze-thawing in 0.1% Triton X-100, and total protein concentration was determined by bicinchoninic acid assay. The radiolabel associated with the protein fraction was isolated by precipitation with 10% trichloroacetic acid, washed three times with cold trichloroacetic acid, and dissolved in neat formic acid. Liquid scintillation counting revealed a time-dependent increase in disintegrations per minute per microgram of protein, with a 3.8-fold increase observed between 2 and 16 hours. This demonstrated that β-N-methylamino-l-alanine was not merely adsorbed to the cell surface or trapped in intracellular vesicles, but was progressively incorporated into newly synthesized proteins.

In the second example, parallel cultures of MRC-5, SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma, and HUVEC endothelial cells were exposed to 31.25 nM of tritiated β-N-methylamino-l-alanine for 16 hours in the presence or absence of 2 µg/mL cycloheximide, a potent inhibitor of eukaryotic translation elongation. A control experiment was performed using 41 nM of tritiated leucine under identical conditions. In all three cell types, cycloheximide reduced the incorporation of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine into proteins by 92% to 96%, with no significant difference in the degree of inhibition observed between β-N-methylamino-l-alanine and leucine. This confirmed that the incorporation of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine was dependent on active ribosomal protein synthesis and not on alternative mechanisms such as non-ribosomal peptide synthesis or post-translational modification. The identical sensitivity to cycloheximide between β-N-methylamino-l-alanine and leucine further supported the hypothesis that both molecules were processed by the same translational machinery.

In the third example, MRC-5 cells were exposed to 31.25 nM of tritiated β-N-methylamino-l-alanine for 16 hours in the presence of 250 µM of each of the 20 canonical amino acids individually. Incorporation of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine was quantified by trichloroacetic acid precipitation and liquid scintillation counting. Of the 20 amino acids tested, only l-serine produced a statistically significant reduction in β-N-methylamino-l-alanine incorporation, with a 78% decrease observed compared to the control condition. All other amino acids, including structurally similar molecules such as threonine, glycine, and asparagine, showed no significant effect. To confirm the specificity of this inhibition, a dose-response curve was generated using l-serine concentrations ranging from 0 to 250 µM. A concentration-dependent inhibition was observed, with 50 µM l-serine reducing incorporation by 32%, 100 µM by 54%, and 250 µM by 78%. When d-serine was substituted for l-serine at 250 µM, no inhibition was observed, confirming that the effect required the l-stereoisomer recognized by seryl-tRNA synthetase.

In the fourth example, MRC-5 cells were exposed to 31.25 nM of tritiated β-N-methylamino-l-alanine for 16 hours under three different amino acid conditions: (1) amino acid-depleted medium, (2) medium supplemented with all 20 canonical amino acids at 400 µM each, and (3) medium supplemented with all 20 amino acids except l-serine. In the complete amino acid mixture, β-N-methylamino-l-alanine incorporation was reduced by 89% compared to the depleted condition, demonstrating that the presence of competing amino acids suppresses misincorporation. However, when l-serine was omitted from the mixture, incorporation increased by 3.7-fold compared to the complete mixture and was nearly equivalent to the level observed in amino acid-depleted medium. This demonstrated that l-serine is the primary physiological competitor of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine and that its absence, even in the presence of all other amino acids, is sufficient to permit high levels of misincorporation.

In the fifth example, SH-SY5Y cells were incubated with 31.25 nM of tritiated β-N-methylamino-l-alanine for 24 hours, and proteins were isolated by trichloroacetic acid precipitation and washed with ice-cold acetone. The protein pellet was then subjected to three different treatments: (1) incubation with 1 mM dithiothreitol and 2% sodium dodecyl sulfate at 37°C for 24 hours, (2) incubation with 2 mg/mL pronase in Tris-HCl buffer at pH 8 for 48 hours, and (3) incubation with 12 M hydrochloric acid for 12 hours at 37°C. After each treatment, the amount of radiolabel released into the supernatant was measured by liquid scintillation counting. Neither dithiothreitol nor sodium dodecyl sulfate released any significant radiolabel, indicating that β-N-methylamino-l-alanine was not bound through disulfide bonds or hydrophobic interactions. In contrast, pronase digestion released 87% of the radiolabel, and acid hydrolysis released 94%, confirming that β-N-methylamino-l-alanine was covalently incorporated into peptide bonds within the protein chain.

In the sixth example, MRC-5 cells were incubated with non-labeled β-N-methylamino-l-alanine at concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 100 µM for 48 hours. Proteins were isolated, hydrolyzed in 6 M hydrochloric acid at 110°C for 16 hours, and the hydrolysates were derivatized with 6-aminoquinolyl-N-hydroxysuccinimidyl carbamate. The samples were analyzed by ultra-high-pressure liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry using selective reaction monitoring. The instrument was configured to monitor six diagnostic transitions from the precursor ion m/z 459 to product ions at m/z 119, 171, 188, 214, 258, and 289. The retention time of the analyte matched that of the synthetic β-N-methylamino-l-alanine standard (4.98 ± 0.21 min), and the ratios of the fragment ion intensities were consistent across all concentrations. A linear calibration curve was generated with a correlation coefficient of 0.99, demonstrating that the amount of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine recovered from hydrolyzed proteins was directly proportional to the concentration of β-N-methylamino-l-alanine in the culture medium. This confirmed that β-N-methylamino-l-alanine was not degraded during hydrolysis and that the method was suitable for quantitative detection of protein-incorporated β-N-methylamino-l-alanine.

In the seventh example, MRC-5 cells were incubated with 300 µM β-N-methylamino-l-alanine for 96 hours with daily medium changes. Cellular autofluorescence was visualized using an inverted fluorescent microscope with excitation at 488 nm and emission detection between 500 and 550 nm. A strong, diffuse autofluorescent signal was observed in the perinuclear and cytosolic regions of the cells, indicative of aggregated protein species. When cells were co-incubated with 300 µM l-serine, the autofluorescence was reduced by 91%, with signal levels comparable to untreated controls. No autofluorescence was observed in cells treated with l-serine alone. This demonstrated that the autofluorescence was specifically induced by β-N-methylamino-l-alanine incorporation and was preventable by l-serine supplementation.

In the eighth example, apoptosis was assessed in MRC-5 and SH-SY5Y cells exposed to 300 µM β-N-methylamino-l-alanine for 96 hours using the Annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide apoptosis detection kit and flow cytometry. The percentage of Annexin V-positive, propidium iodide-negative cells (early apoptotic) increased from 3.2% in controls to 28.7% in β-N-methylamino-l-alanine-treated cells. Co-incubation with 300 µM l-serine reduced this percentage to 5.1%, and co-incubation with 2 µg/mL cycloheximide reduced it to 4.8%. Lactate dehydrogenase release was unchanged across all conditions, confirming that cell death was not due to necrosis. These results demonstrated that β-N-methylamino-l-alanine-induced apoptosis was dependent on protein synthesis and was preventable by either inhibiting translation or by competing with l-serine.

In the ninth example, a pharmaceutical composition was prepared by dissolving 500 mg of l-serine in 10 mL of sterile water, adjusting the pH to 7.4 with sodium hydroxide, and filtering through a 0.22 µm membrane. The solution was aliquoted into sterile vials and lyophilized. Each vial contained 500 mg of l-serine and was reconstituted with 5 mL of sterile water prior to intravenous administration. The composition was administered to a cohort of rats exposed to 10 mg/kg β-N-methylamino-l-alanine via intraperitoneal injection. Plasma l-serine concentrations were maintained above 300 µM for 24 hours following administration. At 72 hours, brain tissue was harvested and analyzed for β-N-methylamino-l-alanine incorporation using the LC-MS/MS method described in Example 6. Rats treated with l-serine showed a 79% reduction in protein-incorporated β-N-methylamino-l-alanine compared to untreated controls, with no signs of neuroinflammation or neuronal loss. This demonstrated the in vivo efficacy of l-serine supplementation in preventing misincorporation.

In the tenth example, a diagnostic and therapeutic kit was assembled comprising a first component containing 10 vials of lyophilized l-serine (500 mg per vial), a second component containing acid hydrolysis tubes with 6 M HCl, derivatization reagent vials containing 6-aminoquinolyl-N-hydroxysuccinimidyl carbamate, internal standard vials containing deuterated β-N-methylamino-l-alanine, calibration curve standards, ultra-high-pressure liquid chromatography columns, and a printed instruction manual detailing sample preparation, hydrolysis, derivatization, and LC-MS/MS analysis parameters. The kit was validated using human peripheral blood mononuclear cells from individuals with known exposure to cyanobacteria and from healthy controls. The kit correctly identified elevated β-N-methylamino-l-alanine incorporation in exposed individuals and provided clear guidance for initiating l-serine therapy. This kit enables widespread screening and early intervention in at-risk populations, representing a transformative tool for precision neuroprotection.
