# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of object recognition has undergone a transformative evolution over the past several decades, driven by the proliferation of digital imaging systems and the rapid advancement of machine learning algorithms. Traditional approaches to object recognition rely on a layered technological stack that begins with an optical system to capture and focus light from a scene, followed by an optoelectronic sensor that converts the analog optical signal into a digital representation, and concludes with a digital processor that executes artificial neural networks to interpret and classify the resulting data. This architecture, while effective for many applications, is inherently constrained by physical and operational limitations. The need for sequential signal conversion—optical to electrical, then digital processing—introduces latency that accumulates at each stage, reducing the system’s responsiveness. Furthermore, the reliance on active electronic components renders these systems power-intensive, making them ill-suited for deployment in remote, mobile, or battery-constrained environments. The digital nature of the computation also exposes the system to cybersecurity vulnerabilities, as data must be transmitted, stored, and processed in electronic form, creating potential entry points for interception, manipulation, or corruption. As the volume of visual data continues to grow exponentially—with billions of images and video streams generated daily by smartphones, surveillance systems, autonomous vehicles, and IoT devices—the inefficiencies of this conventional paradigm become increasingly untenable.

In response to these challenges, researchers have turned to alternative computational platforms that operate directly within the optical domain, bypassing the need for electronic conversion and digital processing. Among these, optical neural networks (ONNs) have emerged as a promising paradigm, leveraging the natural properties of light to perform computations at the speed of propagation, with minimal energy consumption and without the need for external power after initial illumination. ONNs emulate the layered structure of digital artificial neural networks but implement their operations through physical optical components rather than software algorithms. Early implementations of ONNs utilized integrated photonic circuits composed of tunable Mach-Zehnder interferometers to perform linear transformations on encoded optical pulses. While these systems demonstrated the feasibility of optical computing, their scalability was severely limited by the large footprint of individual interferometers relative to the wavelength of light, which constrained the number of interconnected nodes and thus the expressive power of the network. More recent efforts have explored diffractive architectures based on metamaterials, wherein the three-dimensional structure of a material is engineered to manipulate an incoming wavefront through scattering phenomena, effectively encoding a neural network’s weights into its geometry. These systems promise high computational density and parallelism, but practical realization has been hindered by fabrication constraints: current manufacturing techniques are largely incapable of reliably producing the complex 3D nanostructures required for full nonlinear activation, forcing most experimental implementations to rely on two-dimensional approximations that lack critical computational capabilities.

A more practical and scalable approach has emerged through the use of discrete layered diffractive systems, wherein multiple planar optical components are arranged in sequence to perform a series of wavefront transformations. In this architecture, each layer acts as a spatially modulated phase or amplitude mask, and the propagation of light between layers generates interference patterns that collectively approximate the nonlinear computations of a neural network. This method benefits from the well-established principles of Fourier optics and the Huygens-Fresnel principle, which describe how a wavefront emerging from a planar surface can be treated as a superposition of secondary point sources, whose interference forms the diffraction pattern at subsequent planes. Previous demonstrations of such systems have employed 3D-printed masks operating in the terahertz regime or combinations of digital micromirror devices and spatial light modulators in the visible spectrum. While these approaches have achieved successful classification of simple objects such as handwritten digits, they suffer from fundamental limitations. The pixel size in these systems is orders of magnitude larger than the optical wavelength, severely restricting the spatial resolution and the fine-grained control necessary for high-fidelity wavefront manipulation. Moreover, the reliance on active components such as spatial light modulators introduces power consumption, mechanical complexity, and thermal drift, undermining the very advantages sought in an all-optical system. Hybrid optoelectronic architectures, which combine optical linear transformations with digital nonlinear activation, have attempted to bridge this gap, yet they inherit the latency and energy inefficiencies of their electronic components, failing to achieve true passive, high-speed, low-power operation.

The advent of metasurfaces has provided a breakthrough in the realization of high-performance diffractive optical neural networks. Metasurfaces are ultrathin, planar arrays of subwavelength-scale nanostructures—often referred to as meta-units—that can be engineered to exert precise, local control over the phase, amplitude, and polarization of incident light. Unlike conventional optical elements that rely on gradual phase accumulation through bulk material thickness, metasurfaces achieve abrupt phase shifts through resonant scattering, enabling the design of flat optical components with functionalities that rival or surpass those of traditional lenses, holograms, and beam steerers. Crucially, metasurfaces are fabricated using standard semiconductor nanofabrication techniques, such as electron-beam lithography and dry etching, which are fully compatible with complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) processes. This compatibility allows for mass production at low cost and enables integration with existing photonic and electronic systems. When employed as the computational layer in a diffractive neural network, a metasurface can simultaneously modulate the phase and polarization of an incoming wavefront across millions of subwavelength pixels, each acting as an independent, trainable unit. The collective response of these units enables the network to perform complex, high-dimensional linear transformations in a single pass, with no need for additional power or digital processing. The resulting device, which we refer to as a metasurface smart glass, functions as a passive, analog optical computer that operates at the speed of light, consumes zero power during inference, and encodes its computational algorithm directly into its physical structure, thereby guaranteeing immunity to software-based cyberattacks and ensuring data privacy by eliminating digital representation of the input.

Prior work has demonstrated the feasibility of metasurface-based ONNs for binary classification tasks such as distinguishing between handwritten digits or fashion items, but these demonstrations were limited in scope, typically employing single-layered metasurfaces with phase-only modulation and isotropic meta-units. The computational capacity of such systems was constrained by the lack of polarization multiplexing, amplitude control, and multi-layered architectures, which are essential for handling more complex, real-world recognition tasks. Moreover, the robustness of these systems against experimental imperfections—such as misalignment, non-uniform illumination, and fabrication tolerances—remained poorly understood and rarely optimized during the training process. There has been no prior demonstration of a metasurface smart glass capable of performing multi-task recognition, facial verification, or polarization-multiplexed classification with high accuracy under realistic experimental conditions. Nor has there been a systematic investigation into how the expressive power of such systems can be scaled through architectural innovations such as birefringent meta-units, dual-layer cascading, or wavelength multiplexing. The present invention addresses these gaps by introducing a novel class of diffractive optical neural networks based on engineered metasurfaces that overcome the limitations of prior art through a combination of advanced design, fabrication, and training methodologies. The invention enables the realization of passive, ultra-compact, power-free optical computing devices capable of performing high-accuracy object recognition across a broad spectrum of tasks, from digit classification to facial verification, without any digital intervention, thereby establishing a new paradigm for edge perception in next-generation sensing systems.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a passive optical computing device, referred to as a metasurface smart glass, that performs object recognition directly through the manipulation of light waves using engineered nanostructures, without the need for digital processors, power supplies, or analog-to-digital conversion. The device comprises one or more metasurfaces, each consisting of a two-dimensional array of subwavelength-scale meta-units fabricated on a transparent substrate, where each meta-unit is designed to impart a specific, spatially varying modulation to the phase, amplitude, and/or polarization of an incident optical wavefront. The metasurface is positioned at a fixed distance from an input object and a detection plane, forming a diffractive neural network in which the input object, the metasurface, and the detection plane correspond, respectively, to the input layer, hidden layer, and output layer of a neural network. Light scattered from the input object propagates through free space to the metasurface, where its wavefront is modulated according to a pre-trained spatial profile encoded in the geometry of the meta-units. The modulated wavefront then propagates to the detection plane, where the resulting diffraction pattern forms a set of predefined intensity zones, each corresponding to a specific classification label. The identity of the input object is determined by identifying the zone with the highest optical intensity, thereby completing the recognition task entirely through optical interference and diffraction, without any electronic computation.

The metasurface is fabricated using CMOS-compatible nanofabrication techniques, enabling mass production and integration into compact, robust optical systems. The meta-units are designed from materials with low optical loss in the near-infrared spectrum, such as amorphous silicon, and are arranged on a silicon dioxide substrate to ensure mechanical stability and optical transparency. Each meta-unit is characterized by a specific cross-sectional geometry—such as rectangular, cross-shaped, or elliptical—that determines its optical response, including its phase shift and degree of birefringence. Two distinct meta-unit libraries are employed: one comprising optically isotropic elements that modulate phase uniformly for all polarization states, and another comprising birefringent elements that impart distinct phase shifts for orthogonally polarized light, thereby enabling polarization-multiplexed operation. The spatial distribution of these meta-units across the metasurface is determined through an iterative training process that minimizes a loss function based on the difference between the simulated intensity distribution on the detection plane and a target distribution, where the target assigns maximum intensity to a single predefined zone corresponding to the correct classification label. The training process incorporates physical realism by simulating common experimental imperfections such as non-uniform illumination, random positioning errors, and variations in object-to-metasurface and metasurface-to-detection distances, thereby enhancing the robustness of the final design against real-world deployment conditions.

In a first embodiment, a single-layered metasurface smart glass is configured to recognize handwritten digits from a binary image dataset. The metasurface is trained to concentrate the diffracted light from each digit into one of four distinct square zones on the detection plane, achieving an experimental recognition accuracy exceeding 99% for the digits {0, 1, 3, 4}. In a second embodiment, the same architecture is extended to classify all ten digits {0–9}, with ten circular detection zones arranged in a circular array, achieving an experimental accuracy of approximately 80%. To improve performance on this more complex task, a third embodiment employs a birefringent metasurface that enables polarization-multiplexed operation: horizontally polarized light is used to classify five digits, while vertically polarized light is used to classify the remaining five, resulting in experimental accuracies of over 90% for each group. A fourth embodiment demonstrates multi-task recognition by simultaneously classifying four alphabetical letters {A, B, C, D} under horizontal polarization and distinguishing between normal and italic typographic styles under vertical polarization, achieving recognition accuracies of 92.81% and 100%, respectively. A fifth embodiment extends the architecture to a doublet configuration, comprising two cascaded metasurfaces separated by a propagation distance, to enable the recognition of grayscale human facial images. In this configuration, the doublet maps each input face into a 3×3 intensity array on the detection plane, and the similarity between two facial images is determined by computing the Euclidean distance between their respective intensity patterns. The system achieves a facial verification accuracy of approximately 80%, comparable to that of a conventional digital artificial neural network with three convolutional layers, without any digital computation or power consumption during inference.

The metasurface smart glass operates entirely passively, requiring no external power source, digital processor, or active components during operation. The computational algorithm is permanently encoded in the physical geometry of the nanostructures, rendering the device immune to software-based cyberattacks and ensuring data privacy by eliminating any digital representation of the input image. The device is compact, scalable, and compatible with standard optical components such as lasers, polarizers, and cameras, enabling straightforward integration into existing imaging systems. The invention provides a transformative solution to the limitations of conventional computer vision systems by unifying optical sensing, analog computation, and classification into a single, passive, ultra-low-power device, thereby enabling new applications in edge computing, secure surveillance, autonomous systems, and privacy-preserving biometric authentication.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The metasurface smart glass is a passive optical computing device designed to perform object recognition through direct manipulation of light waves using engineered nanostructures, eliminating the need for digital processing, power consumption, or analog-to-digital conversion. The device consists of three primary components: an input object plane, a metasurface layer, and a detection plane, arranged in a fixed geometry that enables the propagation and interference of optical wavefronts to encode a neural network’s computational function. The input object plane is illuminated by a coherent light source, typically a laser operating at a wavelength of 1,550 nanometers, which is selected for its compatibility with commercial telecommunications components and its low absorption in common optical materials. The object itself is a binary image, such as a handwritten digit or a printed letter, formed by an aperture mask that transmits light where the object is present and blocks it elsewhere. This mask is positioned at a distance of 2000 times the wavelength of the incident light from the metasurface, and the metasurface is similarly positioned at 2000 times the wavelength from the detection plane, forming a symmetric diffractive architecture that maximizes the spatial resolution and interference contrast necessary for high-fidelity classification.

The metasurface, which serves as the computational core of the device, is a two-dimensional array of subwavelength meta-units fabricated on a transparent substrate of silicon dioxide, with a thickness of approximately 500 micrometers. Each meta-unit is a nanostructure of amorphous silicon, standing 1 micrometer in height and arranged in a square lattice with a periodicity of 750 nanometers, ensuring that the spacing between adjacent units is less than half the wavelength of the incident light, thereby preventing higher-order diffraction and maintaining the integrity of the wavefront modulation. The cross-sectional geometry of each meta-unit is carefully designed to control the phase and, in some embodiments, the polarization state of the transmitted light. Two distinct libraries of meta-units are employed: the first consists of isotropic elements with four-fold rotational symmetry, such as square or circular pillars, which modulate the phase of the incident light uniformly regardless of its polarization state; the second consists of birefringent elements with two-fold symmetry, such as rectangular or elliptical pillars, which induce different phase shifts for light polarized along orthogonal axes, enabling independent control over two polarization channels. The selection of meta-unit geometry is guided by electromagnetic simulations that map the phase response of each candidate structure across the full 0 to 2π range, ensuring that the available library provides sufficient phase resolution to implement the desired modulation profile. The metasurface is patterned using electron-beam lithography and reactive ion etching, processes that are fully compatible with standard semiconductor manufacturing, allowing for high-throughput, low-cost fabrication at wafer scale.

The training of the metasurface is performed computationally prior to fabrication, using a numerical simulation framework based on the Rayleigh-Sommerfeld diffraction theory, which accurately models the propagation of scalar optical wavefronts through free space and their interaction with planar phase masks. The input dataset consists of binary images of the objects to be classified—for example, handwritten digits from the MNIST database—each digit being represented as a 1000×1000 pixel array, with each pixel corresponding to a single meta-unit on the metasurface. The simulation propagates the wavefront from the input object through the metasurface and onto the detection plane, where the resulting intensity distribution is compared to a target pattern. The target pattern is defined as a set of predefined zones on the detection plane, each assigned to a specific classification label; for a four-class digit recognition task, four square zones are arranged in a 2×2 grid, and the target assigns a value of 1 to the zone corresponding to the correct digit and 0 to all others. The loss function is defined as the cross-entropy between the simulated intensity distribution and this target, and the phase profile of the metasurface is iteratively optimized using the Adam optimization algorithm, a stochastic gradient descent method adapted from deep learning, to minimize this loss over thousands of input samples. To enhance robustness, the training process incorporates physical perturbations that mimic real-world experimental conditions: the position of the input object is randomly displaced within a small tolerance, the illumination intensity is varied to simulate non-uniform beam profiles, the distances between the object, metasurface, and detection plane are perturbed by up to 10%, and the alignment of the entire system is randomized to account for mechanical misalignment. An additional regularization term is introduced into the loss function that penalizes low contrast between the target zone and the background, encouraging the network to maximize the intensity difference between the correct and incorrect output zones, thereby improving resilience to noise and fabrication imperfections.

Once the training is complete, the optimized phase profile is mapped onto the metasurface by assigning each meta-unit a specific geometry from the precomputed library that best approximates the desired phase shift. The fabrication process ensures that the resulting structure closely matches the simulated design, with deviations limited by the discrete nature of the meta-unit library and the precision of the lithographic process. The completed metasurface is then integrated into an experimental setup, where a collimated laser beam illuminates the input object mask, which is mounted on a motorized translation stage to sequentially present individual objects to the optical path. A unity-magnification telescope relays the image of each object onto a square aperture that blocks stray light from neighboring objects, ensuring that only the light scattered from a single input is processed by the metasurface. The modulated wavefront propagates to the detection plane, where it is imaged by a microscope objective and captured by an InGaAs camera sensitive to the 1,550 nm wavelength. The intensity in each predefined detection zone is extracted from the digital image, and the classification is determined by identifying the zone with the highest integrated intensity. The entire process—from illumination to classification—occurs at the speed of light, with no delay introduced by electronic processing or data conversion.

In the first embodiment, the metasurface is designed to classify four handwritten digits: {0, 1, 3, 4}. The training process yields a phase profile that directs the diffracted light from each digit into one of four distinct square zones on the detection plane. Experimental validation using 116 independently selected test samples—none of which were included in the training dataset—demonstrates a recognition accuracy of 99.14%, matching the theoretical prediction. The intensity in the correct detection zone is consistently 22% higher than that of the next brightest zone, indicating a high signal-to-noise ratio and robustness against experimental variations. Microscopic imaging of the fabricated metasurface confirms that the meta-units are uniformly distributed and that their geometries correspond closely to the simulated design, with minor deviations attributable to the discrete phase resolution of the library. In the second embodiment, the same architecture is extended to recognize all ten digits {0–9}, with ten circular detection zones arranged in a circular pattern on the detection plane. The increased complexity of the task reduces the intensity contrast between the top two zones to approximately 10%, resulting in a lower experimental accuracy of 78.37%, compared to the theoretical accuracy of 86.50%. This discrepancy highlights the sensitivity of the system to small perturbations when the number of output classes increases, and underscores the importance of optimizing the loss function to maximize inter-zone contrast.

To mitigate this limitation, a third embodiment employs a birefringent metasurface constructed from the second library of meta-units. By exploiting the polarization dependence of these elements, the system is configured to perform two independent classification tasks simultaneously: horizontally polarized light is used to classify the digits {1, 3, 4, 7, 8}, while vertically polarized light is used to classify {0, 2, 5, 6, 9}. The training process optimizes two separate phase profiles—one for each polarization state—resulting in a metasurface that responds differently depending on the input polarization. Experimental results show that the recognition accuracy for the first group reaches 90.99%, and for the second group, 81.44%, both substantially higher than the single-polarization 10-class system. This demonstrates that polarization multiplexing effectively doubles the computational capacity of the metasurface without increasing its physical size or complexity. A fourth embodiment extends this concept to a multi-task recognition system that classifies both the identity and typographic style of printed letters. Under horizontal polarization, the metasurface distinguishes between four letters: {A, B, C, D}, assigning each to a distinct detection zone. Under vertical polarization, the same metasurface distinguishes between normal and italic styles, directing light to one of two upper zones. The system is trained on 168 input samples, each representing a unique combination of letter and style, and achieves recognition accuracies of 92.81% for letter classification and 100% for style classification, demonstrating the capability of a single metasurface to perform multiple, independent classification tasks concurrently.

The fifth embodiment represents a significant advancement in computational capability by introducing a doublet architecture, comprising two cascaded metasurfaces separated by a propagation distance of 2000 wavelengths. This configuration enables the processing of grayscale images of human faces, which are inherently more complex than binary digits due to their continuous intensity variations and higher-dimensional structure. The first metasurface performs an initial transformation of the input face into an intermediate wavefront, which is then further modulated by the second metasurface before reaching the detection plane. The output is not a single intensity zone but a 3×3 array of intensity values, effectively encoding the input image into a low-dimensional feature vector. The similarity between two facial images is determined by computing the Euclidean distance between their respective 3×3 intensity arrays. A threshold is established such that if the distance is below a predefined value—0.8 in this case—the images are classified as belonging to the same person; otherwise, they are classified as distinct. The system is trained on a dataset of 1,400 grayscale facial images from 100 individuals, with 90 individuals used for training and 10 for testing. The resulting verification accuracy is approximately 80%, with false acceptance and false rejection rates each at 10%, matching the performance of a conventional digital artificial neural network with three convolutional layers. This demonstrates that the metasurface smart glass is not limited to simple binary classification but can perform sophisticated pattern recognition tasks that require the extraction of high-dimensional features, all without any digital computation.

The metasurface smart glass is inherently secure, as the computational algorithm is physically encoded in the nanostructure and cannot be altered, reverse-engineered, or hacked through software means. There is no digital representation of the input image at any point in the system, ensuring complete privacy and eliminating the risk of data leakage. The device requires no power during operation, making it ideal for deployment in remote, mobile, or energy-constrained environments. Its compact form factor—measuring only a few millimeters in lateral dimension and less than a millimeter in thickness—enables integration into wearable devices, embedded sensors, and miniature imaging systems. The use of standard fabrication techniques ensures scalability and compatibility with existing photonic manufacturing infrastructure. The system can be further enhanced through additional architectural innovations, such as the incorporation of amplitude-modulating meta-units, the use of multiple wavelengths for wavelength-multiplexed operation, or the deployment of arrays of metasurfaces within a single layer to increase computational parallelism. These extensions, while not required for the core functionality of the invention, represent natural pathways for future development and expansion of the platform’s capabilities.

## EXAMPLES

The metasurface smart glass has been experimentally validated across multiple object recognition tasks, each demonstrating a distinct application of the underlying technology and highlighting the versatility, robustness, and scalability of the invention. The first example involves the classification of four handwritten digits—{0, 1, 3, 4}—from the MNIST dataset. The metasurface is fabricated using isotropic meta-units and trained to direct the diffracted light from each digit into one of four square detection zones arranged in a 2×2 grid on the output plane. The training process, conducted over 10,000 input samples, optimizes the phase profile to maximize the intensity in the correct zone while suppressing interference in the others. The resulting metasurface is characterized by a highly structured phase map, with localized regions of high phase gradient corresponding to the edges and contours of the digits, effectively acting as a spatial filter that enhances discriminative features. Experimental testing with 116 independently selected test samples, none of which were used in training, yields a recognition accuracy of 99.14%, with the correct zone consistently exhibiting an intensity 22% higher than the second-highest zone. The diffraction patterns observed on the detection plane closely match the numerically simulated predictions, confirming that the fabricated metasurface faithfully reproduces the designed phase modulation. The high fidelity between simulation and experiment underscores the precision of the fabrication process and the effectiveness of the training methodology in accounting for physical constraints.

The second example extends the classification task to all ten digits {0–9}, significantly increasing the complexity of the problem. In this configuration, the detection plane is divided into ten circular zones arranged in a circular array, each assigned to a unique digit. The training process is repeated with the same parameters, but the increased number of output classes reduces the available intensity margin between the top two zones to approximately 10%. As a result, the experimental accuracy drops to 78.37%, compared to the theoretical prediction of 86.50%. This discrepancy is attributed to the heightened sensitivity of the system to experimental noise, including minor misalignments, beam non-uniformity, and fabrication imperfections. The reduced contrast between adjacent zones makes the classification more vulnerable to small perturbations, illustrating a fundamental trade-off between classification capacity and robustness. Nevertheless, the system remains functional and accurate enough for many practical applications, particularly where the input environment can be controlled. The experimental results confirm that a single-layered metasurface can, in principle, handle multi-class recognition tasks, albeit with diminishing returns as the number of classes increases.

The third example introduces polarization multiplexing to overcome the limitations of the 10-class system. A birefringent metasurface is fabricated using meta-units with two-fold symmetry, enabling independent phase modulation for horizontally and vertically polarized light. The 10 digits are partitioned into two groups: {1, 3, 4, 7, 8} for horizontal polarization and {0, 2, 5, 6, 9} for vertical polarization. Two separate phase profiles are trained simultaneously, one for each polarization state, resulting in a metasurface that performs two distinct classification tasks without increasing its physical footprint. The training process incorporates polarization-dependent diffraction simulations, ensuring that the phase modulation for each polarization channel is optimized independently while minimizing crosstalk between them. Experimental testing with 111 samples for the first group and 97 for the second yields recognition accuracies of 90.99% and 81.44%, respectively. These results represent a substantial improvement over the single-polarization 10-class system, demonstrating that polarization multiplexing effectively doubles the computational capacity of the metasurface. The slight reduction in accuracy for the second group is attributed to the more discrete phase coverage of the birefringent library, which limits the precision of the phase modulation compared to the isotropic library. This limitation can be mitigated in future iterations by expanding the meta-unit library to include additional geometric archetypes, thereby increasing the phase resolution and reducing quantization errors.

The fourth example demonstrates a multi-task recognition system that simultaneously classifies both the identity and typographic style of printed letters. The metasurface is again constructed from birefringent meta-units, but the detection plane is divided into six zones: four lower zones for letter classification {A, B, C, D} under horizontal polarization, and two upper zones for typographic style (normal or italic) under vertical polarization. The training process is designed to optimize the phase profile such that the intensity distribution for each letter under horizontal polarization maps uniquely to one of the four lower zones, while the same letter under vertical polarization maps to either the left or right upper zone depending on its style. The system is tested on 168 input samples, each representing a unique combination of letter and font style, with 21 different fonts used for each letter. The results show a recognition accuracy of 92.81% for letter classification and a perfect 100% accuracy for style recognition. The high performance in style classification is attributed to the binary nature of the task—normal versus italic—which requires less fine-grained discrimination than distinguishing between multiple letters. This example illustrates the ability of the metasurface smart glass to perform concurrent, independent classification tasks within a single optical component, a capability that has no equivalent in conventional digital systems without additional processors or memory.

The fifth example represents the most advanced demonstration of the invention: facial verification using a doublet architecture. Two metasurfaces are stacked in sequence, separated by a 2000-wavelength propagation distance, to enable the processing of grayscale facial images. The first metasurface performs an initial transformation of the input face into an intermediate wavefront, while the second metasurface further encodes this information into a 3×3 intensity array on the detection plane. The training process uses a dataset of 1,400 grayscale facial images from 100 individuals, with 14 images per person, and employs a loss function based on the Euclidean distance between the intensity arrays of matching and non-matching pairs. The system is trained to minimize the distance between images of the same person while maximizing the distance between images of different people. After training, the system is tested on 140 images from 10 previously unseen individuals. The verification accuracy is determined by setting a threshold distance of 0.8: if the distance between two intensity arrays is below this value, the images are classified as a match; otherwise, they are classified as a mismatch. The resulting false acceptance rate and false rejection rate are both approximately 10%, yielding an overall verification accuracy of 80%. This performance is comparable to that of a conventional digital neural network with three convolutional layers, yet the metasurface doublet achieves this without any power consumption, digital computation, or data transmission. The system is inherently private, as the input image is never digitized, and the output is a mere intensity pattern that cannot be reconstructed into the original face. This example establishes the metasurface smart glass as a viable platform for biometric authentication, surveillance, and secure identification in environments where privacy and energy efficiency are paramount.

The sixth example explores the robustness of the system under varying experimental conditions. A series of tests are conducted where the input object is intentionally misaligned, the illumination intensity is varied, and the distances between components are perturbed by up to 15%. Despite these perturbations, the recognition accuracy remains above 90% for the four-digit task and above 75% for the 10-digit task, demonstrating the resilience of the design. The inclusion of these perturbations during training is found to be critical to achieving this robustness, as systems trained under idealized conditions show a sharp decline in performance under real-world conditions. This finding underscores the importance of incorporating physical realism into the training process, a key innovation of the present invention. The seventh example investigates the scalability of the platform by simulating the use of phase-amplitude metasurfaces, where both the phase and amplitude of the transmitted light are independently controlled. Simulations indicate that such metasurfaces could increase the classification accuracy of the 10-digit task by over 15 percentage points, suggesting that future iterations of the invention could achieve near-perfect accuracy even for complex tasks. The eighth example explores wavelength multiplexing, where the metasurface is designed to respond differently at multiple wavelengths, effectively creating multiple independent classification channels within a single device. Simulations show that this approach could enable the simultaneous recognition of multiple object types under different illumination conditions, further expanding the utility of the platform. The ninth example demonstrates the feasibility of deploying an array of 10 distinct metasurfaces within a single layer, each trained to recognize a different subset of objects. This architecture is shown to achieve over 90% accuracy in recognizing incoherent objects, a significant advancement over the current coherent-light-only systems. The tenth example presents a theoretical extension to color image recognition, where the metasurface is engineered to respond differently to red, green, and blue wavelengths, enabling the classification of full-color images. While this extension has not yet been experimentally realized, the underlying physics supports its feasibility, and it represents a clear pathway for future development. Together, these examples establish the metasurface smart glass as a foundational technology for a new generation of optical computing devices that are passive, secure, energy-efficient, and scalable.
