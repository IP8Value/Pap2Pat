# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of targeted cancer therapeutics, specifically to fully human monoclonal antibodies directed against the receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 2 (ROR2), and to antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) comprising such antibodies conjugated to cytotoxic payloads. The invention further relates to methods for the discovery and selection of these antibodies based on their ability to bind ROR2 with high specificity and to induce efficient internalization into cancer cells, thereby enabling potent and selective cytotoxic activity when linked to a cytotoxic agent. The antibodies and ADCs described herein are particularly useful for the treatment of cancers characterized by aberrant overexpression of ROR2, including but not limited to renal cell carcinoma, osteosarcoma, melanoma, breast cancer, colorectal cancer, oral cancer, pancreatic cancer, and stromal tumors. The invention encompasses recombinant antibody constructs, pharmaceutical compositions containing these antibodies or ADCs, and methods of using them for the diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic intervention of ROR2-expressing malignancies. The disclosed antibodies exhibit high affinity for human ROR2, minimal cross-reactivity with non-target tissues, and a favorable therapeutic index due to their capacity for selective internalization and intracellular payload delivery, distinguishing them from conventional antibody-based therapies that rely on immune effector functions or lack sufficient internalization kinetics for effective cytotoxic delivery.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Cancer remains one of the most formidable challenges in modern medicine, with millions of deaths annually attributed to the disease despite decades of therapeutic advances. Conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy, while broadly effective in some contexts, is limited by its lack of selectivity, resulting in systemic toxicity that often restricts dosing and compromises patient quality of life. The development of targeted therapies has sought to overcome these limitations by directing therapeutic agents specifically to molecular features unique to or overexpressed on cancer cells. Among the most promising of these approaches are antibody-drug conjugates, which combine the exquisite target specificity of monoclonal antibodies with the potent cell-killing capacity of cytotoxic small molecules. The concept of linking a cytotoxic payload to an antibody that recognizes a tumor-associated antigen has been explored for over four decades, yet only a handful of such agents have achieved regulatory approval, underscoring the complexity of engineering a successful ADC. The success of an ADC depends on a delicate balance of multiple interdependent factors: the antibody must bind its target with high affinity and specificity, the target antigen must be abundantly and selectively expressed on tumor cells while being minimally present on healthy tissues, the antibody-antigen complex must be efficiently internalized via receptor-mediated endocytosis, the linker connecting the antibody to the payload must remain stable in circulation yet cleavable within the target cell, and the payload must retain its potency upon release and be capable of inducing cell death at sub-nanomolar concentrations.

ROR2, a member of the ROR family of receptor tyrosine kinases, has emerged as a compelling target for ADC development due to its restricted expression in adult tissues and its consistent overexpression across a broad spectrum of solid tumors. Unlike its closely related homolog ROR1, which is expressed in certain hematologic malignancies and some epithelial cancers, ROR2 is largely absent from normal adult tissues but is upregulated in a wide array of carcinomas and sarcomas, including breast, renal, colorectal, pancreatic, and osteosarcoma. Its expression has been correlated with aggressive disease phenotypes, metastatic potential, and poor patient prognosis, making it not only a marker of malignancy but also a functionally relevant driver of tumor progression through activation of noncanonical Wnt signaling pathways. Importantly, ROR2 is rarely co-expressed with ROR1, suggesting that targeting ROR2 alone may avoid unintended cross-reactivity and broaden the therapeutic window. Despite its promise, the development of effective ROR2-targeted therapies has been hindered by the relatively low to moderate cell surface density of ROR2 on tumor cells, which necessitates antibodies with exceptionally high internalization efficiency to ensure sufficient payload delivery. Many antibodies that bind ROR2 with high affinity fail to induce robust internalization, rendering them ineffective as ADCs even when conjugated to potent toxins. Furthermore, the majority of antibodies discovered through traditional hybridoma or phage display technologies are of murine or chimeric origin, requiring extensive humanization to mitigate immunogenicity risks in clinical settings—a process that often alters binding characteristics and reduces functional potency.

Current antibody discovery platforms, while capable of identifying high-affinity binders, are largely incapable of simultaneously assessing the critical functional property of internalization during the initial screening phase. Most workflows involve cloning, expressing, and purifying individual antibody candidates before testing their ADC activity, a process that is both time-consuming and resource-intensive. This sequential approach frequently results in the discard of promising candidates that bind well but internalize poorly, or conversely, the advancement of candidates that appear promising in binding assays but fail in functional cytotoxicity assays due to insufficient endocytosis. Moreover, many existing systems rely on non-human antibody scaffolds, necessitating additional steps of humanization and affinity maturation that introduce variability and delay clinical translation. There is therefore a critical unmet need for a streamlined, high-throughput discovery platform that enables the direct identification of fully human antibodies with optimal ADC properties—namely, high target specificity, efficient internalization, and potent cytotoxic activity—without requiring reformatting, re-expression, or extensive downstream validation. The present invention addresses this gap by integrating immunization of transgenic mice expressing fully human immunoglobulin variable regions with a mammalian cell-based display system that permits the direct functional evaluation of secreted antibodies for ADC activity in a single, integrated screening workflow. This approach bypasses the traditional bottlenecks of antibody discovery and enables the rapid isolation of lead candidates with a high probability of clinical success.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel class of fully human monoclonal antibodies that specifically bind to the human receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 2 (ROR2) with high affinity and induce rapid and efficient internalization into ROR2-expressing cancer cells. These antibodies are uniquely suited for use as components of antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) due to their ability to deliver cytotoxic payloads selectively to tumor cells while sparing healthy tissues, thereby achieving potent antitumor activity with an improved therapeutic index. The invention further encompasses a method for the discovery and selection of such antibodies through a functional screening platform that evaluates both antigen binding and ADC-mediated cytotoxicity directly from the supernatants of single-cell-derived antibody-expressing clones, without the need for prior sequence recovery, recombinant expression, or purification. This integrated approach enables the identification of lead candidates that are not only high-affinity binders but also possess the critical functional property of efficient internalization, a determinant of ADC efficacy that is often overlooked in conventional discovery pipelines.

The antibodies of the invention are defined by their heavy and light chain variable regions, which together form a binding site that recognizes a conformational epitope on the extracellular domain of human ROR2. These variable regions are derived entirely from human germline sequences, ensuring low immunogenicity potential and compatibility with human immune systems. The antibodies exhibit dissociation constants (KD) for human ROR2 ranging from sub-nanomolar to low nanomolar affinities, with several clones demonstrating KD values below 10 nM. Importantly, the binding affinity alone does not predict ADC potency; instead, the functional internalization capacity of each antibody, as measured by its ability to mediate cytotoxicity in a secondary ADC assay, is the primary criterion for selection. The invention includes twelve distinct antibody clonotypes, each defined by unique heavy chain complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) sequences, with corresponding light chain sequences that pair specifically to form functional antigen-binding units. These clonotypes include, but are not limited to, GK-1E5, GK-2G8, MK-3B12, GK-5A1, GK-5E1, GK-5G12, GK-6B10, MK-7C3, GK-21D3, GK-22G12, MK-24C10, and MK-24F9. Each of these antibodies is capable of binding to human ROR2 with high specificity and mediating potent cytotoxicity when conjugated to a cytotoxic payload, as demonstrated in multiple cancer cell lines overexpressing ROR2, including T47D breast cancer and L363 multiple myeloma models.

The invention further provides antibody-drug conjugates comprising any one of the disclosed antibodies covalently linked to a cytotoxic agent via a cleavable linker. The cytotoxic agents include, but are not limited to, derivatives of the anthracycline PNU-159682, such as Gly3-EDA-PNU and Gly5-EDA-PNU, which are delivered intracellularly following receptor-mediated endocytosis and lysosomal degradation of the ADC. The drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR) of the conjugates is maintained between 3 and 4, ensuring homogeneity and optimal pharmacokinetic properties. The ADCs of the invention demonstrate IC50 values in the low nanogram per milliliter range against ROR2-positive cancer cell lines, with minimal activity observed against ROR2-negative cell lines, confirming target-dependent cytotoxicity. Notably, the most potent ADCs are not necessarily those with the highest binding affinity, indicating that epitope specificity and internalization kinetics are more critical determinants of efficacy than affinity alone. Several of the disclosed antibodies also exhibit cross-reactivity with ROR2 from cynomolgus monkey and, in select cases, mouse orthologs, facilitating preclinical toxicology studies and enabling the use of relevant animal models for in vivo evaluation.

The invention further encompasses a method for the discovery of such antibodies, comprising the steps of immunizing transgenic mice expressing fully human immunoglobulin variable regions with the extracellular domain of human ROR2, enriching ROR2-specific B lymphocytes from the spleen of immunized animals, constructing transposable expression libraries encoding heavy and light chain variable regions fused to human IgG1 and kappa constant domains, transposing these libraries into a murine B cell line capable of expressing membrane-bound and secreted IgG, isolating single-cell clones that bind ROR2 by fluorescence-activated cell sorting, culturing the clones to produce secreted antibody-containing supernatants, and evaluating these supernatants in a functional secondary ADC assay that measures cytotoxicity against ROR2-overexpressing target cells using a toxin-conjugated anti-human Fcγ secondary reagent. Clones exhibiting both strong ROR2 binding and potent cytotoxicity in this assay are selected for sequence recovery and recombinant expression, thereby bypassing the need for conventional cloning and re-expression steps. This method enables the direct identification of lead candidates with optimal ADC properties in a single integrated workflow, significantly accelerating the discovery timeline and increasing the probability of clinical success.

The invention further provides pharmaceutical compositions comprising any one of the disclosed antibodies or ADCs in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, for use in the treatment of ROR2-expressing cancers. The compositions may be administered alone or in combination with other therapeutic modalities, including chemotherapy, immunotherapy, radiation therapy, or targeted small molecule inhibitors. The invention also includes diagnostic methods for detecting ROR2 expression in tumor tissues using the disclosed antibodies as detection reagents, enabling patient stratification for ADC therapy. The antibodies and ADCs of the invention represent a significant advancement in the field of targeted cancer therapy, offering a new class of therapeutics with high specificity, potent cytotoxic activity, and a favorable safety profile for the treatment of a broad range of solid tumors.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Definitions

For the purposes of this disclosure, the term “ROR2” refers to the human receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 2, encoded by the ROR2 gene located on chromosome 9q22.3, which encodes a type I transmembrane protein consisting of an extracellular domain containing an immunoglobulin-like domain, a cysteine-rich domain, and a kringle domain, followed by a transmembrane segment and an intracellular tyrosine kinase-like domain. The term “human ROR2” specifically denotes the protein sequence derived from the human gene, including its naturally occurring isoforms and variants, and encompasses the extracellular domain comprising amino acids 1 through 403 as defined by the UniProt entry NP_004551.2. The term “fully human antibody” refers to an antibody in which the variable regions, including the complementarity-determining regions (CDRs), are derived entirely from human germline sequences, with no murine, rat, or other non-human amino acid residues introduced during the discovery or engineering process. The term “antibody-drug conjugate” or “ADC” refers to a molecular construct comprising a monoclonal antibody chemically linked to a cytotoxic agent via a covalent linker, wherein the antibody mediates targeted delivery of the cytotoxic agent to cells expressing the cognate antigen, and the linker is designed to remain stable in circulation but release the payload upon internalization and lysosomal degradation. The term “cytotoxic agent” refers to a compound capable of inducing cell death at low concentrations, including but not limited to microtubule-disrupting agents, DNA-damaging agents, topoisomerase inhibitors, and protein synthesis inhibitors, with specific examples including derivatives of PNU-159682, monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE), monomethyl auristatin F (MMAF), calicheamicin, duocarmycin, and pyrrolobenzodiazepine dimers. The term “cleavable linker” refers to a chemical moiety connecting the antibody to the cytotoxic agent that is susceptible to enzymatic or chemical cleavage under intracellular conditions, such as within the lysosome, and includes peptide linkers cleavable by cathepsins, disulfide linkers cleavable by glutathione, and acid-labile hydrazone linkers. The term “drug-to-antibody ratio” or “DAR” refers to the average number of cytotoxic molecules conjugated per antibody molecule, with a DAR of 3 to 4 being preferred to ensure homogeneity, stability, and optimal pharmacokinetics. The term “internalization” refers to the process by which an antibody-antigen complex is taken up into the cell via receptor-mediated endocytosis, leading to trafficking through endosomal and lysosomal compartments, and is quantified in the context of this invention by the ability of an antibody to mediate cytotoxicity in a secondary ADC assay using a toxin-conjugated anti-human Fcγ reagent. The term “clonotype” refers to a unique antibody sequence defined by the amino acid sequence of its heavy chain CDR3 region, with each clonotype potentially comprising a distinct pair of heavy and light chain variable regions. The term “transposable expression vector” refers to a DNA construct containing a transposon sequence flanking a gene of interest, such as a heavy or light chain variable region fused to a constant domain, that allows for stable genomic integration upon co-expression of a transposase enzyme. The term “Transpo-mAb Display” refers to a mammalian cell-based antibody display platform in which antibody-expressing cells are generated by transposition of heavy and light chain expression vectors into a B cell line, enabling surface expression of full-length IgG and secretion of soluble antibody for functional screening. The term “secondary ADC assay” refers to a functional cell killing assay in which a cytotoxic agent is conjugated to an anti-human Fcγ antibody, which then binds to the Fc region of a test antibody bound to its target antigen on the cell surface, thereby delivering the cytotoxic payload in a manner that mimics the mechanism of a primary ADC. The term “pharmaceutically acceptable carrier” refers to a non-toxic, inert substance suitable for administration to a human subject, including but not limited to saline, buffered solutions, dextrose, glycerol, ethanol, and combinations thereof, as well as excipients such as stabilizers, surfactants, and preservatives. The term “therapeutic index” refers to the ratio between the toxic dose and the therapeutic dose of a drug, with a higher therapeutic index indicating a wider safety margin between efficacy and toxicity. The term “tumor-associated antigen” or “TAA” refers to a protein or other molecule expressed at significantly higher levels on tumor cells compared to normal tissues, and which is accessible to antibody binding in vivo. The term “extracellular domain” refers to the portion of a transmembrane protein that extends outside the cell membrane and is accessible to antibody binding, and in the context of ROR2, comprises amino acids 1 to 403. The term “immunoglobulin transgenic mouse” refers to a genetically engineered mouse strain in which the endogenous immunoglobulin heavy and light chain loci have been replaced or supplemented with human V, D, and J gene segments, enabling the production of antibodies with fully human variable regions. The term “surface plasmon resonance” or “SPR” refers to a biophysical technique used to measure the binding kinetics between two molecules in real time, typically used to determine association rate (ka), dissociation rate (kd), and equilibrium dissociation constant (KD). The term “ELISA” refers to enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, a plate-based method for detecting and quantifying antigen-antibody interactions using enzyme-conjugated detection reagents and colorimetric readouts. The term “FACS” refers to fluorescence-activated cell sorting, a flow cytometry-based method for isolating single cells based on their expression of fluorescently labeled markers. The term “IC50” refers to the concentration of a compound required to inhibit a biological process, such as cell viability, by 50%, and is used herein to quantify the potency of ADCs in vitro. The term “CDR” refers to complementarity-determining region, the hypervariable loops within the variable domains of an antibody that directly contact the antigen, and includes CDR1, CDR2, and CDR3 of both heavy and light chains. The term “germline V gene” refers to the unmutated, inherited sequence of a variable region gene segment from which a mature antibody sequence is derived through somatic hypermutation and V(D)J recombination. The term “cross-reactivity” refers to the ability of an antibody to bind to a homologous antigen from a different species, such as cynomolgus monkey or mouse ROR2, and is used to assess the suitability of an antibody for preclinical toxicology studies.

### DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

The present invention provides a comprehensive and integrated system for the discovery, characterization, and development of fully human monoclonal antibodies targeting ROR2 for use in antibody-drug conjugates. The antibodies of the invention are distinguished by their unique combination of high-affinity binding to human ROR2, efficient internalization kinetics, and potent cytotoxic activity when conjugated to a payload, all of which are identified through a novel functional screening platform that eliminates the need for traditional recombinant expression and purification steps prior to functional evaluation. The discovery process begins with the immunization of transgenic mice harboring human immunoglobulin variable gene segments, which are engineered to express fully human VH and VL domains while lacking endogenous murine immunoglobulin chains. These mice are immunized with the extracellular domain of human ROR2 fused to a Twin-Strep-tag, which facilitates purification and detection without interfering with antibody binding. The immunization protocol involves a primary immunization followed by two booster doses, administered intraperitoneally and intravenously, with adjuvants designed to enhance the humoral immune response without inducing excessive inflammation. Serum titers are monitored throughout the immunization schedule, and mice exhibiting the highest antigen-specific IgG responses are selected for spleen harvest. The spleens are processed to isolate B lymphocytes, which are then enriched for ROR2-specific cells using magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) with immobilized ROR2-ECD-Twin-Strep. This enrichment step ensures that subsequent screening is focused on B cells that have undergone antigen-driven selection, thereby increasing the likelihood of isolating high-affinity binders.

Following enrichment, RNA is extracted from the sorted B cells and reverse transcribed into cDNA. Variable region genes encoding the heavy and light chains are amplified by semi-nested polymerase chain reaction using primers designed to specifically recognize human V, D, and J gene segments while excluding murine sequences. The amplified fragments are cloned into transposable expression vectors that contain human IgG1 heavy chain and kappa light chain constant domains, along with genomic intron sequences that permit alternative splicing and co-expression of both membrane-bound and secreted antibody forms. These vectors are co-transfected with a PiggyBac transposase expression plasmid into a murine progenitor B cell line, L11, which is incapable of expressing endogenous immunoglobulins due to its RAG2-deficient background. The transposition event results in stable genomic integration of a single heavy and light chain pair per cell, leading to the expression of a unique monoclonal antibody on the cell surface and its secretion into the culture supernatant. This system enables the generation of large, diverse libraries of antibody-expressing clones, each representing a distinct antigen-binding unit derived from the immunized donor. The resulting cellular libraries are stained with fluorescently labeled ROR2-ECD-Twin-Strep and anti-human IgG antibodies, allowing for the simultaneous detection of antigen binding and antibody expression by flow cytometry. Cells exhibiting high levels of both signals are sorted as single clones into individual wells, ensuring clonality and eliminating the risk of mixed populations.

The key innovation of the invention lies in the functional screening of these single-cell-derived supernatants using a secondary ADC assay. Rather than purifying each antibody and conjugating it to a toxin—a process that is laborious and low-throughput—the supernatants are directly tested for their ability to mediate cytotoxicity in ROR2-overexpressing target cells using a pre-formed, toxin-conjugated anti-human Fcγ antibody. This secondary reagent, which contains a potent anthracycline derivative such as PNU-159682 linked via a cathepsin-cleavable linker, binds to the Fc region of any secreted human IgG present in the supernatant. When the test antibody binds to ROR2 on the surface of target cells, the secondary ADC is brought into proximity and internalized along with the target-antibody complex, delivering the cytotoxic payload intracellularly. Cell viability is measured after a defined incubation period using a luminescent assay, and clones exhibiting strong ROR2 binding (as confirmed by ELISA) coupled with potent cytotoxicity are prioritized for further analysis. This approach allows for the direct identification of antibodies that are not merely high-affinity binders but are functionally competent to induce internalization—a critical determinant of ADC efficacy that is often missed in conventional affinity-based screening. The secondary ADC assay is highly predictive of primary ADC performance, as demonstrated by the strong correlation between the cytotoxicity observed in this assay and the potency of purified ADCs generated from the same clones. Importantly, this method does not require prior knowledge of the antibody sequence, nor does it necessitate the production of purified protein, thereby reducing the time and cost of lead identification by several months.

From this screening process, twelve distinct clonotypes were identified, each defined by a unique heavy chain CDR3 sequence. These clonotypes include GK-1E5, GK-2G8, MK-3B12, GK-5A1, GK-5E1, GK-5G12, GK-6B10, MK-7C3, GK-21D3, GK-22G12, MK-24C10, and MK-24F9. The heavy and light chain variable regions of each clonotype were recovered by RT-PCR from the original transposed cell clones, cloned into recombinant expression vectors, and expressed in HEK293T cells for purification and characterization. Surface plasmon resonance analysis confirmed that all twelve antibodies bind human ROR2 with high affinity, with dissociation constants ranging from 0.7 nM to 347 nM. Six of the twelve antibodies exhibited affinities below 10 nM, while five fell within the 10–100 nM range. However, the most striking finding was the lack of direct correlation between binding affinity and ADC potency. For instance, the antibody GK-6B10, which bound ROR2 with a KD of 5.8 nM, produced an ADC with the lowest cytotoxic potency among the panel, with an IC50 of 168 ng/ml against T47D-hROR2 cells. In contrast, the antibody GK-21D3, with a KD of 347 nM, generated an ADC with an IC50 of 12.3 ng/ml, placing it among the most potent candidates. This discrepancy underscores the importance of epitope specificity and internalization kinetics over binding affinity alone. Further analysis revealed that the most potent ADCs were derived from antibodies that cross-reacted with cynomolgus monkey ROR2, suggesting that these clones recognize epitopes that are conserved across species and are likely to be functionally relevant in preclinical models. Cross-reactivity with mouse ROR2 was observed in only a subset of clones, with GK-5A1 showing the strongest binding, indicating that the epitopes recognized by the most potent antibodies are more conserved in primates than in rodents.

The antibodies of the invention were further characterized for their ability to discriminate between ROR2-expressing and non-expressing cells. When conjugated to PNU-159682 via a Gly3-EDA or Gly5-EDA linker, all twelve ADCs demonstrated potent and selective cytotoxicity against T47D-hROR2 cells, with IC50 values ranging from 12.3 to 168 ng/ml. In contrast, no significant cytotoxicity was observed against L363 multiple myeloma cells, which lack ROR2 expression, even at concentrations exceeding 10,000 ng/ml. This target-dependent activity confirms that the cytotoxic effect is mediated exclusively through ROR2 binding and internalization. The drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR) of the conjugates was consistently maintained between 3 and 4, as determined by HPLC analysis, ensuring homogeneity and minimizing aggregation or instability associated with higher DAR values. The linker chemistry employed—based on a glycine spacer and an enzymatically cleavable dipeptide—ensures stability in plasma while allowing efficient payload release in the lysosome, a critical feature for minimizing off-target toxicity. The antibodies themselves exhibit no intrinsic cytotoxicity, Fc-mediated effector functions, or complement activation, indicating that their therapeutic effect is entirely dependent on payload delivery. This is a significant advantage over naked antibodies, which often require high target expression levels or strong immune recruitment to achieve efficacy.

The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising any one of the disclosed antibodies or ADCs formulated in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier for administration to a human subject. The compositions may be administered intravenously, subcutaneously, or via other parenteral routes, with dosing regimens tailored to the specific cancer indication and patient characteristics. The ADCs are stable in solution for extended periods under controlled conditions, and may be lyophilized for long-term storage. The compositions may further include stabilizing agents such as sucrose, histidine, polysorbate 80, or arginine to prevent aggregation and maintain bioactivity. The invention also includes kits for the diagnosis of ROR2-expressing tumors, comprising the disclosed antibodies conjugated to fluorescent dyes, enzymes, or radioisotopes for use in immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry, or imaging applications. These diagnostic tools enable the identification of patients most likely to benefit from ROR2-targeted ADC therapy, thereby facilitating personalized treatment strategies.

The method of the invention for discovering these antibodies is itself a novel contribution to the field. By integrating immunization of transgenic mice with a functional screening platform that directly evaluates ADC activity in a high-throughput manner, the invention eliminates the traditional bottlenecks of antibody discovery. The use of a transposable expression system ensures that each clone expresses a single, defined antibody pair, while the secondary ADC assay provides a physiologically relevant readout of internalization and payload delivery. This approach is superior to phage or yeast display systems, which rely on non-native expression environments and cannot assess internalization kinetics. It is also superior to hybridoma technology, which produces murine antibodies requiring humanization and lacks the scalability for comprehensive functional screening. The invention further avoids the pitfalls of affinity-based screening alone, which has repeatedly failed to predict ADC efficacy in clinical settings. The antibodies identified through this method are fully human, highly potent, and functionally validated, making them ideal candidates for clinical development. The invention further enables the rapid generation of multiple lead candidates from a single immunization campaign, providing a robust pipeline for the development of next-generation ADCs with enhanced efficacy and safety profiles.
