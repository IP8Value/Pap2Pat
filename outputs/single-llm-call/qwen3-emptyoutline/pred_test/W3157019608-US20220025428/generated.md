# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of molecular information storage and retrieval, specifically to a novel system and method for encoding, storing, and reading digital data using engineered nucleic acid nanostructures and super-resolution optical imaging. The invention integrates principles from DNA nanotechnology, digital information theory, and fluorescence microscopy to create a non-volatile, high-density memory platform that does not rely on traditional sequencing technologies for data retrieval. This system, termed digital Nucleic Acid Memory (dNAM), enables the storage of binary digital information in the spatial arrangement of staple strands within DNA origami nanostructures, where each bit is represented by the presence or absence of a specific docking sequence that binds a fluorescently labeled imager strand. Data is read by detecting the spatial localization of transient binding events of these imager strands using DNA-Points Accumulation for Imaging in Nanoscale Topography (DNA-PAINT), a super-resolution microscopy technique capable of resolving molecular features below the diffraction limit of light. The invention further encompasses a multi-layered error-correction algorithm that combines fountain coding with bi-level parity and checksum encoding to ensure robust recovery of digital information even in the presence of high noise, incomplete strand incorporation, fluorophore bleaching, or background fluorescence. This approach decouples data storage from sequencing-based readout mechanisms, thereby enabling a fundamentally new paradigm for archival data storage that leverages the chemical stability, extreme information density, and low energy requirements of DNA as a molecular substrate. The invention is particularly suited for applications requiring long-term, high-density, and redundant storage of small to moderate volumes of digital data, such as cryptographic keys, biological barcodes, metadata for high-throughput sequencing, or archival records in environments where conventional electronic storage is impractical or unsustainable.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The exponential growth of global digital data has created an urgent need for next-generation storage technologies that offer higher information density, longer archival stability, and lower energy consumption than current magnetic, optical, or solid-state media. Conventional storage systems, such as magnetic tape and hard drives, are approaching fundamental physical limits in areal density and face significant environmental costs in manufacturing, cooling, and power consumption. While DNA has emerged as a promising alternative due to its unparalleled information density—estimated at approximately 1 exabyte per cubic millimeter—and its potential for multi-millennial stability under controlled conditions, existing approaches to DNA-based data storage have been constrained by their reliance on high-throughput sequencing for data retrieval. Sequencing-based methods require the amplification, fragmentation, and reassembly of vast pools of synthetic oligonucleotides, which introduces significant costs, time delays, and error rates that scale with data volume. Moreover, sequencing is inherently destructive, as it requires enzymatic cleavage and readout of nucleotide sequences, rendering data editing or selective retrieval impossible without full re-sequencing of the entire pool. These limitations have hindered the practical adoption of DNA as a viable memory medium, despite its theoretical advantages.

Recent advances in DNA nanotechnology have enabled the precise spatial organization of DNA strands into programmable nanostructures, such as DNA origami, which can serve as scaffolds for arranging molecular components with nanometer precision. These structures have been used to create molecular patterns, logic gates, and sensor arrays, but have not previously been leveraged for digital data storage with optical readout. Super-resolution microscopy techniques, particularly DNA-PAINT, have demonstrated the ability to resolve individual DNA strands and their binding events with sub-10-nm precision by detecting transient hybridization of fluorescently labeled imager strands. However, these techniques have been primarily applied to structural imaging and molecular mapping, not to the encoding and decoding of digital information. Prior attempts to use DNA for data storage have focused on encoding bits directly into nucleotide sequences, necessitating sequencing for readout, or have employed bulk fluorescence signals that lack the spatial resolution required for high-density bit encoding. There exists no prior art that combines the site-specific, addressable placement of molecular docking sites within DNA origami with a super-resolution imaging modality to enable non-sequencing-based, binary digital data storage and retrieval. Furthermore, no system has been developed that integrates fountain coding—a robust error-correction scheme designed for packet loss in noisy communication channels—with bi-level parity and checksum encoding to correct errors at the level of individual nanostructures, thereby enabling reliable data recovery even when the majority of stored structures contain significant readout errors. The absence of such a system represents a critical gap in the field: a method that exploits the spatial programmability of DNA to create a read-only, non-destructive, massively parallel, and error-resilient memory architecture that operates independently of sequencing technologies. The present invention fills this gap by introducing a novel architecture wherein digital data is encoded in the physical presence or absence of extended staple strands within DNA origami, read via localized fluorescence blinking events, and decoded using a multi-layer algorithm that corrects for both structural and optical noise, thereby achieving a level of reliability and scalability previously unattainable in DNA-based memory systems.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a system and method for digital information storage and retrieval using engineered DNA origami nanostructures and super-resolution fluorescence imaging. The invention comprises a plurality of DNA origami nanostructures, each comprising a scaffold strand and a set of staple strands, wherein a subset of the staple strands are extended to include a docking sequence that is complementary to a fluorescently labeled imager strand, and wherein the presence or absence of each extended staple strand at a predefined spatial location on the origami encodes a binary digit, such that a two-dimensional array of binary states is formed across the surface of each origami. The binary state of each location is determined by the binding of the imager strand, which, upon hybridization, emits a transient fluorescent signal detectable by DNA-PAINT microscopy. The invention further comprises a data encoding algorithm that divides a digital message into multiple overlapping data droplets, each droplet encoded onto a distinct DNA origami structure, and that appends to each origami a set of error-correction bits including an index bit, an orientation bit, a checksum bit, and a plurality of parity bits arranged in a bi-level parity matrix that is invariant to rotational orientation. The encoding algorithm employs a fountain code to generate droplets such that each droplet is formed by the exclusive OR (XOR) of a random subset of the original data segments, enabling recovery of the full message from any sufficient subset of droplets regardless of order. The invention further comprises a decoding algorithm that, upon acquisition of super-resolution localization data from a population of DNA origami structures, first performs error correction on each individual origami by analyzing discrepancies between the expected and observed parity and checksum values, assigning weights to each bit based on the number of parity violations it influences, and iteratively flipping bits in order of highest likelihood of error until a valid configuration is found or a maximum number of corrections is reached. The decoding algorithm then reconstructs the original message by identifying droplets using their index bits, and applying XOR operations to recover the original data segments from the droplets, using a priority queue that prioritizes droplets with the fewest number of unknown segments. The system is capable of storing and retrieving digital data without the need for sequencing, with a data density exceeding 300 Gbit/cm², and with a recovery probability of 100% even when fewer than 70% of the stored origami structures are successfully decoded. The invention enables non-destructive, optically mediated readout, selective data editing through strand replacement, and massive parallelism in data acquisition, making it uniquely suited for archival storage applications requiring high redundancy, long-term durability, and low operational energy.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Definitions

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms shall have the meanings ascribed herein. A “DNA origami” refers to a self-assembled nanostructure formed by the folding of a long single-stranded DNA scaffold strand into a predetermined two-dimensional or three-dimensional shape through hybridization with a set of shorter complementary DNA staple strands. The term “staple strand” refers to a short synthetic DNA oligonucleotide designed to bind to multiple non-contiguous regions of the scaffold strand, thereby stabilizing the overall structure and defining its geometry. An “extended staple strand” is a staple strand that contains, in addition to its scaffold-binding domain, a terminal extension comprising a nucleotide sequence that is not involved in scaffold hybridization but is instead designed to serve as a docking site for a complementary imager strand. A “data domain” refers to the spatial location on a DNA origami where an extended staple strand is positioned, such that its presence or absence encodes a binary value. A “binary state” refers to the logical value of 0 or 1 assigned to a data domain based on whether an extended staple strand is present (1) or absent (0) at that location. A “docking sequence” is a defined nucleotide sequence appended to an extended staple strand that is complementary to a specific imager strand and enables specific hybridization under controlled buffer conditions. An “imager strand” is a short, fluorescently labeled DNA oligonucleotide designed to transiently and repeatedly hybridize to a docking sequence, producing a blinking fluorescence signal detectable by super-resolution microscopy. “DNA-PAINT” refers to a super-resolution microscopy technique that achieves sub-diffraction resolution by detecting the stochastic binding and unbinding events of imager strands to target docking sites, thereby localizing the position of each binding event with nanometer precision. A “droplet” is a data unit generated by the fountain code algorithm, comprising the XOR combination of one or more original data segments, along with associated metadata such as index and parity bits, encoded onto a single DNA origami structure. An “index bit” is a set of binary digits encoded on each origami that uniquely identifies the droplet to which it belongs, enabling the decoding algorithm to group droplets by their source. An “orientation bit” is a set of binary digits encoded on each origami that indicates the rotational orientation of the origami relative to a reference frame, allowing the decoding algorithm to correct for rotational misalignment during imaging. A “checksum bit” is a set of binary digits computed from a mathematical function of the data bits and parity bits on an origami, used to verify the integrity of the entire encoded matrix. A “parity bit” is a binary digit computed as the XOR of a subset of data bits, used to detect and correct single or multiple bit errors within the matrix. A “bi-level parity matrix” refers to a configuration of parity bits arranged such that each parity bit is derived from a distinct subset of data bits, and each data bit is involved in multiple parity calculations, enabling the detection of multiple concurrent errors and the assignment of error likelihood weights to individual bit positions. A “fountain code” is an error-correcting code that generates an unlimited number of encoded droplets from a finite set of source data segments, such that any sufficiently large subset of droplets can be used to reconstruct the original data, regardless of order or redundancy. A “localization” refers to the determined spatial position of a single fluorescent emission event detected during a DNA-PAINT recording, corresponding to the transient binding of an imager strand to a docking site. A “cluster” refers to a group of localizations that are spatially proximal and consistent with the expected geometry of a DNA origami structure, used to infer the presence and orientation of a single origami molecule. A “template matching” refers to the computational process of comparing an observed origami pattern against a pre-defined library of known origami designs to identify the best-fitting match, a method used for quality control but not for data retrieval in the claimed invention. A “decoding algorithm” refers to the computational procedure that processes localization data from multiple DNA origami structures to correct errors, identify droplets, and reconstruct the original digital message using the encoded index, parity, checksum, and fountain code information. A “non-volatile memory” refers to a storage medium that retains stored information without the need for continuous power, which in this invention is achieved through the inherent chemical stability of double-stranded DNA under ambient conditions. A “super-resolution imaging system” refers to an optical microscope configured to resolve features below the diffraction limit of light, typically using techniques such as DNA-PAINT, STORM, or PALM, and comprising a laser excitation source, an objective lens with high numerical aperture, an electron-multiplying charge-coupled device (EMCCD) camera, and software for frame-by-frame localization and drift correction. A “glass coverslip” refers to a thin, flat substrate of borosilicate glass used as a support for immobilizing DNA origami structures during imaging, optionally treated with silane or other surface modifiers to promote controlled deposition. A “deposition buffer” refers to a solution containing magnesium ions and a buffered salt solution, such as TBE or TAE, used to promote the stable attachment of DNA origami to a glass surface prior to imaging. An “imaging buffer” refers to a solution containing the deposition buffer supplemented with an oxygen scavenging system, such as protocatechuate 3,4-dioxygenase and trolox, and a low concentration of fluorescent imager strands, optimized to sustain prolonged blinking of fluorophores during DNA-PAINT imaging. A “bit error rate” refers to the proportion of binary bits that are incorrectly read due to noise, incomplete hybridization, fluorophore bleaching, or mislocalization during imaging. A “recovery probability” refers to the likelihood that the complete original digital message can be reconstructed from a given number of successfully decoded origami structures, as determined by the decoding algorithm under specified error conditions. A “data strand library” refers to a collection of pre-synthesized staple strands, each either extended or unextended, that can be selectively combined to form DNA origami structures encoding different binary patterns. A “read head” refers to a single imaging field of view in a super-resolution microscope, capable of simultaneously resolving thousands of DNA origami structures, such that the number of read heads is proportional to the concentration of imager strands and the size of the imaging area.

### Nucleic Acid Architecture

The nucleic acid architecture of the digital Nucleic Acid Memory (dNAM) system is designed to enable the deterministic encoding of binary digital information into the spatial arrangement of staple strands within DNA origami nanostructures. Each origami is constructed from a long, single-stranded M13 bacteriophage scaffold strand, approximately 7,249 nucleotides in length, which is folded into a rectangular planar geometry measuring approximately 90 nanometers by 70 nanometers, with a honeycomb lattice structure defined by the precise hybridization of 48 staple strands arranged in a 6 by 8 grid. The spacing between adjacent staple strand binding sites is maintained at approximately 10 nanometers, corresponding to the helical periodicity of double-stranded DNA, ensuring that each data domain is spatially resolvable under super-resolution microscopy. Of these 48 potential binding sites, a subset is designated as data domains, each of which may be occupied by either an extended staple strand or an unmodified staple strand. The extended staple strand contains a 15- to 25-nucleotide sequence appended to its 5′ or 3′ terminus, which is not complementary to any region of the scaffold strand but is instead fully complementary to a specific imager strand labeled with a fluorophore such as Cy3B, Alexa Fluor 647, or ATTO 655. The unmodified staple strand lacks this extension and serves only to stabilize the origami structure through scaffold hybridization. The presence of an extended staple strand at a given site encodes a binary value of 1, while the absence of such a strand, replaced by its unmodified counterpart, encodes a binary value of 0. This binary encoding scheme is fundamentally distinct from sequence-based encoding, as the information is not contained in the primary sequence of the DNA itself but in the topological arrangement of extended versus non-extended strands across the origami surface. The spatial addressability of each data domain is preserved because the position of each staple strand on the origami is determined by its sequence complementarity to the scaffold, and the scaffold is folded in a predetermined, reproducible manner. The extended domains project perpendicularly from the plane of the origami, ensuring that they are accessible to imager strands in solution and not occluded by the surface or by neighboring structures. The design of the origami ensures that the extended staple strands are positioned such that their docking sequences are oriented away from the glass surface, thereby maximizing their exposure to the bulk solution and minimizing steric hindrance during imager strand binding. The origami structure is stabilized by the presence of magnesium ions in the buffer, which neutralize the negative charge of the phosphate backbone and promote tight hybridization between scaffold and staple strands. The resulting nanostructures are highly stable under ambient conditions and can be stored dry or in aqueous solution for extended periods without degradation of the encoded information. The system is scalable in that the number of data domains per origami can be increased by designing larger origami structures with higher staple strand densities, or by reducing the spacing between binding sites to as little as 3.5 nanometers, which corresponds to the helical rise of DNA and has been resolved in prior super-resolution studies. The architecture is also modular, as the same scaffold strand can be reused with different sets of staple strands to encode different data patterns, enabling the creation of a library of pre-synthesized strands that can be selectively combined to form new data structures. The use of a single scaffold strand across all origami designs ensures uniform folding kinetics and structural fidelity, while the use of distinct staple strand sequences for each data domain ensures that each origami design is uniquely identifiable by its pattern of extended strands. The entire architecture is designed to be compatible with standard DNA synthesis and purification techniques, allowing for high-throughput production of the staple strand library and the assembly of origami structures using thermal annealing protocols. The resulting nanostructures are deposited onto a glass coverslip in a manner that ensures random orientation and uniform distribution, enabling simultaneous imaging of thousands of individual origami molecules in a single field of view. The architecture thus provides a physical substrate for digital information that is both highly dense and optically readable, forming the foundation for a non-sequencing-based DNA memory system.

### Dyes

The dyes employed in the digital Nucleic Acid Memory system are fluorescent molecules covalently attached to the imager strands to enable their detection via super-resolution microscopy. The choice of dye is critical to the performance of the system, as it must possess photophysical properties that support prolonged, stochastic blinking under physiological buffer conditions, high quantum yield, resistance to photobleaching, and compatibility with the excitation and emission spectra of the imaging system. In the preferred embodiment, the dye Cy3B is used due to its high brightness, excellent photostability in the presence of oxygen scavenging systems, and well-characterized blinking behavior in DNA-PAINT applications. The Cy3B dye is conjugated to the 5′ terminus of the imager strand via a C6 spacer arm, ensuring that the fluorophore does not interfere with hybridization to the docking sequence. Alternative dyes such as Alexa Fluor 647, ATTO 655, or Janelia Fluor 646 may also be employed, provided they exhibit similar blinking kinetics and are compatible with the excitation wavelength of the laser source, which in the preferred embodiment is 561 nm. The imager strand itself is a short, single-stranded DNA oligonucleotide, approximately 15 to 20 nucleotides in length, designed to be fully complementary to the docking sequence on the extended staple strand. The concentration of the imager strand in the imaging buffer is maintained at a low level, typically between 1 and 5 nanomolar, to ensure that only a small fraction of docking sites are occupied at any given time, thereby enabling the temporal separation of individual binding events. This low concentration is essential to prevent saturation of the imaging system and to allow for the detection of discrete blinking events that can be localized with sub-10-nanometer precision. The imaging buffer further contains an oxygen scavenging system composed of protocatechuate 3,4-dioxygenase (PCD) and (+)-6-hydroxy-2,5,7,8-tetramethylchromane-2-carboxylic acid (Trolox), which together reduce the concentration of reactive oxygen species that cause photobleaching and extend the duration of fluorescence blinking. The buffer is supplemented with 3-mM protocatechuic acid (PCA), which serves as the substrate for PCD and sustains the catalytic cycle that continuously removes oxygen. The combination of these components enables sustained imaging for tens of thousands of frames, with each imager strand undergoing hundreds of binding and unbinding cycles before photobleaching occurs. The fluorescence signal from each binding event is captured by an electron-multiplying charge-coupled device (EMCCD) camera with a pixel size of approximately 16 nanometers, allowing for the precise determination of the centroid position of each localization. The use of a single dye type across all imager strands ensures that the system does not rely on multiplexing through spectral separation, which would require complex optical filters and increase the risk of crosstalk. Instead, the spatial location of each localization, not its spectral signature, encodes the binary information, simplifying the imaging hardware and increasing the robustness of the system. The fluorophore is chosen to have minimal non-specific binding to the glass surface or to non-target DNA structures, and the imaging buffer is optimized to minimize background fluorescence from free dye or non-specifically bound imager strands. The fluorescence lifetime of the dye is sufficiently long to allow for accurate localization even at low signal-to-noise ratios, and the blinking kinetics are tuned to ensure that the average dwell time of the imager strand on the docking site is between 100 and 500 milliseconds, which balances the need for sufficient photon collection with the need to avoid overlapping localization events. The system is designed to be compatible with a wide range of commercially available fluorophores, and the selection of dye can be adjusted based on the availability of laser lines, camera sensitivity, and environmental constraints. The use of a single, well-characterized dye system ensures reproducibility across experiments and facilitates scaling to larger data sets without the need for additional optical components or complex calibration procedures.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In a working embodiment of the digital Nucleic Acid Memory system, a digital message consisting of the text string “Data is in our DNA!\n” was encoded into 15 distinct DNA origami nanostructures, each designed to store a unique 16-bit data droplet as part of a fountain code encoding scheme. The message was first converted into its ASCII binary representation, resulting in a total of 160 bits. These bits were divided into 10 data segments of 16 bits each, and a fountain code algorithm was applied to generate 15 droplets, each formed by the XOR combination of a random subset of the 10 segments, with the number of segments per droplet drawn from a Soliton distribution optimized for robust decoding. Each droplet was then encoded onto a separate DNA origami structure, which was designed as a 6 by 8 matrix of 48 potential staple strand binding sites, spaced 10 nanometers apart. For each origami, the 16 data bits of the droplet were mapped to 16 of the 48 sites, with the remaining 32 sites reserved for error-correction and metadata. Specifically, four bits were allocated to an index field, uniquely identifying the droplet among the 15 designs; four bits were allocated to an orientation field, encoding the rotational state of the origami; four bits were allocated to a checksum field, computed as the XOR of all data and parity bits; and twenty bits were allocated to a bi-level parity matrix, where each parity bit was derived from a unique combination of data bits such that each data bit participated in multiple parity calculations. The origami structures were synthesized by mixing the M13 scaffold strand with a custom library of 48 staple strands per design, of which 16 were extended to include a 20-nucleotide docking sequence complementary to a Cy3B-labeled imager strand, while the remaining 32 were unmodified. The mixture was annealed in a thermal cycler using a slow cooling protocol from 80°C to 25°C over 12 hours, followed by purification via agarose gel electrophoresis to isolate intact origami structures. The purified origami were mixed in equal molar amounts to form a pooled sample containing approximately 20 femtomoles of total origami. This mixture was deposited onto a glass coverslip that had been cleaned by sonication in Liquinox detergent and treated with a glow discharge to enhance DNA adhesion. Fiducial markers composed of 150-nanometer silanized gold nanoparticles were deposited on the same surface to enable drift correction during imaging. The coverslip was sealed with a flow cell and incubated with a deposition buffer containing 0.5× TBE and 18 mM MgCl₂ for 30 minutes to allow origami to bind to the surface. The buffer was then replaced with an imaging buffer containing 60 nM protocatechuate 3,4-dioxygenase, 1 mM Trolox, 3 nM Cy3B-labeled imager strand, and 10 mM protocatechuic acid. The sample was imaged using a total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscope equipped with a 100× oil-immersion objective (numerical aperture 1.49), a 561-nm laser, and an EMCCD camera with 512 by 512 pixel resolution and 300-millisecond exposure time. A total of 40,000 frames were recorded from a single field of view covering approximately 2,982 square micrometers, yielding an estimated 4,500 individual origami structures. The raw image stack was processed using the ThunderSTORM plugin in ImageJ to identify and localize individual fluorescence events, resulting in a list of over 1.2 million localizations. A custom localization processing algorithm was then applied to cluster these localizations into groups consistent with the expected 6 by 8 grid geometry of the origami structures. The algorithm sampled random points in the localization list, computed the average position of nearby localizations, and accepted clusters only if the density of localizations within a 15-nanometer radius exceeded a threshold and the density in an outer annulus remained below 15% of the inner density, thereby filtering out noise and non-specific signals. Each accepted cluster was then fitted to an idealized 6 by 8 grid using a maximum likelihood estimation that optimized the position, rotation, and intensity of each emitter, while accounting for localization uncertainty and drift-induced blurring. The resulting binary matrix for each origami was generated by applying an empirically determined intensity threshold to assign a value of 1 or 0 to each grid position. These matrices were then passed to the decoding algorithm, which first performed error correction by computing the expected parity and checksum values for each matrix and comparing them to the observed values. Discrepancies were used to assign weights to each bit based on the number of parity violations it contributed to, and a greedy search algorithm iteratively flipped bits with the highest likelihood of error, using a priority queue ordered by the resulting reduction in overall matrix weight. The algorithm was allowed up to nine bit flips per origami before terminating. Once a valid matrix was obtained, the index and droplet data were extracted and grouped by index. The fountain code decoding step then reconstructed the original message by identifying droplets with the fewest number of unknown segments and using XOR operations to progressively resolve the remaining segments. After processing all 4,500 origami structures, the algorithm successfully recovered all 10 data segments and reconstructed the original message with 100% accuracy, despite the fact that the average number of errors per origami was 7.3 and that some origami designs were present in the sample at less than 1% abundance. The system demonstrated that the message could be recovered from a single imaging session using only 20 femtomoles of total origami, corresponding to approximately 750 successfully decoded structures, and that the recovery probability reached 100% when at least 14 of the 15 droplets were recovered. The entire process, from origami synthesis to message reconstruction, required no sequencing, no amplification, and no destruction of the stored material, demonstrating the feasibility of a non-sequencing-based, optically readable DNA memory system with high redundancy, error resilience, and archival stability.

## Materials and Methods

### Buffers

The digital Nucleic Acid Memory system employs two distinct buffer systems: a deposition buffer and an imaging buffer, each optimized for specific stages of the data storage and retrieval workflow. The deposition buffer is formulated to promote the stable and uniform immobilization of DNA origami structures onto a glass surface while maintaining their structural integrity. It consists of 0.5× Tris-borate-EDTA (TBE) buffer supplemented with 18 millimolar magnesium chloride (MgCl₂), which provides the necessary ionic strength to neutralize the electrostatic repulsion between the negatively charged DNA backbone and the glass surface, thereby facilitating adhesion. The pH of the buffer is maintained at approximately 8.0 to ensure optimal hybridization stability of the staple strands. This buffer is prepared using nuclease-free water and filtered through a 0.22-micrometer membrane to remove particulates and microbial contaminants. The imaging buffer is derived from the deposition buffer but is further supplemented with components that enable prolonged, low-noise fluorescence blinking of the imager strands during DNA-PAINT imaging. Specifically, it contains 60 nanomolar protocatechuate 3,4-dioxygenase (PCD), 1 millimolar (+)-6-hydroxy-2,5,7,8-tetramethylchromane-2-carboxylic acid (Trolox), and 3 nanomolar Cy3B-labeled imager strand, all dissolved in the deposition buffer. Additionally, 10 millimolar protocatechuic acid (PCA) is added immediately prior to imaging to serve as the enzymatic substrate for PCD, which catalyzes the removal of molecular oxygen from the solution, thereby suppressing photobleaching and extending the lifetime of the fluorophore. The concentration of the imager strand is carefully calibrated to ensure that, on average, fewer than one imager strand is bound to any given docking site at any moment, thereby enabling the temporal isolation of individual binding events. The buffer is prepared fresh for each imaging session and stored in the dark at 4°C until use to prevent degradation of the fluorophore and enzymatic components. The use of TBE rather than TAE as the base buffer is preferred due to its superior buffering capacity at the pH range required for DNA hybridization and its lower tendency to form precipitates with magnesium ions. The inclusion of Trolox as a radical scavenger further reduces oxidative damage to the fluorophore, while the low concentration of PCA ensures that the enzymatic reaction proceeds at a controlled rate, avoiding excessive local pH shifts that could destabilize the origami structure. All buffers are filtered and degassed prior to use to minimize the formation of bubbles and to ensure homogeneity during deposition and imaging. The composition of the buffers is critical to the performance of the system, as deviations in magnesium concentration can lead to incomplete origami folding or excessive aggregation, while improper oxygen scavenging can result in rapid photobleaching and loss of signal. The system is designed to be robust to minor variations in buffer preparation, but optimal performance requires strict adherence to the specified concentrations and handling protocols.

### Encoding Algorithm

The encoding algorithm employed in the digital Nucleic Acid Memory system is a multi-layered, hierarchical procedure that transforms a digital message into a set of encoded DNA origami structures, each containing a unique combination of data, index, orientation, checksum, and parity bits. The algorithm begins by accepting a digital message of arbitrary length, which is first converted into a binary string using the ASCII encoding standard. This binary string is then divided into k equally sized segments, where k is determined by the number of data bits that can be encoded per origami, which in the preferred embodiment is 16 bits. For each segment, a unique identifier is assigned, and a fountain code is applied to generate a set of droplets, each of which is formed by the exclusive OR (XOR) of a random subset of the k segments. The number of segments included in each droplet is drawn from a Soliton distribution, which is defined mathematically as p(1) = 1/k and p(d) = 1/(d(d−1)) for d > 1, ensuring that the algorithm generates an optimal number of droplets containing a single segment, which are essential for initiating the decoding process. The total number of droplets generated is set to K + ε, where K is the number of original segments and ε is an excess number of droplets added to ensure robust recovery in the presence of errors. In the disclosed embodiment, K = 10 and ε = 5, resulting in 15 droplets. Each droplet is then mapped onto a distinct DNA origami structure, which is designed as a 6 by 8 matrix of 48 potential data sites. The 16 data bits of the droplet are assigned to 16 of these sites, while the remaining 32 sites are allocated to metadata: four bits for the index, which uniquely identifies the droplet among the 15 designs; four bits for the orientation, which encodes the rotational state of the origami as one of four possible orientations (0°, 90°, 180°, or 270°); four bits for the checksum, which is computed as the XOR of all data bits and parity bits on the origami; and twenty bits for the parity matrix, which is constructed as a bi-level parity code such that each parity bit is derived from a unique combination of data bits, and each data bit is involved in at least two parity calculations. The layout of the matrix is designed to be invariant to rotation, such that the relative positions of the index, orientation, checksum, and parity bits remain consistent regardless of the origami’s orientation during imaging. This allows the decoding algorithm to perform error correction before determining the orientation, thereby increasing the robustness of the system. The parity matrix is constructed using a predefined set of parity functions, each of which operates on a distinct subset of the data bits, and the resulting parity values are stored in fixed positions on the origami. The checksum is computed as a single parity over the entire matrix, providing a final integrity check. The encoding algorithm ensures that no two origami structures have identical patterns of extended staple strands, thereby preventing ambiguity during decoding. The output of the algorithm is a library of 15 distinct origami design matrices, each corresponding to a unique droplet and its associated metadata. These matrices are then used to synthesize the corresponding staple strand sets, with extended strands placed at the positions designated as “1” in the matrix. The algorithm is implemented in software and is designed to be scalable, such that larger messages can be encoded by increasing the number of segments and the number of origami structures, while maintaining the same error-correction framework. The algorithm is also designed to be deterministic, such that the same input message will always generate the same set of droplets and origami designs, ensuring reproducibility. The encoding process is entirely independent of the physical synthesis of the origami, allowing for the generation of encoding schemes in silico prior to laboratory implementation.

### DNA Origami Folding

The DNA origami structures used in the digital Nucleic Acid Memory system are fabricated using a well-established thermal annealing protocol that ensures high yield and structural fidelity. The scaffold strand, a single-stranded M13mp18 bacteriophage DNA molecule of 7,249 nucleotides, is mixed in equimolar ratio with a set of staple strands, each designed to bind to specific regions of the scaffold and fold it into the desired rectangular geometry. The staple strands are divided into two categories: extended strands, which contain a 20-nucleotide docking sequence appended to their terminus, and unmodified strands, which lack this extension and serve only to stabilize the structure. The ratio of extended to unmodified strands is determined by the encoding algorithm, such that each origami design contains exactly 16 extended strands and 32 unmodified strands, arranged in the 6 by 8 grid pattern. The mixture is prepared in nuclease-free water and supplemented with Tris-Acetate-EDTA (TAE) buffer to a final concentration of 1× and magnesium chloride to a final concentration of 18 millimolar. The total volume of the reaction is 100 microliters, and the mixture is loaded into a thermal cycler programmed with a slow cooling protocol: the sample is first heated to 90°C for 1 minute to denature any secondary structures, then held at 80°C for 2 minutes to initiate hybridization, and finally cooled from 80°C to 25°C over a period of 12 hours at a rate of 0.01°C per second. This slow cooling rate is critical to ensure that the staple strands hybridize in the correct order and that misfolded intermediates are given time to dissociate and re-form correctly. After annealing, the sample is cooled to 4°C and purified by agarose gel electrophoresis using a 0.8% agarose gel containing 0.5× TBE and 8 millimolar magnesium chloride. The gel is run at 80 volts for 90 minutes at 4°C to maintain structural integrity, and the band corresponding to the intact origami structure is excised and crushed in a microcentrifuge tube. The DNA is extracted from the gel slice by incubating at 4°C overnight with gentle agitation, followed by centrifugation and collection of the supernatant. The purified origami is quantified using a spectrophotometer and stored in the dark at 4°C until use. The folding efficiency is assessed by atomic force microscopy (AFM) to confirm the presence of the expected rectangular shape and the absence of aggregates or incomplete structures. The use of a single scaffold strand across all 15 designs ensures that the folding kinetics are consistent, and the use of a standardized annealing protocol ensures that the yield and purity of each origami design are comparable. The system is designed to be scalable, such that larger origami structures with more staple strands can be fabricated using the same protocol, and the use of pre-synthesized staple strand libraries allows for rapid prototyping of new data patterns without the need for re-optimization of the folding conditions.

### Glass Coverslip Preparation

The preparation of the glass coverslip is a critical step in the digital Nucleic Acid Memory system, as it determines the uniformity, density, and orientation of the deposited DNA origami structures, which in turn affects the quality of the super-resolution imaging. The coverslips used are borosilicate glass, sized at either 25 by 75 millimeters or 22 by 22 millimeters, and are obtained from a commercial supplier with a #1 thickness grade to ensure optical clarity. Prior to use, the coverslips are sonicated for one minute in a 0.1% (v/v) solution of Liquinox detergent to remove organic contaminants, followed by a second sonication for one minute in ultra-pure water to rinse away residual detergent. The coverslips are then dried at 40°C for at least 30 minutes in a clean environment to prevent recontamination. To facilitate the immobilization of DNA origami, fiducial markers in the form of 150-nanometer silanized gold nanoparticles are deposited onto the surface. A 200-microliter solution of 0.2 picomolar gold nanoparticles is applied to the coverslip and incubated at room temperature for 10 minutes, after which the surface is rinsed twice with methanol and twice with ultra-pure water to remove unbound particles. The coverslips are then stored at 40°C until use. For DNA deposition, the coverslips are subjected to a glow discharge treatment using an air-plasma vacuum system. The coverslip is placed in a chamber evacuated to 2 Torr and exposed to a high-frequency alternating current generated by a 115 V Electro-Technic BD-10A generator for 75 seconds. This treatment renders the glass surface hydrophilic and negatively charged, promoting electrostatic adhesion of the negatively charged DNA origami structures. After glow discharge, a sticky-Slide flow cell with a 0.2-microliter channel volume is attached to the coverslip using a UV-curable adhesive. The origami mixture is introduced into the flow cell, and the sample is incubated at room temperature for 30 minutes to allow the origami to bind to the surface. After incubation, the flow cell is rinsed with 1 milliliter of deposition buffer to remove unbound origami, and then refilled with imaging buffer for microscopy. The entire process is performed in a clean environment to minimize dust and particulate contamination, which can interfere with imaging. The use of a flow cell ensures that the origami are deposited in a single monolayer and prevents aggregation, while the glow discharge treatment ensures that the origami are uniformly distributed across the surface. The system is designed to be compatible with a wide range of glass substrates and surface treatments, but the described protocol has been optimized to maximize the density of well-oriented, accessible origami structures for super-resolution imaging.

### Fluorescence Microscopy

The fluorescence microscopy system used to read data from the digital Nucleic Acid Memory is a custom-built total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscope configured for high-resolution, single-molecule imaging. The system is based on an inverted Nikon Eclipse Ti2 microscope platform equipped with a Perfect Focus System for real-time focus stabilization and an oil-immersion CFI Apochromat ×100 TIRF objective with a numerical aperture of 1.49, which provides the necessary resolution and light collection efficiency to detect single fluorophore binding events. The excitation source is a 561-nm solid-state laser, selected to match the excitation maximum of the Cy3B fluorophore used in the imager strands. The laser light is directed through a 405/488/561/647 nm Laser Quad Band Set TIRF filter cube from Chroma, which separates excitation and emission light and ensures minimal crosstalk. The emitted fluorescence is collected by the objective and imaged onto a ProEM EMCCD camera from Princeton Instruments, which has a 512 by 512 pixel sensor with a pixel size of 16 micrometers, corresponding to a lateral resolution of approximately 16 nanometers per pixel under the imaging conditions. The camera is operated in frame-transfer mode with a 300-millisecond exposure time, resulting in a frame rate of approximately 3 frames per second. The entire imaging system is enclosed in a temperature-controlled chamber maintained at 25°C to prevent thermal drift during long recordings. The sample is mounted on a motorized stage that allows for precise positioning of the field of view. The imaging buffer is introduced into the flow cell immediately before data acquisition, and a recording of 40,000 frames is acquired for each sample, which typically spans a field of view of approximately 2,982 square micrometers. The system is calibrated using fiducial markers composed of 150-nanometer silanized gold nanoparticles, which are deposited on the same coverslip as the origami and serve as reference points for drift correction during post-processing. The laser power is kept at a low intensity, typically between 1 and 5 milliwatts at the sample, to minimize photobleaching while still providing sufficient signal for localization. The system is designed to be modular, such that alternative lasers, cameras, or objectives can be substituted without altering the core principles of the system. The use of TIRF illumination ensures that only fluorophores within approximately 100 nanometers of the glass surface are excited, reducing background fluorescence from the bulk solution and improving the signal-to-noise ratio. The system is capable of simultaneously resolving thousands of origami structures in a single field of view, enabling massively parallel data acquisition without the need for mechanical scanning or multiplexing.

### DNA-PAINT Fluorophore Localization

The localization of fluorescent imager strand binding events is performed using the ThunderSTORM plugin for ImageJ, which is a widely validated software tool for processing super-resolution microscopy data. The raw image stack, consisting of 40,000 consecutive frames, is imported into the software, and each frame is processed to identify individual fluorescent spots using a two-dimensional Gaussian fitting algorithm. The algorithm identifies local maxima in intensity that exceed a predefined threshold, determined empirically as three times the standard deviation of the background noise, and fits each spot to a 2D Gaussian point spread function (PSF) to determine its centroid position with sub-pixel accuracy. The fitting process accounts for the Poisson statistics of photon emission and the spatial extent of the PSF, which is calibrated using a known standard such as fluorescent beads. The resulting list of localizations includes the x and y coordinates of each detected event, the intensity of the signal, and an estimate of the localization uncertainty. The localization data is then drift-corrected using the Picasso-Render software package, which aligns the positions of the fiducial markers across all frames to correct for stage drift and sample movement. The drift correction is performed using a rigid-body transformation model that minimizes the mean squared displacement of the fiducials between frames. After drift correction, the localizations are filtered to remove outliers based on their localization uncertainty and intensity, and the remaining localizations are clustered to identify groups that correspond to individual origami structures. The clustering is performed using a nearest-neighbor algorithm that groups localizations within a 15-nanometer radius, and only clusters containing between 10 and 100 localizations are retained for further analysis. This filtering step removes noise from non-specific binding, free imager strands, and background fluorescence. The resulting set of clusters is then passed to the localization data processing algorithm, which performs the final assignment of binary values to each data domain on the origami.

### Localization Data Processing

The localization data processing algorithm is a custom-developed computational pipeline that transforms the raw list of fluorescent localizations into a binary matrix representing the encoded data on each DNA origami structure. The algorithm begins by sampling random points from the list of localizations and computing the average position of nearby localizations within a 15-nanometer radius. If the number of localizations within this inner circle exceeds a threshold of 15, and the number of localizations in an outer annulus (15 to 30 nanometers radius) is less than 15% of the inner count, the cluster is accepted as a candidate origami structure. This criterion ensures that only bright, isolated clusters are selected, minimizing the inclusion of fragmented or overlapping structures. For each accepted cluster, the algorithm constructs an idealized 6 by 8 grid of emitter positions based on the average geometry of the origami, as determined from a reference dataset of thousands of successfully decoded structures. The algorithm then performs a maximum likelihood estimation to determine the optimal position, rotation, and scale of this grid relative to the observed localizations. The likelihood function is defined as the product of the probabilities of observing each localization given the positions of the emitters, the background intensity, and the localization uncertainty reported by ThunderSTORM. The optimization is performed using the L-BFGS-B algorithm, which minimizes the negative log-likelihood subject to the constraint that all emitter intensities are positive. The resulting fit yields the coordinates of each of the 48 potential data sites, the rotation angle of the origami, and the intensity of each emitter. The binary value of each site is then assigned by comparing the intensity of the emitter to an empirically derived threshold, which is determined by analyzing the intensity distribution of known “1” and “0” sites from a training dataset. Sites with intensity above the threshold are assigned a value of 1, and those below are assigned a value of 0. The resulting 6 by 8 binary matrix is then passed to the decoding algorithm. The algorithm also performs a parallel template matching step, in which the observed matrix is compared to a library of known origami designs to assess the quality of the data and to identify potential errors, but this step is not used for actual data retrieval, as it requires prior knowledge of the encoded message. The entire processing pipeline is implemented in Python and is designed to be computationally efficient, enabling the analysis of tens of thousands of origami structures in a single session.

### Decoding Algorithm

The decoding algorithm is the central component of the digital Nucleic Acid Memory system, responsible for reconstructing the original digital message from the binary matrices obtained from each DNA origami structure. The algorithm operates in two sequential stages: error correction at the origami level, followed by fountain code decoding at the message level. In the first stage, the algorithm receives a binary matrix from each origami and computes the expected values of the parity and checksum bits based on the observed data bits. Any discrepancy between the expected and observed values is recorded, and a weight is assigned to each bit based on the number of parity violations it contributes to. For each data bit, the weight is calculated as the sum of the absolute differences between the expected and observed parity values for all parity functions that include that bit. For each parity bit, the weight is calculated as the sum of the weights of all data bits that contribute to its calculation, but only if those data bits have a weight greater than 1. This ensures that bits that are involved in multiple parity violations are assigned higher weights, indicating a higher probability of error. The algorithm then computes an overall matrix weight by summing all individual bit weights and normalizing by the number of correctly matched parity bits. A priority queue is initialized with all possible single-bit flips, ranked by the reduction in overall matrix weight that each flip would produce. The algorithm iteratively selects the bit flip with the highest priority, applies it to the matrix, recomputes the weights, and updates the queue with new candidate flips. This process continues until either a matrix is found with no parity or checksum violations, or until the maximum number of allowed bit flips (nine in the disclosed embodiment) is reached. If a valid matrix is found, the index, orientation, and data bits are extracted. If no valid matrix is found, the origami is discarded. In the second stage, the algorithm collects all successfully decoded matrices and groups them by their index bits. Each group corresponds to a droplet, and the algorithm initializes a priority queue of droplets ordered by the number of unknown data segments they contain. The algorithm then iteratively selects the droplet with the lowest number of unknown segments (i.e., the “degree one” droplet), extracts its known data segments, and uses them to XOR with other droplets to reduce their unknown segment count. This process continues until all segments are resolved or until no further progress can be made. If all 10 original segments are recovered, the algorithm reconstructs the original message by concatenating the segments in order and converting the binary string back to ASCII text. The algorithm is designed to be deterministic, such that the same input will always produce the same output, and it is robust to the loss of up to five droplets, as demonstrated by the successful recovery of the message even when fewer than 70% of the origami structures were successfully decoded. The algorithm is implemented in Python and is optimized for speed and memory efficiency, enabling the processing of thousands of origami structures in under one hour on a standard desktop computer.

## Data Simulation Test

To evaluate the theoretical performance and scalability of the digital Nucleic Acid Memory system, a series of in silico simulations were performed to assess the recovery rate of encoded messages under varying conditions of data size, bit error rate, and origami decoding efficiency. Random binary messages of lengths ranging from 160 bits to 12,800 bits were generated at 320-bit intervals, and each message was divided into segments of size b, where b varied between 8 and 12 bits depending on the message length to accommodate the increasing number of index bits required for larger messages. Each segment was encoded into droplets using the same fountain code algorithm as in the experimental system, with 15 droplets generated per message. Ten copies of each unique origami design were simulated, and each copy was subjected to a random bit flip rate ranging from 0 to 9 bits per origami. The simulated origami matrices were then processed using the same decoding algorithm as in the experimental system, with a maximum of nine bit flips permitted per origami. The recovery rate of the original message was recorded for each combination of message size and bit error rate. The simulations demonstrated that the system could recover 100% of messages with up to 7.4 bit errors per origami, and that the recovery rate dropped to 97.5% at 7.4 errors, 55% at 8.2 errors, and 7.5% at 9 errors. The number of origami required to achieve 100% recovery was found to scale linearly with message size up to 5,000 bytes, after which the increasing number of index bits reduced the available data bits per origami, limiting the maximum message size to approximately 64 kilobytes in the current architecture. The simulations also confirmed that the system could recover the full message even when as few as 63% of the origami structures were successfully decoded, demonstrating the robustness of the fountain code and error-correction scheme. The results of the simulations were consistent with the experimental data, validating the accuracy of the model and providing a predictive framework for optimizing future iterations of the system. The simulations further showed that increasing the number of data bits per origami by reducing the number of index bits or by using larger origami structures would significantly increase the maximum message size and improve the overall information density. The simulation framework is implemented as an open-source software package and is available for public use to facilitate further development and optimization of the system.
