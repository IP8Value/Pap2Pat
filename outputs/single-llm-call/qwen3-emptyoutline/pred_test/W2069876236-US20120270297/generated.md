# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The genetic manipulation of hyperthermophilic bacteria has long presented formidable challenges due to their obligate anaerobic nature, extreme growth temperatures, and the presence of robust restriction-modification systems that degrade foreign DNA. Among these organisms, members of the genus Thermotoga stand out not only for their remarkable ability to thrive at temperatures approaching 90°C, but also for their phylogenetic position near the base of the bacterial domain, suggesting they may retain ancestral metabolic and genetic features critical to understanding early life on Earth. Isolated from diverse geothermal environments—including deep-sea hydrothermal vents, continental hot springs, and subsurface oil reservoirs—Thermotoga species exhibit a unique capacity to ferment a broad spectrum of polysaccharides, producing molecular hydrogen as a primary metabolic end product. This metabolic profile has positioned them as promising candidates for biohydrogen production through metabolic engineering, offering a sustainable alternative to fossil fuel-derived energy sources. Despite decades of biochemical and genomic investigations, the absence of reliable genetic tools has severely limited functional studies in Thermotoga, leaving their physiology largely unexplored at the molecular level.

Prior efforts to introduce heterologous genes into Thermotoga were confined to transient expression systems relying on liposome-mediated delivery of plasmid DNA. These early attempts, while demonstrating the feasibility of DNA uptake, failed to yield stable transformants capable of sustained growth on selective media. The inability to isolate clonal populations rendered these systems unsuitable for long-term experimentation, strain engineering, or industrial applications. Subsequent studies attempted to overcome this barrier by optimizing culture conditions, modifying vector backbones, and exploring alternative transformation methods, yet none succeeded in establishing a reproducible, scalable, and genetically stable transformation protocol. The prevailing consensus in the field was that Thermotoga, like many other strict anaerobes, were recalcitrant to genetic manipulation due to a combination of low plating efficiency, oxygen sensitivity, and potent restriction systems that rapidly cleaved unmethylated foreign DNA.

Compounding these difficulties was the lack of a suitable selection marker compatible with Thermotoga’s physiology. Antibiotic resistance genes commonly used in mesophilic hosts such as Escherichia coli—such as those conferring resistance to ampicillin or tetracycline—were ineffective in Thermotoga, either due to intrinsic resistance mechanisms or the absence of functional expression systems capable of driving transcription and translation at elevated temperatures. The identification of a thermostable kanamycin adenyl transferase gene, capable of conferring resistance to kanamycin under hyperthermophilic conditions, represented a critical breakthrough. However, even with the availability of a functional selectable marker, the persistent failure to recover stable transformants suggested that the underlying barriers extended beyond mere gene expression and involved fundamental issues of DNA delivery, persistence, and replication.

Further complicating matters was the absence of a well-characterized replicon capable of autonomous maintenance in Thermotoga. Although cryptic plasmids such as pRQ7, pMC24, and pRKU1 had been identified in various Thermotoga strains, their small size, minimal genetic content, and lack of detailed functional characterization rendered them unsuitable for direct use as cloning vectors. Their replication mechanism—believed to proceed via a rolling-circle mode—was inferred from sequence homology and supercoiling analysis, but no studies had demonstrated their utility as a basis for engineered shuttle vectors. Moreover, the stability of plasmid DNA in Thermotoga under non-selective conditions remained entirely unexplored, raising questions about whether any introduced DNA could be maintained over multiple generations without continuous antibiotic pressure.

The present invention addresses these longstanding limitations by providing a comprehensive and integrated system for the genetic transformation, stable maintenance, and functional expression of heterologous genes in Thermotoga species. This system encompasses a novel shuttle vector, pDH10, which combines a thermostable kanamycin resistance determinant with the minimal replication origin derived from the endogenous plasmid pRQ7, enabling autonomous replication in both E. coli and Thermotoga. Crucially, the invention introduces a suite of optimized culture and handling techniques that dramatically enhance plating efficiency and viability under aerobic conditions, circumventing the need for expensive anaerobic chambers or complex gas-handling infrastructure. These methods include an embedded growth protocol that suspends cells within a semi-solid matrix of Gelrite-containing medium, thereby minimizing oxygen exposure during plating, and a soft SVO medium formulation that facilitates seamless transfer of single colonies from solid to liquid culture without compromising viability. Together, these innovations enable the routine isolation of stable Thermotoga transformants for the first time.

Furthermore, the invention reveals an unexpected and previously undocumented phenomenon: the incorporation of the pRQ7-derived replication origin into a standard E. coli vector backbone significantly enhances the plasmid’s stability in its heterologous host, even in the absence of selective pressure. This stabilization effect is independent of copy number and cannot be explained by known mechanisms such as increased replication efficiency or enhanced partitioning. Instead, it suggests the presence of an as-yet-uncharacterized genetic element within pRQ7 that actively promotes plasmid persistence in bacterial cells, potentially through interaction with host replication or segregation machinery. This discovery not only provides a powerful tool for improving vector stability in industrial applications but also opens new avenues for understanding the evolutionary mechanisms underlying plasmid maintenance across phylogenetically distant organisms.

The successful transformation and stable maintenance of pDH10 in multiple Thermotoga strains—including T. neapolitana, T. maritima, and T. sp. RQ7—demonstrates the broad applicability of this system across the genus. The vector’s functionality has been rigorously validated through multiple independent methods, including PCR amplification of the kan gene from plasmid extracts, restriction enzyme digestion of amplified fragments, and long-term passage experiments under non-selective conditions, all of which confirm the autonomous, extrachromosomal nature of the plasmid. The absence of chromosomal integration events, coupled with the consistent recovery of intact plasmid DNA from transformants, further supports the conclusion that pDH10 replicates independently within Thermotoga cells. This represents a paradigm shift in the field, as it establishes Thermotoga as a genetically tractable organism for the first time, enabling targeted gene knockouts, promoter engineering, metabolic pathway optimization, and synthetic biology applications previously deemed impossible.

The implications of this invention extend far beyond basic research. The ability to stably engineer Thermotoga opens the door to the rational design of hyperthermophilic microbial cell factories capable of operating under industrial conditions that would inactivate conventional mesophilic hosts. These include high-temperature fermentation processes, simultaneous saccharification and fermentation of lignocellulosic biomass, and biohydrogen production in bioreactors where contamination by mesophiles is a persistent problem. Moreover, the stabilization mechanism conferred by the pRQ7 origin may be harnessed to improve the performance of other plasmid-based systems in diverse bacterial hosts, particularly those with high rates of plasmid loss under non-selective conditions—such as Clostridia, Bacilli, and other industrially relevant anaerobes. The methods described herein are simple, cost-effective, and scalable, requiring only standard laboratory equipment and widely available reagents, making them accessible to research groups worldwide. This invention thus represents not merely an incremental improvement in genetic tools, but a foundational advancement that redefines the possibilities for studying and engineering one of the most enigmatic and promising groups of microorganisms on Earth.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel and highly effective system for the genetic transformation, stable maintenance, and functional expression of heterologous genes in hyperthermophilic bacteria of the genus Thermotoga, which have previously been considered genetically intractable due to their obligate anaerobic nature, extreme growth temperatures, and potent restriction-modification systems. The invention comprises a recombinant shuttle vector, designated pDH10, which enables autonomous replication and selection in both Escherichia coli and multiple Thermotoga species, including T. neapolitana, T. maritima, and T. sp. RQ7. The vector incorporates a thermostable kanamycin adenyl transferase gene under the control of a promoter derived from Thermus thermophilus, which is functionally active at elevated temperatures, and a minimal replication origin derived from the endogenous Thermotoga plasmid pRQ7, which is sufficient to support extrachromosomal replication in Thermotoga cells. The pDH10 vector further contains a ColE1 origin of replication and a β-lactamase gene for selection and amplification in E. coli, rendering it a fully functional shuttle vector capable of seamless cloning and propagation in mesophilic hosts prior to transformation into Thermotoga.

The invention further encompasses a suite of optimized culture and handling techniques that dramatically enhance the plating efficiency and viability of Thermotoga under aerobic conditions, thereby eliminating the need for costly anaerobic chambers or complex gas-handling systems. A key innovation is the embedded growth method, wherein Thermotoga cells are suspended in a semi-solid medium composed of double-strength SVO broth supplemented with 0.3% Gelrite, poured directly into Petri dishes, and allowed to solidify around the cells. This method reduces oxygen exposure during plating and enables plating efficiencies approaching 50%, which is approximately ten thousand-fold higher than conventional surface spreading techniques. Additionally, the invention introduces a soft SVO medium containing 0.075% Gelrite, which serves as an intermediate culture medium that permits the transfer of single colonies from solid plates to liquid culture under ambient atmospheric conditions without loss of viability. This medium prevents oxygen penetration while maintaining sufficient fluidity for inoculation via loop or syringe, enabling the routine isolation and propagation of transformants under standard laboratory conditions.

The invention further demonstrates that the pDH10 vector is stably maintained in Thermotoga over multiple generations, even in the complete absence of selective pressure, with nearly 100% retention of the plasmid after six consecutive transfers. This unprecedented stability is not attributable to chromosomal integration, as confirmed by the exclusive detection of the kanamycin resistance gene in plasmid DNA extracts and its absence from genomic DNA preparations. The vector is maintained as an autonomous, low-copy-number plasmid, and its persistence is not due to increased replication frequency, as copy number comparisons with control vectors reveal equivalent levels of backbone DNA. Remarkably, the inclusion of the pRQ7-derived replication origin in the pDH10 vector also confers a previously unrecognized stabilization effect in its heterologous host, Escherichia coli, where pDH10 exhibits dramatically improved persistence compared to its parent vector pKT1 under non-selective conditions. After six serial transfers in the absence of ampicillin, approximately 32% of E. coli cells still retain pDH10, whereas the control vector is completely lost after three transfers. This stabilization effect is independent of copy number and suggests the presence of a novel genetic element within the pRQ7 sequence that actively promotes plasmid maintenance across phylogenetically distant bacterial species.

Transformation of Thermotoga is achieved through two distinct and equally effective methods: electroporation and liposome-mediated delivery. Electroporation using optimized parameters—specifically, a voltage of 1.8 to 2.0 kV, 25 μF capacitance, and 200 Ω resistance—yields transformation efficiencies of approximately two transformants per microgram of DNA in T. sp. RQ7 and 0.25 transformants per microgram in T. maritima. Liposome-mediated transformation using DOTAP reagent yields higher efficiencies, with four to five transformants per microgram of DNA. Both methods produce viable, antibiotic-resistant colonies that grow robustly in liquid and solid media, and all transformants retain the kanamycin resistance gene over extended culture periods. The invention further confirms that kanamycin is an effective selection marker for T. sp. RQ7 and T. maritima, with a minimum inhibitory concentration of 150 μg/ml in liquid medium and 250 μg/ml in solid or semi-solid media, while T. neapolitana exhibits partial resistance and requires higher concentrations for selection.

The invention also establishes that the pDH10 vector is not subject to degradation by Thermotoga restriction systems when properly methylated, and that the observed transformation efficiencies are limited primarily by oxygen sensitivity during cell handling rather than by restriction activity. The successful isolation of transformants using both transformation methods, coupled with the absence of spontaneous mutants in control experiments, confirms that the observed resistance is due to the functional expression of the introduced kan gene and not to endogenous mutations. The invention thus overcomes the longstanding barriers to Thermotoga genetic manipulation and provides a complete, reproducible, and scalable platform for the engineering of hyperthermophilic bacteria. The implications of this invention are profound, enabling the development of genetically modified Thermotoga strains for industrial biohydrogen production, high-temperature biocatalysis, and synthetic biology applications that were previously inaccessible. Furthermore, the novel plasmid stabilization mechanism conferred by the pRQ7 origin offers a broadly applicable strategy for enhancing the persistence of recombinant vectors in diverse bacterial hosts, particularly those prone to plasmid loss under non-selective conditions.

The term “shuttle vector” denotes a recombinant DNA molecule that is capable of autonomous replication and selection in two or more distinct host organisms, in this case, Escherichia coli and Thermotoga species, and contains genetic elements necessary for replication, selection, and propagation in both hosts. The term “autonomous replication” refers to the ability of a plasmid to replicate independently of the host chromosome, without integration into genomic DNA, and to be stably inherited by daughter cells during cell division, as confirmed by the exclusive detection of the plasmid in plasmid DNA extracts and its absence from chromosomal DNA preparations. The term “extrachromosomal” describes a genetic element that exists as a separate, non-integrated molecule within the cell, as opposed to being physically incorporated into the host genome. The term “plating efficiency” refers to the percentage of viable cells in a given sample that are capable of forming a discrete colony on solid or semi-solid growth medium under defined conditions, and in this invention, it is demonstrated to reach approximately 50% using the embedded growth method, which is substantially higher than conventional surface spreading techniques. The term “non-selective conditions” refers to growth environments in which the selective agent, such as kanamycin or ampicillin, is absent, thereby allowing for the assessment of plasmid stability based solely on the inherent ability of the vector to persist in the host over multiple generations. The term “stabilization effect” refers to the unexpected and significant enhancement in plasmid persistence observed when the pRQ7-derived replication origin is incorporated into a standard E. coli vector backbone, resulting in markedly improved retention rates under non-selective conditions compared to control vectors lacking this sequence. The term “semi-solid medium” refers to a culture medium containing a gelling agent such as Gelrite at a concentration sufficient to impart structural integrity and prevent oxygen diffusion, yet sufficiently fluid to allow mechanical manipulation via inoculation loop or syringe, and in this invention, it is defined as SVO medium containing between 0.05% and 0.1% Gelrite. The term “embedded growth” describes the method of plating Thermotoga cells by suspending them within a solidifying matrix of semi-solid medium prior to complete gelation, thereby minimizing exposure to atmospheric oxygen during the plating process. The term “electroporation” refers to the application of a controlled electrical pulse to bacterial cells to transiently permeabilize the cell membrane and facilitate the uptake of exogenous DNA, and in this invention, it is performed using specific parameters including voltage, capacitance, and resistance that are optimized for Thermotoga. The term “liposome-mediated transformation” refers to the delivery of DNA into cells via encapsulation within lipid vesicles, specifically using DOTAP as the cationic lipid reagent, which facilitates fusion with the bacterial membrane and intracellular release of the plasmid. The term “thermostable” describes a protein or genetic element that retains its structural integrity and functional activity at temperatures exceeding 70°C, and in this context, refers specifically to the kanamycin adenyl transferase enzyme encoded by the kan gene, which remains active under the hyperthermophilic growth conditions of Thermotoga. The term “restriction-modification system” refers to a bacterial defense mechanism comprising a restriction endonuclease that cleaves foreign DNA and a corresponding methyltransferase that protects host DNA by methylation, and in this invention, it is acknowledged as a potential barrier to transformation, though its activity is overcome through the use of appropriately methylated DNA and optimized handling procedures. The term “copy number” refers to the average number of copies of a plasmid per bacterial cell, and in this invention, it is determined by comparative analysis of plasmid and chromosomal DNA bands on agarose gels following restriction digestion. The term “Gelrite” refers to a gelling agent composed of a sulfated galactan polysaccharide derived from the red alga Gelidium, which is used in place of agar due to its ability to remain solid at temperatures above 80°C and its compatibility with hyperthermophilic organisms. The term “SVO medium” refers to a defined synthetic growth medium specifically formulated for the cultivation of Thermotoga species, containing sodium thiosulfate, vitamin B12, and other essential nutrients required for growth under anaerobic, hyperthermophilic conditions. The term “transformant” refers to a Thermotoga cell that has successfully taken up and stably maintained the recombinant vector pDH10 and expresses the kanamycin resistance phenotype, as confirmed by growth on selective media and molecular verification of the inserted gene. ### Improved Method for Cultivation of Thermotoga.

The cultivation of Thermotoga species under laboratory conditions has historically been constrained by their strict anaerobic requirements and extreme thermophilicity, which together render standard microbiological techniques inadequate for routine genetic manipulation. Conventional methods for plating bacterial colonies, such as surface spreading or streaking, expose cells to atmospheric oxygen during handling, leading to rapid cell death and negligible plating efficiency. This has been a primary reason for the failure of prior attempts to isolate stable Thermotoga transformants, as even a small fraction of viable cells capable of DNA uptake would be lost before colony formation could occur. The present invention overcomes this fundamental limitation by introducing an embedded growth method that dramatically enhances plating efficiency while eliminating the need for anaerobic chambers or complex gas-handling infrastructure. In this method, a suspension of Thermotoga cells in logarithmic growth phase is mixed with molten, double-strength SVO medium containing 0.3% Gelrite, which is maintained at a temperature just below its gelling point (approximately 65°C to 70°C). The mixture is then poured directly into standard Petri dishes and allowed to solidify at ambient temperature. As the medium solidifies, the cells become uniformly suspended within the Gelrite matrix, effectively shielding them from oxygen exposure during the critical early stages of colony formation. This approach ensures that the majority of viable cells remain in a low-oxygen microenvironment, permitting them to initiate growth and divide without the lethal oxidative damage typically inflicted by aerobic handling.

The plating efficiency achieved through this embedded method is approximately 50%, as determined by comparing the number of colonies formed to the total cell count in the original culture, as measured by optical density and direct microscopic enumeration. This represents an increase of more than four orders of magnitude over conventional surface spreading techniques, which typically yield fewer than 10^4 colony forming units per milliliter under identical conditions. The high efficiency is not due to increased cell viability per se, but rather to the preservation of viability during the plating process itself. In control experiments, when the same cell suspension was spread on the surface of solidified SVO medium, the resulting colony count was reduced by a factor of approximately 10,000, confirming that the embedded method specifically mitigates oxygen-induced cell death rather than enhancing intrinsic growth rates. The method is compatible with multiple Thermotoga strains, including T. neapolitana, T. maritima, and T. sp. RQ7, and produces colonies that are morphologically indistinguishable from those grown under anaerobic conditions in glove boxes. Colonies typically appear within 24 hours and reach full size within 48 hours, allowing for rapid screening and selection of transformants. The simplicity of the method—requiring only standard laboratory glassware, a hot plate, and a standard incubator—makes it accessible to any research laboratory without the need for specialized equipment or extensive training.

To facilitate the transfer of single colonies from solid to liquid media under aerobic conditions, the invention further introduces a soft SVO medium formulation containing 0.075% Gelrite. This concentration of Gelrite is sufficient to prevent significant oxygen diffusion into the medium while maintaining a semi-fluid consistency that permits the physical manipulation of cells using a sterile inoculation loop or a syringe. Single colonies picked from embedded plates are gently pressed into the soft medium, where they are immediately surrounded by a locally anaerobic microenvironment created by the gelling agent’s barrier effect. After 12 to 24 hours of incubation at 77°C, the culture becomes sufficiently dense to be withdrawn using a sterile syringe and transferred to liquid SVO medium for further propagation. This intermediate step eliminates the need for cumbersome procedures such as Hungate tubes or rolling bottles, which are prone to contamination and difficult to scale. The soft SVO medium also serves as an excellent storage medium for Thermotoga strains, as cultures maintained in this form at room temperature retain viability and growth competence for up to two months without observable loss of function or phenotypic drift. This feature is particularly valuable for archiving transformants, sharing strains between laboratories, or maintaining working stocks without the need for cryopreservation. The combination of embedded plating and soft SVO transfer creates a seamless workflow that bridges the gap between genetic selection and functional analysis, enabling the routine isolation, propagation, and characterization of Thermotoga transformants under standard laboratory conditions.

### Strains and Cultivation Conditions

The strains utilized in this invention include Thermotoga neapolitana ATCC 49049, Thermotoga maritima MSB8, and Thermotoga sp. RQ7, each of which was obtained from established culture collections or directly from collaborating laboratories. T. neapolitana was acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), while T. maritima MSB8 and T. sp. RQ7 were kindly provided by Dr. Harald Huber of the University of Regensburg, Germany. All strains were maintained in SVO medium, a synthetic, chemically defined broth specifically formulated to support the growth of hyperthermophilic Thermotoga species under anaerobic conditions. The medium contains sodium thiosulfate as a sulfur source, yeast extract for vitamins and trace elements, peptone for nitrogen, and other essential nutrients required for metabolic activity at elevated temperatures. Cultures were grown in 100 mL serum bottles sealed with rubber stoppers and aluminum crimp caps, which were pre-sparged with nitrogen gas to remove dissolved oxygen and create an anaerobic headspace. Inoculation was performed using a sterile syringe and needle, with a typical inoculum size of 2% v/v of an overnight culture. Liquid cultures were incubated at 77°C with orbital shaking at 100 rpm to ensure adequate mixing and nutrient distribution. Growth was monitored by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD600), and cultures were harvested during mid-logarithmic phase, typically at an OD600 of 0.2 to 0.4, for transformation experiments.

For solid and semi-solid media, double-strength SVO was prepared by doubling the concentration of all components except Gelrite, which was added at varying concentrations depending on the intended application. For embedded plating, 0.3% Gelrite was incorporated into the double-strength medium, which was then mixed with cell suspensions and poured into Petri dishes. For soft SVO, a concentration of 0.075% Gelrite was used, which produced a gel with sufficient structural integrity to prevent oxygen penetration while remaining sufficiently fluid for inoculation. All media components were autoclaved separately to prevent degradation of heat-sensitive nutrients, and Gelrite was dissolved in distilled water prior to addition to the medium. After autoclaving, the media were cooled to approximately 65°C and mixed under sterile conditions to avoid premature gelation. For anaerobic incubation of plates, Petri dishes were placed inside a Vacu-Quik jar equipped with a palladium catalyst and a hydrogen-generating packet, which facilitated the conversion of residual oxygen to water and maintained a reducing atmosphere of 96% nitrogen and 4% hydrogen. The jar was then sealed and incubated at 77°C for 24 to 48 hours, during which time colonies became visible.

Kanamycin was used as the sole selective agent in all transformation experiments and was added to the medium at concentrations of 150 μg/mL for liquid cultures and 250 μg/mL for soft and solid media. These concentrations were determined through extensive sensitivity testing, which revealed that lower concentrations permitted the emergence of spontaneous resistant mutants, while higher concentrations did not significantly improve selection stringency. The antibiotic was added to the medium after autoclaving and cooling to 65°C to prevent thermal degradation. All manipulations involving Thermotoga cultures, including pipetting, inoculation, and plating, were performed on the laboratory bench top under ambient atmospheric conditions, with no use of anaerobic chambers, glove boxes, or gas lines. The success of the transformation protocol under these conditions underscores the robustness of the embedded growth and soft SVO transfer methods, which effectively shield cells from oxygen-induced damage without requiring specialized infrastructure. The ability to handle Thermotoga under standard aerobic conditions represents a major practical advancement, as it removes a significant barrier to adoption by laboratories without access to anaerobic facilities.

### Purification and Analyses of CTN 0339 and CTN 0340 Gene Products

The identification and characterization of the CTN 0339 and CTN 0340 gene products were conducted as part of a parallel investigation into the native restriction-modification systems of Thermotoga species, which were hypothesized to play a role in limiting the efficiency of foreign DNA uptake. These genes, originally annotated in the genome sequences of T. maritima and T. sp. RQ7, were predicted to encode putative restriction endonucleases based on homology to known Type II restriction enzymes. To determine their functional activity, the coding sequences of CTN 0339 and CTN 0340 were amplified from genomic DNA using gene-specific primers designed to include flanking restriction sites for cloning into an E. coli expression vector. The amplified fragments were ligated into pET-28a(+) downstream of a T7 promoter and an N-terminal His6-tag sequence, and the resulting constructs were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) for protein expression. Cultures were grown at 37°C to mid-log phase, induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), and incubated for an additional 4 hours at 30°C to facilitate soluble protein production.

Cells were harvested by centrifugation and lysed by sonication in buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, and 10 mM imidazole. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation, and the supernatant was applied to a Ni-NTA affinity column equilibrated with the same buffer. The column was washed with 20 column volumes of wash buffer containing 20 mM imidazole to remove non-specifically bound proteins, followed by elution with buffer containing 250 mM imidazole. Fractions containing the purified proteins were analyzed by SDS-PAGE, which revealed single bands at approximately 35 kDa for CTN 0339 and 38 kDa for CTN 0340, consistent with their predicted molecular weights. The purified proteins were dialyzed into storage buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, and 50% glycerol and stored at −80°C.

To assess enzymatic activity, each purified protein was incubated with plasmid DNA isolated from E. coli, including pUC19 and pDH10, under a range of buffer conditions and temperatures. Reactions were carried out in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 10 mM MgCl2, 100 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT, with incubation temperatures ranging from 50°C to 90°C. After 1 hour, samples were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis. CTN 0339 exhibited no detectable nuclease activity under any condition tested, even at 90°C, suggesting it may be non-functional, pseudogenized, or require an unknown cofactor for activity. In contrast, CTN 0340 demonstrated robust endonuclease activity at 77°C, cleaving supercoiled plasmid DNA into linear and nicked forms, with a preference for unmethylated substrates. The cleavage pattern was consistent with a Type II restriction enzyme, producing discrete fragments that corresponded to specific recognition sequences. The enzyme exhibited optimal activity at 77°C, with a sharp decline in activity below 65°C and above 85°C, indicating adaptation to the physiological temperature of Thermotoga. Methylation of the substrate DNA using E. coli Dam and Dcm methyltransferases significantly reduced cleavage efficiency, confirming that CTN 0340 functions as a restriction endonuclease sensitive to host methylation status.

These findings indicate that Thermotoga possesses at least one active restriction system capable of degrading unmethylated foreign DNA, which may contribute to the low transformation efficiencies observed in prior studies. However, the absence of activity from CTN 0339 and the specificity of CTN 0340 for unmethylated DNA suggest that transformation efficiency can be significantly enhanced by pre-methylating the plasmid DNA prior to introduction into Thermotoga cells. This insight, combined with the successful isolation of transformants using pDH10, demonstrates that while restriction systems are present, they are not insurmountable barriers to genetic manipulation. The characterization of CTN 0340 further provides a molecular basis for future improvements in transformation protocols, such as the use of methylase-deficient E. coli strains for plasmid preparation or the co-expression of methyltransferases in Thermotoga to protect incoming DNA.

### Determination of the Cleavage Site of the REase

To precisely define the DNA sequence recognized and cleaved by the restriction endonuclease encoded by CTN 0340, a series of cleavage assays were performed using plasmid DNA substrates of known sequence. The purified CTN 0340 protein was incubated with pUC19 DNA under optimal reaction conditions (77°C, 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl2, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT) for 1 hour, and the resulting fragments were separated by agarose gel electrophoresis. The cleavage pattern revealed two major fragments of approximately 1.8 kb and 1.2 kb, suggesting a single cleavage site within the plasmid. To map the exact cleavage site, the fragments were excised from the gel, purified, and subjected to Sanger sequencing using primers designed to anneal adjacent to the predicted cut site. The sequence analysis revealed that the enzyme cleaved within the sequence 5'-GCTCGAG-3', generating cohesive ends with 5'-overhangs of four nucleotides. The cleavage occurred between the C and G residues, producing 5'-GCTC and GAG-3' termini. This sequence was confirmed as the recognition site by testing additional plasmids containing multiple copies of the motif, which were cleaved at all predicted sites, yielding the expected number of fragments.

To further validate the specificity of the enzyme, synthetic oligonucleotides containing the 5'-GCTCGAG-3' sequence, as well as variants with single-base substitutions, were synthesized and incubated with CTN 0340. Oligonucleotides with perfect matches to the recognition sequence were cleaved efficiently, while those with substitutions at any of the seven positions showed no detectable cleavage, even after prolonged incubation. Mutations at the central CG dinucleotide were particularly disruptive, indicating that this region is critical for enzyme binding or catalysis. The enzyme showed no activity against methylated versions of the sequence, as confirmed by pre-treatment of the oligonucleotides with Dam methyltransferase, which methylates the adenine residue in the GATC motif, and Dcm methyltransferase, which methylates the cytosine in the CCAGG motif. Neither methylation pattern affected the GCTCGAG sequence, confirming that the enzyme’s sensitivity to methylation is not mediated by these common bacterial methyltransferases, but rather by an as-yet-unidentified Thermotoga-specific methylase.

The determination of the cleavage site provides critical information for the design of future cloning strategies in Thermotoga. By avoiding the presence of the GCTCGAG sequence in recombinant constructs, researchers can circumvent degradation by this endogenous restriction system. Alternatively, the enzyme can be exploited as a molecular tool for targeted DNA cleavage in thermophilic systems. The identification of this restriction site also explains why prior attempts to transform Thermotoga with plasmids derived from E. coli—where the GCTCGAG sequence is frequently present due to its occurrence in common cloning vectors—failed to produce stable transformants. The present invention overcomes this limitation by using pDH10, which was constructed from pRQ7 and pKT1, neither of which contains the GCTCGAG recognition site, thereby ensuring compatibility with the native restriction machinery of Thermotoga. This insight, combined with the use of appropriately methylated DNA, provides a comprehensive strategy for achieving high-efficiency transformation in Thermotoga species.

### Restriction and Modification Assays of the Thermotoga Proteins

To determine whether Thermotoga species possess endogenous modification systems capable of protecting their own DNA from cleavage by their restriction enzymes, a series of restriction-modification assays were performed using purified genomic DNA and plasmid DNA from both Thermotoga and E. coli. Genomic DNA was extracted from T. maritima and T. sp. RQ7 using standard phenol-chloroform extraction followed by isopropanol precipitation. Plasmid DNA was isolated from E. coli DH5α harboring pDH10 and from Thermotoga transformants carrying the same plasmid. Each DNA sample was digested with CTN 0340 under optimal conditions, and the resulting fragments were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Genomic DNA from Thermotoga was completely resistant to cleavage, even at high enzyme concentrations, whereas plasmid DNA isolated from E. coli was fully degraded. In contrast, plasmid DNA isolated from Thermotoga transformants remained intact, suggesting that the plasmid had been modified within the Thermotoga host to resist cleavage.

To confirm this hypothesis, the plasmid DNA recovered from Thermotoga transformants was used as a substrate in a second round of digestion with CTN 0340. The DNA remained uncleaved, indicating that a protective modification had been introduced during replication in Thermotoga. To determine the nature of this modification, the plasmid DNA was subjected to bisulfite sequencing, which detects cytosine methylation, and to restriction enzyme digestion with methylation-sensitive enzymes such as HpaII and MspI, which distinguish between methylated and unmethylated CpG sites. No evidence of cytosine methylation was detected, suggesting that the protective modification was not mediated by a typical DNA methyltransferase acting on CpG motifs. Further analysis using mass spectrometry of nucleosides following enzymatic digestion of the plasmid DNA revealed the presence of N6-methyladenine residues at positions corresponding to the adenine within the GCTCGAG recognition site. This modification was absent in plasmid DNA isolated from E. coli, confirming that Thermotoga possesses a novel adenine-specific methyltransferase that recognizes the same sequence as CTN 0340 and modifies the adenine residue to prevent cleavage.

This finding establishes the existence of a functional Type II restriction-modification system in Thermotoga, comprising a restriction endonuclease (CTN 0340) and a cognate methyltransferase that specifically methylates the adenine within the GCTCGAG sequence. The system functions as a classic self/non-self discrimination mechanism, protecting the host genome from cleavage while degrading foreign DNA lacking the appropriate methylation pattern. This system explains the historical difficulty in transforming Thermotoga with plasmids derived from E. coli, as these plasmids are unmethylated and thus susceptible to degradation upon entry into the cell. The present invention circumvents this barrier by using pDH10, which was constructed from sequences derived from pRQ7, a native Thermotoga plasmid that likely already carries the protective methylation pattern. When pDH10 is propagated in E. coli, it retains the unmethylated state, but upon transformation into Thermotoga, it is rapidly methylated by the endogenous methyltransferase, rendering it resistant to cleavage. This dynamic interplay between restriction and modification ensures that only plasmids that have been successfully introduced and replicated in Thermotoga survive, providing a natural selection mechanism that enhances the fidelity of transformation.

The discovery of this system has significant implications for the design of future genetic tools in Thermotoga. It suggests that plasmid DNA can be protected from degradation by either pre-methylating it in vivo using a Thermotoga-compatible methyltransferase or by selecting for plasmids that originate from native Thermotoga sources, such as pRQ7. The identification of the methyltransferase gene associated with CTN 0340 opens the possibility of engineering a dedicated methylation system for use in synthetic biology applications, enabling the creation of orthogonal restriction-modification systems that do not interfere with host genome integrity. Furthermore, the system provides a molecular basis for the observed stability of pDH10 in Thermotoga, as the plasmid is not only replicated autonomously but also protected from degradation by the host’s own defense machinery.

### Overexpression of R.TneDI

The gene encoding R.TneDI, a putative restriction endonuclease from Thermotoga neapolitana, was identified through bioinformatic analysis of the strain’s genome sequence, which revealed a cluster of genes homologous to known Type II restriction-modification systems. The open reading frame corresponding to R.TneDI was amplified from genomic DNA using primers designed to include NdeI and XhoI restriction sites for cloning into the pET-28a(+) expression vector. The resulting construct, designated pET-R.TneDI, was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) and induced with IPTG for protein expression. Cultures were grown at 37°C to an OD600 of 0.6, then shifted to 20°C for 16 hours to enhance soluble protein production. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, lysed by sonication, and the lysate was clarified by centrifugation. The supernatant was applied to a Ni-NTA affinity column, and the bound protein was eluted with imidazole-containing buffer. SDS-PAGE analysis of the purified protein revealed a single band at approximately 36 kDa, consistent with the predicted molecular weight of R.TneDI.

To assess the enzymatic activity of R.TneDI, purified protein was incubated with plasmid DNA from E. coli, including pUC19, pDH10, and pKT1, under a range of buffer conditions and temperatures. Reactions were carried out in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 10 mM MgCl2, 100 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT, with incubation temperatures ranging from 50°C to 90°C. After 1 hour, samples were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis. R.TneDI exhibited robust endonuclease activity at 77°C, cleaving pUC19 into two major fragments of approximately 1.9 kb and 1.1 kb, indicating a single recognition site. The cleavage pattern was consistent with a Type II restriction enzyme, producing cohesive ends with 5'-overhangs. The enzyme showed no activity at temperatures below 65°C or above 85°C, indicating a sharp thermal optimum matching the physiological growth temperature of Thermotoga neapolitana.

To determine the recognition sequence, the cleaved fragments were purified and sequenced, revealing that R.TneDI cleaves within the sequence 5'-GGATCC-3', with the cut occurring between the G and G residues, producing 5'-GG and ATCC-3' overhangs. This sequence was confirmed as the recognition site by testing synthetic oligonucleotides containing the motif and its variants. Oligonucleotides with perfect matches were cleaved efficiently, while those with single-base substitutions showed no cleavage. Methylation of the adenine residue within the GGATCC sequence using E. coli Dam methyltransferase abolished cleavage, confirming that R.TneDI is sensitive to methylation at this site. The enzyme exhibited no activity against plasmid DNA isolated from Thermotoga transformants, suggesting that the plasmid had been methylated in vivo by a cognate methyltransferase.

The overexpression and characterization of R.TneDI provide direct evidence that Thermotoga neapolitana possesses a functional restriction-modification system capable of recognizing and cleaving foreign DNA. The presence of multiple restriction systems in different Thermotoga strains—CTN 0340 in T. maritima and T. sp. RQ7, and R.TneDI in T. neapolitana—indicates that restriction activity is a widespread feature of the genus and likely represents a major barrier to genetic transformation. The present invention overcomes this barrier by utilizing pDH10, which lacks the recognition sequences for these enzymes and is therefore not cleaved upon entry into the cell. Furthermore, the discovery that plasmid DNA becomes methylated and protected after replication in Thermotoga suggests that transformation efficiency can be further enhanced by allowing a short recovery period post-electroporation, during which the plasmid is methylated by the host’s endogenous methyltransferase before selection is applied. This insight provides a mechanistic explanation for the high stability of pDH10 in Thermotoga and underscores the importance of considering restriction-modification systems in the design of genetic tools for hyperthermophilic organisms.

### Determination of the Cleavage Site of R.TneDI

The precise cleavage site of R.TneDI was determined through a combination of DNA sequencing and synthetic oligonucleotide digestion assays. Plasmid DNA from E. coli harboring pUC19 was digested with purified R.TneDI under optimal reaction conditions (77°C, 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl2, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT) for 1 hour, and the resulting fragments were separated by agarose gel electrophoresis. Two major bands were observed, corresponding to fragments of approximately 1.9 kb and 1.1 kb, indicating a single cleavage event within the circular plasmid. The fragments were excised from the gel, purified, and subjected to Sanger sequencing using primers designed to anneal adjacent to the predicted cut site. Sequence analysis revealed that the enzyme cleaved between the two guanine residues in the sequence 5'-GGATCC-3', generating 5'-GG and ATCC-3' overhangs. This sequence was confirmed as the recognition site by testing synthetic double-stranded oligonucleotides containing the motif and its variants. Oligonucleotides with the perfect GGATCC sequence were cleaved efficiently, while those with substitutions at any of the six positions showed no cleavage, even after prolonged incubation. Mutations at the central AT dinucleotide were particularly disruptive, suggesting that this region is critical for enzyme binding or catalytic activity.

To determine whether methylation of the recognition site conferred resistance to cleavage, the oligonucleotides were treated with Dam methyltransferase, which methylates the adenine residue in the GATC motif. Methylation of the adenine within the GGATCC sequence completely abolished cleavage by R.TneDI, confirming that the enzyme is sensitive to N6-methyladenine modification. This finding indicates that R.TneDI is part of a Type II restriction-modification system in which a cognate methyltransferase protects the host genome by methylating the adenine within the GGATCC sequence. The methyltransferase is likely encoded by a gene located in close proximity to r.TneDI in the Thermotoga neapolitana genome, forming a classic restriction-modification operon.

The identification of the GGATCC recognition site has significant implications for the design of genetic constructs in Thermotoga. Many commonly used cloning vectors, including pUC19 and pET vectors, contain multiple GGATCC sites due to the presence of BamHI restriction sites, which are frequently used in molecular cloning. The presence of these sites renders such vectors susceptible to degradation by R.TneDI upon transformation into Thermotoga neapolitana. The present invention circumvents this problem by constructing pDH10 from pRQ7 and pKT1, neither of which contains the GGATCC sequence, thereby ensuring compatibility with the native restriction machinery of Thermotoga. Furthermore, the discovery that plasmid DNA becomes methylated and protected after replication in Thermotoga suggests that transformation efficiency can be enhanced by allowing a brief recovery period post-transformation, during which the plasmid is methylated by the host’s endogenous methyltransferase before selection is applied. This insight provides a mechanistic basis for the high stability of pDH10 in Thermotoga and underscores the importance of considering restriction-modification systems in the design of genetic tools for hyperthermophilic organisms.
