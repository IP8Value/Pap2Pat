# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to a non-invasive method and apparatus for the early detection of pathogenic infection in crop plants through the selective extraction and fluorescence-based quantification of blue-emitting phenolic compounds from leaf surfaces. More specifically, the invention provides a hydrogel-based extraction system that captures water-soluble phytochemicals—particularly phenylpropanoids such as chlorogenic acid and salicylic acid—from the apoplastic fluid of living plant tissues without requiring tissue destruction, chemical solvents, or laboratory instrumentation. The extracted compounds are then detected via their characteristic blue fluorescence under ultraviolet excitation, enabling the identification of pathogenic infection prior to the emergence of visible symptoms. This method is particularly applicable to agricultural settings where rapid, field-deployable, and cost-effective diagnostic tools are urgently needed to prevent the spread of bacterial and fungal pathogens such as Ralstonia solanacearum, Clavibacter michiganense, and Pseudomonas syringae. The invention further encompasses the use of this extraction technique in conjunction with standardized fluorescence measurement protocols to establish quantitative thresholds for infection presence, thereby enabling predictive disease management strategies such as targeted pesticide application, quarantine of infected zones, or selective plant removal. The system is designed to be compatible with portable UV light sources and low-cost fluorescence detectors, making it suitable for use by farmers, agronomists, and field inspectors without specialized training or laboratory infrastructure.

## BACKGROUND ART

The global agricultural industry suffers substantial economic losses annually due to plant diseases caused by pathogenic microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, and viruses. These pathogens often spread rapidly through wind, water, insects, or human activity, leading to widespread crop failure if not detected and controlled at an early stage. Conventional diagnostic methods for plant disease detection rely heavily on laboratory-based techniques such as enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) and polymerase chain reaction (PCR), which require the destructive sampling of plant tissues, lengthy processing times, expensive reagents, and trained personnel. These methods are typically applied only after the onset of visible symptoms—such as leaf discoloration, wilting, or necrosis—by which time the pathogen has already established itself within the vascular system and may have spread to neighboring plants, rendering control measures significantly less effective.

Alternative approaches have emerged in recent years involving non-destructive imaging technologies, particularly those utilizing multispectral fluorescence under ultraviolet excitation. These methods exploit the natural fluorescence properties of plant tissues, which emit characteristic spectral signatures in the blue-green (400–520 nm) and red-far-red (680–740 nm) regions due to the presence of endogenous fluorophores such as chlorophyll, cinnamic acids, and other phenylpropanoid derivatives. While these techniques have demonstrated potential in controlled laboratory environments, their practical application in field conditions has been severely limited by several critical challenges. First, the blue fluorescence signal, which is most indicative of defense-related phenolic compounds, is strongly quenched by the high concentration of chlorophyll present in mesophyll cells, leading to unreliable quantification and false-negative results. Second, the spatial heterogeneity of plant surfaces, including variations in cuticle thickness, wax composition, stomatal density, and leaf orientation, introduces significant noise and variability into fluorescence measurements, making it difficult to establish consistent diagnostic thresholds. Third, existing imaging systems require bulky, expensive, and power-intensive equipment such as hyperspectral cameras, laser excitation sources, and cooled CCD detectors, which are impractical for deployment in remote or resource-limited agricultural settings.

Another limitation of prior art lies in the inability to isolate and concentrate the target analytes responsible for the fluorescence signal. Most fluorescence-based detection systems attempt to measure emissions directly from the intact leaf surface, where the signal is diluted by the surrounding tissue matrix and obscured by competing fluorophores. Furthermore, the hydrophobic nature of the plant cuticle—a protective layer composed of aliphatic hydrocarbons with carbon chain lengths of C20–C40—acts as a formidable barrier to the diffusion of aqueous-phase metabolites from the apoplast to the external environment. Although some studies have attempted to enhance surface contact using adhesive tapes or pressure-sensitive films, these methods fail to selectively extract the target phenolic compounds and often introduce artifacts through mechanical damage or contamination.

Hydrogel-based extraction technologies have been successfully employed in biomedical applications for the non-invasive sampling of sweat components such as lactate, glucose, cortisol, and pH from human skin. These systems utilize biocompatible hydrogels, particularly agarose, due to their porous structure (pore diameter 0.1–1.0 μm), mechanical stability, and ability to passively absorb aqueous solutes through diffusion and capillary action. However, the application of such hydrogel systems to plant tissues has not been previously explored, primarily due to the structural and chemical differences between human skin and plant epidermis. The plant cuticle, unlike human skin, is highly hydrophobic and lacks sweat glands or active transport mechanisms for extracellular fluid release. Moreover, the concentration of target metabolites in plant apoplastic fluid is orders of magnitude lower than that found in human sweat, necessitating a more efficient and selective extraction mechanism.

Prior attempts to detect plant stress responses through fluorescence have focused on changes in chlorophyll fluorescence, which is sensitive to photosynthetic efficiency but not directly correlated with the biochemical pathways activated during pathogen recognition. While some studies have reported increases in blue fluorescence intensity following pathogen challenge, these observations have been confounded by concurrent changes in chlorophyll content, leaf hydration, and environmental stressors such as drought or nutrient deficiency. As a result, no prior method has been able to reliably distinguish between pathogen-induced metabolic changes and other physiological perturbations using non-destructive, field-deployable means.

There remains, therefore, a critical and unmet need in agricultural diagnostics for a simple, rapid, non-invasive, and quantitative method capable of detecting pathogenic infection in crops at a presymptomatic stage, without requiring tissue destruction, chemical extraction, or complex instrumentation. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel hydrogel extraction system that selectively captures blue-emitting phenolic compounds from the leaf surface, effectively bypassing the limitations of chlorophyll quenching and cuticular hydrophobicity, and enabling the direct, quantitative detection of infection biomarkers through fluorescence spectroscopy.

## SUMMARY OF INVENTION

### Technical Problem

The technical problem addressed by the present invention is the inability of existing diagnostic methods to detect pathogenic infection in crop plants prior to the manifestation of visible symptoms, without requiring destructive sampling, laboratory infrastructure, or expensive instrumentation. Conventional techniques such as ELISA and PCR are unsuitable for field use due to their reliance on tissue homogenization, reagent consumption, and time-consuming processing. Non-invasive fluorescence imaging techniques, while promising, suffer from severe signal interference due to chlorophyll quenching, poor reproducibility caused by leaf surface heterogeneity, and the inability to concentrate low-abundance biomarkers from the apoplast. Furthermore, the hydrophobic nature of the plant cuticle prevents the passive diffusion of water-soluble defense metabolites to the leaf surface, rendering direct surface measurements ineffective. There is no prior art that provides a method capable of selectively extracting, concentrating, and quantifying blue-emitting phenolic compounds—such as chlorogenic acid and salicylic acid—from living plant tissues in a manner that is both non-destructive and compatible with field deployment. The absence of such a method impedes the implementation of early intervention strategies, resulting in delayed disease control, increased pesticide usage, and substantial crop losses.

### Solution to Problem

The solution to this technical problem is provided by a novel hydrogel-based extraction and fluorescence detection system that enables the non-invasive, selective, and quantitative detection of pathogenic infection in crop plants at a presymptomatic stage. The system comprises a hydrogel film composed of agarose, a plant-derived polysaccharide, which is placed in direct contact with the surface of a living plant leaf. Upon contact, the hydrogel film passively extracts water-soluble phenolic compounds—including chlorogenic acid, salicylic acid, and related phenylpropanoids—from the apoplastic fluid of the leaf through natural leaching mechanisms occurring at micropores, trichome bases, and micro-damaged regions of the cuticle. The hydrogel film is engineered to have a porous structure that permits the selective diffusion of small, hydrophilic molecules while excluding larger, hydrophobic components such as chlorophyll, lipids, and cuticular waxes. After a defined period of contact, typically between one and four hours, the hydrogel film is removed from the leaf surface and irradiated with ultraviolet light at a wavelength of approximately 310 nm. The extracted phenolic compounds emit a characteristic blue fluorescence with a peak intensity between 420 nm and 440 nm, which is measured using a portable fluorescence detector. The intensity of this fluorescence is directly proportional to the concentration of the extracted phenolic compounds, which in turn correlates with the level of pathogen-induced metabolic activation in the plant. By comparing the measured fluorescence intensity to a pre-established calibration curve derived from known concentrations of reference phenolic compounds, the presence and severity of pathogenic infection can be quantitatively assessed. The hydrogel film may be optionally pre-treated with a mild ethanol solution to reduce cuticular hydrophobicity and enhance adhesion, thereby improving extraction efficiency and reproducibility. This method eliminates the need for tissue destruction, chemical solvents, or complex instrumentation, and provides a rapid, field-deployable diagnostic tool capable of detecting infection before symptom onset.

### Advantageous Effect of Invention

The advantageous effects of the present invention are numerous and transformative for agricultural diagnostics. First, the method enables the detection of pathogenic infection at a presymptomatic stage, typically within 24 to 72 hours following pathogen exposure, long before visible symptoms such as chlorosis, necrosis, or wilting appear. This early detection capability allows for the timely implementation of targeted control measures, such as localized pesticide application, removal of infected plants, or alteration of irrigation practices, thereby minimizing disease spread and reducing overall chemical usage. Second, the method is entirely non-destructive, preserving the integrity of the plant and allowing for repeated measurements on the same leaf or plant over time, which facilitates longitudinal monitoring of infection progression and treatment efficacy. Third, the hydrogel extraction system selectively captures only the water-soluble phenolic compounds responsible for the blue fluorescence signal, effectively excluding chlorophyll and other interfering fluorophores, thereby eliminating the problem of fluorescence quenching that plagues prior art methods. Fourth, the system requires no laboratory infrastructure, reagents, or specialized training, and can be implemented using low-cost, portable components such as a UV LED light source and a simple photodiode-based fluorescence sensor, making it accessible to smallholder farmers and field inspectors in resource-limited settings. Fifth, the agarose hydrogel is biodegradable, non-toxic, and derived from renewable plant sources, ensuring environmental compatibility and regulatory compliance. Sixth, the method demonstrates high reproducibility and sensitivity, with a limit of detection for salicylic acid as low as 1.0 μmol/L and for chlorogenic acid at approximately 0.05 ppm, corresponding to a minimum detectable concentration of 13.8 ng/cm² on the hydrogel surface. Seventh, the method is broadly applicable across multiple crop species, including tomato, tobacco, potato, and other solanaceous plants, and is compatible with a wide range of pathogens, including bacterial, fungal, and viral agents that induce phenylpropanoid biosynthesis. Finally, the system can be integrated into automated monitoring networks, where multiple hydrogel sensors are deployed across a field and read by a mobile device, enabling real-time, spatially resolved disease mapping and predictive modeling of infection hotspots. These advantages collectively represent a paradigm shift in plant disease diagnostics, transitioning from reactive, post-symptomatic interventions to proactive, presymptomatic management strategies that enhance crop resilience, reduce economic losses, and promote sustainable agriculture.

## DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

### <Fluorescence Emission Phenomenon>

The fluorescence emission phenomenon central to the present invention arises from the accumulation of specific phenolic compounds within plant tissues in response to pathogenic infection. These compounds, primarily phenylpropanoids such as chlorogenic acid, salicylic acid, scopoletin, and ferulic acid, are naturally occurring secondary metabolites that serve as phytoanticipins in plant defense mechanisms. Upon recognition of a pathogen, the plant activates a suite of biochemical pathways, including the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis route, leading to the upregulation of enzymes such as phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) and cinnamate 4-hydroxylase (C4H), which catalyze the production of cinnamic acid derivatives. These derivatives are subsequently modified through hydroxylation, methylation, and esterification reactions to form a diverse array of fluorescent compounds. Among these, chlorogenic acid—a conjugate of caffeic acid and quinic acid—exhibits a strong and characteristic blue fluorescence when excited at wavelengths between 300 nm and 320 nm, with a maximum emission peak between 420 nm and 440 nm. Salicylic acid, a key signaling molecule in systemic acquired resistance, also emits fluorescence in this region, albeit with lower quantum yield, and its presence is often correlated with the early stages of infection. The fluorescence of these compounds is attributed to their conjugated π-electron systems, which facilitate electronic transitions upon UV excitation, resulting in the emission of photons in the visible blue spectrum.

Importantly, the fluorescence of these phenolic compounds is not subject to the quenching effects that plague traditional leaf fluorescence measurements. In intact leaves, the high concentration of chlorophyll a and b absorbs incident blue light and re-emits fluorescence at longer wavelengths (680 nm and 740 nm), effectively masking the weaker blue emission from phenolic compounds. Furthermore, chlorophyll molecules act as efficient energy acceptors, dissipating excitation energy non-radiatively through internal conversion and vibrational relaxation. This quenching effect renders direct fluorescence measurements of the leaf surface unreliable for detecting phenolic accumulation. In contrast, the hydrogel extraction system of the present invention circumvents this limitation by selectively extracting only the water-soluble phenolic compounds from the apoplast, leaving behind the chlorophyll-containing cellular components. The hydrogel, composed of a hydrophilic agarose matrix with pore sizes in the range of 0.1 to 1.0 micrometers, permits the diffusion of small molecules such as salicylic acid (molecular weight 138 Da) and chlorogenic acid (molecular weight 354 Da) while excluding larger macromolecules and hydrophobic pigments such as chlorophyll (molecular weight ~893 Da) and carotenoids. Consequently, the fluorescence signal measured from the hydrogel film is derived exclusively from the target phenolic compounds, free from interference by chlorophyll or other cellular fluorophores. This selective extraction results in a signal-to-noise ratio that is orders of magnitude higher than that achievable through direct leaf surface measurements.

The fluorescence emission profile of the extracted compounds is also remarkably consistent across different plant species and pathogen types. When the hydrogel film is exposed to leaves infected with Ralstonia solanacearum, Clavibacter michiganense, or Pseudomonas syringae, the resulting fluorescence spectrum exhibits a dominant peak at approximately 428 nm, with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of less than 30 nm. Minor spectral shifts of up to ±5 nm may occur due to variations in the relative abundance of different phenolic compounds—for example, a slight red-shift may be observed if scopoletin contributes more significantly to the signal—but the overall spectral shape remains diagnostic. This spectral consistency enables the development of standardized detection protocols that are not dependent on the specific crop or pathogen involved. Moreover, the fluorescence intensity increases in a dose-dependent manner with the concentration of the extracted phenolic compounds, demonstrating a linear correlation over a wide dynamic range—from nanomolar to micromolar concentrations—with a correlation coefficient exceeding 0.99. This quantitative relationship allows for the establishment of calibrated thresholds that distinguish between healthy, mildly infected, and severely infected plants. The absence of background fluorescence from the hydrogel itself, combined with the high specificity of the extracted analytes, ensures that the measured signal is a direct and reliable indicator of pathogen-induced metabolic activity. This phenomenon, therefore, forms the analytical foundation of the invention, transforming an otherwise obscured biological signal into a robust, quantifiable diagnostic parameter.

### <Electrochemical Behavior>

Although the primary detection modality of the present invention relies on optical fluorescence, the hydrogel extraction system also exhibits electrochemical behavior that enhances its functionality and opens avenues for future multi-modal sensing platforms. The agarose hydrogel, while primarily employed as a passive extraction matrix, possesses inherent ion-conducting properties due to its hydrated polymeric structure. When the hydrogel is saturated with apoplastic fluid containing phenolic compounds, it also absorbs a range of ionic species—including potassium, calcium, chloride, and organic acids—that are co-released during pathogen-induced stress. These ions, while not directly fluorescent, can be detected electrochemically using embedded microelectrodes or conductive polymer layers integrated into the hydrogel substrate. For instance, the presence of chloride ions, which are known to increase in concentration during pathogen infection due to membrane permeability changes, can be measured using a silver/silver chloride reference electrode. Similarly, the oxidation potential of phenolic compounds such as chlorogenic acid and caffeic acid occurs within a range of +0.4 to +0.7 V versus Ag/AgCl, enabling their detection via differential pulse voltammetry or amperometric sensing. 

The electrochemical behavior of the hydrogel is not intended to replace fluorescence detection but rather to serve as a complementary signal that enhances diagnostic specificity and reduces false positives. In cases where environmental stressors such as salinity, drought, or nutrient deficiency induce phenolic accumulation independently of pathogen infection, the accompanying ionic profile may differ significantly from that observed during true pathogenic challenge. For example, salinity stress typically elevates sodium and chloride concentrations without a corresponding increase in salicylic acid, whereas pathogen infection triggers a selective upregulation of salicylic acid and chlorogenic acid alongside moderate increases in potassium and organic anions. By combining fluorescence intensity with electrochemical ion profiles, the system can differentiate between biotic and abiotic stress responses with greater accuracy than either modality alone. This dual-mode detection capability can be implemented in next-generation sensor designs by embedding microfabricated electrodes—such as carbon nanotube-modified electrodes or gold nanoparticle arrays—into the hydrogel substrate during fabrication. The electrodes may be connected to a low-power, battery-operated potentiostat housed within a handheld device, enabling simultaneous optical and electrochemical readings. The data from both sensors can be processed using machine learning algorithms trained on known infection and stress profiles to generate a composite diagnostic score. This approach transforms the hydrogel from a passive extraction medium into an active, multi-analyte biosensing platform capable of resolving complex physiological states with high fidelity. Furthermore, the electrochemical signal can be used to confirm the successful extraction of analytes, as the ionic conductivity of the hydrogel increases proportionally with the volume of apoplastic fluid absorbed, providing an internal validation mechanism that ensures measurement reliability even under suboptimal contact conditions.

### <Methyl Salicylate Sensor>

While the present invention primarily focuses on the detection of salicylic acid and chlorogenic acid as biomarkers of pathogenic infection, the hydrogel extraction system is also capable of detecting methyl salicylate, a volatile ester derivative of salicylic acid that plays a critical role in plant-to-plant signaling during systemic acquired resistance. Methyl salicylate is synthesized from salicylic acid by the enzyme salicylic acid carboxyl methyltransferase (SAMT) and is known to be emitted from infected leaves into the surrounding atmosphere, where it acts as an airborne signal to prime neighboring plants for defense. Although methyl salicylate is volatile and typically detected using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), the hydrogel system of the present invention can capture this compound through a unique adsorption mechanism. When the hydrogel film is placed on the leaf surface, it not only extracts aqueous-phase metabolites but also absorbs vapor-phase methyl salicylate that diffuses from the stomata or cuticular pores. The hydrophilic nature of agarose, combined with its porous structure, allows for the dissolution and retention of methyl salicylate within the gel matrix, where it remains stable for several hours under ambient conditions. Once absorbed, methyl salicylate can be detected indirectly through its hydrolysis product, salicylic acid, which is liberated upon mild heating or enzymatic treatment of the hydrogel. Alternatively, the hydrogel can be functionalized with a thin layer of immobilized esterase enzyme, which catalyzes the hydrolysis of methyl salicylate to salicylic acid and methanol. The resulting salicylic acid then emits the characteristic blue fluorescence under UV excitation, providing a quantitative measure of methyl salicylate concentration. 

This capability significantly expands the diagnostic scope of the invention, as methyl salicylate is often produced in the earliest stages of infection—sometimes within six hours of pathogen exposure—before salicylic acid accumulates to detectable levels in the apoplast. The detection of methyl salicylate therefore enables an even earlier window of diagnosis than salicylic acid alone. Moreover, because methyl salicylate is emitted into the air, the hydrogel sensor can be positioned above the leaf surface—not in direct contact—to capture airborne signals, enabling non-contact detection that is particularly useful for monitoring large plant canopies or for detecting infection in plants with thick cuticles that impede direct extraction. The hydrogel can be mounted on a small, adjustable arm that hovers 1–5 mm above the leaf surface, allowing it to sample the microenvironmental air layer where methyl salicylate concentrates. This configuration, when combined with fluorescence detection, provides a dual-pathway diagnostic system: one pathway measures apoplastic salicylic acid and chlorogenic acid through direct contact, and the other measures airborne methyl salicylate through proximity-based absorption. The convergence of these two signals—both originating from the same biochemical pathway—provides an exceptionally high level of diagnostic confidence, as the simultaneous detection of both compounds strongly indicates active pathogen recognition and systemic defense activation. This embodiment of the invention thus represents a significant advancement over prior art, which has never combined the detection of volatile and non-volatile defense metabolites into a single, integrated sensor platform. The ability to detect methyl salicylate not only enhances sensitivity and early detection capability but also opens new possibilities for monitoring plant-plant communication in agricultural systems, enabling the prediction of infection spread across entire fields based on the emission patterns of neighboring plants.

### <Method for Early Detection of Pathogen Infection in Crop>

The method for early detection of pathogenic infection in crop plants, as embodied by the present invention, comprises a series of sequential, non-destructive steps that transform a living plant leaf into a diagnostic substrate for pathogen detection. The method begins with the preparation of a hydrogel film composed of agarose, a natural polysaccharide derived from red algae, which is dissolved in distilled water at a concentration of 3 to 5 weight percent and cast into a mold to form a thin, transparent film with a thickness of 0.5 to 2.0 millimeters. The hydrogel film is allowed to gel at ambient temperature for a minimum of one hour, ensuring structural integrity and uniform porosity. Prior to application, the surface of the target plant leaf may be optionally treated with a minimal volume of ethanol (e.g., 1–5 microliters) applied via a fine-tipped pipette and gently blotted dry with a lint-free tissue. This ethanol treatment temporarily disrupts the hydrophobic cuticular wax layer, enhancing the adhesion of the hydrogel to the leaf surface and facilitating the diffusion of apoplastic fluid into the gel matrix. The hydrogel film is then placed in direct contact with the leaf surface, preferably on the abaxial side where stomatal density is higher and cuticle thickness is typically thinner, and held in place for a period of 1 to 4 hours under ambient environmental conditions. During this time, water-soluble phenolic compounds—including salicylic acid, chlorogenic acid, and related phenylpropanoids—are passively extracted from the apoplast through micropores, trichome bases, and micro-fractures in the cuticle, while larger cellular components such as chlorophyll, proteins, and lipids are excluded.

Following extraction, the hydrogel film is carefully removed from the leaf surface and placed on a transparent substrate, such as a glass slide or quartz plate, for fluorescence measurement. The film is then irradiated with ultraviolet light at a wavelength of 300 to 320 nm, preferably using a portable LED source with a narrow bandwidth of ±10 nm to minimize background excitation. The resulting fluorescence emission is collected using a photodetector, such as a silicon photodiode with a bandpass filter centered at 430 nm, or a miniature spectrometer with a resolution of 5 nm or better. The intensity of the emitted light is recorded and compared against a pre-established calibration curve generated from hydrogel films exposed to known concentrations of salicylic acid and chlorogenic acid. This calibration curve, which may be stored in a mobile application or embedded in a handheld device, allows for the quantitative determination of the concentration of extracted phenolic compounds. A fluorescence intensity exceeding a predetermined threshold—such as 150% of the mean signal from healthy plants of the same species and age—is interpreted as indicative of pathogenic infection. The method may be repeated on multiple leaves or plants within a field to generate a spatial map of infection prevalence. The entire process, from hydrogel application to result interpretation, can be completed in less than five hours, with minimal equipment and no requirement for refrigeration, chemical reagents, or laboratory infrastructure. The method is applicable to a wide range of crop species, including Solanum lycopersicum (tomato), Nicotiana tabacum (tobacco), Solanum tuberosum (potato), Capsicum annuum (pepper), and other members of the Solanaceae family, as well as to other plant families such as Brassicaceae and Cucurbitaceae, provided that they exhibit a measurable increase in phenolic compound production in response to pathogen challenge. The method is also compatible with automated deployment systems, such as drone-mounted hydrogel dispensers or field-embedded sensor arrays, enabling large-scale, real-time monitoring of crop health. This method represents a fundamental departure from conventional diagnostic paradigms, replacing destructive, laboratory-dependent, and symptom-based detection with a non-invasive, field-deployable, and presymptomatic diagnostic tool that empowers proactive disease management.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

A hydrogel film was prepared by dissolving 0.8 grams of agarose powder (Nakalai Tesque, Japan) in 19.2 milliliters of distilled water to form a 4 weight percent aqueous solution. The solution was heated in a microwave oven until fully dissolved, then poured into a 2 cm × 2 cm square cutout formed in a 1 mm-thick polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) frame placed on a glass slide. The assembly was sandwiched between two glass slides and secured with clips, then allowed to gel at room temperature for one hour. The resulting hydrogel film had a thickness of 1.0 mm and a transmittance of greater than 90% in the visible spectrum. A cherry tomato plant (Solanum lycopersicum cv. Regina) was grown hydroponically in a nutrient solution containing Ralstonia solanacearum at a concentration of 10⁶ colony-forming units per milliliter for 14 days. On the 14th day, a fully expanded leaf was selected for analysis. The hydrogel film was placed in direct contact with the abaxial surface of the leaf and held in place for three hours under ambient laboratory conditions (25°C, 60% relative humidity). After removal, the hydrogel film was placed on a glass slide and irradiated with ultraviolet light from a xenon lamp (excitation wavelength: 310 nm, slit width: 10 nm excitation, 5 nm emission). The fluorescence emission spectrum was recorded using a spectrofluorometer (Jasco FP-8600). The spectrum exhibited a dominant peak at 428 nm with an intensity of 1,850 arbitrary units (AU), significantly higher than the baseline signal of 210 AU observed for a hydrogel film not exposed to any plant tissue. The intensity was 8.8 times greater than the mean signal from hydrogel films placed on healthy, uninfected cherry tomato leaves (mean: 210 AU, standard deviation: 18 AU, n=10). Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis of the hydrogel film confirmed the presence of chlorogenic acid at a concentration of 0.025 ppm and salicylic acid at 0.012 ppm. The detection of these compounds, which are known to accumulate during pathogenic infection, confirmed the specificity of the hydrogel extraction system. The method demonstrated high reproducibility, with a coefficient of variation of 5.2% across five replicate measurements.

### Comparative Example 1

A cherry tomato plant (Solanum lycopersicum cv. Regina) was grown under identical conditions as in Example 1, but instead of applying the hydrogel film, the abaxial surface of the leaf was directly irradiated with ultraviolet light at 310 nm using the same spectrofluorometer. The fluorescence emission spectrum was recorded without any extraction step. The spectrum exhibited a dominant peak at 680 nm, corresponding to chlorophyll fluorescence, with a secondary, broad peak between 400 and 500 nm. The intensity of the blue fluorescence region (420–440 nm) was measured at 240 AU, which was only slightly higher than the background signal from a healthy leaf (215 AU). The signal-to-noise ratio was poor, with a coefficient of variation of 18.7% across five measurements. No distinct peak was observed at 428 nm, and the spectral shape was inconsistent with that of pure chlorogenic acid or salicylic acid. The fluorescence signal was heavily quenched by chlorophyll, and no quantitative distinction could be made between infected and healthy leaves based on direct surface measurement. This example demonstrates the critical limitation of prior art methods, which rely on direct leaf fluorescence and are rendered ineffective by chlorophyll interference.

### Example 2

A hydrogel film was prepared as described in Example 1. A cherry tomato plant (Solanum lycopersicum cv. Regina) was grown hydroponically in a nutrient solution containing Ralstonia solanacearum for 7 days. A leaf was selected and treated with a single drop (5 μL) of 70% ethanol applied to the abaxial surface using a glass pipette and gently blotted dry with a lint-free tissue. The hydrogel film was then placed on the treated leaf surface for three hours. After removal, the fluorescence spectrum was recorded under 310 nm excitation. The peak intensity at 428 nm was measured at 3,120 AU, which was 1.7 times higher than the intensity obtained in Example 1 without ethanol treatment (1,850 AU). The increase in signal was attributed to enhanced hydrogel adhesion and improved extraction efficiency due to the temporary disruption of cuticular wax. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry confirmed a 1.6-fold increase in chlorogenic acid concentration (0.041 ppm) and a 1.5-fold increase in salicylic acid concentration (0.018 ppm) compared to Example 1. The coefficient of variation across five replicates decreased to 3.8%, indicating improved reproducibility. This example demonstrates that surface pre-treatment with ethanol significantly enhances the sensitivity and reliability of the hydrogel extraction method.

### Example 3

A hydrogel film was prepared as described in Example 1. A tobacco plant (Nicotiana tabacum L. cv. Tsukuba ichi-gou) was grown in soil and inoculated with Ralstonia solanacearum by root immersion in a bacterial suspension (10⁶ cfu/mL) for 14 days. A fully expanded leaf was selected, and the hydrogel film was applied without ethanol pre-treatment for three hours. The fluorescence spectrum exhibited a peak at 426 nm with an intensity of 1,580 AU, which was significantly higher than the mean signal from healthy tobacco leaves (205 AU, standard deviation: 15 AU, n=10). LC–MS/MS analysis confirmed the presence of chlorogenic acid at 0.021 ppm and salicylic acid at 0.010 ppm. The signal intensity was comparable to that observed in infected cherry tomato plants, demonstrating the broad applicability of the method across different plant species. The coefficient of variation was 6.1%, indicating consistent performance despite differences in leaf morphology and cuticle thickness. This example confirms that the hydrogel extraction system is not limited to solanaceous crops but is effective in other agriculturally important species.

### Comparative Example 2

A tobacco plant (Nicotiana tabacum L. cv. Tsukuba ichi-gou) was grown under identical conditions as in Example 3 but was subjected to drought stress by withholding water for seven days. A hydrogel film was applied to a leaf for three hours without ethanol pre-treatment. The fluorescence spectrum exhibited a peak at 424 nm with an intensity of 410 AU, which was higher than the baseline (205 AU) but significantly lower than the signal from infected plants (1,580 AU). LC–MS/MS analysis revealed no detectable levels of salicylic acid or chlorogenic acid. Instead, elevated concentrations of proline and glucose were detected, indicating an abiotic stress response. The fluorescence signal was attributed to non-specific phenolic accumulation due to osmotic stress, but the absence of pathogen-specific biomarkers confirmed that the method can distinguish between biotic and abiotic stress. This example demonstrates the diagnostic specificity of the invention, as it does not generate false positives in response to non-pathogenic environmental stressors.

### Example 4

A hydrogel film was prepared as described in Example 1. A cherry tomato plant (Solanum lycopersicum cv. Regina) was grown hydroponically in a nutrient solution containing Ralstonia solanacearum for 3 days. A leaf was selected, and the hydrogel film was applied for three hours without ethanol pre-treatment. The fluorescence spectrum exhibited a peak at 429 nm with an intensity of 980 AU, which was significantly higher than the mean signal from healthy plants (210 AU) and only 47% lower than the signal from 14-day-infected plants (1,850 AU). LC–MS/MS analysis confirmed the presence of salicylic acid at 0.007 ppm and chlorogenic acid at 0.011 ppm, indicating that the accumulation of these biomarkers begins within 72 hours of pathogen exposure. This example demonstrates that the method can detect presymptomatic infection as early as three days after pathogen challenge, well before any visible symptoms such as leaf yellowing or wilting appear. The ability to detect infection at this early stage enables timely intervention, significantly improving the potential for disease control.

### Comparative Example 3

A cherry tomato plant (Solanum lycopersicum cv. Regina) was grown under identical conditions as in Example 4, but the hydrogel film was replaced with a commercial adhesive tape (Scotch® Transparent Tape) pressed onto the leaf surface for three hours. The tape was then removed and irradiated with 310 nm UV light. The fluorescence spectrum exhibited a broad, low-intensity signal between 400 and 500 nm with a peak intensity of 280 AU, which was not significantly different from the background signal of a healthy leaf. No distinct peak was observed at 428 nm, and LC–MS/MS analysis detected no salicylic acid or chlorogenic acid. The adhesive tape failed to extract any target phenolic compounds, confirming that passive adhesion alone is insufficient for selective biomarker capture. This example underscores the unique functionality of the agarose hydrogel, which provides both selective permeability and aqueous compatibility that conventional tapes lack.

### Example 5

A hydrogel film was prepared as described in Example 1. A cherry tomato plant (Solanum lycopersicum cv. Regina) was grown hydroponically in a nutrient solution containing Ralstonia solanacearum for 14 days. A leaf was selected, and the hydrogel film was applied for three hours. After removal, the hydrogel film was placed in a sealed container with a small volume of immobilized esterase enzyme (10 U/mL in phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) for two hours at 37°C. The hydrogel was then removed, rinsed with distilled water, dried, and irradiated with 310 nm UV light. The fluorescence intensity at 428 nm increased to 2,450 AU, a 32% increase compared to the untreated hydrogel (1,850 AU). LC–MS/MS analysis confirmed that methyl salicylate had been absorbed into the hydrogel and hydrolyzed to salicylic acid, which then emitted the characteristic fluorescence. The increase in signal was directly proportional to the amount of methyl salicylate present, as confirmed by GC-MS analysis of the hydrogel headspace. This example demonstrates that the hydrogel system can detect volatile defense signals such as methyl salicylate, thereby extending the diagnostic window to even earlier stages of infection and enhancing the reliability of detection through multi-metabolite confirmation.

### Comparative Example 4

A cherry tomato plant (Solanum lycopersicum cv. Regina) was grown hydroponically in a nutrient solution containing Ralstonia solanacearum for 14 days. A leaf was selected, and the hydrogel film was applied for three hours. The hydrogel was then placed in a sealed container with distilled water for two hours at 37°C, without enzyme treatment. The hydrogel was removed, rinsed, dried, and irradiated with 310 nm UV light. The fluorescence intensity at 428 nm was measured at 1,820 AU, which was statistically indistinguishable from the untreated control (1,850 AU). LC–MS/MS analysis confirmed no increase in salicylic acid concentration and no detection of methyl salicylate. This example demonstrates that the hydrolysis of methyl salicylate requires enzymatic catalysis and that passive diffusion of volatile compounds into water is insufficient for detection. The inclusion of esterase enzyme is therefore essential for the detection of methyl salicylate, and its absence renders this pathway non-functional. This comparison validates the necessity of the enzymatic enhancement step for the detection of volatile biomarkers.
