## BACKGROUND

The measurement of optical absorption in complex media has long been a cornerstone of analytical science, particularly in fields such as molecular biology, materials science, and biomedical diagnostics. For over a century, ultraviolet-visible (UV-VIS) spectrophotometry has served as the standard technique for quantifying the concentration of molecular species in solution by measuring the attenuation of transmitted light. The underlying assumption of this method is that the reduction in light intensity is attributable solely to absorption by the target molecules, a premise that holds true for optically homogeneous, dilute solutions such as pure aqueous salts or simple organic dyes. However, in real-world applications involving biological tissues, colloidal suspensions, nanoparticle dispersions, polymer films, and cellular environments, the assumption of pure absorption is fundamentally flawed. In such media, light attenuation arises not only from absorption but also from scattering — a phenomenon wherein photons are redirected by refractive index inhomogeneities without being absorbed. This scattering component, which can be orders of magnitude greater than the absorption signal, distorts the measured extinction spectrum, leading to systematic overestimation of absorptivity and rendering molecular identification unreliable or impossible.

The problem of distinguishing absorption from scattering has been recognized for decades, particularly in the context of biological tissues and plasmonic nanoparticles. Conventional UV-VIS spectrophotometers, despite their widespread use and ease of operation, are inherently incapable of separating these two contributions because they measure only the ratio of incident to transmitted light intensity, without regard to the physical mechanism responsible for the attenuation. As a result, even simple mixtures of molecular absorbers and inert scatterers, such as cupric sulfate in a suspension of polystyrene microspheres, yield spectra that cannot be decomposed into their constituent components using linear superposition. This non-additivity arises because scattering alters the spatial distribution of photons within the sample, modifying the effective path length and fluence profile experienced by the absorbing species. The resulting spectral distortion is highly sensitive to sample geometry, cuvette dimensions, detector aperture, and the angular distribution of scattered light, making reproducibility across instruments and laboratories extremely difficult.

Attempts to overcome this limitation have led to the development of specialized optical techniques, each with significant practical constraints. Integrating spheres, for example, can capture nearly all scattered light and provide a more complete measure of total extinction, but they require precise sample positioning, extensive calibration across wavelengths, and are incompatible with standard cuvette-based sample formats used in clinical and laboratory settings. Time-resolved techniques employing ultrafast lasers and picosecond or femtosecond detection systems can, in principle, separate absorption and scattering by analyzing the temporal broadening of a laser pulse as it propagates through the medium. However, such methods demand expensive, non-commercial laser sources, complex detection electronics, and are only applicable to thick, strongly scattering samples where diffusive light transport dominates — conditions that exclude many nanoscale and thin-film systems. Similarly, photothermal methods that detect thermal lensing or beam deflection induced by absorbed light offer high sensitivity to absorption but are severely limited by low concentrations, susceptibility to background scattering, and an inability to function in optically dense environments where fluence modulation by scattering becomes dominant.

In the field of nanotechnology, particularly with gold nanoparticles (GNPs), the challenge is even more acute. GNPs exhibit strong plasmonic resonances that simultaneously enhance both absorption and scattering in a wavelength-dependent manner. The relative contribution of each component depends critically on particle size, shape, aggregation state, and local dielectric environment. For instance, small gold nanorods (GNRs) with aspect ratios near 3–4 exhibit absorption-dominated extinction spectra, while larger GNRs with similar aspect ratios show scattering that exceeds absorption. When such particles are introduced into biological systems — such as cells — they are internalized via endocytosis and often aggregate within endosomes or lysosomes, forming clusters that induce plasmonic coupling. This coupling leads to dramatic spectral shifts, splitting of resonance peaks, and suppression of absorption intensity, all of which are misinterpreted by conventional UV-VIS spectrophotometry as changes in concentration or chemical state. Yet, without a method to isolate the true absorption component, it is impossible to determine whether a spectral shift arises from a change in the intrinsic optical properties of the nanoparticle, from environmental perturbations, or from the emergence of new scattering pathways due to clustering.

Moreover, the inability to measure true absorption spectra in situ has profound implications for biomedical applications. Photothermal therapy, for example, relies on precise knowledge of the absorption cross-section of nanoparticles at the irradiation wavelength to calculate thermal dose and avoid under- or over-treatment. Similarly, photoacoustic imaging, which depends on the conversion of absorbed light into acoustic waves, requires accurate absorption spectra to enable quantitative molecular mapping. Yet current imaging protocols often use extinction spectra measured in buffer solutions as proxies for intracellular absorption — a practice that introduces substantial error due to unaccounted scattering from cellular organelles, membranes, and cytoplasmic proteins. Even in diagnostic assays such as enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA), where gold nanoparticles serve as colorimetric labels, clustering induced by antigen binding alters the extinction spectrum in ways that cannot be reliably interpreted without knowledge of the underlying absorption profile. Consequently, the field lacks a simple, robust, and universally applicable method to measure the true optical absorption of any substance — molecular, nanoscale, or biological — in the presence of significant scattering.

Existing approaches to this problem are either too complex, too restrictive, or too indirect to be adopted in routine analytical workflows. There is a critical and unmet need for a technique that can measure absolute optical absorption coefficients independently of scattering, without requiring physical contact with the sample, without altering the sample’s physical or chemical state, and without the need for prior knowledge of the scattering properties of the medium. Such a method would enable accurate quantification of molecular constituents in turbid environments, facilitate the development of label-free biosensors, improve the design of nanoparticle-based therapeutics, and revolutionize the interpretation of optical data in complex biological systems. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel, non-contact photoacoustic spectrophotometry technique that decouples absorption from scattering, providing the first practical means to obtain true molecular absorption spectra in optically dense, heterogeneous media.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method and apparatus for non-contact photoacoustic spectrophotometry capable of measuring the true optical absorption coefficient of a substance in a medium regardless of the magnitude or nature of concurrent light scattering. Unlike conventional UV-VIS spectrophotometry, which conflates absorption and scattering into a single extinction signal, the disclosed method isolates the absorption component by detecting acoustic transients generated exclusively by the conversion of absorbed photons into localized heat. The technique operates in reflection mode, utilizing a pulsed laser to excite photoacoustic signals within a thin subsurface layer of the sample, and employs a non-contact, fiber-optic interferometric detector to capture the resulting ultrasonic waves from the sample’s surface without physical coupling. This configuration renders the measurement insensitive to scattering effects, even when the scattering coefficient exceeds the absorption coefficient by several orders of magnitude, and eliminates the need for direct acoustic transducers, cuvette-specific calibration, or prior knowledge of the medium’s optical properties.

The method is applicable to a broad range of materials, including molecular solutions, colloidal suspensions, plasmonic nanoparticles, cellular suspensions, and tissue-like media. It enables the accurate determination of absorption spectra for substances such as cupric sulfate in the presence of polystyrene microspheres, gold nanorods of varying sizes and surface coatings, and nanoparticles internalized within living cells — all without interference from the scattering background. The system operates within a tunable wavelength range of 700 to 900 nanometers, with the capability for extension into the ultraviolet and near-infrared regions through the use of alternative laser sources. The sample is contained within a simple, disposable chamber formed by two transparent, non-absorbing plates separated by a thin diaphragm, allowing seamless integration with standard laboratory practices. The detection system employs a low-power probe beam and a Sagnac interferometer to achieve high sensitivity and spatial resolution, with lateral resolution determined by the optical probe spot size rather than acoustic diffraction limits, enabling sub-micrometer spatial characterization.

The invention further includes a calibration protocol based on a reference solution with a known absorption coefficient, such as aqueous cupric sulfate, to convert the measured photoacoustic signal amplitude into absolute absorption coefficients in units of inverse centimeters. The method demonstrates linear response over a wide dynamic range of concentrations, with calibration accuracy better than 2% for molecular absorbers and consistent performance across varying scatterer concentrations, sizes, and types. The technique is non-destructive, requiring only low laser fluences (on the order of microjoules per square centimeter) that do not alter the morphology, aggregation state, or function of nanoparticles or cells, even after repeated exposure. The entire measurement process is rapid, with full spectral acquisition over a 200-nanometer range completed in under fifteen minutes, making it suitable for high-throughput screening and real-time monitoring of dynamic processes.

Importantly, the invention enables the first direct measurement of the true absorption spectrum of plasmonic nanoparticles following cellular internalization, revealing spectral changes induced by intracellular clustering and plasmonic coupling that are entirely masked by scattering in conventional UV-VIS measurements. This capability provides unprecedented insight into the behavior of nanomaterials in biological environments, opening new avenues for optimizing targeted drug delivery, improving photothermal therapy protocols, and developing next-generation label-free biosensors. The method is not limited to biological systems and is equally applicable to industrial and materials science applications, including the characterization of thin-film semiconductors, colloidal quantum dots, metamaterials, and photovoltaic absorbers. Furthermore, the non-contact nature of the detection system allows for easy integration with existing UV-VIS spectrophotometers, creating a hybrid instrument capable of simultaneously measuring both extinction and true absorption, thereby providing a complete optical fingerprint of any sample.

The invention thus represents a fundamental advancement in optical spectroscopy by decoupling absorption from scattering for the first time in a practical, scalable, and instrumentally simple manner. It resolves longstanding limitations in molecular quantification, nanoparticle characterization, and biomedical diagnostics, and establishes a new standard for accurate optical absorption measurement in complex and turbid media.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention relates to a novel method and apparatus for non-contact photoacoustic spectrophotometry that enables the accurate, scattering-insensitive measurement of optical absorption in complex media. The invention is based on the principle that the photoacoustic signal generated by a pulsed laser source is directly proportional to the amount of light absorbed by the medium, and is largely independent of the mechanisms responsible for the redistribution of unabsorbed photons. Unlike conventional spectrophotometry, which measures the total attenuation of transmitted light and cannot distinguish between absorption and scattering, the disclosed technique detects only the mechanical response induced by localized heating from absorbed photons. This response is captured remotely, without physical contact, using an optical interferometric sensor that detects minute surface displacements caused by the propagating acoustic wave. The combination of controlled optical excitation and non-contact acoustic detection results in a measurement that is fundamentally immune to the influence of scattering, regardless of its magnitude, spatial distribution, or spectral dependence.

The apparatus comprises three primary components: a tunable nanosecond laser source, a sample chamber configured for optical and acoustic access, and a non-contact optical interferometric detector. The laser source is a diode-pumped, solid-state laser capable of wavelength tuning across the range of 700 to 900 nanometers, with a pulse duration of approximately 5 to 10 nanoseconds and a pulse repetition rate of at least 1 kilohertz. The laser emits a collimated beam that is focused through a precision lens system to a spot size of approximately 0.5 millimeters in diameter on the surface of the sample. The sample is contained within a sealed, flat-bottomed chamber formed by two optically transparent, non-absorbing plates — typically made of fused silica or high-purity quartz — separated by a rigid, non-deformable diaphragm of thickness 0.1 millimeters and inner diameter 6 millimeters. The diaphragm is designed to maintain a uniform sample thickness and to provide a flat, planar interface for both optical penetration and acoustic wave generation. The sample volume is filled with the medium under investigation, which may include molecular solutions, nanoparticle suspensions, cellular suspensions, or any other optically heterogeneous material. The chamber is mounted on a motorized XY translation stage that moves the sample at a constant velocity of 80 millimeters per second, synchronized with the laser firing rate such that each laser pulse strikes a new, unexposed location on the sample surface. This motion ensures homogenous sampling across the entire illuminated area, minimizes localized heating, and averages out any spatial inhomogeneities or sedimentation effects that might otherwise bias the measurement.

The photoacoustic signal is generated when the incident laser pulse is absorbed by the medium, leading to rapid thermal expansion and the subsequent emission of an acoustic wave that propagates perpendicularly from the subsurface layer of absorption. The amplitude of this acoustic transient is governed by the product of the local absorption coefficient, the laser fluence, and the Grüneisen parameter of the medium, which quantifies the efficiency of thermal-to-mechanical energy conversion. Crucially, the fluence distribution within the sample is engineered to remain nearly constant across varying scattering conditions by limiting the excitation beam diameter to less than the transport mean free path of photons in the medium. For scattering coefficients up to 20 inverse centimeters, which encompasses the vast majority of biological and colloidal systems, a beam diameter of 0.5 millimeters ensures that the photons do not undergo multiple scattering events before being absorbed, thereby maintaining a linear relationship between the incident fluence and the absorbed energy. This design eliminates the dependence of the photoacoustic signal on the scattering coefficient, rendering the measurement insensitive to changes in turbidity, particle concentration, or aggregate morphology.

The acoustic wave generated within the sample propagates to the upper surface of the transparent plate, where it induces a minute displacement on the order of nanometers. This displacement is detected using a non-contact, fiber-optic Sagnac interferometer, which employs a low-power continuous-wave probe beam at a wavelength of 1550 nanometers directed onto the same surface. The probe beam is reflected off a thin, optically reflective coating deposited on the inner surface of the upper plate, which is designed to be highly reflective at the probe wavelength while remaining transparent to the excitation laser. The reflected probe beam recombines with a reference beam within the interferometer loop, producing an interference pattern whose intensity varies with the surface displacement caused by the photoacoustic wave. The interferometer is configured to operate in a balanced, differential mode, which suppresses environmental noise, thermal drift, and mechanical vibrations, ensuring high signal-to-noise ratio and long-term stability. The output of the interferometer is converted into an electrical signal by a photodetector and digitized using a 14-bit analog-to-digital converter operating at a sampling rate sufficient to resolve the temporal profile of the acoustic transient, typically 50 megasamples per second.

The digitized signal is processed by a computer system that applies a bandpass filter to isolate the photoacoustic frequency component, averages multiple pulses to enhance signal fidelity, and normalizes the amplitude against a reference calibration. The calibration is performed using a reference solution with a known, well-characterized absorption coefficient, such as an aqueous solution of cupric sulfate pentahydrate at a precisely measured concentration. The relationship between the measured photoacoustic voltage amplitude and the absorption coefficient is established through a linear regression over a range of concentrations, yielding a calibration factor with an accuracy better than 2%. This calibration is valid across the entire wavelength range of the laser and is not affected by the presence of scatterers, as demonstrated by consistent results obtained in solutions containing polystyrene microspheres of varying sizes and concentrations. The resulting absorption spectrum is then plotted as a function of wavelength, with the vertical axis representing the absorption coefficient in units of inverse centimeters, enabling direct comparison with theoretical models and literature values.

The method is particularly advantageous for the characterization of plasmonic nanoparticles, such as gold nanorods, whose optical properties are dominated by both absorption and scattering in a size-dependent manner. For small nanorods with lengths of approximately 40 nanometers and aspect ratios near 3.8, the absorption component dominates the extinction spectrum, while for larger nanorods of approximately 100 nanometers in length with similar aspect ratios, the scattering component becomes predominant. Conventional UV-VIS spectrophotometry measures the sum of these contributions and reports a single extinction peak, which is often misinterpreted as the absorption maximum. In contrast, the disclosed method reveals the true absorption profile, showing that the absorption coefficient for larger nanorods is significantly lower than the extinction peak would suggest. This distinction has critical implications for applications such as photothermal therapy, where the thermal dose delivered to a target is directly proportional to the absorption cross-section, not the extinction cross-section. Using extinction-based estimates for larger nanoparticles would lead to substantial overestimation of heating efficiency, potentially resulting in ineffective treatment or unintended tissue damage. The present invention provides the first practical means to determine the true absorption spectrum of such particles, enabling accurate dosimetry and optimization of therapeutic protocols.

The invention further enables the measurement of nanoparticle absorption within complex biological environments, such as living cells. When gold nanorods are internalized by human umbilical vein endothelial cells, they are sequestered into endosomal compartments where they aggregate and undergo plasmonic coupling. This coupling alters the local electromagnetic environment around the particles, leading to spectral shifts, peak splitting, and suppression of the longitudinal plasmon resonance. Conventional UV-VIS spectrophotometry of the cell suspension reports a dramatically altered extinction spectrum, which is commonly interpreted as evidence of nanoparticle transformation or chemical modification. However, the present method reveals that the true absorption spectrum of the internalized nanoparticles is markedly different from the extinction spectrum — exhibiting reduced intensity and broadened features consistent with the formation of non-parallel, clustered arrangements of nanorods. This finding demonstrates that the spectral changes observed in cellular environments are not due to alterations in the intrinsic optical properties of the nanoparticles themselves, but rather to the emergence of new scattering pathways induced by their spatial organization within the cell. By isolating the absorption component, the invention provides a direct, label-free method to monitor the intracellular fate of nanoparticles, offering insights into endocytic trafficking, clustering dynamics, and nanoparticle-cell interactions that were previously inaccessible.

The method is also applicable to the characterization of multi-component mixtures, such as biological tissues or suspensions containing both molecular absorbers and particulate scatterers. For example, in a solution of cupric sulfate mixed with polystyrene microspheres of diameters ranging from 0.5 to 10 micrometers, the UV-VIS extinction spectrum increases monotonically with scatterer concentration, even though the concentration of the absorber remains unchanged. The photoacoustic measurement, in contrast, remains invariant, confirming that the absorption coefficient of cupric sulfate is unaffected by the presence of scatterers. This result demonstrates the robustness of the method in environments where scattering dominates by orders of magnitude — conditions that render conventional spectrophotometry useless. The technique is equally effective for suspensions of biological cells, which themselves act as strong scatterers due to their refractive index mismatch with the surrounding medium. Measurements performed on cell suspensions with and without nanoparticles show that the absorption spectrum of the nanoparticles is preserved, even when embedded within a highly scattering cellular matrix. This capability is transformative for in situ analysis of nanoparticle behavior in physiologically relevant conditions, eliminating the need for cell lysis, purification, or other disruptive sample preparation steps.

The system is designed for ease of integration into existing laboratory workflows. The sample chamber is compatible with standard cuvette dimensions and can be fabricated from disposable, low-cost materials, allowing for single-use operation to prevent cross-contamination. The laser source and interferometric detector are housed in a compact, benchtop unit that connects to a standard personal computer for data acquisition and analysis. The entire measurement sequence — from wavelength selection to spectral output — is automated and requires minimal operator intervention. A single full-spectrum acquisition, covering 21 wavelengths spaced at 10-nanometer intervals across the 700–900 nanometer range, takes approximately 10.5 minutes, making it feasible for high-throughput screening applications. The system can be extended to broader spectral ranges by incorporating alternative laser sources, such as optical parametric oscillators or supercontinuum lasers, to access the ultraviolet and longer infrared regions. The detection system can also be adapted for imaging applications by scanning the excitation and probe beams in two dimensions, enabling spatially resolved absorption mapping with sub-micrometer lateral resolution — a capability not achievable with conventional photoacoustic microscopy due to its acoustic diffraction limit.

The invention further includes a computational framework for data interpretation and spectral decomposition. The measured absorption spectra can be compared with theoretical models derived from Mie theory, finite-difference time-domain simulations, or other electromagnetic solvers to infer nanoparticle size, shape, and aggregation state. For example, the ratio of absorption to scattering can be used to estimate the effective radius of a nanoparticle population, and deviations from expected spectral shapes can indicate the presence of clustering, surface functionalization, or environmental changes. The method is also compatible with multivariate analysis techniques, such as principal component analysis or machine learning algorithms, to classify complex mixtures based on their absorption fingerprints. This opens the door to new forms of label-free diagnostics, where the absorption spectrum of a nanoparticle probe serves as a direct readout of its biochemical state, without the need for fluorescent tags, enzymatic amplification, or other indirect indicators.

The method is non-destructive and operates under conditions that preserve the integrity of biological samples. Laser fluences are kept below 2 microjoules per square centimeter, well below the threshold for thermal damage, photobleaching, or structural alteration of nanoparticles or cells. Repeated measurements over thousands of laser pulses show no measurable change in the absorption spectrum, confirming that the technique does not induce artifacts or alter the system under investigation. The absence of physical contact between the detector and the sample eliminates the risk of contamination, sample deformation, or acoustic coupling artifacts that plague conventional piezoelectric transducers. The entire system is sealed and enclosed, minimizing exposure to ambient light, dust, and vibration, ensuring high reproducibility across multiple operators and laboratories.

The invention has broad applicability across scientific and industrial domains. In biomedical research, it enables accurate quantification of nanoparticle uptake, intracellular distribution, and therapeutic efficacy. In pharmaceutical development, it facilitates the characterization of drug delivery vehicles, liposomes, and polymeric nanoparticles in complex biological matrices. In materials science, it allows for the direct measurement of absorption in thin-film semiconductors, quantum dot arrays, and metamaterials without interference from surface roughness or substrate scattering. In environmental monitoring, it permits the detection of trace contaminants in turbid water samples, such as suspended particulates or dissolved organic matter, with unprecedented specificity. In industrial quality control, it provides a rapid, non-contact method for verifying the optical properties of pigments, coatings, and optical filters in production environments.

The invention represents a paradigm shift in optical spectroscopy by separating the fundamental physical processes of absorption and scattering for the first time in a practical, scalable, and instrumentally simple manner. It resolves a century-old limitation in the interpretation of UV-VIS spectra and provides a universal tool for measuring true molecular absorption in any medium, regardless of its optical complexity. The method is not constrained by sample type, concentration, or scattering strength, and it requires no prior knowledge of the medium’s composition or structure. It is robust, reproducible, and ready for immediate implementation in laboratories, clinics, and industrial settings. The invention thus establishes a new standard for optical absorption measurement and opens new frontiers in molecular analysis, nanotechnology, and biomedical diagnostics.
