# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to a method and system for the early molecular detection of Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD) in neonates through the identification of specific genomic deletions in the DMD gene that are amenable to exon-skipping therapies. More particularly, the invention concerns a multiplex quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assay designed to detect hemi- or homozygous deletions of exons 44, 46, 50, 52, and 54 of the DMD gene in dried blood spot samples collected from newborns. This assay enables the rapid, high-throughput, and cost-effective identification of infants who are candidates for approved or investigational antisense oligonucleotide-based therapies targeting exon 45, exon 51, or exon 53 skipping. The invention is particularly suited for implementation within newborn screening programs where early diagnosis of treatable genetic disorders can significantly alter clinical outcomes. The method leverages the established infrastructure of existing neonatal screening platforms that routinely perform molecular diagnostics for conditions such as spinal muscular atrophy, thereby minimizing the need for new laboratory equipment or complex procedural overhauls. The invention is not intended for the detection of all forms of DMD, nor does it identify point mutations, duplications, or non-deletion variants; rather, it is precisely calibrated to detect a subset of deletions that, when present, predict eligibility for currently available or near-term therapeutic interventions. By focusing on a genetically defined subgroup of DMD patients who stand to benefit from targeted treatment, the invention represents a paradigm shift in newborn screening from broad disease detection to precision screening for treatable genotypes.

## BACKGROUND

Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy is a severe, progressive, X-linked recessive neuromuscular disorder characterized by the absence of functional dystrophin protein, leading to the degeneration of skeletal and cardiac muscle tissue. The disease typically manifests in early childhood, with symptoms such as delayed motor milestones, Gowers’ sign, and elevated serum creatine kinase levels becoming apparent between the ages of two and five years. Without intervention, affected individuals lose ambulation by early adolescence and face life-threatening complications involving respiratory and cardiac failure, often resulting in premature death in the second or third decade of life. The underlying genetic cause of DMD lies in pathogenic variants within the DMD gene located on the X chromosome, which encodes the dystrophin protein essential for maintaining the structural integrity of muscle fibers. While the majority of pathogenic variants in DMD are large deletions or duplications affecting one or more exons, a significant proportion are point mutations leading to premature stop codons. Among deletions, approximately 60 to 70 percent are out-of-frame, resulting in a complete loss of dystrophin expression, whereas in-frame deletions are typically associated with the milder Becker Muscular Dystrophy phenotype.

Historically, the diagnosis of DMD has been delayed due to the nonspecific nature of clinical symptoms and the lack of reliable biomarkers detectable in the neonatal period. As a result, the average age of diagnosis remains between four and five years, despite the onset of muscle dysfunction occurring much earlier. This diagnostic delay precludes the opportunity for early therapeutic intervention, which is now recognized as critical for maximizing functional outcomes. In recent years, advances in molecular medicine have led to the development of several disease-modifying therapies aimed at restoring dystrophin expression. Among these, antisense oligonucleotide-mediated exon skipping has emerged as a clinically validated approach. This strategy involves the use of synthetic oligonucleotides that bind to pre-messenger RNA and induce the exclusion of specific exons during splicing, thereby converting an out-of-frame deletion into an in-frame transcript that encodes a partially functional, internally truncated dystrophin protein. Two such therapies—eteplirsen, which targets exon 51 skipping, and golodirsen, which targets exon 53 skipping—have received regulatory approval in the United States, with additional agents such as viltolarsen, casimersen, and suvodirsen under evaluation or in clinical use in other jurisdictions. These therapies are indicated only for patients whose underlying DMD mutations are amenable to skipping of the targeted exon, meaning that the deletion must result in an out-of-frame disruption that can be corrected by removal of a specific flanking exon.

The clinical utility of these therapies is intrinsically linked to the timing of initiation. Preclinical and early clinical data suggest that treatment commenced before the onset of significant muscle degeneration—ideally in the first months of life—can preserve muscle architecture, delay loss of ambulation, and improve long-term cardiac and respiratory outcomes. However, the current diagnostic pathway, which relies on clinical suspicion followed by confirmatory genetic testing, is too slow to enable such early intervention. Furthermore, the most widely studied first-tier screening method for DMD in newborn populations has been the measurement of creatine kinase (CK) or its muscle-specific isoform (CK-MM) in dried blood spots. While elevated CK levels are a hallmark of muscle damage, they are neither specific nor sensitive enough for population-based screening. False negatives occur in premature infants, those with low muscle mass, or those in the early stages of disease, while false positives arise from transient muscle injury, hypotonia, or other myopathies. Moreover, CK levels do not reflect the underlying genetic defect and therefore cannot determine whether a child is eligible for exon-skipping therapy. Consequently, a positive CK result necessitates follow-up molecular testing, which delays diagnosis and increases the burden on healthcare systems.

The need for a direct, genotype-specific screening method for DMD has become increasingly urgent as therapeutic options expand. Yet, no method currently exists that can be integrated into routine newborn screening to identify infants who are candidates for exon-skipping therapies. Whole-genome or exome sequencing, while comprehensive, remains cost-prohibitive for population-scale implementation. Targeted next-generation sequencing panels are more feasible but still require complex bioinformatics, longer turnaround times, and higher per-sample costs than what is acceptable for high-throughput neonatal screening. There is therefore a clear and unmet need for a rapid, inexpensive, robust, and scalable molecular assay capable of detecting the specific deletions in the DMD gene that predict eligibility for exon-skipping treatments. Such an assay must be compatible with dried blood spot samples, which are the standard matrix for newborn screening worldwide, and must be capable of being performed on existing qPCR platforms already deployed in public health laboratories. The present invention fulfills this need by providing a multiplex qPCR assay that simultaneously interrogates five key flanking exons—44, 46, 50, 52, and 54—whose deletion patterns are predictive of amenable genotypes for exon 45, 51, or 53 skipping. The assay is designed to achieve 100% sensitivity and specificity for the targeted deletions in a cohort of known DMD patients, and its implementation would allow for the identification of approximately 20 percent of all DMD cases as candidates for immediate therapeutic intervention, with an additional 17 percent identified for early supportive care, thereby transforming the natural history of the disease for a substantial subset of affected neonates.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method for the early detection of Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy in newborns through the identification of specific deletions in the DMD gene that are predictive of eligibility for exon-skipping therapies. The method comprises the use of a multiplex quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assay to detect hemi- or homozygous deletions of exons 44, 46, 50, 52, and 54 in genomic DNA extracted from dried blood spot samples collected from neonates. The assay employs five distinct primer-probe sets, each specific to one of the target exons, and utilizes a real-time PCR platform capable of detecting multiple fluorescent signals simultaneously. The presence or absence of amplification for each exon is determined by the normalized fluorescence ratio, a metric derived from the endpoint fluorescence intensity of each target relative to the median intensity of all samples within the same run. A complete absence of amplification for a given exon is interpreted as a deletion of that exon, while the presence of amplification indicates the retention of the exon. The assay is designed to identify deletions that, when present, indicate a genotype amenable to exon-skipping therapy targeting exon 45, exon 51, or exon 53. Specifically, a deletion of exon 44 predicts eligibility for exon 45 skipping; a deletion of exon 46 or 50 predicts eligibility for exon 51 skipping; and a deletion of exon 52 or 54 predicts eligibility for exon 53 skipping. The method does not require the determination of exact deletion boundaries, as the presence of any one of the five target exons in a deleted state is sufficient to indicate a genotype that may be rescued by one of the three approved or investigational exon-skipping therapies. The assay is highly specific, achieving 100 percent sensitivity and 100 percent specificity in a validation cohort of 120 samples, including male DMD patients with confirmed deletions, female carriers, and healthy controls. The method is compatible with standard dried blood spot collection and processing protocols used in newborn screening programs and requires only minimal sample input—1 microliter of crude DNA extract—without the need for DNA quantification or purification beyond basic extraction. The assay is performed in a single reaction tube, reducing the risk of contamination and enabling high-throughput processing. The results are interpretable within hours, allowing for rapid notification of positive cases and immediate referral for confirmatory testing and therapeutic evaluation. The invention further provides a screening algorithm that incorporates a two-step validation protocol: samples showing no amplification across all five target exons are retested using a two-plex qPCR reaction with a reference gene (RPP30) to rule out DNA extraction failure or technical artifact. Positive results are confirmed using multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) on a fresh EDTA blood sample to determine the precise deletion boundaries and establish eligibility for specific exon-skipping therapies. The method is not intended to detect all forms of DMD, nor does it identify point mutations, duplications, or non-deletion variants; rather, it is specifically engineered to identify those patients who stand to benefit from currently available or near-term targeted therapies. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in newborn screening, moving from the detection of disease in general to the identification of treatable genotypes, thereby enabling precision medicine to be implemented at the earliest possible stage of life.

## DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

The invention is embodied in a multiplex quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assay designed to detect deletions of exons 44, 46, 50, 52, and 54 of the DMD gene in genomic DNA extracted from dried blood spots collected from neonates. The assay is performed using a real-time PCR instrument equipped with five distinct fluorescence detection channels, each corresponding to a unique fluorophore conjugated to a probe specific for one of the five target exons. The primers and probes are designed to be highly specific to their respective exon sequences, with careful attention paid to avoid regions containing known single nucleotide polymorphisms or repetitive elements that could compromise amplification efficiency or specificity. Each primer pair flanks the target exon with amplicons of approximately 80 to 120 base pairs in length, ensuring optimal amplification kinetics under standardized thermal cycling conditions. The probe sequences are internally labeled with a fluorescent dye—FAM, YakimaYellow, ROX, Atto 647 N, or Atto 700—each chosen for its non-overlapping emission spectrum to allow simultaneous detection in a single reaction. The concentration of each primer is optimized at 300 nanomoles per liter, while probe concentrations are individually calibrated between 80 and 180 nanomoles per liter to balance signal intensity and minimize background noise. The assay is conducted in a total reaction volume of 25 microliters, using a commercially available 5× master mix containing hot-start DNA polymerase, dNTPs, magnesium chloride, and buffer components optimized for multiplex amplification. One microliter of crude DNA extract, obtained from a 3.1-mm punch of a dried blood spot without prior quantification, is added to each reaction. The thermal cycling protocol consists of an initial denaturation step at 95 degrees Celsius for five minutes, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 degrees Celsius for 30 seconds and annealing/extension at 61 degrees Celsius for 75 seconds. The use of a single annealing temperature across all five targets ensures uniform amplification conditions and facilitates high-throughput processing. Amplification is monitored in real time, and endpoint fluorescence values are recorded for each target in each sample. To account for variations in DNA input, extraction efficiency, and instrument performance, a normalized fluorescence ratio (NFR) is calculated for each target by dividing the endpoint fluorescence of the sample by the median endpoint fluorescence of all samples within the same run for that particular exon. An NFR below 0.2 is defined as indicative of a deletion, while an NFR above 0.2 is interpreted as the presence of the exon. This threshold was empirically determined through validation studies involving 120 samples with known genotypes, including 51 male patients with confirmed DMD deletions, 50 female carriers, and 19 healthy controls. In all cases, the NFR values for deleted exons were consistently below 0.2, while those for non-deleted exons in carriers and controls were uniformly above 0.2, with no overlap between the groups. The assay is capable of detecting deletions regardless of their size or extent, as long as at least one of the five target exons is absent. For example, a deletion encompassing exons 45 to 47 will result in the absence of amplification for exon 46, thereby identifying the patient as eligible for exon 45 skipping, even though exon 45 itself is not directly targeted by the assay. Similarly, a deletion spanning exons 46 to 55 will result in the absence of amplification for exons 46, 50, and 52, all of which are predictive of eligibility for exon 51 or 53 skipping. The assay does not require sequencing or breakpoint analysis to determine eligibility; the presence of a deletion in any one of the five flanking exons is sufficient to indicate a genotype that may be corrected by one of the three exon-skipping therapies. The method is not designed to distinguish between hemizygous deletions in males and heterozygous deletions in females, as carrier females are not candidates for exon-skipping therapy and are not intended to be treated. However, the assay will detect deletions in female carriers, and in the context of newborn screening, such findings may prompt further genetic counseling and family testing. The assay is not intended to detect duplications, point mutations, or intronic variants, and a negative result does not rule out a diagnosis of DMD. In the event that a sample shows no amplification for any of the five target exons, a secondary validation step is performed using a two-plex qPCR reaction that includes one of the five target exons and the reference gene RPP30, which is located on chromosome 2 and serves as a control for DNA integrity. If amplification of RPP30 is absent, the sample is considered invalid and must be retested with a new dried blood spot. If RPP30 amplifies but none of the DMD targets do, this confirms a large deletion encompassing all five flanking exons, which is rare but possible, and warrants confirmatory testing by MLPA. All positive results are confirmed on a fresh EDTA blood sample using a commercially available MLPA assay, which provides precise delineation of deletion boundaries and enables definitive determination of eligibility for specific exon-skipping therapies. The method is scalable and can be performed on standard clinical laboratory equipment, requiring no specialized instrumentation beyond a real-time PCR machine with five fluorescence channels. The entire process—from DNA extraction to result interpretation—can be completed within 24 hours, making it suitable for integration into existing newborn screening workflows. The cost per sample is less than five euros, primarily due to the reuse of existing DNA extraction protocols and the low reagent cost of qPCR. The invention further encompasses a computer-implemented system for automated interpretation of qPCR results, wherein the normalized fluorescence ratios are processed by software that compares each value against the predefined threshold of 0.2 and generates a binary output for each exon, followed by a composite genotype report indicating which, if any, of the five exons are deleted and whether the pattern is predictive of eligibility for exon 45, 51, or 53 skipping. The system may also be configured to flag samples with incomplete amplification profiles for manual review and to generate automated alerts to clinical laboratories for immediate follow-up. The invention also includes a protocol for population-based screening in which both male and female neonates are tested, thereby enabling the identification of rare cases of DMD in females with skewed X-chromosome inactivation or Turner syndrome, although such cases are not candidates for exon-skipping therapy. The method is designed to be robust under variable environmental conditions and is not affected by the gestational age or birth weight of the neonate, unlike serum biomarker-based approaches. The assay is also unaffected by the presence of maternal DNA in the dried blood spot, as the deletion is present in the neonate’s genome and is detectable regardless of maternal contamination. The invention thus provides a complete, integrated system for the early, precise, and cost-effective identification of newborns with DMD genotypes amenable to targeted therapy, enabling the initiation of treatment at a time when muscle degeneration is minimal and therapeutic benefit is maximized.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1—DMD Genotyping Assay

The DMD genotyping assay was developed and validated using a cohort of 120 archived dried blood spot samples collected from neonates with known DMD genotypes. The samples were categorized into three groups: a deleted group consisting of 51 male patients with confirmed DMD deletions, a carrier group comprising 50 female carriers of DMD deletions, and a control group of 19 healthy individuals with no known DMD variants. The deleted group was further subdivided into 34 patients whose deletions overlapped at least one of the five target exons—44, 46, 50, 52, or 54—and 17 patients whose deletions did not involve any of these exons. The carrier group included 32 females with deletions overlapping at least one target exon and 18 females with deletions outside the targeted region. All samples had been previously genotyped using multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA), and the deletion boundaries were confirmed against the Leiden Open Variation Database for the DMD gene. Genomic DNA was extracted from each dried blood spot using a standardized protocol involving a 3.1-mm punch, lysis in proteinase K-containing buffer, and heat inactivation, without subsequent quantification or purification. One microliter of the crude extract was used as template in a multiplex qPCR reaction containing a 5× Takyon master mix, primers, and probes specific for exons 44, 46, 50, 52, and 54, each labeled with a distinct fluorophore. The primer and probe sequences were selected to avoid known polymorphisms and to generate amplicons of consistent length. The qPCR was performed on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR System under standardized thermal cycling conditions: an initial denaturation at 95°C for five minutes, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 30 seconds and 61°C for 75 seconds. Fluorescence was measured at the end of each cycle, and endpoint values were recorded for each target. The normalized fluorescence ratio (NFR) was calculated for each sample by dividing the endpoint fluorescence of each target by the median endpoint fluorescence of all samples within the same run for that exon. The NFR values were then analyzed to determine the diagnostic accuracy of the assay. In the deleted group, all 34 patients with deletions overlapping one or more target exons exhibited complete absence of amplification for the corresponding exon(s), with NFR values consistently below 0.2. For example, a patient with a deletion of exons 45–47 showed no amplification for exon 46, while a patient with a deletion of exons 46–55 showed absence of amplification for exons 46, 50, and 52. In contrast, the 17 patients with deletions outside the target region showed normal amplification for all five exons, with NFR values above 0.2. In the carrier group, all 50 females displayed amplification for all five exons, with NFR values indistinguishable from those of the control group, indicating that the assay does not detect heterozygous deletions as deletions due to the presence of the wild-type allele. In the control group, all 19 individuals showed robust amplification for all five exons, with NFR values uniformly above 0.2. The assay demonstrated 100 percent sensitivity for detecting deletions overlapping the target exons and 100 percent specificity for distinguishing them from non-deleted genotypes. No false positives or false negatives were observed. The assay was also reproducible across multiple runs, with intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation for NFR values below 5 percent. The results confirmed that the assay reliably identifies neonates with deletions predictive of eligibility for exon 45, 51, or 53 skipping therapies, without misclassifying carriers or healthy individuals. The method was further validated by testing samples from neonates with known point mutations and duplications, all of which showed normal amplification profiles, confirming that the assay is specific to deletions and does not respond to other variant types. The assay was also tested under suboptimal conditions, including reduced DNA input, extended storage of dried blood spots, and variable extraction efficiency, and remained robust in all scenarios. The assay was therefore determined to be suitable for implementation in a population-based newborn screening program.

### Example 2—DMD Genotyping Assay

In a second validation cohort, the DMD genotyping assay was applied to a prospective set of 2,150 neonatal dried blood spot samples collected from a regional newborn screening program. The samples were processed in batches of 96 using automated liquid handling systems, and the multiplex qPCR assay was run in parallel with quality control samples, including positive controls (samples with known deletions of exon 46), negative controls (healthy controls), and no-template controls. All samples were processed using the same extraction protocol and qPCR conditions as described in Example 1. The normalized fluorescence ratio was calculated for each target exon in each sample, and samples with NFR values below 0.2 for one or more exons were flagged for confirmatory testing. Of the 2,150 samples, 2,143 showed normal amplification for all five exons, with NFR values consistently above 0.2. Seven samples exhibited absence of amplification for at least one target exon. Of these, five samples showed deletion of exon 46, one sample showed deletion of exon 50, and one sample showed deletion of exon 52. All seven samples were retested using a two-plex qPCR reaction with the RPP30 reference gene, and all showed robust amplification of RPP30, confirming that the absence of DMD amplification was not due to DNA degradation or extraction failure. The seven positive samples were then recalled for fresh EDTA blood draws, and MLPA analysis was performed to determine the precise deletion boundaries. The five samples with exon 46 deletion were confirmed to have deletions ranging from exon 45–47 to exon 46–55, all of which are amenable to exon 51 skipping. The sample with exon 50 deletion was confirmed to have a deletion spanning exons 50–54, which is amenable to exon 51 or 53 skipping. The sample with exon 52 deletion was confirmed to have a deletion extending from exon 51 to 55, which is amenable to exon 53 skipping. All seven cases were thus identified as eligible for exon-skipping therapy and were immediately referred to a specialized neuromuscular clinic for genetic counseling and therapeutic evaluation. No false positives were observed, and no samples with deletions outside the target region were misclassified. The incidence of eligible deletions in this population was estimated at approximately 1 in 307 live births, which is consistent with published estimates of the prevalence of amenable DMD genotypes. The assay demonstrated 100 percent concordance with MLPA results and successfully identified all cases of eligible deletions without delay. The turnaround time from sample receipt to result notification was less than 48 hours, enabling the earliest possible initiation of clinical follow-up. The cost of the assay, including reagents, labor, and consumables, was calculated at 4.75 euros per sample, which is comparable to the cost of screening for spinal muscular atrophy and significantly lower than the cost of serum creatine kinase testing followed by confirmatory molecular analysis. The assay was integrated into the existing newborn screening workflow without requiring additional instrumentation or personnel training beyond standard qPCR proficiency. The results of this prospective study confirm the feasibility, accuracy, and scalability of the assay for population-based newborn screening and demonstrate its potential to transform the early management of Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy by identifying treatable genotypes at birth. The method is now ready for implementation in public health screening programs in jurisdictions where exon-skipping therapies are approved or under regulatory review.
