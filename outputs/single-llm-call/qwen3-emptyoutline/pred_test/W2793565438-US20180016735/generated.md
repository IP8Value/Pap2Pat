# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of biomaterials and composite engineering, specifically to the fabrication of lignocellulosic composites derived entirely from unprocessed, naturally occurring plant biomass without the use of synthetic adhesives, resins, or chemical modifiers. The invention encompasses a novel method for producing structurally robust, biodegradable, and compostable composite panels through the controlled partial dissolution of cellulose-rich biomass in cold aqueous alkaline solvent systems, followed by the integration of undissolved lignocellulosic reinforcement materials to form a cohesive, interpenetrating matrix-reinforcement architecture. These composites are uniquely characterized by their high mechanical performance relative to their low-cost, renewable feedstocks, their complete absence of petroleum-derived components, and their ability to retain the natural chemical integrity of their constituent biomass while achieving structural cohesion through physical and molecular interactions induced by low-temperature alkaline treatment. The resulting materials are particularly suited for applications in sustainable packaging, non-load-bearing construction elements, agricultural substrates, and disposable consumer goods where environmental impact, end-of-life biodegradability, and material origin are critical design parameters. The invention further extends to the optimization of process variables—including solvent composition, reaction temperature, matrix-to-reinforcement ratio, solvent volume, and post-treatment protocols—that collectively govern the microstructural development, interfacial adhesion, and macroscopic mechanical properties of the final composite.

## BACKGROUND

The global demand for sustainable alternatives to petroleum-based plastics and synthetic composites has intensified in recent decades, driven by environmental concerns over plastic pollution, carbon emissions, and resource depletion. Lignocellulosic biomass, comprising cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, represents the most abundant renewable organic material on Earth, yet its inherent structural complexity and resistance to deconstruction have historically limited its utility in engineered materials. Traditional approaches to valorizing lignocellulosic feedstocks have relied heavily on chemical pretreatments—including acid hydrolysis, steam explosion, alkali delignification, and organosolv processes—to separate and isolate individual components, particularly cellulose, for downstream applications such as biofuel production or regenerated fiber formation. While these methods have demonstrated efficacy in breaking down recalcitrant biomass structures, they often involve high energy inputs, corrosive reagents, toxic solvents, and generate significant waste streams that undermine their environmental sustainability.

In parallel, efforts to develop biocomposites from lignocellulosic materials have focused on blending natural fibers with synthetic polymer matrices—such as polypropylene, polylactic acid, or epoxy resins—to enhance mechanical performance. However, these composites remain fundamentally non-biodegradable due to the persistence of synthetic binders, and their end-of-life management often results in landfill accumulation or incineration, negating the ecological benefits of the natural reinforcement. Alternative approaches have sought to eliminate synthetic adhesives entirely by developing all-cellulose composites (ACCs), wherein cellulose fibers are dissolved in specialized solvents and reconstituted into a homogeneous matrix that binds undissolved cellulose fibrils. These ACCs, typically fabricated using cold alkaline solvent systems such as NaOH/urea or LiOH·H₂O/urea at sub-zero temperatures, have shown promise in producing mechanically competent, fully biodegradable materials. However, ACCs require near-complete dissolution of the cellulose feedstock—often exceeding 80% by mass—to achieve sufficient matrix continuity and inter-fiber bonding, rendering them economically unviable due to the high cost of purified cellulose sources such as cotton linters or microcrystalline cellulose.

Moreover, existing ACC methodologies are limited in their ability to incorporate non-cellulosic lignocellulosic components—such as hemicellulose-rich agave bagasse or lignin-dense wood residues—into the composite structure without compromising mechanical integrity. The presence of lignin and hemicellulose in these feedstocks is typically viewed as a contaminant in ACC production, leading to their removal via pre-treatment or exclusion from the formulation entirely. Consequently, the potential to leverage low-value, high-volume agricultural and forestry by-products—such as osage orange wood sawdust, blue agave bagasse, or other lignocellulosic wastes—as primary reinforcement agents within a cellulose-based composite framework has remained largely unexplored. Furthermore, prior methods have not systematically investigated the role of partial cellulose dissolution as a mechanism for generating a viscous, adhesive gel matrix capable of encapsulating and binding heterogeneous lignocellulosic particles without requiring full dissolution of the matrix component.

There exists, therefore, a critical gap in the art: a scalable, solvent-based method for producing high-performance, fully biodegradable composites from low-cost, unprocessed lignocellulosic biomass that avoids synthetic additives, minimizes energy input, and leverages the natural chemical heterogeneity of plant materials to enhance interfacial cohesion. The present invention addresses this gap by introducing a paradigm shift in composite fabrication: rather than seeking to dissolve and homogenize the entire biomass feedstock, the invention intentionally employs a controlled degree of cellulose dissolution to generate a semi-fluid, adhesive gel matrix that physically and chemically entangles with undissolved lignocellulosic reinforcement particles. This approach enables the use of inexpensive, non-purified biomass sources—such as cotton as a partial cellulose donor and osage orange wood sawdust and agave bagasse as lignocellulosic reinforcements—without chemical pretreatment, while simultaneously achieving mechanical properties that rival or exceed those of commercial bioplastics and packaging materials. The invention further demonstrates that the mechanical performance of these composites is exquisitely sensitive to process parameters—including solvent type, concentration, temperature, volume, and matrix-to-reinforcement ratio—each of which can be optimized to tailor the composite’s density, porosity, tensile strength, flexural modulus, and dimensional stability. The resulting materials represent a new class of lignocellulosic composites that are not merely blends of natural fibers and cellulose, but are structurally integrated, solvent-induced architectures formed through controlled phase separation and reassembly under low-temperature alkaline conditions.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for fabricating lignocellulosic composites composed entirely of unprocessed plant biomass without the addition of synthetic adhesives, resins, or chemical crosslinkers. The method comprises the steps of: (a) preparing a cold aqueous alkaline solvent system comprising lithium hydroxide monohydrate and urea in a specific molar ratio, cooled to a temperature between -5°C and -15°C; (b) introducing a cellulose-rich biomass material into the solvent system and agitating under controlled conditions to achieve partial dissolution, thereby forming a viscous, translucent cellulose gel matrix; (c) incorporating a lignocellulosic reinforcement material comprising non-cellulosic plant residues into the gel matrix while maintaining the low temperature; (d) consolidating the resulting mixture into a mold under ambient pressure; (e) removing the solvent through a series of water exchange and vacuum-assisted washing steps; and (f) drying and densifying the consolidated composite under controlled temperature and pressure conditions to produce a solid, coherent panel. The cellulose-rich biomass material is selected from the group consisting of cotton, cotton linters, or other high-purity cellulose sources, and constitutes between 15% and 35% by dry weight of the total composite. The lignocellulosic reinforcement material is selected from the group consisting of osage orange wood sawdust, blue agave bagasse, or combinations thereof, and constitutes between 65% and 85% by dry weight of the total composite. The cold aqueous alkaline solvent system comprises 4.0% to 5.0% lithium hydroxide monohydrate and 12% to 18% urea by weight relative to the total solvent volume, with a preferred concentration of 4.6% LiOH·H₂O and 15% urea. The solvent volume employed is between 45 mL and 105 mL per gram of total dry biomass, with an optimal volume of approximately 60 mL per panel. The agitation of the cellulose-rich biomass in the solvent is conducted at a temperature between -10°C and -15°C, with a preferred temperature of -12.5°C, to induce partial dissolution without complete solvation. The lignocellulosic reinforcement is introduced after the formation of the viscous gel matrix and is mixed for a duration sufficient to achieve uniform dispersion without disrupting the gel structure. The consolidation step employs a mold with porous mesh interfaces to facilitate solvent drainage while maintaining structural integrity. The water exchange process involves multiple submersions in tap water under vacuum followed by prolonged immersion in ambient water, with periodic water replacement, to remove residual alkaline components and promote gel reformation. Drying is conducted under vacuum at a temperature of 50°C to 70°C, followed by hot-pressing at 160°C to 200°C under a pressure of 5 to 10 tons to achieve final densification and interfacial bonding. The resulting composite exhibits a density of 0.8 to 1.2 g/cm³, a tensile strength exceeding 25 MPa, a flexural modulus greater than 3 GPa, and a water absorption rate of less than 180% after 24-hour immersion, with the capability of being fully composted under industrial conditions within 90 days. The invention further encompasses composites wherein the lignocellulosic reinforcement comprises a binary mixture of osage orange wood sawdust and blue agave bagasse in a 1:1 ratio, which synergistically enhances mechanical performance by combining fine particulate filler properties with fibrous reinforcement. The method is scalable, requires no pretreatment of feedstocks, operates at ambient pressure, and utilizes non-toxic, water-soluble reagents, rendering it environmentally benign and economically viable for industrial application.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention disclosed herein is directed to a novel method for fabricating lignocellulosic composites that are structurally robust, entirely biodegradable, and composed exclusively of unprocessed plant-derived materials without the use of synthetic adhesives, resins, or chemical modifiers. The method leverages the unique physicochemical behavior of cellulose under cold, concentrated alkaline conditions to generate a semi-dissolved, viscous gel matrix that functions as an intrinsic adhesive, binding together undissolved lignocellulosic reinforcement particles. This approach fundamentally diverges from prior art in which cellulose is either fully dissolved to form homogeneous all-cellulose composites or used in its native fibrous state as a reinforcement within synthetic polymer matrices. In contrast, the present invention intentionally limits the extent of cellulose dissolution to preserve the structural integrity of the matrix source while generating a gel phase with sufficient viscosity and cohesive strength to envelop and mechanically interlock with heterogeneous lignocellulosic components. The resulting composite is not a blend of discrete phases but a dynamically reassembled, solvent-mediated architecture wherein the cellulose gel acts as both a binder and a structural scaffold, while the lignocellulosic residues serve as reinforcing elements that impart stiffness, dimensional stability, and cost efficiency.

The process begins with the preparation of a cold aqueous alkaline solvent system. The solvent is formulated from lithium hydroxide monohydrate and urea dissolved in deionized water, with the lithium hydroxide concentration maintained between 4.0% and 5.0% by weight and the urea concentration between 12% and 18% by weight. The preferred composition is 4.6% LiOH·H₂O and 15% urea, a formulation previously demonstrated to effectively solvate cellulose at low temperatures while minimizing degradation of the polysaccharide backbone. The solvent is cooled to a temperature ranging from -5°C to -15°C, with the optimal temperature for gel formation identified as -12.5°C. At this temperature range, the solvent exhibits a unique capacity to penetrate the amorphous regions of cellulose chains, disrupting hydrogen bonding networks and inducing partial solvation without complete depolymerization. The cooling process is conducted under controlled conditions to ensure uniform thermal distribution, preventing localized crystallization or phase separation. The solvent system is maintained under inert atmosphere or sealed to prevent contamination and evaporation, and is allowed to equilibrate for a minimum of two hours prior to the introduction of biomass.

The cellulose-rich biomass material, selected from cotton, cotton linters, or other high-purity cellulose sources, is introduced into the cold solvent system at a mass ratio corresponding to 15% to 35% of the total dry weight of the composite. Cotton is the preferred source due to its exceptionally high cellulose content (approximately 96%), low lignin and hemicellulose content, and commercial availability at low cost. The biomass is added in flaked or shredded form to maximize surface area exposure and is agitated using a mechanical mixer equipped with a three-blade propeller at a rotational speed of 300 to 400 rpm. The agitation duration is maintained between 10 and 20 minutes, during which time the cellulose fibers undergo partial dissolution, transforming from a fibrous, opaque solid into a translucent, viscous gel. This gel is not a true solution but a swollen, entangled network of partially solvated cellulose chains, with residual crystalline domains preserved to provide structural integrity. The formation of this gel is visually identifiable by its increased viscosity, loss of fibrous morphology, and the development of a smooth, homogeneous, white-to-translucent appearance. The gel phase is critical to the invention, as it serves as the adhesive medium that will bind the lignocellulosic reinforcement. If the temperature is too high (above -10°C), dissolution is insufficient, and the cellulose remains largely undissolved, resulting in poor matrix cohesion. If the temperature is too low (below -15°C), solvent viscosity increases excessively, impeding mixing and leading to inhomogeneous gel formation. The -12.5°C threshold represents the precise balance at which cellulose chains are sufficiently solvated to form a cohesive, flowable gel while retaining enough structural memory to reassemble into a strong, interconnected network upon solvent removal.

Following the formation of the cellulose gel matrix, the lignocellulosic reinforcement material is introduced. The reinforcement is selected from osage orange wood sawdust, blue agave bagasse, or a combination thereof, and constitutes 65% to 85% of the total dry weight of the composite. Osage orange wood sawdust, derived from an invasive tree species commonly discarded in midwestern United States, is rich in lignin and contains fine particulate matter that enhances packing density and interfacial contact. Blue agave bagasse, a by-product of tequila production, consists of long, fibrous residues with high hemicellulose and lignin content, providing tensile reinforcement and structural continuity. The reinforcement materials are added in dry, unprocessed form, without any prior chemical, thermal, or mechanical pretreatment. Their introduction occurs after the cellulose gel has fully formed, ensuring that the gel can effectively encapsulate the reinforcement particles rather than dissolve them. The mixture is agitated for an additional 5 to 15 minutes at the same rotational speed to achieve uniform dispersion. During this phase, the viscous gel coats the surfaces of the lignocellulosic particles, filling interstitial voids and forming a continuous network around the reinforcement. The size and morphology of the reinforcement particles are critical: osage orange sawdust, with its fine particle size (typically less than 2 mm), provides a high surface area for gel adhesion, while agave fibers, with their length-to-diameter ratio exceeding 10:1, act as micro-reinforcing elements that bridge the gel matrix and resist crack propagation. The synergistic combination of these two materials results in a composite with superior mechanical properties compared to those composed of either reinforcement alone. The presence of lignin and hemicellulose in the reinforcement is not detrimental; rather, it contributes to the overall structural complexity and enhances interfacial interactions through hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces with the cellulose gel.

Once the mixture is homogenized, it is transferred into a mold constructed from polyethylene foam, with internal dimensions of 105 mm width by 130 mm length by 5 mm depth, and an internal cavity of 80 mm by 100 mm by 5 mm. The mold is sandwiched between two polyethylene meshes with 2 mm² openings, which serve to facilitate solvent drainage while preventing particle migration during consolidation. The mold is then subjected to a series of solvent-water exchange treatments designed to remove residual alkaline components and promote the reformation of the cellulose gel into a solid, cohesive structure. The first step involves submerging the mold under vacuum in tap water for 15 minutes, during which time the alkaline solvent is displaced by water, and the cellulose gel begins to precipitate and re-hydrogen-bond. Following this, the composite is immersed in ambient tap water for a continuous period of 24 hours, during which the water is replaced six times at intervals of approximately four hours. This prolonged washing process ensures the complete removal of lithium hydroxide, urea, and any dissolved hemicellulose or lignin fragments, while allowing the cellulose chains to reorganize into a denser, more ordered structure. The removal of the composite from the mold after the first hour of soaking is critical, as it allows for improved diffusion of solutes and prevents the formation of a dense, impermeable outer layer that could hinder internal solvent extraction. The composite becomes sufficiently rigid during this phase to be handled without deformation, yet remains pliable enough to allow for continued structural reorganization.

After washing, the composite panels are damp-dried on paper towels to remove excess surface water and then placed between two steel plates under a pressure of 0.5 psi (0.0034 MPa) to maintain dimensional stability during drying. The panels are then transferred to a vacuum oven maintained at 60°C and a pressure of 25 inches of mercury for a period of 24 hours. This low-temperature drying process allows for the slow removal of residual moisture without inducing thermal degradation or cracking. The resulting dried composite exhibits a density of approximately 0.7 to 0.9 g/cm³ and retains a porous, heterogeneous microstructure. To enhance mechanical performance and reduce porosity, the panels undergo a final densification step involving hot-pressing. The panels are subjected to a pressure of 8 tons (approximately 78.5 kN) for a duration of 10 minutes at a temperature of 180°C. This step induces plastic deformation of the cellulose gel matrix, promotes interfacial bonding between the matrix and reinforcement, and collapses residual air voids, resulting in a final composite with a density of 0.8 to 1.2 g/cm³. The hot-pressing temperature is carefully controlled: temperatures below 160°C are insufficient to induce adequate flow and bonding, while temperatures above 200°C risk thermal degradation of cellulose and lignin, leading to embrittlement and reduced mechanical strength. The combination of pressure and heat results in a composite with a smooth, uniform surface, minimal surface porosity, and significantly enhanced tensile and flexural properties.

The resulting lignocellulosic composite is characterized by a unique microstructure in which the cellulose gel forms a continuous, fibrous network that coats and interpenetrates the lignocellulosic reinforcement particles. Optical microscopy reveals that the fine osage orange sawdust particles are almost entirely encapsulated by the cellulose gel, appearing as light-colored, spherical inclusions embedded within a darker, fibrous matrix. The longer agave fibers, by contrast, protrude partially from the surface, their fibrous nature preserved and aligned along the direction of compression, contributing to anisotropic mechanical behavior. The absence of synthetic binders is confirmed by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy, which shows no evidence of carbonyl or ester peaks characteristic of resins or adhesives. Instead, the spectra exhibit strong cellulose absorption bands at 1000–1200 cm⁻¹ and lignin aromatic ring vibrations at approximately 1500 cm⁻¹, with no detectable hemicellulose peak at 1742 cm⁻¹, indicating that the alkaline treatment effectively extracts hemicellulose during processing. Thermogravimetric analysis confirms the thermal stability of the composite, with decomposition onset occurring at approximately 240°C, consistent with cellulose degradation, and ash content of less than 17% at 600°C, reflecting the inorganic content of the natural biomass. The composite exhibits a tensile strength exceeding 25 MPa, a flexural modulus greater than 3 GPa, and a water absorption rate of less than 180% after 24-hour immersion—properties that place it in direct competition with commercially available bioplastics such as polylactic acid and cellulose acetate. Furthermore, the composite is fully compostable under industrial conditions, degrading completely within 90 days without leaving microplastic residues.

The invention further encompasses variations in process parameters that allow for the tailoring of composite properties. For instance, increasing the cellulose matrix concentration from 15% to 35% results in a proportional increase in tensile strength and flexural modulus, with diminishing returns observed above 30%. This suggests that a 30% cellulose matrix represents the point of optimal mechanical efficiency, beyond which additional cellulose contributes little to performance but significantly increases material cost. Similarly, solvent volume is a critical variable: volumes below 45 mL per panel result in insufficient gel formation and poor reinforcement encapsulation, while volumes exceeding 75 mL lead to excessive dilution, weakening the gel network and reducing interfacial adhesion. The optimal solvent volume of 60 mL per panel corresponds to a solvent-to-biomass ratio of approximately 4:1, which provides sufficient solvation capacity without compromising structural integrity. The choice of alkaline agent is also pivotal: lithium hydroxide monohydrate is superior to sodium hydroxide or potassium hydroxide in generating a viscous, cohesive gel, likely due to the smaller ionic radius of lithium, which facilitates deeper penetration into cellulose fibrils and more effective disruption of hydrogen bonding networks. The inclusion of urea is essential, as it acts as a hydrogen bond disruptor and cryoprotectant, preventing ice crystal formation and enhancing solvent penetration at sub-zero temperatures.

The mechanical performance of the composite is further enhanced by the synergistic combination of osage orange sawdust and blue agave bagasse. When used individually, each reinforcement material produces composites with inferior properties: osage orange alone yields a dense but brittle material with low impact resistance, while agave bagasse alone produces a fibrous, porous composite with poor compressive strength. The binary mixture, however, combines the fine particle packing of the sawdust with the tensile reinforcement of the fibers, resulting in a composite that resists both crack initiation and propagation. This synergy is not observed in prior art, which typically employs single-source reinforcements or requires chemical modification to achieve similar effects. The invention thus represents a novel, additive-free strategy for achieving high-performance biomaterials through intelligent selection of feedstock chemistry and process control.

The method is inherently scalable, requiring no specialized equipment beyond standard laboratory mixers, molds, vacuum systems, and ovens. The raw materials—cotton, osage orange sawdust, and agave bagasse—are all low-cost, widely available, and often considered waste streams, rendering the process economically attractive. The solvent system is non-toxic, water-soluble, and can be recovered and recycled through evaporation and reconstitution, minimizing waste generation. The entire process operates at ambient pressure and sub-ambient temperatures, reducing energy consumption compared to conventional thermal pretreatment methods. The resulting composite is not only mechanically competitive with commercial bioplastics but also possesses superior environmental credentials: it is derived entirely from renewable resources, requires no chemical additives, and is fully compostable. The invention thus provides a transformative pathway for the utilization of low-value biomass residues to produce high-value, sustainable materials that meet the growing demand for eco-friendly alternatives to synthetic polymers.

### Examples

In a first example, a lignocellulosic composite was fabricated using 25% cotton (by dry weight) and 75% reinforcement composed of equal parts osage orange wood sawdust and blue agave bagasse. The cold aqueous alkaline solvent system was prepared by dissolving 4.6 grams of lithium hydroxide monohydrate and 15 grams of urea in 60 milliliters of deionized water, followed by cooling to -12.5°C in a controlled refrigerated bath. The cotton was added in flaked form (2.5 grams) and agitated for 15 minutes at 350 rpm. The reinforcement materials (3.75 grams each of osage orange sawdust and agave bagasse) were then introduced and mixed for an additional 10 minutes. The resulting mixture was transferred to a polyethylene foam mold sandwiched between two 2 mm² mesh screens and subjected to a vacuum-assisted water exchange protocol: 15 minutes under vacuum in tap water, followed by 24 hours of continuous immersion with six water replacements. After removal from the mold, the panel was damp-dried and dried under vacuum at 60°C for 24 hours. The panel was then hot-pressed at 180°C under 8 tons of pressure for 10 minutes. The final composite exhibited a density of 1.05 g/cm³, a tensile strength of 28.3 MPa, a flexural modulus of 3.4 GPa, and a water absorption of 172% after 24 hours. The composite retained its shape under ambient humidity and exhibited no delamination or cracking upon handling. FTIR analysis confirmed the absence of synthetic additives, and thermogravimetric analysis showed a single-stage decomposition profile consistent with cellulose degradation, with an ash content of 15.8% at 600°C.

In a second example, the influence of reaction temperature on composite performance was evaluated. Five composites were prepared using identical formulations (25% cotton, 75% OOW/AF, 60 mL solvent) but at different temperatures: -5°C, -7.5°C, -10°C, -12.5°C, and -15°C. Composites produced at -5°C and -7.5°C exhibited poor gel formation, resulting in brittle, crumbly structures with tensile strengths below 12 MPa. The composite produced at -10°C showed moderate improvement, with a tensile strength of 18.7 MPa, while the -12.5°C composite achieved the peak performance of 28.3 MPa. The -15°C composite exhibited slightly reduced strength (25.1 MPa), attributed to excessive solvent viscosity limiting homogeneous mixing and gel penetration. This demonstrated that -12.5°C is the optimal temperature for balancing solvation efficiency with processability.

In a third example, the effect of cellulose matrix concentration was investigated using formulations of 15%, 25%, 30%, and 35% cotton, with the remainder comprising 75%, 65%, 60%, and 55% OOW/AF, respectively. All composites were prepared at -12.5°C using 60 mL solvent. The 15% cotton composite exhibited a tensile strength of 12.1 MPa and a flexural modulus of 1.8 GPa, while the 35% cotton composite achieved 31.8 MPa and 4.7 GPa. The 30% cotton composite yielded 30.5 MPa and 4.5 GPa, indicating that the marginal gains in mechanical performance above 30% cellulose did not justify the increased material cost. The 25% formulation was selected for further optimization as the most economically viable balance of performance and cost.

In a fourth example, the effect of solvent volume was tested using volumes of 45 mL, 60 mL, 75 mL, 90 mL, and 105 mL per panel, with a fixed 25% cotton and 75% OOW/AF formulation at -12.5°C. The 45 mL and 75 mL volumes produced composites with tensile strengths of 21.5 MPa and 23.9 MPa, respectively, while the 90 mL and 105 mL volumes resulted in strengths of 15.2 MPa and 14.1 MPa. The 60 mL volume yielded the highest tensile strength (28.3 MPa), confirming that a solvent-to-biomass ratio of approximately 4:1 is optimal for gel formation without dilution-induced weakening.

In a fifth example, alternative alkaline solvent systems were tested, including 7% NaOH/12% urea, 9.8% KOH/12% urea, and 4.6% LiOH·H₂O/12% urea. The NaOH-based system produced a composite with a tensile strength of 19.4 MPa, while the KOH-based system yielded 17.8 MPa. The LiOH·H₂O/15% urea system, as described in the primary example, achieved 28.3 MPa, demonstrating the superiority of lithium hydroxide in generating a cohesive, high-strength gel matrix. The lower performance of sodium and potassium hydroxide is attributed to their larger ionic radii, which limit penetration into cellulose fibrils and result in less effective hydrogen bond disruption.

In a sixth example, composites were fabricated using 100% cotton, 100% osage orange sawdust, 100% agave bagasse, and binary mixtures of 25% cotton with 75% osage orange sawdust or 25% cotton with 75% agave bagasse. The 100% cotton composite exhibited a tensile strength of 27.1 MPa, comparable to the 25% cotton/OOW/AF composite. The 100% osage orange sawdust and 100% agave bagasse composites failed to form coherent structures, collapsing into loose aggregates upon drying. The 25% cotton/75% osage orange composite achieved 22.6 MPa, while the 25% cotton/75% agave composite yielded only 16.3 MPa. The binary reinforcement (OOW/AF) composite, however, achieved 28.3 MPa, demonstrating the synergistic effect of combining fine particulate and fibrous reinforcement.

In a seventh example, the dimensional stability of the composite was assessed by immersing panels in water for 24 hours. The composite absorbed 172% of its dry weight in water and expanded in thickness by 81%. This high water uptake indicates that the composite is not inherently waterproof and would require surface coating for outdoor or moisture-prone applications. However, the absorption was reversible, and the composite retained its mechanical integrity upon drying, suggesting potential for use in temporary or controlled-environment applications.

In an eighth example, the composite was subjected to accelerated composting conditions in accordance with ASTM D5338, using a thermophilic microbial inoculum at 58°C and 60% humidity. After 60 days, the composite exhibited 89% mass loss, and after 90 days, complete disintegration was observed, with no residual fragments detectable by microscopy. This confirms the material’s full biodegradability under industrial composting conditions.

### Exemplary Embodiments

In a first exemplary embodiment, the lignocellulosic composite is used as a biodegradable packaging material for food products, replacing polystyrene foam trays or plastic clamshells. The composite is molded into tray shapes using custom dies, with a thickness of 3 to 5 mm, and is coated with a thin, water-resistant, food-grade cellulose derivative such as methylcellulose or carboxymethylcellulose to reduce water absorption. The composite exhibits sufficient rigidity to support the weight of fruits, vegetables, or baked goods, and its natural, fibrous texture provides an aesthetic appeal consistent with sustainable branding. Upon disposal, the packaging decomposes in municipal composting facilities within 90 days, leaving no microplastic residue.

In a second exemplary embodiment, the composite is employed as a substrate for agricultural seedling pots or planters. The composite is formed into cylindrical or rectangular containers with perforated bottoms to facilitate root penetration and drainage. The material’s natural porosity allows for air and water exchange, promoting healthy root development, while its biodegradability eliminates the need for removal and disposal after transplanting. The composite degrades in soil within 6 to 12 months, enriching the substrate with organic matter and reducing plastic waste in horticultural applications.

In a third exemplary embodiment, the composite is utilized in the production of disposable tableware, including plates, bowls, and cutlery. The material is injection-molded or thermoformed into desired shapes and surface-treated with a natural wax or plant-based polymer to enhance water resistance and durability during use. The resulting products are lightweight, heat-resistant up to 100°C, and fully compostable, offering a sustainable alternative to single-use plastic or paper-based tableware that often contains PFAS or polyethylene coatings.

In a fourth exemplary embodiment, the composite is used as an interior paneling material for non-load-bearing applications in green building construction. Panels are produced in standard dimensions of 1.2 m × 2.4 m and thicknesses of 5 to 10 mm, with a surface finish achieved through sanding and coating with a natural linseed oil or beeswax sealant. The composite provides thermal insulation, acoustic damping, and aesthetic appeal through its natural grain and coloration. Its low density reduces structural load, and its biodegradability at end-of-life aligns with circular economy principles in sustainable architecture.

In a fifth exemplary embodiment, the composite is employed as a core material in sandwich panels for lightweight, non-structural applications such as signage, display stands, or partition walls. The lignocellulosic composite forms the central core, which is laminated between thin layers of biodegradable biopolymer films or cellulose-based paper to enhance surface hardness and moisture resistance. The resulting sandwich structure exhibits improved flexural rigidity while maintaining a low overall weight and full compostability.

In a sixth exemplary embodiment, the composite is used as a filler material in 3D printing filaments for biodegradable additive manufacturing. The composite is ground into fine particles and blended with a thermoplastic biopolymer such as polylactic acid at a concentration of 20% to 40% by weight. The filament is extruded and printed into complex geometries, with the lignocellulosic component enhancing stiffness and reducing shrinkage during cooling. Upon disposal, the printed object degrades in composting environments, leaving behind only organic residues.

In a seventh exemplary embodiment, the composite is incorporated into the production of biodegradable mulch films for agricultural use. The composite is extruded into thin sheets (0.1 to 0.3 mm thickness) and embossed with a textured surface to promote soil contact and moisture retention. The film suppresses weed growth, retains soil moisture, and degrades within a single growing season, eliminating the need for plastic mulch removal and reducing soil contamination.

In an eighth exemplary embodiment, the composite is used as a substrate for fungal-based biofabrication. The composite is inoculated with mycelium cultures, which grow through the porous structure, forming a hybrid mycelium-lignocellulosic composite with enhanced mechanical strength and water resistance. The resulting material is used in packaging, insulation, or furniture applications, with the mycelium acting as a natural binder and protective layer.

In a ninth exemplary embodiment, the composite is employed in the manufacture of biodegradable acoustic panels for interior sound absorption. The composite is formed into porous, perforated panels with varying thicknesses and densities, tuned to absorb specific frequency ranges. The natural fibrous structure provides excellent sound-dampening properties, and the material’s biodegradability makes it ideal for temporary installations in theaters, studios, or public spaces.

In a tenth exemplary embodiment, the composite is used as a base material for biodegradable paper products, such as notebooks, folders, or packaging envelopes. The composite is ground into a pulp, reformed into sheets, and calendared to produce a smooth, printable surface. The resulting paper exhibits higher tear resistance and water resistance than conventional paper, while remaining fully compostable and free of synthetic additives.
