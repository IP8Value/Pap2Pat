# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of radiation oncology and more specifically to a novel method and system for delivering spatially fractionated radiotherapy in three dimensions within a tumor volume, known as LATTICE Radiotherapy (LRT). This invention encompasses a precise, clinically implementable technique for generating a three-dimensional array of high-dose radiation islands—each exceeding 12 Gy—distributed at defined spatial intervals within the target tumor volume, while simultaneously minimizing radiation exposure to surrounding healthy tissues and critical organs. The invention further includes the associated treatment planning algorithms, delivery protocols, and quality assurance methodologies required to reliably reproduce the peak-to-valley dose gradient essential for triggering the biological effects observed in spatially fractionated radiation. The LATTICE Radiotherapy technique is designed to be compatible with existing clinical radiotherapy platforms, including linear accelerators equipped with dynamic multileaf collimators, robotic stereotactic systems such as CyberKnife, and proton or heavy ion beam delivery systems with spot-scanning capabilities. The invention is particularly applicable to the treatment of bulky, hypoxic, or radioresistant solid tumors, where conventional uniform-dose radiotherapy has demonstrated limited efficacy. By concentrating high-dose regions exclusively within the tumor boundaries and ensuring rapid dose fall-off between these regions, LATTICE Radiotherapy enables a paradigm shift in radiation delivery—moving from homogeneous dose distributions to controlled, heterogeneous, lattice-based dose architectures that exploit the bystander and abscopal effects to enhance tumor control without increasing normal tissue toxicity. The invention further enables the use of LATTICE Radiotherapy as an induction modality prior to standard fractionated radiotherapy or systemic therapy, thereby amplifying the therapeutic window and potentially improving survival outcomes in patients with advanced malignancies.

## BACKGROUND

Historically, spatially fractionated radiotherapy has been employed in the form of two-dimensional GRID therapy, a technique first introduced in the 1930s using orthovoltage X-ray machines and physical grids composed of alternating shielded and open regions. These grids, typically featuring circular or square apertures ranging from 0.5 to 1.5 cm in diameter and spaced 1.5 to 2 cm apart, were used to deliver non-uniform radiation fields to large, bulky tumors that were otherwise considered untreatable with conventional radiotherapy due to their size or proximity to critical structures. The rationale for this approach was based on the observation that, despite delivering only partial coverage of the tumor volume with high-dose radiation, significant tumor regressions were frequently observed, often exceeding those achieved with uniformly irradiated fields. This phenomenon was attributed to the preservation of viable skin and subcutaneous tissue surrounding the high-dose regions, which facilitated rapid repair and reduced the incidence of severe skin reactions. Over time, the technique was adapted to megavoltage linear accelerators, but the fundamental geometry remained two-dimensional, with high-dose beams passing vertically through the tumor and exposing large volumes of superficial normal tissue to unnecessarily high radiation doses. The most significant limitation of traditional GRID therapy was its inability to confine the high-dose regions to the tumor volume; instead, the highest radiation intensities often occurred at the skin surface or in non-targeted tissues anterior to the tumor, resulting in suboptimal therapeutic ratios and limiting its broader clinical adoption.

In parallel with advances in radiotherapy delivery technology, radiobiological research has revealed that the efficacy of spatially fractionated radiation is not solely attributable to direct cell killing within the high-dose regions. Rather, a complex interplay of biological phenomena—including the bystander effect, abscopal responses, and immune-mediated tumor regression—appears to be central to the mechanism of action. Cells located in the low-dose regions between the high-dose islands, which receive insufficient radiation to cause direct DNA damage, nonetheless undergo apoptosis or senescence due to signaling molecules released by irradiated neighboring cells. These signals include tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), TRAIL, and ceramide, which propagate cell death cascades through gap junctions and extracellular vesicles, affecting both epithelial and endothelial compartments of the tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that GRID therapy can induce systemic immune activation, leading to regression of non-irradiated metastatic lesions—a phenomenon known as the abscopal effect. These findings indicate that the therapeutic benefit of spatially fractionated radiation is not merely a function of physical dose distribution but is deeply rooted in the spatial organization of radiation exposure and its subsequent biological consequences.

Despite these compelling biological insights, the clinical translation of spatially fractionated radiotherapy has been hindered by technical limitations. Conventional 2D GRID techniques cannot be easily integrated into modern radiotherapy workflows, which rely on three-dimensional treatment planning, image guidance, and motion management systems. Moreover, the superficial nature of the high-dose regions in 2D GRID exposes large volumes of normal tissue to unnecessary radiation, increasing the risk of acute and late toxicities. As a result, GRID therapy has remained a niche, rarely used modality, primarily reserved for palliative cases in which no other options exist. The advent of intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), robotic stereotactic radiosurgery, and particle beam therapy has created new opportunities to overcome these limitations. These technologies enable the precise sculpting of radiation dose distributions in three dimensions, allowing for the creation of highly localized, non-overlapping high-dose regions within the tumor volume while maintaining steep dose gradients between them. However, no prior system or method has been developed to systematically exploit this capability to generate a three-dimensional lattice of high-dose vertices, each exceeding 12 Gy, with controlled spacing and minimal dose spillage into surrounding tissues.

Previous attempts to create heterogeneous dose distributions in radiotherapy have focused on dose painting, dose escalation to subvolumes, or boosting regions of hypoxia. These approaches, while sophisticated, aim to increase the uniformity of tumor coverage or target specific biological subregions. In contrast, the present invention is fundamentally distinct in that it deliberately creates a deliberately heterogeneous, periodic, and non-uniform dose architecture—where the low-dose regions are not merely incidental but are essential components of the therapeutic strategy. The goal is not to eliminate the valleys but to preserve them as functional conduits for biological signaling. No prior patent, publication, or clinical protocol has described a method for generating a three-dimensional array of high-dose islands within a tumor volume, where the spatial separation between islands is optimized to maximize the peak-to-valley dose ratio while ensuring that all high-dose regions are confined strictly within the clinical target volume. Furthermore, no prior system has been developed to integrate this dose architecture into a standardized, reproducible, and clinically viable workflow that can be implemented on commercial radiotherapy platforms without requiring hardware modifications. The present invention fills this critical gap by providing a comprehensive framework for the planning, delivery, verification, and clinical application of three-dimensional LATTICE Radiotherapy as a standalone or induction modality, thereby transforming spatially fractionated radiotherapy from an outdated, poorly understood technique into a precision oncology tool grounded in modern physics, biology, and engineering.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a novel method and system for delivering three-dimensional LATTICE Radiotherapy (LRT), a form of spatially fractionated radiotherapy in which a tumor volume is irradiated with a precisely controlled array of high-dose radiation islands, each exceeding 12 Gray, separated by low-dose regions of 3 centiGray or less, such that the entire dose distribution is confined within the boundaries of the clinical target volume. The invention enables the creation of a three-dimensional lattice of dose peaks with rapid fall-off between them, thereby maximizing the peak-to-valley dose ratio while minimizing exposure to surrounding healthy tissues. This is achieved through the use of existing clinical radiotherapy platforms—including linear accelerators equipped with dynamic multileaf collimators, robotic stereotactic systems, and proton or heavy ion beam delivery systems with spot-scanning capabilities—without requiring any hardware modifications. The method involves the computational design of a three-dimensional lattice structure composed of discrete high-dose vertices, each corresponding to a focal point of radiation delivery, with spatial separation between vertices determined by the size of the radiation field, the desired peak-to-valley ratio, and the anatomical constraints of the tumor and adjacent organs. The number, location, and intensity of these vertices are optimized using treatment planning software to ensure that the cumulative dose distribution achieves a minimum of 12 Gy at each vertex, with valley doses below 3 cGy, and that the entire high-dose region is contained within the target volume. The delivery of the LATTICE plan is accomplished through a sequence of non-coplanar, non-overlapping radiation beams or arcs, each directed toward a specific vertex, with real-time image guidance and motion tracking employed to ensure submillimeter accuracy. The invention further includes a quality assurance protocol involving three-dimensional dosimetric verification using radiochromic film or detector arrays, with tolerance thresholds of 3% dose difference and 1 millimeter distance-to-agreement between planned and delivered dose distributions. The LATTICE Radiotherapy technique is intended for use as a primary treatment modality for bulky, hypoxic, or radioresistant tumors, or as an induction therapy preceding conventional fractionated radiotherapy or systemic chemotherapy. The biological rationale for this approach is based on the induction of bystander and abscopal effects, wherein cells in the low-dose valleys are killed not by direct radiation damage but by signaling molecules released from irradiated high-dose regions, leading to widespread tumor cell death and immune activation. The invention provides a clinically feasible, reproducible, and scalable solution that transforms spatially fractionated radiotherapy from a historical curiosity into a modern, evidence-based oncology intervention with the potential to significantly improve local control and survival outcomes in patients with previously untreatable malignancies.

## DESCRIPTION OF INVENTION

The invention comprises a comprehensive system and method for the delivery of three-dimensional LATTICE Radiotherapy, a novel approach to spatially fractionated radiation that fundamentally redefines the relationship between physical dose distribution and biological response in tumor treatment. The core innovation lies in the deliberate and precise generation of a three-dimensional array of high-dose radiation islands, each exceeding 12 Gray, distributed at controlled spatial intervals within the boundaries of the clinical target volume, with intervening low-dose regions maintained at or below 3 centiGray. This architecture is designed not merely to deliver radiation to the tumor, but to orchestrate a complex biological cascade through the spatial segregation of high and low radiation doses. The method begins with the delineation of the gross tumor volume and the identification of critical organs at risk using standard imaging modalities such as computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, or positron emission tomography. A three-dimensional computational model of the tumor volume is then generated, and a lattice structure is algorithmically defined within this volume, consisting of discrete vertices corresponding to the centers of high-dose regions. The number of vertices is determined by the size and shape of the tumor, with a minimum of two and a maximum of twenty or more vertices, depending on tumor volume and anatomical constraints. Each vertex is assigned a target dose of at least 12 Gray, with the spatial separation between adjacent vertices optimized to ensure that the dose fall-off between them results in valley doses no greater than 3 centiGray. This separation is not arbitrary but is calculated based on the effective radius of the radiation beam’s high-dose region, the penumbra characteristics of the delivery system, and the desired peak-to-valley dose ratio, which must exceed 10:1 to ensure biological efficacy. The lattice structure is not required to exhibit geometric symmetry or regularity; rather, it is tailored to the individual tumor morphology, allowing for irregular, non-uniform arrangements that conform to the tumor’s contours and avoid critical structures such as the spinal cord, esophagus, bowel, or major vasculature.

Once the lattice structure is defined, a treatment plan is generated using commercially available treatment planning systems, such as Eclipse, Pinnacle, or Monaco, without requiring any software modifications. The plan is constructed by assigning a series of non-coplanar radiation beams or arcs to each vertex, with each beam or arc directed to deliver a portion of the total dose to its corresponding vertex. In the case of a linear accelerator equipped with a dynamic multileaf collimator, multiple volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) arcs are employed, with each arc configured to deliver a unique beamlet pattern that converges on a specific vertex. The multileaf collimator is dynamically modulated during gantry rotation to shape the radiation field such that the cumulative dose from all arcs results in the desired lattice pattern. Alternatively, for robotic stereotactic systems such as CyberKnife, a series of non-coplanar beams, each originating from a distinct direction and focused on a single vertex, are delivered sequentially. Each beam is collimated to a small field size, typically less than 10 millimeters in diameter, to ensure high spatial precision and rapid dose fall-off. The number of beams per vertex may vary between 40 and 60, depending on the desired dose conformity and the need to minimize dose to surrounding tissues. In all cases, real-time image guidance and motion tracking systems—such as Synchrony, Calypso, or respiratory gating—are employed to ensure that the position of the tumor remains stable during delivery, thereby preserving the integrity of the peak-to-valley dose gradient. The entire treatment is delivered in a single session, with total treatment time typically ranging from 30 to 90 minutes, depending on the number of vertices and the complexity of the beam arrangement.

The biological rationale underlying this method is grounded in the observation that spatially fractionated radiation induces a cascade of intercellular signaling events that extend far beyond the directly irradiated regions. Cells located within the high-dose islands are subjected to lethal levels of radiation, resulting in DNA double-strand breaks, mitochondrial dysfunction, and the release of damage-associated molecular patterns. These signals trigger the production and secretion of cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha, TRAIL, and ceramide, which diffuse through the tumor microenvironment and activate apoptotic pathways in neighboring cells that receive only low-dose radiation. This bystander effect is not limited to direct cell-to-cell contact but can propagate through extracellular vesicles and soluble factors, leading to widespread tumor cell death even in regions that received negligible direct radiation. Furthermore, the destruction of tumor vasculature within the high-dose regions leads to hypoxia and acidosis in the surrounding areas, which further sensitizes tumor cells to apoptosis. The low-dose valleys are not merely passive regions but are active participants in the therapeutic mechanism, serving as conduits for the propagation of biological signals. The spatial separation between high-dose islands is critical: if the islands are too close, the valleys become saturated with radiation, eliminating the gradient and diminishing the bystander effect; if they are too far apart, the signaling molecules may not reach sufficient numbers of cells to elicit a systemic response. The invention defines the optimal separation as being sufficient to maintain valley doses below 3 centiGray while ensuring that the diffusion range of signaling molecules can bridge the gap between islands. This separation is typically in the range of 1.5 to 3.0 centimeters, depending on tumor size and tissue density.

The invention further includes a robust quality assurance protocol to ensure the accuracy and reproducibility of the LATTICE delivery. A three-dimensional dosimetric phantom, such as a solid water phantom embedded with radiochromic film or a detector array, is used to verify the planned dose distribution. The phantom is irradiated with the same parameters used in the patient plan, and the resulting dose distribution is measured and compared to the treatment planning system’s calculation. Agreement is considered acceptable if the dose difference between planned and measured values is within 3% and the spatial displacement of dose features is less than 1 millimeter. This level of accuracy is achieved through the use of high-resolution imaging, precise beam modeling, and real-time motion management. The system also includes a verification algorithm that cross-checks the position of each vertex against the planned coordinates using cone-beam CT or stereoscopic imaging prior to each treatment fraction. In the event of a discrepancy exceeding 2 millimeters, the treatment is automatically paused and the plan is re-evaluated. The invention further encompasses a clinical workflow that integrates LATTICE Radiotherapy into standard oncology practice, including patient selection criteria, simulation procedures, treatment planning protocols, delivery schedules, and follow-up imaging. LATTICE Radiotherapy may be administered as a standalone treatment for patients with inoperable, bulky tumors, or as an induction therapy prior to conventional fractionated radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or immunotherapy. In the latter case, the LATTICE session is delivered first, followed by a conventional course of radiotherapy 7 to 14 days later, allowing time for the biological effects of the LATTICE treatment to manifest and sensitize the tumor to subsequent therapy. This sequential approach has been shown in preclinical models to significantly enhance tumor control compared to either modality alone.

The invention is not limited to photon-based delivery systems. It is equally applicable to proton and heavy ion beam therapy systems equipped with spot-scanning nozzles. In this configuration, the treatment planning system defines a set of discrete Bragg peak locations corresponding to the lattice vertices. The scanning nozzle is then programmed to deliver a series of pencil beams, each targeted to a specific vertex, with the energy and intensity of each beam adjusted to deposit the required dose at the desired depth. The Bragg peak’s sharp distal fall-off ensures that the high-dose region is confined to the vertex location with minimal exit dose, further enhancing the peak-to-valley ratio. This approach is particularly advantageous for deep-seated tumors, such as those in the pancreas or liver, where photon-based techniques may struggle to achieve sufficient dose conformity. The invention also includes a method for adapting the lattice structure to tumors that exhibit internal motion, such as those in the lung or liver, by incorporating four-dimensional imaging and motion-adaptive planning. In these cases, the lattice vertices are defined not as static points but as dynamic trajectories that account for respiratory motion, ensuring that the high-dose regions remain confined to the tumor throughout the breathing cycle. The system further includes a machine learning algorithm that predicts the optimal lattice configuration based on tumor volume, location, histology, and prior treatment history, enabling personalized treatment planning with minimal manual intervention.

The clinical implementation of this invention represents a paradigm shift in radiotherapy. Rather than attempting to uniformly irradiate the entire tumor volume, the LATTICE approach deliberately creates a mosaic of high and low dose regions, leveraging the tumor’s own biology to amplify the therapeutic effect. This method is not dependent on tumor radiosensitivity, making it effective against hypoxic, quiescent, or radioresistant cells that are typically refractory to conventional radiotherapy. It is also inherently safe, as the majority of the tumor volume receives only low-dose radiation, minimizing the risk of acute toxicity. The technique is fully compatible with existing infrastructure, requires no new hardware, and can be implemented in any clinic with modern linear accelerators or robotic radiosurgery systems. The invention thus provides a practical, scalable, and biologically grounded solution to one of the most persistent challenges in radiation oncology: how to effectively treat large, complex, and resistant tumors without increasing normal tissue toxicity.

## EQUIVALENTS

It is understood that various modifications and adaptations of the disclosed invention may be made without departing from the essential scope and spirit of the invention. For instance, while the preferred embodiment utilizes dynamic multileaf collimators, robotic stereotactic systems, or proton spot-scanning nozzles for delivery, equivalent results may be achieved using other advanced radiation delivery platforms that permit precise spatial control of radiation beams, including but not limited to linear accelerators with micro-multileaf collimators, compact proton therapy systems with pencil beam scanning, or even future-generation adaptive radiotherapy systems incorporating real-time MRI guidance. The invention is not limited to any specific energy level or beam type; it is equally applicable to photons, electrons, protons, carbon ions, or other charged particles, provided that the delivery system can generate localized high-dose regions with rapid fall-off and submillimeter accuracy. The definition of the high-dose threshold may be adjusted based on clinical context; while 12 Gray is specified as the minimum dose per vertex for optimal biological effect, equivalent outcomes may be achieved with higher or lower thresholds, such as 10 Gray or 15 Gray, depending on tumor type, size, and radiosensitivity, as long as the peak-to-valley dose ratio remains above 10:1 and valley doses remain below 3 centiGray. Similarly, the spatial separation between vertices may be varied within a range of 1.0 to 4.0 centimeters, depending on the tumor’s anatomical constraints and the size of the radiation field, without altering the fundamental principle of the invention. The number of vertices may be increased or decreased based on tumor volume, with a minimum of two vertices required to establish a peak-to-valley gradient and a maximum determined by the physical limits of beam delivery and planning system capabilities. The invention encompasses not only the delivery of the LATTICE pattern as a single fraction but also its delivery in multiple fractions, with each fraction targeting a subset of the vertices, thereby allowing for dose escalation while preserving normal tissue sparing. The use of real-time motion tracking, respiratory gating, or tumor tracking systems such as Synchrony or Calypso is considered an essential component of the invention, but equivalent motion management techniques, including breath-hold protocols, abdominal compression, or electromagnetic transponder arrays, may be substituted without departing from the invention’s scope. The dosimetric verification protocol using radiochromic film or detector arrays may be replaced by alternative methods such as liquid ionization chambers, diamond detectors, or Monte Carlo-based dose reconstruction, provided that the accuracy of the measurement meets the specified tolerance of 3% dose difference and 1 millimeter spatial agreement. The invention further encompasses the use of artificial intelligence algorithms to automate the generation of the lattice structure, including machine learning models trained on historical treatment outcomes to predict the optimal number, location, and dose of vertices based on tumor imaging features, histopathological markers, or patient-specific biomarkers. The LATTICE Radiotherapy technique may be combined with systemic therapies such as chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or targeted agents, either before, during, or after the radiation delivery, with the timing and sequence optimized to maximize synergistic biological effects. The invention is not limited to any specific tumor type and is applicable to any solid malignancy that can be visualized and contoured on imaging, including but not limited to lung, pancreatic, liver, prostate, head and neck, and sarcomas. The method may be adapted for use in pediatric patients, re-irradiation scenarios, or palliative settings, with dose constraints adjusted according to clinical need. The invention further includes the use of the LATTICE technique as a neoadjuvant or adjuvant therapy in combination with surgery, with the radiation delivered preoperatively to reduce tumor burden or postoperatively to eliminate microscopic residual disease. The term “lattice” as used herein is not meant to imply geometric regularity or symmetry; any arrangement of high-dose vertices that results in a spatially heterogeneous, peak-to-valley dose distribution confined within the target volume falls within the scope of the invention. The invention also encompasses the use of non-spherical high-dose regions, such as ellipsoidal, cylindrical, or irregularly shaped dose volumes, as long as the dose gradient between adjacent regions meets the specified criteria. The invention further includes the integration of the LATTICE delivery system with electronic health records, treatment management software, and radiation oncology information systems to enable seamless workflow, automated documentation, and longitudinal outcome tracking. All such variations, adaptations, and substitutions that achieve the same functional result—namely, the creation of a three-dimensional, spatially fractionated radiation pattern with high-dose islands and low-dose valleys confined within the tumor volume—are considered equivalents of the invention and are intended to be encompassed within the scope of the claims.
