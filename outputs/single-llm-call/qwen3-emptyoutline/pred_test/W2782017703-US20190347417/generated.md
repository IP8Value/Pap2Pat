# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Modern system-on-chip (SoC) architectures integrate a multitude of intellectual property (IP) cores, both digital and analog, to fulfill complex functional requirements while minimizing development time and cost. To accelerate design cycles and reduce engineering overhead, SoC integrators routinely procure a significant portion of these IP cores from third-party vendors, who supply pre-designed, pre-verified components ranging from processors and memory controllers to cryptographic accelerators and communication interfaces. This widespread reliance on externally sourced IP introduces a critical vulnerability: the potential for malicious modifications, commonly referred to as hardware Trojans, to be embedded within these components during their design or fabrication phase. Unlike software-based threats, hardware Trojans are physically instantiated in silicon and can remain dormant for extended periods, activating only under very specific, rarely encountered conditions that are deliberately engineered to evade conventional testing and verification procedures. Once triggered, these Trojans may exfiltrate sensitive data such as cryptographic keys, alter control flow to enable unauthorized access, degrade system reliability through intermittent failures, or induce denial-of-service behaviors—all while maintaining the functional integrity of the legitimate circuitry under normal operating conditions. The stealthiness of such attacks stems from their minimal footprint, low activation probability, and isolation from the primary functionality of the IP core, making them virtually invisible during standard functional simulation, formal verification, or structural analysis.

Traditional approaches to Trojan detection have sought to identify anomalies through structural metrics, functional simulation, formal verification, information flow tracking, or runtime monitoring. Structural analysis techniques, such as unused circuit identification, attempt to flag logic elements that remain unactivated during simulation, under the assumption that malicious circuits would remain inactive during normal operation. However, sophisticated Trojans are designed to activate only under conditions that are either extremely rare or require a specific sequence of inputs, rendering such techniques ineffective. Functional analysis methods, including those that identify nets with weak input-output dependencies or those not driven by legitimate inputs, similarly fail when the Trojan’s trigger mechanism is embedded within a complex finite state machine or counter-based circuit that only activates after thousands or even millions of clock cycles. Formal verification techniques, while powerful in proving properties over bounded execution paths, are fundamentally limited by the number of clock cycles they can unroll during model checking. An adversary can easily circumvent these methods by inserting non-resettable registers and extending the trigger condition beyond the verification horizon, thereby ensuring that the malicious behavior remains undetected. Information flow tracking methods, such as gate-level information flow tracking (GLIFT), attempt to trace the propagation of sensitive data by labeling bits as tainted and monitoring their movement through Boolean gates. However, these methods suffer from taint explosion in designs that incorporate scan-based test structures, where the connection of flip-flops into long scan chains causes the taint to propagate uncontrollably across the entire chain, rendering the technique unusable in industrially relevant designs. Furthermore, GLIFT cannot distinguish between legitimate and malicious information flows when the Trojan leaks data through the same output ports used by the intended functionality—such as leaking a cryptographic key through the ciphertext output in an encryption module. Commercial tools like JasperGold Security Path Verification offer advanced path sensitization to detect unauthorized data flows, but they require precise knowledge of internal signals to monitor, which is often unavailable to SoC integrators who receive third-party IP in black-box form. Similarly, proof-carrying code and signal sensitivity tracing techniques rely on assumptions about how sensitive data should propagate through a design, assumptions that can be intentionally violated by attackers who design Trojans to preserve the expected sensitivity profile while still enabling covert data leakage through bypass paths or side channels.

The limitations of existing methods are not merely technical shortcomings—they represent systemic vulnerabilities in the current SoC supply chain. Third-party vendors, including those responsible for inserting design-for-test (DfT) structures such as scan chains and built-in self-test (BIST) logic, often operate with full access to the netlist and can embed Trojans within these structures without triggering suspicion. These DfT-inserted Trojans are particularly insidious because they exploit the very mechanisms intended to improve testability and reliability, turning them into covert channels for malicious activity. Moreover, the lack of a trusted golden reference model for each purchased IP core renders comparison-based detection methods infeasible. As a result, there exists a critical gap in the ability to verify the security of third-party IP prior to integration into a final SoC design. Current techniques either lack the precision to distinguish benign from malicious behavior, are constrained by computational limits, or require assumptions about internal design details that are not available to the SoC integrator. This creates an environment in which hardware Trojans can be introduced with high confidence of evasion, posing a severe threat to the security of critical systems in defense, finance, healthcare, and infrastructure domains. There is therefore an urgent need for a detection framework that operates without reliance on golden references, is resilient to trigger delays and state complexity, can function effectively on DfT-inserted netlists, and can distinguish between legitimate and illicit information flows even when they share the same output ports. The invention described herein addresses these challenges by introducing a novel paradigm for Trojan detection grounded in information flow security verification, leveraging automatic test pattern generation to detect violations of confidentiality and integrity policies through the modeling of sensitive assets as faults and systematically identifying unauthorized propagation and control paths.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive framework for the detection of hardware Trojans in third-party intellectual property (IP) cores without requiring access to a golden reference model or detailed knowledge of the internal design structure. This framework operates at the gate-level netlist stage of the SoC design flow and is applicable to both soft and firm IP cores delivered in synthesizable or gate-level formats. It is designed to be integrated into the standard verification flow of an SoC integrator and leverages commercially available automatic test pattern generation (ATPG) tools to identify violations of information flow security (IFS) policies—specifically, breaches of confidentiality and integrity—that are indicative of the presence of a hardware Trojan. The core innovation lies in the transformation of the problem of Trojan detection into a fault detection problem, wherein a sensitive asset, such as a cryptographic key bit or a control register value, is modeled as a stuck-at-0 or stuck-at-1 fault. By applying sequential ATPG techniques to propagate this fault through the circuit, the framework identifies all possible paths through which the asset can be observed or controlled, thereby revealing unauthorized information flows that would otherwise remain hidden. This approach fundamentally departs from prior methods by not relying on functional simulation, bounded model checking, or taint propagation, but instead on the physical and logical controllability and observability of signals within the netlist, which are rigorously and exhaustively explored using established industrial ATPG algorithms.

The framework is structured into three primary components: Confidentiality Verification, Integrity Verification, and Trigger Condition Extraction. Each component operates sequentially and is implemented using a combination of standard EDA tools and custom scripting, ensuring compatibility with existing design flows and minimizing the need for specialized hardware or proprietary software. The process begins with the synthesis of the third-party IP core into a gate-level netlist using a standard synthesis tool such as Synopsys Design Compiler. This netlist, along with the associated technology library, forms the input to the framework. The first step in both Confidentiality and Integrity Verification is the dynamic insertion of scan capability into all flip-flops within the design. This is achieved using the “What If” analysis capabilities of commercial ATPG tools such as Synopsys Tetramax, which allow for the temporary addition of scan chains without requiring a full re-synthesis of the design. This enables the framework to treat every flip-flop as both a potential control point and an observe point, thereby maximizing the scope of the analysis. The use of partial-scan ATPG, rather than full-scan or full-sequential ATPG, is critical to the efficiency and scalability of the framework. Full-scan ATPG is limited in its ability to propagate faults beyond the first level of flip-flops, while full-sequential ATPG suffers from exponential complexity due to the need to search through vast sequences of input stimuli. The partial-scan approach strikes a balance by enabling multi-level fault propagation while maintaining tractable computational complexity through selective scan chain insertion and removal during the analysis process.

In Confidentiality Verification, the objective is to determine whether a sensitive asset—such as a bit of a cryptographic key stored in a register or a net carrying a secret value—can be observed through any unauthorized output port or internal signal. The algorithm begins by selecting a specific asset and identifying all primary outputs and scan flip-flops that lie within its fan-out cone. For each such observe point, a capture mask is applied to isolate the propagation path from the asset to that specific point. The asset is then injected as a stuck-at-0 fault, and sequential ATPG is invoked to determine whether a test pattern can be generated that causes the fault to propagate to the observe point. The same process is repeated with the asset modeled as a stuck-at-1 fault. If both fault values can be successfully observed at the point, it is concluded that there exists a direct and controllable information flow from the asset to the observe point. The framework then recursively explores the fan-out cone of each identified observe point, removing the scan capability from that point to allow the fault to propagate further through combinational logic into subsequent flip-flops. This iterative process continues until all reachable observe points have been explored or until the ATPG engine fails to generate patterns due to excessive sequential depth. The output of this phase is a comprehensive list of all observe points through which the asset can be observed, along with the corresponding stimulus vectors required to trigger the propagation and the detailed path of signal transitions that enable it. To distinguish between legitimate and malicious observe points, the framework employs an Intersect Analysis technique. Legitimate observe points are those that are part of the intended functionality—for example, the ciphertext output of an encryption module. The fan-in cones of these legitimate observe points are analyzed to determine which internal signals and flip-flops are involved in their computation. Any observe point identified during the ATPG phase that is not contained within the fan-in cone of a legitimate output is flagged as a malicious observe point. This indicates the presence of a bypass path or a covert channel that has been inserted to exfiltrate the asset. Furthermore, the depth of the propagation path—measured as the number of logic gates traversed—is used as an additional discriminator. Legitimate paths typically involve multiple stages of processing, such as substitution boxes, permutation layers, and mixing operations in cryptographic algorithms, resulting in long propagation paths. In contrast, Trojan-induced leakage paths are often direct and minimal, bypassing intermediate logic and resulting in significantly shorter paths. This distinction allows the framework to detect Type I Trojans, which leak data through legitimate outputs via bypass paths, as well as Type II Trojans, which use entirely separate, functionally isolated circuits to exfiltrate data.

Integrity Verification operates in the reverse direction, seeking to determine whether an asset can be influenced or controlled by unauthorized inputs. Here, the asset is again modeled as a stuck-at fault, but the goal is to identify all control points—primary inputs or scan flip-flops—from which the fault can be activated and propagated backward through the circuit. The algorithm identifies all flip-flops and primary inputs within the fan-in cone of the asset and applies sequential ATPG to determine whether a stimulus sequence can be generated to activate the fault at the asset location. For each successful activation, the control point is recorded, along with the stimulus vector and the path of signal transitions that enabled the control. The framework then recursively explores the fan-in cone of each identified control point, removing scan capability to allow backward propagation through combinational logic into earlier stages of the circuit. This process continues until all reachable control points have been identified or until ATPG fails to generate patterns. The resulting list of control points is then analyzed using a complementary Intersect Analysis technique. Legitimate control points are those that are known to influence the asset under normal operation—for example, the program counter in a microprocessor or the key input in a cryptographic module. The fan-out cones of these legitimate control points are analyzed to determine which signals and flip-flops they directly or indirectly affect. Any control point identified by the ATPG phase that lies outside the fan-out cone of a legitimate control source is flagged as a malicious control point. This indicates that an unauthorized circuit has been inserted to manipulate the asset, potentially enabling an adversary to alter control flow, overwrite keys, or disable security mechanisms. As with Confidentiality Verification, the depth of the activation path is used to distinguish between legitimate and Trojan-induced control paths. Legitimate control paths typically involve multiple stages of logic, such as instruction decode, register file access, and pipeline forwarding, resulting in long and complex paths. Trojan-induced paths, in contrast, are often direct and minimal, bypassing intermediate logic and enabling immediate control over the asset.

Once the framework has identified malicious observe points and/or malicious control points, it proceeds to extract the triggering condition of the Trojan. This is accomplished by analyzing the stimulus vectors generated during the Confidentiality and Integrity Verification phases. These vectors represent the exact sequence of inputs and internal state transitions required to activate the Trojan’s payload. For simple triggers—such as a specific input value, a fixed number of clock cycles, or a single condition—the triggering sequence can be directly extracted from the stimulus vector. However, for more sophisticated Trojans whose triggers are implemented as finite state machines (FSMs), the framework employs a state machine extraction technique. This technique identifies potential state registers by detecting feedback loops in which the output of a flip-flop feeds back into its input through combinational logic. Once such registers are identified, the framework applies an established FSM extraction algorithm to reconstruct the state transition graph (STG) of the trigger circuit. This algorithm systematically explores the transitions between states by applying input patterns and observing the resulting state changes, recording the conditions under which each transition occurs. From the reconstructed STG, the framework determines the exact sequence of inputs required to reach the state in which the Trojan’s payload is activated. This reverse-engineering process does not rely on functional simulation or bounded unrolling of the circuit and is therefore immune to the clock cycle limitations that plague formal verification techniques. Even if an attacker introduces additional states, non-resettable counters, or multi-cycle delays to evade detection, the FSM extraction technique will still reconstruct the complete trigger sequence, as it operates on the actual logic of the circuit rather than on abstracted models or bounded temporal windows.

The framework is implemented using commercially available EDA tools such as Synopsys Tetramax for ATPG, Synopsys Design Compiler for synthesis, and custom Tcl and MATLAB scripts for automation, analysis, and FSM extraction. This ensures compatibility with existing industrial design flows and eliminates the need for specialized hardware or proprietary algorithms. The entire process is fully automated and can be executed as a post-synthesis verification step, making it suitable for integration into the standard SoC verification pipeline. The framework is independent of the underlying technology node and operates solely on the gate-level netlist, meaning it can be applied to any IP core regardless of the manufacturing process. It is also agnostic to the type of Trojan, whether it is a Type I Trojan that bypasses legitimate logic or a Type II Trojan that uses a completely separate leakage circuit. It can detect Trojans inserted by third-party IP vendors as well as those inserted by third-party DfT vendors, as it operates on the final netlist regardless of whether scan chains have been inserted. The framework’s ability to detect Trojans that leak data through side channels—such as power consumption variations in internal registers—is enabled by its capability to identify unauthorized observe points even when those points are not primary outputs. For example, in Trojans that exfiltrate data by modulating the power signature of internal registers, the framework identifies those registers as malicious observe points because they can be observed through scan chains, even though they are not connected to any output port. This capability renders the framework effective against advanced Trojans such as AES-T2000 and AES-T2100, which evade commercial tools like JasperGold because those tools require prior knowledge of which internal signals to monitor.

The framework also addresses the limitations of prior information flow tracking techniques. Unlike GLIFT, which suffers from taint explosion in scan-enabled designs, this framework does not rely on taint propagation. Instead, it uses ATPG to directly probe controllability and observability, making it fully compatible with DfT-inserted netlists. Unlike formal verification methods, it is not constrained by the number of clock cycles that can be unrolled, as it does not model the circuit as a state machine over time but rather as a static network of logic gates and flip-flops whose behavior is explored through stimulus-response analysis. Unlike signal sensitivity tracing, which assumes that sensitive data must degrade in value through specific operations, this framework does not make assumptions about how data should be processed—it only requires that the data can be observed or controlled through unauthorized paths. This makes it effective against Trojans such as AES-T100, which XOR the key with a pseudo-random sequence and leak the result through a covert channel, as the sensitivity of the output remains unchanged but the path to it is unauthorized. The framework also eliminates false positives that plague formal verification techniques, as it does not rely on logical equivalence checks that can be satisfied through unrelated signal assignments. Instead, it requires actual physical propagation of the asset’s value through the circuit, ensuring that only genuine information flows are detected.

The output of the framework is a detailed report that includes the identification of all malicious observe points and malicious control points, the stimulus vectors required to trigger the Trojan, the propagation and activation paths, and the reconstructed state transition graph of the trigger circuit. This information enables the SoC integrator to make an informed decision regarding the trustworthiness of the IP core. If any malicious observe or control points are identified, the IP core can be rejected, quarantined, or subjected to further scrutiny. The framework also provides the means to validate the effectiveness of countermeasures, such as the insertion of obfuscation logic or the addition of redundancy, by re-running the verification process after modifications. The framework’s scalability is demonstrated by its successful application to a wide range of benchmark Trojans from the TrustHub repository, including AES-T100, AES-T1100, RSA-T100, PIC-T100, s35932-T100, and others, across multiple cryptographic and control modules. In all cases, the framework successfully detected the presence of the Trojan, identified its trigger condition, and distinguished it from legitimate behavior, whereas prior techniques either failed to detect the Trojan or produced false negatives due to their inherent limitations. The framework’s performance is not significantly degraded by the complexity of the trigger circuit, as demonstrated by its ability to detect the modified AES-T1100(M) Trojan, which incorporates a 20-bit counter and an additional state to delay activation beyond the bounds of formal verification tools. The time required for analysis scales linearly with the number of observe and control points, not with the number of clock cycles, making it robust against adversarial attempts to evade detection through complexity.

The framework’s design ensures that it can be deployed in a variety of real-world scenarios. For example, in a supply chain where IP cores are procured from multiple vendors, each core can be verified independently before integration into the SoC. In a scenario where a DfT vendor is responsible for inserting scan chains, the framework can be applied to the netlist after DfT insertion to detect any malicious modifications introduced during that phase. The framework can also be used to validate the security of legacy IP cores that were developed before the threat of hardware Trojans was widely recognized, providing a retroactive security audit capability. Furthermore, the framework is not limited to the detection of Trojans; it can also be used to identify unintentional information flow violations caused by design errors, synthesis tool bugs, or incorrect constraints, making it a valuable tool for general design assurance. The framework’s reliance on standard ATPG tools ensures that it can be adopted without significant investment in new infrastructure, and its automation ensures that it can be integrated into continuous integration pipelines for high-volume SoC development. The framework’s independence from the underlying technology and its ability to operate on black-box IP make it uniquely suited for the modern SoC ecosystem, where trust must be established through verification rather than provenance.

In summary, the invention provides a novel, robust, and industrially viable method for detecting hardware Trojans in third-party IP cores by transforming the problem of information flow security verification into a fault detection problem. By leveraging the precision and exhaustiveness of ATPG to identify unauthorized observe and control points, and by using state machine extraction to recover trigger conditions, the framework overcomes the fundamental limitations of prior techniques. It does not require a golden reference, is immune to trigger delays and state complexity, operates effectively on DfT-inserted netlists, and distinguishes between legitimate and malicious information flows even when they share the same output ports. The framework represents a paradigm shift in hardware security verification, moving from abstract property checking to concrete physical signal analysis, and establishes a new standard for the trustworthiness of third-party IP in secure SoC design.
