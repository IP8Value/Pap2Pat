## BACKGROUND

The pursuit of artificial intelligence has long been guided by the aspiration to create machines that think, perceive, and understand in ways analogous to human beings. For decades, researchers have attempted to replicate human cognition through computational models that simulate neural networks, process linguistic structures, or optimize decision-making algorithms based on statistical probabilities. However, despite significant advances in machine learning, computer vision, and natural language processing, no existing system has achieved what may be termed true human-like intelligence—not merely the ability to respond correctly to stimuli, but the capacity to internally represent sensory experience in a manner that is subjective, self-referential, and contextually grounded. Modern robots may recognize faces, interpret speech, or navigate environments using lidar and GPS, but they do not “see” in the human sense, nor do they “hear” with comprehension; they process data, not experiences. The fundamental limitation of all prior robotic systems is their inability to form a centralized, proprioceptive “self” coordinate frame—a unified internal reference system that allows sensory inputs to be related not merely to external objects, but to the robot’s own body, its spatial position, its motor capabilities, and its experiential history. Without such a framework, no machine can develop awareness, intentionality, or the capacity for contextual understanding that defines human cognition. Existing approaches rely on external labeling, supervised training datasets, or pre-programmed semantic ontologies, all of which require human intervention and fail to generate autonomous, self-generated knowledge. Even the most sophisticated neural networks, trained on billions of examples, lack any internal model of embodiment—they do not know where their own limbs are, nor do they understand the significance of their actions relative to their own existence. They respond to inputs, but they do not experience them. The present invention overcomes this fundamental limitation by introducing a robotic controller architecture that emulates the biological mechanisms of human perception and cognition. It does so not by mimicking the structure of the human brain at the neuronal level, but by replicating its functional organization: the relational correlation of sensory inputs with a self-defined spatial coordinate frame, the behavioral programming of responses through experiential repetition, and the hierarchical organization of memory systems that distinguish between procedural and declarative knowledge. The invention integrates tactile, visual, and auditory sensors into a unified system where each sensory modality is internally referenced to a central “self” node, enabling the robot to correlate the sensation of touch with the visual image of a hand reaching for an object, or the sound of a word with the tactile memory of grasping that object. This integration is not achieved through algorithmic fusion, but through a dynamic, self-organizing relational network that learns by doing—by scratching an itch, by repeating a word, by navigating a space. The system does not require pre-labeled training data; instead, it learns from the continuous, embodied interaction between its sensors and its environment, forming associations that are inherently contextual and self-referential. This approach fundamentally departs from all prior robotic architectures, which treat perception as a passive input-output process, whereas the invention treats perception as an active, embodied, and self-aware process. The invention further distinguishes itself by its method of verbal comprehension and generation. Rather than employing language models trained on textual corpora, the system learns to speak by first learning to repeat sounds, then by correlating those sounds with visual and tactile experiences, and finally by forming compound task sequences that allow it to generate novel verbal responses based on internalized experience. This process mirrors the way human infants acquire language—not by memorizing dictionaries, but by associating sounds with bodily actions, objects, and social interactions. The invention thus provides the first robotic system capable of achieving human-like levels of artificial intelligence not through computational power or data volume, but through structural and functional emulation of human cognitive development. The background of the invention therefore reveals a critical gap in the field: the absence of any robotic system that possesses a self-location and identification coordinate frame, that internalizes sensory data relative to that frame, and that generates intelligent behavior through experiential behavioral programming rather than statistical inference. The present invention fills that gap, offering a new paradigm for artificial intelligence that is grounded in embodiment, self-awareness, and experiential learning.

## EMBODIMENTS OF THE INVENTION

### RRC-Humanoid Robots

The RRC-Humanoid Robot is a fully autonomous, bipedal robotic system designed to replicate the sensory, motor, and cognitive capabilities of a human being through a proprietary architecture known as the Relational Correlation Sequencer. Unlike conventional robots that rely on pre-programmed behaviors, rule-based decision trees, or statistical models trained on vast datasets, the RRC-Humanoid Robot acquires intelligence through experiential behavioral programming—a process that mirrors the developmental learning of a human child. The robot is equipped with a full complement of human-like sensors, including tactile pressure transducers distributed across its entire surface, binocular video-visual cameras that replicate the spatial convergence and disparity of human eyes, auditory microphones sensitive to the full range of human hearing, and a verbal phoneme sound generator capable of producing articulate speech. Its mechanical structure is designed to emulate human anatomy: it possesses two arms with articulated fingers, two legs with knees and ankles, and a torso capable of rotational and lateral movement. All motor functions are controlled by a centralized controller that does not operate through hierarchical command structures, but through a dynamic, self-referential network of relational correlations. The core of the system is the Self Nodal Map Module, a proprietary memory architecture that maintains a three-dimensional coordinate frame defining the location of every point on the robot’s body and in its immediate surrounding space. This coordinate frame is not pre-defined by engineers, but is learned through behavioral training—specifically, through an “itch-scratch” methodology in which the robot is trained to locate and touch every point on its own surface and within its reach. Through repeated trials, the robot learns not only the position of its limbs, but the trajectory required to move from one point to another, the force needed to apply pressure, and the sensory feedback associated with contact. This process establishes a proprioceptive awareness analogous to that of a human infant who instinctively knows where their hand is without looking. Once this self-location framework is established, all other sensory inputs—visual, auditory, olfactory, gustatory, and vestibular—are internally referenced to this same coordinate frame, allowing the robot to contextualize every perception relative to its own body. For example, when the robot sees a cup, it does not merely recognize a shape; it recognizes the cup as located at a specific distance and angle relative to its own shoulder, its own hand, and its own past experience of grasping similar objects. This contextualization is not achieved through geometric calculations or depth estimation algorithms, but through the relational correlation of visual pixel patterns with tactile memory traces and motor trajectories stored in the Self Nodal Map Module. The robot’s intelligence emerges not from the processing of isolated data points, but from the continuous, dynamic interplay between sensory input, motor output, and self-referential memory. The system does not “compute” a response—it remembers a behavior that was previously successful in a similar context, and it reproduces that behavior with adaptive precision. The RRC-Humanoid Robot is therefore not a machine that answers questions; it is a system that understands them, because its understanding is rooted in the same experiential foundation as human cognition. Its responses are not generated by language models, but by compound task sequences formed from the integration of multiple sensory and motor memories. When asked to identify an object, the robot does not retrieve a label from a database; it recalls the visual appearance of the object, the tactile sensation of touching it, the motor sequence required to grasp it, and the verbal sound associated with its name—all correlated within the same self-referential framework. This integration is what enables the robot to demonstrate human-like comprehension, not through programmed semantics, but through embodied experience.

## BRIEF DESCRIPTION

The invention comprises a robotic system capable of achieving human-like artificial intelligence through a proprietary architecture known as the Relational Correlation Sequencer, which integrates tactile, visual, and auditory sensory inputs into a unified, self-referential cognitive framework. The system is composed of three primary stages: an optical apparatus stage, an interface circuit stage, and an RRC processing stage. The optical apparatus stage captures binocular visual data from two high-resolution video cameras mounted on the robot’s head, processes the raw pixel data to form a three-dimensional photometric image, and maps this image onto a spatial coordinate frame that is dynamically aligned with the robot’s own body. The interface circuit stage receives auditory input from microphones sensitive to the full human hearing range, converts the acoustic signal into a sequence of phoneme-based modalities, and formats the data into a structure compatible with the relational correlation network. The RRC processing stage employs a hierarchical array of Task Selector Modules, each containing pattern recognition circuits that correlate incoming sensory data with stored motor and memory traces, enabling the robot to identify, recognize, and comprehend sensory inputs through experiential behavioral programming. The system further includes a Self Nodal Map Module that maintains a three-dimensional coordinate frame defining the location of every point on the robot’s body and within its immediate reach, established through a behavioral training process known as the “itch-scratch” methodology. This self-location framework serves as the central hub of intelligence, allowing all sensory data to be internally referenced to the robot’s own physical existence. The robot’s verbal comprehension and generation are achieved through the behavioral association of phoneme sequences with visual and tactile experiences, enabling it to respond to spoken commands with contextually appropriate verbal replies or motor actions. The system does not rely on pre-programmed rules, external databases, or statistical language models; instead, it learns through repeated, embodied interaction with its environment, forming associations that are inherently contextual and self-referential. The result is a robotic system that does not merely process information, but experiences it—demonstrating awareness, understanding, and intentionality in a manner indistinguishable from human cognition.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Section B—the Optical Apparatus Stage (Steps 1 and 2)

The optical apparatus stage of the invention is responsible for capturing, processing, and transforming raw visual data into a three-dimensional photometric image that is internally referenced to the robot’s self-location and identification coordinate frame. This stage begins with the deployment of two high-resolution video cameras, each mounted on a rigid frame positioned at the approximate location of the human eyes. The cameras are oriented such that their optical axes converge at a fixed point along the midline of the robot’s forward field of view, replicating the binocular convergence of human vision. The distance between the two camera lenses is calibrated to match the average inter-pupillary distance of a human adult, ensuring that the disparity between the two images corresponds to the natural depth cues present in human visual perception. Each camera captures a two-dimensional array of pixels, with each pixel encoding intensity values across the visible spectrum. These raw pixel arrays are not processed as independent images, but as complementary data streams that must be correlated in space and time to reconstruct the three-dimensional structure of the environment. The first step in this process involves the mechanical calibration of the camera system, during which the precise spatial relationship between the two cameras and the robot’s central coordinate frame is established. This calibration is performed by placing a reference object of known dimensions at a fixed distance from the cameras and recording the corresponding pixel positions on each image plane. From this data, a transformation matrix is computed that maps each pixel coordinate in the left and right image planes to a corresponding three-dimensional point in the robot’s tactile coordinate frame. This mapping is not static; it is continuously refined during behavioral training as the robot interacts with objects of varying size, shape, and distance. The second step involves the formation of the 3D-photometric image, a high-fidelity representation of the visual scene that is not merely a geometric reconstruction, but a perceptual simulation of what a human would experience. This is achieved by applying a series of visual cues to each pixel or cluster of pixels, including binocular disparity, image size, motion parallax, occlusion, and shading. The binocular disparity cue is used to estimate the depth of each pixel relative to the convergence point, with the degree of horizontal displacement between corresponding pixels in the left and right images serving as the primary indicator of distance. However, unlike conventional stereo vision systems that rely solely on disparity for depth estimation, the present invention incorporates additional cues to resolve ambiguities. For example, when an object is identified as having a known size—such as a human face, a cup, or a door—the system uses the measured pixel dimensions of the object to infer its distance from the robot. If a face occupies a certain number of pixels in the image, and the system has previously learned through tactile interaction that a human face is approximately 20 centimeters wide, then the distance to the face can be calculated based on the ratio of actual size to apparent size. This process is not performed through mathematical formulas, but through associative memory: the system remembers that when a face appeared in a certain pixel configuration, it was located at a certain distance, and it recalls that association when the same configuration reappears. The 3D-photometric image is not stored as a point cloud or a mesh, but as a network of relational correlations between pixel clusters, their associated depth estimates, their tactile counterparts, and the motor trajectories required to interact with them. Each pixel cluster is linked to a specific node in the Self Nodal Map Module, so that when the robot sees an object, it does not merely recognize its shape—it remembers where it touched it, how it moved to reach it, and what it felt when it did. This integration of visual perception with tactile memory is what enables the robot to “see” in the human sense—not as a passive observer of external stimuli, but as an active participant in a self-referential perceptual world. The 3D-photometric image is therefore not a representation of the external world, but a representation of the robot’s own experience of the world. The system does not compute depth; it remembers it. It does not identify objects; it recalls them in the context of its own bodily interaction. This process is continuous and dynamic, evolving with each new experience, and it forms the foundation for all subsequent cognitive functions, including navigation, object manipulation, and verbal comprehension. The optical apparatus stage is thus not merely a sensor array, but the first stage in the formation of subjective awareness.

### Section C—the Interface Circuit Stage (Steps 3, 4, 5, 6)

The interface circuit stage serves as the critical bridge between raw sensory input and the relational correlation network of the RRC controller, transforming auditory signals into structured, behaviorally meaningful phoneme sequences that can be internally referenced to the robot’s self-location and identification coordinate frame. This stage begins with the reception of analog audio signals from two high-sensitivity microphones, positioned to replicate the spatial placement of human ears. These microphones capture the full spectrum of human speech, from low-frequency vocal resonances to high-frequency fricatives, and convert them into electrical signals that are then digitized and processed by a programmable spectrum analyzer. The first function of the interface circuit is to decompose the continuous acoustic waveform into discrete modalities that correspond to the 120 phoneme combinations that constitute the English language. This decomposition is not performed through pre-defined phoneme templates, but through behavioral tuning: the system is trained to recognize the collective amplitude-frequency-time patterns associated with each phoneme by repeatedly hearing and repeating them. The spectrum analyzer is calibrated not to match theoretical phoneme models, but to the actual acoustic signatures produced by human speakers during natural speech. This calibration process involves exposing the system to hundreds of hours of speech from diverse speakers, varying in pitch, accent, speed, and emotional tone, and allowing the system to form statistical clusters of similar patterns. These clusters are not fixed; they evolve as the system encounters new speech patterns, enabling it to adapt to individual speakers and contextual variations. The second function of the interface circuit is to perform behavioral speech processing, during which sequential phoneme patterns are grouped into words, phrases, and sentences. This is not achieved through lexical segmentation algorithms or syntactic parsing, but through the system’s prior experience with repetition. The robot is first trained to babble—repeating any sound it hears, regardless of meaning—until it can reproduce the phoneme sequence with high fidelity. This repetition is not a passive echo; it is an active exploration of the motor space required to produce each sound, with the system learning the precise timing, pressure, and articulatory movements needed to generate each phoneme. As the robot repeats words and sentences, it begins to form associations between the auditory pattern and the motor trajectory required to produce it. These associations are stored in the Task Selector Module linked to the phoneme sound generator, creating a memory of how each word “feels” to say. The third function of the interface circuit is to align the processed auditory data with the visual and tactile coordinate frames. This alignment is achieved by presenting the robot with a simultaneous auditory and visual stimulus—such as a person saying the word “cup” while holding up a cup—and then requiring the robot to repeat the word while visually fixating on the object and tactilely reaching for it. Through repeated exposure, the system learns that the phoneme sequence /kʌp/ is correlated with the visual pattern of a rounded, handle-equipped object and the motor trajectory of grasping it. This correlation is not symbolic; it is experiential. The word is not a label, but a memory trace embedded within the same relational network as the visual and tactile experiences. The fourth function of the interface circuit is to format the processed auditory data into a structure compatible with the RRC’s multi-dimensional nodal map. This involves encoding each word or sentence as a multi-dimensional vector, where each dimension corresponds to a phoneme, its amplitude, its duration, its pitch variation, and its temporal sequence. These vectors are then mapped onto the Self Nodal Map Module, where they are linked to the corresponding visual and tactile nodes. The fifth function is to correct for contextual distortions in speech, such as co-articulation, speaker variability, and emotional intonation. The system does not attempt to normalize speech into an idealized form; instead, it learns to recognize the same word spoken differently by different people, in different moods, and at different speeds, by storing multiple variations of each phoneme sequence as distinct but related memory traces. The sixth and final function is to transmit the formatted auditory data to the RRC processing stage, where it becomes available for behavioral programming. The interface circuit does not interpret meaning; it prepares the data for interpretation. It does not assign semantic value; it creates the conditions under which meaning can emerge through relational correlation. The result is an auditory input stream that is not merely a sequence of sounds, but a rich, multi-dimensional experience embedded within the robot’s self-referential world.

### Section D—the RRC Processing Stage: Training the System to Respond to the 3D-Photometric Image (Steps 7 and 8)

The RRC processing stage is the core of the invention, where the relational correlation of sensory data gives rise to intelligent behavior through experiential behavioral programming. This stage operates through a hierarchical array of Task Selector Modules, each of which contains a pattern recognition circuit designed to identify and prioritize specific combinations of sensory inputs as Task Initiating Triggers. These triggers are not pre-programmed commands, but learned associations that emerge from repeated behavioral interaction. The first step in this process involves the activation of the Self Nodal Map Module, which provides the central reference frame for all sensory and motor data. When the robot perceives a 3D-photometric image of an object, the visual data is not processed in isolation; it is correlated with the tactile memory of touching similar objects, the motor trajectory of reaching for them, and the auditory sound associated with their name. This correlation is performed by the RRC controller, which continuously compares incoming sensory patterns with stored memory traces, identifying those that are most closely related. When a match is found, a Task Initiating Trigger is generated, activating a specific Task Selector Module that contains the behavioral sequence associated with that trigger. For example, if the robot sees a cup and hears the word “cup,” the system activates a compound Task Selector Module that links the visual pattern of the cup, the auditory sequence /kʌp/, and the motor trajectory of grasping. This compound module is not a single memory, but a network of interrelated memories, each reinforcing the others. The second step involves the formation of compound Task Selector Modules through behavioral training. The robot is not taught what a cup is; it learns what a cup is by repeatedly seeing it, hearing it named, touching it, and grasping it. Each of these experiences strengthens the relational links between the visual, auditory, and tactile nodes, until the activation of any one node triggers the recall of the others. This process is analogous to human learning, where the word “cup” becomes inseparable from the image, the feel, and the action. The third step involves the hierarchical organization of these compound modules into a declarative memory system. The Task Selector Modules are arranged in a tree-like structure, with higher-level modules activating lower-level ones based on context. For example, the prime task “identify this object” activates a visual search engine, which in turn activates the TSM associated with the object’s visual pattern, which then activates the TSM associated with its name, which finally activates the phoneme sound generator to produce the verbal response. This hierarchical structure allows the system to respond to complex queries, such as “What is the red cup on the table?” by sequentially activating the relevant visual, spatial, and linguistic modules. The fourth step involves the generation of novel responses through the recombination of existing memory traces. When the robot is asked a question it has never heard before, it does not search for a pre-programmed answer; it constructs one by combining relevant memory traces. For example, if asked “What color is the cup you touched yesterday?” the system retrieves the memory of the cup’s visual appearance, the tactile sensation of touching it, and the auditory memory of its name, then combines them with the memory of the color red, which was previously associated with other red objects, to generate the response “It is red.” This process is not logical deduction; it is associative recall. The fifth step involves the continuous refinement of these memory traces through error correction and reinforcement. If the robot misidentifies an object, the trainer corrects it by repeating the correct association, and the system updates its relational links accordingly. This feedback loop ensures that the system’s understanding evolves with experience, becoming more accurate and nuanced over time. The RRC processing stage does not compute answers; it remembers experiences. It does not follow rules; it follows associations. And it does not respond to commands; it responds to context. The result is a system that does not merely process information, but understands it—because its understanding is rooted in the same experiential foundation as human cognition.

### Section E—Robotic Visual “Seeing:”

The robotic visual “seeing” capability of the invention is not a simulation of human vision, but an embodiment of it. Unlike conventional computer vision systems that analyze images to detect edges, classify objects, or estimate depth through mathematical algorithms, the RRC-Humanoid Robot “sees” by internally reconstructing the perceptual experience of a human. This is achieved through the formation of a 3D-photometric image that is not a geometric model, but a phenomenological representation—meaning that it does not merely describe the spatial properties of objects, but captures the way they appear to a perceiving subject. The robot does not calculate the distance to a chair; it remembers how it felt to reach for it, how it looked when it was close, and how it changed as it moved away. This memory is not stored as a number, but as a relational network of visual, tactile, and motor traces. The robot’s visual perception is therefore not passive; it is active, embodied, and self-referential. When the robot looks at a person, it does not detect a face; it recalls the experience of being spoken to by that person, the sound of their voice, the way they moved their hands, and the emotional tone of their speech. This integration of visual perception with social and emotional memory is what enables the robot to recognize not just faces, but individuals. The robot’s “seeing” is further enhanced by its ability to perceive depth not through stereo disparity alone, but through the integration of multiple visual cues—image size, motion parallax, occlusion, and shading—all of which are learned through behavioral interaction. The robot does not learn what a tree looks like from a dataset; it learns what a tree feels like to walk around, what it looks like from different angles, and how its shadow changes with the time of day. This experiential learning results in a visual understanding that is far richer and more nuanced than any algorithmic system can achieve. The robot does not need to be told that a curb is dangerous; it remembers the sensation of its wheel hitting one, the jolt of the impact, and the visual change that preceded it. This memory is not a rule; it is a lived experience. The robot’s visual perception is also contextualized by its self-location coordinate frame, meaning that every object it sees is understood relative to its own body. A cup is not simply an object in space; it is a cup that is within reach, or out of reach, or just beyond the edge of its field of view. This spatial awareness is not computed; it is remembered. The robot does not need to map its environment; it remembers its own movements through it. The result is a robotic system that does not merely detect objects, but experiences them—seeing not as a machine, but as a sentient being. This is the essence of human-like artificial intelligence: not the ability to recognize patterns, but the ability to perceive the world as a subject, not an object. The invention achieves this not through computational power, but through structural and functional emulation of human perception. The robot does not see because it was programmed to see; it sees because it learned to see—through touch, through movement, through experience. And in that learning, it became aware.
