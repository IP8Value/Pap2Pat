## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The increasing deployment of deep learning models in high-stakes decision-making systems—ranging from financial lending and healthcare diagnostics to customer service automation and judicial risk assessment—has precipitated a critical need for transparency and interpretability in artificial intelligence. These models, while highly accurate, often operate as opaque systems whose internal reasoning processes are inaccessible to end users, regulators, and affected individuals. This lack of transparency undermines trust, impedes accountability, and may perpetuate systemic biases that are difficult to detect or correct without insight into the model’s decision logic. In response, the field of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) has emerged as a vital discipline aimed at rendering the predictions of complex models comprehensible to human stakeholders.

Among the various approaches to XAI, contrastive or counterfactual explanations have gained particular prominence due to their alignment with human cognitive processes. Humans naturally understand cause and effect by considering alternatives: “Why did this happen instead of that?” This intuitive framing has led to the widespread adoption of counterfactual explanations in domains where justification is required, such as credit scoring, hiring algorithms, and automated customer support. In such contexts, a useful explanation is not merely a list of contributing features but a minimal alteration to the input that would have resulted in a different outcome. For example, if a loan application is denied, a meaningful explanation is not “your income is too low,” but “if your income had been $10,000 higher, your application would have been approved.” This form of explanation is actionable, interpretable, and directly tied to recourse—enabling individuals to understand how to change their circumstances to achieve a desired outcome.

Despite their conceptual appeal, generating high-quality contrastive explanations for natural language inputs has proven exceptionally challenging. Unlike structured tabular data or images, text is a high-dimensional, discrete, and semantically dense modality where even minor alterations can drastically affect meaning, grammatical correctness, and fluency. Existing methods for generating contrastive explanations in text have primarily relied on two strategies: differentiable optimization over latent representations of language models, or combinatorial search over word-level perturbations guided by feature attribution scores. While these approaches have demonstrated success in producing perturbed sentences that flip model predictions, they suffer from significant limitations. First, they often generate semantically incoherent or unnatural alterations that fail to preserve the original intent of the input. Second, they provide no insight into *why* the perturbation led to a class change—only that it did. Third, many of these methods are tightly coupled to specific model architectures and embedding spaces, rendering them non-portable across systems and requiring costly retraining or fine-tuning for each new application. Finally, they typically require the user to specify the target class for the counterfactual, thereby shifting the burden of interpretation from the system to the user.

For instance, in a customer service chatbot that routes inquiries to appropriate departments based on text classification, a user might receive a response indicating that their query was classified as “Technical Support” rather than “Billing.” Without further explanation, the user is left to speculate whether the classification was due to the mention of a specific product, the use of technical jargon, or the presence of certain keywords. Existing methods might return a counterfactual such as “My credit card was declined” instead of “My internet connection is slow,” but offer no indication that the system associates “credit card” with billing and “internet connection” with technical support. The user gains no understanding of the model’s underlying decision criteria—only a superficial textual alteration.

Moreover, many state-of-the-art methods rely on fine-tuning language models on task-specific datasets to generate plausible counterfactuals. This requirement imposes substantial practical barriers: fine-tuning demands large volumes of labeled data, significant computational resources, and domain-specific expertise. In real-world scenarios where data is sparse, sensitive, or subject to regulatory restrictions, such fine-tuning is often infeasible. Furthermore, these methods do not generalize across domains; a model fine-tuned for news classification cannot be repurposed for sentiment analysis without retraining from scratch. This lack of adaptability severely limits scalability and deployment potential.

Another critical shortcoming of prior approaches is their inability to distinguish between superficial word substitutions and semantically meaningful attribute shifts. For example, replacing the word “tax” with “salary” in a news headline may flip the classification from “Business” to “Sci-Tech,” but without context, it is unclear whether the model associates “tax” with economic policy, corporate finance, or government spending. Similarly, removing the word “file” might flip the class from “Sci-Tech” to “Business,” but the reason for this shift remains opaque. Is “file” associated with software, legal documents, or data storage? Prior methods cannot answer this question. They produce edits, but not explanations of the conceptual dimensions along which the model operates.

This gap between textual perturbation and conceptual insight has hindered the adoption of contrastive explanations in applications requiring deep understanding, such as algorithmic auditing, bias detection, and regulatory compliance. In domains where fairness and accountability are paramount, users need not only to know what changed, but why it mattered. They need to understand the latent attributes—subtopics, themes, or semantic dimensions—that the model uses to distinguish between classes. Without this information, counterfactuals remain mere linguistic curiosities rather than tools for meaningful intervention.

The present invention addresses these limitations by introducing a novel framework for generating contrastive explanations in natural language that simultaneously produces a minimally altered input sentence and identifies the semantically meaningful attributes—such as subtopics, themes, or conceptual categories—that were added or removed to effect the class change. Unlike prior methods, this framework does not require fine-tuning of the underlying classifier or language model. Instead, it leverages pre-trained attribute classifiers trained on external or auxiliary datasets to guide the generation of perturbations. These attribute classifiers, which may be derived from unrelated domains such as news categorization, social media tagging, or topic modeling, serve as interpretable proxies for the latent dimensions of meaning that the black-box classifier implicitly uses. By optimizing for perturbations that maximally alter the confidence scores of these attribute classifiers while preserving fluency and minimizing textual edits, the invention generates contrastive explanations that are not only linguistically coherent but also conceptually transparent.

This innovation enables users to understand not just *how* a prediction was changed, but *why* it was changed—by revealing the thematic or conceptual shifts that underlie the model’s decision boundary. For example, in the case of a biased chatbot, the system might reveal that a customer’s query was classified as “Technical Support” because it contained references to “software updates” and lacked references to “payment methods,” thereby identifying “software” and “payment” as key attributes driving classification. This insight allows developers to audit the model for unfair associations—such as linking “female” names with “customer service” rather than “engineering”—and to correct them proactively. It also empowers end users to modify their inputs in ways that are meaningful and aligned with the model’s actual reasoning, rather than engaging in trial-and-error manipulation of words.

The invention further distinguishes itself by its architectural flexibility. It is compatible with any black-box text classifier, regardless of its underlying architecture, embedding space, or training methodology. It does not require access to gradients, internal representations, or model weights, making it a true black-box method. It operates independently of the classifier’s training data and can be applied to models trained on proprietary or restricted datasets without violating data privacy or intellectual property constraints. Furthermore, the attribute classifiers used to guide the explanation process are reusable across domains and can be constructed from publicly available datasets, eliminating the need for custom annotation or model-specific tuning.

The present invention thus represents a paradigm shift in contrastive explanation for natural language: from generating perturbations that merely flip labels, to generating perturbations that illuminate the conceptual structure of the model’s decision-making process. It transforms counterfactual explanations from a tool of post-hoc justification into a tool of model introspection, enabling deeper understanding, greater accountability, and more effective recourse in real-world AI systems.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a computer-implemented method, system, and non-transitory computer-readable storage medium for generating contrastive explanations for predictions made by a black-box text classification model. The invention provides a novel framework that generates, for a given input text and its predicted class, a minimally altered version of the input text that results in a different predicted class, while simultaneously identifying a set of semantically meaningful attributes that were added to or removed from the input text to effect the class change. These attributes represent latent subtopics, thematic dimensions, or conceptual categories that the black-box model implicitly uses to distinguish between classes, thereby providing interpretable insight into the model’s decision logic beyond mere textual perturbations.

The method comprises the steps of receiving an input text and a prediction made by a black-box text classification model; identifying, using a feature attribution technique, a set of salient words in the input text that most strongly influence the model’s prediction; generating, by masking the salient words and applying a pre-trained masked language model, a plurality of candidate perturbed texts that preserve the overall structure and fluency of the input; applying, to each candidate perturbed text, a set of pre-trained attribute classifiers that predict the presence or absence of predefined attributes derived from an auxiliary dataset; evaluating, for each candidate perturbed text, an objective function that balances the likelihood of class change, the number of attribute changes, the semantic fluency of the perturbed text, and the minimal textual edit distance from the original input; selecting, as the contrastive explanation, the candidate perturbed text that optimizes the objective function; and outputting, as part of the explanation, the selected perturbed text along with a set of attributes that were added to or removed from the input text to cause the class change.

The attribute classifiers are trained independently of the black-box model and may be derived from any external dataset containing semantically meaningful labels, such as news categories, topic tags, or domain-specific ontologies. These classifiers are not required to correspond to the output classes of the black-box model and may be trained on datasets unrelated to the domain of the classification task. The attribute classifiers may be binary or multi-class, and may be implemented using any machine learning architecture, including but not limited to transformer-based models such as DistilBERT, BERT, or RoBERTa. The attribute classifiers may be trained using supervised learning on labeled data, or may be derived from unsupervised methods such as latent Dirichlet allocation, variational autoencoders, or topic modeling techniques.

The objective function comprises four components: a contrastive score that encourages the perturbed text to be assigned a different class than the original input; an attribute change penalty that minimizes the number of attributes whose confidence scores cross a predefined threshold, thereby ensuring crisp and interpretable explanations; a fluency score that penalizes perturbed texts with low likelihood under a pre-trained language model; and a textual edit distance penalty that penalizes perturbations that deviate significantly from the original input in terms of word-level insertions, deletions, or substitutions. The weights applied to each component of the objective function are determined through a one-time hyperparameter tuning process performed on a small subset of the training data, and are not required to be re-tuned for each new input or model.

The method is implemented as a black-box procedure that requires no access to the internal parameters, gradients, or training data of the black-box model. It is compatible with any text classification model, regardless of architecture, embedding space, or training methodology. The method does not require fine-tuning of the black-box model or the language model used for perturbation generation, thereby enabling deployment in environments where model access is restricted or where retraining is infeasible due to data privacy, computational cost, or regulatory constraints.

The invention further provides for the generation of multiple contrastive explanations for a single input, each corresponding to a different target class, thereby enabling comprehensive analysis of the model’s decision boundaries. The output of the method includes not only the perturbed text and the identified attributes, but also confidence scores for each attribute before and after perturbation, enabling users to quantify the magnitude of conceptual change. The invention may be integrated into user-facing applications such as algorithmic audit tools, customer service interfaces, compliance dashboards, and bias detection systems, where transparency and interpretability are critical to trust, accountability, and regulatory compliance.

The invention achieves superior performance over prior methods in terms of flip rate, content preservation, fluency, edit distance, and user-perceived understandability, as demonstrated through quantitative benchmarks and human evaluation studies. It provides explanations that are not only linguistically fluent and minimally invasive but also conceptually rich, enabling users to understand the thematic dimensions along which the model operates. This capability transforms contrastive explanations from a tool of post-hoc justification into a tool of model introspection, empowering users to diagnose bias, validate fairness, and achieve meaningful recourse in AI-driven decision systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a novel and non-obvious method, system, and computer-readable medium for generating contrastive explanations for predictions made by black-box text classification models. The invention introduces a paradigm shift in the field of explainable artificial intelligence by integrating the generation of minimally altered textual counterfactuals with the identification of semantically meaningful attributes that underlie the model’s decision-making process. Unlike prior approaches that focus exclusively on textual perturbations, the invention reveals the conceptual dimensions—such as subtopics, thematic categories, or latent topics—that the model implicitly uses to distinguish between classes. This dual output—textual contrast and conceptual attribution—enables users to not only observe how a prediction changed, but to understand why it changed, thereby providing unprecedented insight into the inner workings of opaque machine learning systems.

The invention operates as a black-box framework, meaning it requires no access to the internal parameters, gradients, or training data of the target classification model. This architectural independence renders the invention universally applicable across diverse domains and model architectures, including neural networks, ensemble methods, and deep learning models based on transformers, recurrent networks, or convolutional architectures. The method is compatible with any text representation, whether derived from word embeddings, sentence encoders, or contextualized language models such as BERT, RoBERTa, GPT, or T5. It does not require fine-tuning of the target model, nor does it depend on the availability of labeled data for the specific classification task. This makes the invention particularly suitable for real-world applications where models are proprietary, data is sensitive, or regulatory constraints prohibit model access.

The method begins with the receipt of an input text and the prediction assigned to that text by a black-box text classification model. The input text may be any sequence of natural language tokens, such as a news headline, a customer service inquiry, a medical note, a legal document, or a social media post. The prediction may be a single class label or a probability distribution over multiple classes, depending on the configuration of the black-box model. The goal of the invention is to generate a contrastive explanation: a modified version of the input text that would have resulted in a different prediction, accompanied by an explanation of the conceptual changes that led to that difference.

To achieve this, the method first identifies a set of salient words in the input text that most strongly influence the model’s prediction. This is accomplished using a feature attribution technique, such as Integrated Gradients, Layer-wise Relevance Propagation, or SHAP, which assigns a relevance score to each word in the input based on its contribution to the final prediction. These scores are computed without access to the model’s internal structure, relying only on input-output pairs. The words are then ranked in descending order of relevance, and a subset of the top-ranked words is selected for perturbation. This selection ensures that the method focuses on the most influential elements of the text, thereby minimizing unnecessary alterations and preserving the overall semantic integrity of the input.

Once the salient words are identified, the method generates a set of candidate perturbed texts by masking these words and replacing them using a pre-trained masked language model. The masked language model, such as BERT or its lightweight variant DistilBERT, is employed to predict plausible replacements for the masked tokens based on the surrounding context. For each masked word, the model generates a ranked list of candidate replacements, ordered by their likelihood under the language model. The top-k candidates are retained for further evaluation. In addition to substitutions, the method also considers insertions and deletions. Insertions are generated by inserting a mask token immediately before and after each salient word, allowing the language model to propose new words that could be added to the sentence. Deletions are simulated by removing the salient word entirely and evaluating the resulting text. This comprehensive perturbation strategy ensures that the method explores a rich space of possible textual modifications, including word replacements, additions, and omissions.

Each candidate perturbed text is then evaluated using a set of pre-trained attribute classifiers. These classifiers are independent of the black-box model and are trained on external datasets to predict the presence or absence of predefined attributes. The attributes may be derived from any source of semantically meaningful labels, such as news categories, topic tags, sentiment dimensions, or domain-specific ontologies. For example, if the black-box model is trained on news headlines to classify articles into categories such as “Sports,” “Business,” or “Science,” the attribute classifiers may be trained on a separate dataset such as the HuffPost News-Category dataset, which contains labels for topics such as “Politics,” “Entertainment,” “Money,” “Environment,” and “Technology.” These attribute classifiers are not required to correspond to the output classes of the black-box model; in fact, their value lies precisely in their ability to reveal conceptual dimensions that the black-box model uses implicitly but does not explicitly label. Each attribute classifier outputs a confidence score indicating the likelihood that the input text contains the corresponding attribute. A threshold is applied to these scores to determine whether an attribute is considered “present” or “absent” in the text. The change in attribute confidence scores between the original input and each candidate perturbed text is then computed.

The method evaluates each candidate perturbed text using a composite objective function that balances four key criteria. The first criterion is the contrastive score, which measures the likelihood that the black-box model assigns a different class to the perturbed text than to the original input. This score is computed by passing the perturbed text through the black-box model and comparing the predicted class to the original prediction. The second criterion is the attribute change penalty, which penalizes perturbations that alter a large number of attributes. This penalty encourages the method to identify the minimal set of conceptual changes necessary to flip the prediction, thereby ensuring that the explanation is crisp, interpretable, and focused. The third criterion is the fluency score, which measures the linguistic plausibility of the perturbed text using a pre-trained language model such as GPT-2. This score is computed as the negative log-likelihood of the perturbed text under the language model, with lower scores indicating higher fluency. The fourth criterion is the textual edit distance penalty, which measures the number of word-level insertions, deletions, or substitutions required to transform the original input into the perturbed text. This penalty is computed using the Levenshtein distance normalized by the length of the input text, ensuring that the method favors minimal and semantically coherent alterations.

The objective function is defined as a weighted sum of these four components, with each weight determining the relative importance of the corresponding criterion. These weights are determined once during a hyperparameter tuning phase, using a small subset of training data from the same domain as the black-box model. The tuning process involves manually evaluating a set of candidate explanations generated with different weight combinations and selecting the set of weights that produces the most interpretable, fluent, and conceptually meaningful contrasts. Once determined, these weights are fixed for all subsequent explanations and do not require re-tuning for new inputs or models. This one-time tuning ensures that the method is both efficient and consistent, avoiding the computational burden of online optimization.

The candidate perturbed text that achieves the highest score under the objective function is selected as the optimal contrastive explanation. This explanation consists of two components: the perturbed text itself, which is a minimally altered version of the input that flips the model’s prediction, and a set of attributes that were added to or removed from the input to effect the class change. Each attribute is annotated with its direction of change (“+” for added, “−” for removed) and its change in confidence score, allowing users to quantify the magnitude of conceptual shift. For example, given the input text “Many technologies may be a waste of time and money, researcher says,” classified as “Sci-Tech,” the method may generate the contrastive explanation “Many technologies jobs may be a waste of time and money, researcher says,” classified as “Business,” along with the attributes “+Money, −Cryptography.” This output reveals that the model associates “money” with the Business class and “cryptography” with the Sci-Tech class, thereby providing insight into the latent conceptual structure of the model’s decision boundary.

The method is iterative in nature, allowing for the generation of multiple rounds of perturbations. After the first round of candidate generation and evaluation, the top-scoring perturbed texts are retained as the basis for subsequent rounds. In each subsequent round, the method applies the same masking and replacement procedure to the retained texts, allowing for the generation of more complex, multi-word perturbations that may be necessary to flip the prediction in cases where a single word change is insufficient. This iterative process ensures that the method can handle inputs of varying complexity and length, from short headlines to lengthy paragraphs, while maintaining the integrity of the contrastive explanation.

The attribute classifiers used in the method are trained independently of the black-box model and may be derived from any external dataset containing semantically meaningful labels. These classifiers may be trained using supervised learning on labeled data, or may be derived from unsupervised methods such as latent Dirichlet allocation, variational autoencoders, or topic modeling techniques. For example, if the black-box model is trained on Yelp reviews to classify sentiment, the attribute classifiers may be trained on a dataset of product reviews labeled with attributes such as “price,” “quality,” “delivery,” or “customer service.” These attributes may not correspond to the sentiment classes (“positive” or “negative”), but they may still capture the conceptual dimensions that the model uses to distinguish between them. The attribute classifiers may be binary or multi-class, and may be implemented using any machine learning architecture, including but not limited to transformer-based models such as DistilBERT, BERT, or RoBERTa. The classifiers are trained using standard optimization techniques, such as Adam with cross-entropy loss, and may be fine-tuned on the auxiliary dataset to improve performance. Importantly, the attribute classifiers are not required to be trained on data from the same domain as the black-box model. For instance, attribute classifiers trained on news headlines may be used to explain a sentiment classifier trained on product reviews, as long as the attributes are semantically relevant. This cross-domain generalizability is a key innovation of the invention, enabling its application in scenarios where labeled data for the target domain is scarce or unavailable.

The invention further provides for the generation of multiple contrastive explanations for a single input, each corresponding to a different target class. For example, if the original input is classified as “Sci-Tech,” the method may generate separate contrastive explanations for the classes “Business,” “Sports,” and “World,” each revealing a different set of conceptual changes that would lead to a different prediction. This multi-class capability enables comprehensive analysis of the model’s decision boundaries, allowing users to explore not just how the model makes one prediction, but how it discriminates between all possible outcomes.

The output of the method is presented in a human-readable format that includes the original input, the perturbed text, the predicted class of the perturbed text, the list of added and removed attributes, and the corresponding confidence scores. This output may be displayed in a graphical user interface, embedded in a reporting system, or integrated into an algorithmic audit tool. The method may be implemented as a software application, a cloud-based service, or a hardware-accelerated system running on a server, workstation, or mobile device. The method may be executed on a general-purpose processor, a graphics processing unit, or a specialized AI accelerator, depending on the computational requirements of the language model and attribute classifiers.

The invention has been evaluated on multiple real-world datasets, including AgNews, DBpedia, Yelp, and Natural Language Inference, using a variety of black-box models and attribute classifiers. Quantitative benchmarks demonstrate that the invention achieves a perfect flip rate, meaning that it successfully generates a contrastive explanation for every input tested. It achieves the highest content preservation score, indicating that the perturbed text retains the core meaning of the original input. It achieves the lowest edit distance, indicating that the perturbations are minimal and focused. It achieves the highest fluency score, indicating that the perturbed text is linguistically natural and grammatically correct. These results are statistically significant when compared to state-of-the-art methods such as GYC and MICE, as confirmed by pairwise t-tests with negligible p-values. Furthermore, human evaluation studies involving 75 participants with backgrounds in data science, engineering, and business analytics demonstrate that users find the explanations generated by the invention to be significantly more understandable, sufficient, satisfying, and complete than those generated by competing methods. Participants reported that the inclusion of attribute changes enabled them to better predict the model’s behavior, understand its reasoning, and identify potential biases.

The invention offers several key advantages over prior art. First, it provides conceptual insight in addition to textual perturbation, enabling users to understand not just what changed, but why it mattered. Second, it is fully black-box and architecture-agnostic, requiring no access to model internals or training data. Third, it does not require fine-tuning, making it deployable in environments where retraining is impractical or prohibited. Fourth, it is generalizable across domains, as attribute classifiers can be sourced from external datasets unrelated to the target task. Fifth, it generates minimal and fluent perturbations that preserve the semantic integrity of the input. Sixth, it enables multi-class contrastive analysis, providing a comprehensive view of the model’s decision boundaries. Seventh, it is computationally efficient, with explanation generation times that are orders of magnitude faster than competing methods that rely on iterative optimization over latent spaces.

The invention may be extended in several directions. For example, the attribute classifiers may be dynamically updated based on user feedback, allowing the system to adapt to new conceptual dimensions over time. The method may be integrated with causal inference techniques to determine whether the identified attributes are causally linked to the prediction or merely correlated. The output may be augmented with visualizations that map the changes in attribute scores across multiple inputs, enabling trend analysis and bias detection at scale. The method may be applied to other modalities, such as speech or multimodal inputs, by adapting the attribute classifiers to non-textual features. The invention may also be used to generate counterfactually augmented data for model training, thereby improving the robustness and fairness of downstream models by exposing them to conceptually diverse examples.

In summary, the invention represents a fundamental advancement in the field of explainable artificial intelligence by transforming contrastive explanations from a tool of textual manipulation into a tool of conceptual discovery. It enables users to see not just the surface-level changes that alter a prediction, but the underlying conceptual structure that governs the model’s behavior. This capability is essential for building trustworthy, accountable, and fair AI systems in high-stakes domains where transparency is not merely desirable, but imperative.
