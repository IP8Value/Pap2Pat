# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of heterogeneous catalysis for the conversion of oxygenated hydrocarbons, particularly bio-derived ethanol, into high-value liquid hydrocarbon blend-stock suitable for transportation fuels. More specifically, the invention pertains to a novel heterobimetallic zeolite catalyst composed of vanadium and indium species incorporated into the framework of ZSM-5 zeolite, wherein the synergistic interaction between the two metal cations results in a material with enhanced selectivity toward C3+ hydrocarbons, reduced ethylene production, and significantly improved resistance to deactivation by coking compared to monometallic analogues or physical mixtures thereof. The catalyst is particularly effective under conditions representative of industrial biomass-to-fuel processes, including the use of aqueous ethanol streams containing minor impurities derived from fermentation, thereby eliminating the need for energy-intensive dewatering steps. The invention further encompasses the method of synthesizing such a catalyst via sequential ion-exchange procedures and its application in continuous fixed-bed reactors for the production of renewable gasoline-range hydrocarbons with high octane numbers and low vapor pressure, making it uniquely suited for direct blending into conventional fuel pools without requiring downstream refining.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The global demand for sustainable transportation fuels has intensified efforts to convert renewable feedstocks such as lignocellulosic biomass into drop-in hydrocarbon fuels. Ethanol, produced in large quantities via fermentation of sugars derived from agricultural residues, remains one of the most promising bio-based feedstocks due to its high availability and established production infrastructure. However, ethanol possesses inherent limitations as a transportation fuel, including low energy density, hygroscopicity, and incompatibility with existing fuel distribution systems. Consequently, catalytic upgrading of ethanol into higher molecular weight hydrocarbons has emerged as a critical pathway to bridge the gap between bio-ethanol production and conventional fuel specifications. Numerous catalytic systems have been explored for this transformation, with zeolite-based materials, particularly H-ZSM-5, serving as the most extensively studied platforms due to their strong acidity, shape-selective pore structure, and proven efficacy in hydrocarbon conversion reactions.

Early efforts focused on the direct dehydration of ethanol over acidic zeolites to produce ethylene, followed by oligomerization and aromatization to form C3+ hydrocarbons. While H-ZSM-5 demonstrates high activity for ethanol conversion, its strong Brønsted acidity promotes excessive dehydration, resulting in high selectivity toward ethylene—a low-value product—and limited formation of the desired liquid-range hydrocarbons. Furthermore, the rapid formation of coke on the strong acid sites leads to swift catalyst deactivation, necessitating frequent regeneration cycles that increase operational costs and reduce process efficiency. To mitigate these issues, researchers have attempted to modify H-ZSM-5 by introducing metal cations to modulate acidity, suppress undesired side reactions, and enhance aromatization. Vanadium-modified ZSM-5 has been investigated for its ability to promote oxidative dehydrogenation pathways and reduce ethylene yield by consuming Brønsted acid sites through ion exchange. However, while V-ZSM-5 reduces ethylene production, it does not sufficiently enhance the formation of C3+ hydrocarbons, and its catalytic performance remains inferior to H-ZSM-5 in terms of overall hydrocarbon yield and liquid fraction quality.

Simultaneously, indium-modified ZSM-5 has been recognized for its exceptional ability to catalyze alkane aromatization, particularly in the context of propane and butane upgrading. Indium species, when incorporated into the zeolite lattice, are believed to form isolated cationic centers that facilitate dehydrogenation and cyclization reactions without promoting excessive cracking or coking. However, In-ZSM-5 alone exhibits low activity for ethanol conversion, as it lacks the necessary dehydration functionality to initiate the reaction cascade from ethanol. Attempts to combine the benefits of both metals through physical mixtures of V-ZSM-5 and In-ZSM-5 have yielded no synergistic improvement, as the resulting catalyst behaves as a simple additive of the two monometallic components, with no evidence of electronic or structural interaction between the metal species. Moreover, conventional characterization techniques such as X-ray diffraction, electron microscopy, and even X-ray absorption spectroscopy have proven inadequate in elucidating the precise nature of metal incorporation in bimetallic zeolites, leading to ambiguity regarding whether the observed catalytic behavior arises from true heterobimetallic sites or merely from spatial proximity of distinct monometallic domains.

The prevailing mechanistic understanding of ethanol conversion to hydrocarbons has long been dominated by the dehydration-oligomerization-aromatization pathway, wherein ethanol first undergoes acid-catalyzed dehydration to ethylene, which then undergoes oligomerization, cyclization, and hydrogen transfer reactions to form aromatics. Alternative mechanisms, including the hydrocarbon pool and free radical pathways, have been proposed but remain controversial due to insufficient experimental evidence distinguishing between them. In particular, the role of the initial dehydration step has been assumed to be obligatory, with little consideration given to the possibility of direct ethanol activation via alternative routes that bypass ethylene formation entirely. This assumption has constrained catalyst design strategies, leading to an overreliance on strong Brønsted acidity and neglect of alternative active site architectures that might enable more efficient and selective conversion pathways.

Furthermore, practical implementation of ethanol upgrading processes has been hindered by the economic burden associated with feedstock purification. Fermentation-derived ethanol streams invariably contain water, along with trace oxygenates such as acetaldehyde, methanol, acetic acid, and esters. Conventional processes require extensive dewatering to achieve anhydrous ethanol, a step that consumes substantial energy and increases capital expenditure. Previous studies have suggested that water may interfere with aromatic selectivity or accelerate catalyst deactivation, leading to the widespread belief that only highly concentrated or anhydrous ethanol streams are viable feedstocks. This perception has limited the scalability and economic feasibility of ethanol-to-hydrocarbon technologies, as the cost of dehydration often outweighs the value of the produced hydrocarbons. Thus, there exists a critical unmet need for a catalyst system that simultaneously achieves high selectivity toward liquid-range hydrocarbons, minimizes ethylene production, resists deactivation under prolonged operation, tolerates the presence of water and minor impurities in the feed, and operates efficiently at industrially relevant space velocities—all without requiring complex or costly feed pretreatment.

Despite extensive research, no prior art discloses a catalyst capable of meeting all these requirements in a single, integrated system. The development of a heterobimetallic zeolite catalyst that leverages the complementary properties of vanadium and indium in a structurally integrated manner, thereby enabling a novel reaction mechanism that circumvents the limitations of traditional pathways, represents a significant technological advancement. The discovery that such a catalyst not only outperforms its monometallic counterparts in terms of activity, selectivity, and durability but also functions effectively with aqueous ethanol feeds containing fermentation impurities constitutes a breakthrough with profound implications for the commercial viability of biomass-to-fuel processes.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a heterobimetallic zeolite catalyst comprising vanadium and indium species incorporated into the framework of a ZSM-5 zeolite, wherein the vanadium and indium are present in a synergistic arrangement that results in a material with substantially improved catalytic performance for the conversion of ethanol to liquid hydrocarbon blend-stock compared to any monometallic variant or physical mixture thereof. The catalyst is characterized by a vanadium loading of approximately 0.5 to 0.8 weight percent and an indium loading of approximately 1.5 to 1.8 weight percent, as determined by elemental analysis, with both metals being ion-exchanged into cationic sites within the zeolite pores rather than present as surface oxides or extraneous phases. The catalyst exhibits a unique spectroscopic signature in UV-Vis and Raman spectra, including an absorption band near 300 nm indicative of isolated In³⁺ and V⁵⁺ species and the absence of a characteristic band at 402 nm typically associated with octahedral vanadium oxide clusters, thereby confirming the absence of bulk vanadium oxide phases. The Raman spectrum further reveals two sharp, previously unobserved peaks at 484 cm⁻¹ and 919 cm⁻¹, which are not attributable to any known vanadium-indium oxide compound or surface adsorbate, and which persist after high-temperature pretreatment, indicating their origin in a stable, intrazeolitic heterobimetallic structure. The catalyst demonstrates complete ethanol conversion across a temperature range of 250 to 450 °C, with negligible formation of oxygenated byproducts, and produces a hydrocarbon product stream consisting of 19% C3–C4 olefins, 35% C3–C4 paraffins, and 33% C5+ liquid fraction, wherein the liquid fraction has an average molecular weight of approximately 97.6 g/mol, a specific gravity of 0.81, a Reid vapor pressure of 5.14 psi, and calculated research and motor octane numbers of 105.7 and 90.6, respectively. The liquid hydrocarbon product contains over 60% aromatics by volume, with minimal paraffin and olefin content, rendering it highly suitable for direct blending into gasoline pools without further refining.

Crucially, the catalyst exhibits a marked reduction in ethylene selectivity compared to V-ZSM-5 in the temperature range of 250 to 350 °C, while concurrently producing significantly higher yields of C3+ hydrocarbons than either V-ZSM-5 or In-ZSM-5 across the entire operating window. This selectivity profile is maintained over extended periods of operation, with the catalyst demonstrating a doubling of operational lifetime before requiring regeneration compared to V-ZSM-5 under identical conditions, due to a substantial reduction in coking rate. Regeneration by calcination at 500 °C fully restores catalytic activity, whereas regeneration at 450 °C results in only partial recovery, indicating that complete removal of carbonaceous deposits requires elevated temperatures. The catalyst retains its performance when exposed to aqueous ethanol streams containing up to 60% water and trace impurities such as acetaldehyde, methanol, diacetyl, ethyl acetate, and acetic acid at total concentrations below 0.1%, with no measurable change in product distribution or catalyst stability, thereby eliminating the necessity for energy-intensive feed dewatering. The catalyst is synthesized via a sequential ion-exchange procedure wherein a pre-formed V-ZSM-5 material is subjected to a second ion-exchange step with an indium salt solution under reflux conditions, ensuring that indium is incorporated into the zeolite lattice subsequent to vanadium, thereby promoting the formation of a structurally integrated heterobimetallic site rather than a physical mixture. The synthesis method is scalable, reproducible, and compatible with commercial zeolite precursors, enabling industrial implementation without requiring novel or proprietary equipment.

The invention further encompasses a method for converting ethanol to hydrocarbon blend-stock comprising contacting ethanol, optionally in aqueous form, with the heterobimetallic zeolite catalyst described herein at a temperature between 250 °C and 450 °C and a weight hourly space velocity between 1.0 and 6.0 h⁻¹, under conditions wherein the ethanol is completely converted to hydrocarbons and water, with the hydrocarbon product stream being separated from water by condensation and phase separation. The method is applicable to fermentation-derived ethanol streams without prior purification, thereby reducing process complexity and capital cost. The invention further provides that the catalyst operates via a reaction mechanism wherein ethanol does not necessarily undergo initial dehydration to ethylene as a prerequisite step, as evidenced by deuterium labeling experiments demonstrating the incorporation of deuterium into all hydrocarbon products except ethylene when deuterated ethanol or a mixture of deuterated ethanol and deuterated water is employed as feedstock, thereby supporting a hydrocarbon pool mechanism wherein ethanol directly adds to olefinic or aromatic intermediates within the zeolite pores, followed by dehydrogenation, cyclization, and hydrogen transfer reactions. The absence of deuterium incorporation in ethylene, despite its presence in the product stream, suggests that ethylene may be formed via secondary reactions within the hydrocarbon pool rather than as a primary intermediate. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the mechanistic understanding of ethanol upgrading, demonstrating that high selectivity to liquid hydrocarbons can be achieved without reliance on strong Brønsted acid sites for initial dehydration, and instead through the cooperative action of vanadium and indium species that facilitate direct ethanol activation and controlled C–C coupling.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The heterobimetallic zeolite catalyst of the present invention is a structurally and functionally distinct material that cannot be replicated by any combination of monometallic zeolites or physical mixtures thereof. The catalyst is composed of a ZSM-5 zeolite framework, characterized by a silica-to-alumina molar ratio of approximately 20 to 30, into which vanadium and indium cations have been incorporated via ion exchange, resulting in a homogeneous distribution of both metals within the microporous channels. The vanadium is present predominantly as V⁵⁺ species in tetrahedral coordination, as evidenced by the UV-Vis absorption band at approximately 286 nm, while the indium is present as isolated In³⁺ cations, as indicated by the absorption at 300 nm. The absence of a distinct absorption band at 402 nm, which is characteristic of octahedral V⁵⁺ species in bulk V₂O₅ or aggregated vanadium oxide clusters, confirms that vanadium is not present as a separate phase but is instead integrated into the zeolite lattice. This structural integration is further corroborated by the Raman spectroscopic data, wherein the spectrum of the heterobimetallic catalyst is not a superposition of the individual V-ZSM-5 and In-ZSM-5 spectra, but rather a distinct profile featuring two sharp, previously unreported peaks at 484 cm⁻¹ and 919 cm⁻¹. These peaks do not correspond to any known vanadium-indium oxide compound, nor do they arise from surface-adsorbed species, as their persistence after high-temperature treatment in air at 500 °C confirms their intrinsic nature. The fact that the Raman spectrum of a mechanical mixture of V-ZSM-5 and In-ZSM-5 is indistinguishable from that of pure V-ZSM-5, and lacks these two characteristic peaks, provides definitive evidence that the heterobimetallic catalyst is not a physical blend but a chemically unified material with a unique local structure.

The catalyst's performance in ethanol conversion is markedly superior to that of its monometallic counterparts. Under identical reaction conditions, V-ZSM-5 produces a hydrocarbon product stream with high ethylene selectivity, particularly in the lower temperature range of 250 to 350 °C, where ethylene accounts for over 40% of the hydrocarbon products. In contrast, the heterobimetallic catalyst reduces ethylene selectivity by more than 50% in this temperature window, while simultaneously increasing the yield of C3+ hydrocarbons by over 60% compared to V-ZSM-5 and by more than 80% compared to In-ZSM-5. This shift in selectivity is not merely a consequence of reduced acidity, as In-ZSM-5 alone exhibits negligible activity for ethanol conversion, nor is it attributable to a simple dilution of acid sites, as physical mixtures of V-ZSM-5 and In-ZSM-5 yield no improvement over V-ZSM-5 alone. Instead, the enhanced performance arises from a synergistic interaction between vanadium and indium species, wherein vanadium serves to moderate the strength and density of Brønsted acid sites, thereby suppressing excessive dehydration, while indium introduces dehydrogenation functionality that promotes the formation of unsaturated intermediates necessary for oligomerization and aromatization. The result is a catalyst that operates through a pathway distinct from the conventional dehydration-oligomerization-aromatization sequence, instead facilitating direct ethanol activation and coupling via a hydrocarbon pool mechanism.

The hydrocarbon product stream generated by the catalyst is of exceptional quality for fuel blending. The liquid fraction, which constitutes approximately one-third of the total hydrocarbon output, has an average molecular weight of 97.6 g/mol, placing it squarely within the gasoline range (C5–C10), and a specific gravity of 0.81, which is comparable to conventional gasoline. The Reid vapor pressure of 5.14 psi is well below the typical summer gasoline specification of 7 psi, indicating low volatility and reduced evaporative emissions. The calculated research octane number of 105.7 and motor octane number of 90.6 are significantly higher than those of conventional gasoline (typically 87–93 and 85–88, respectively), owing to the high aromatic content of 60.2% by volume. The liquid fraction contains over 350 distinct hydrocarbon compounds, with the majority being branched aromatics such as alkylbenzenes, alkyltoluenes, and naphthalene derivatives, which contribute to the high octane rating. The low olefin content (6.5%) and minimal paraffin content (3.8%) further enhance stability and reduce the tendency for gum formation during storage. The catalyst’s ability to produce such a high-quality liquid product without requiring downstream hydrotreating or isomerization represents a significant economic advantage over competing technologies.

The durability of the catalyst under continuous operation is another defining feature of the invention. When operated at a weight hourly space velocity of 4.0 h⁻¹ and a temperature of 350 °C, the catalyst maintains high C3+ hydrocarbon yield for approximately 12 hours before the yield drops below 80% of its initial value, whereas V-ZSM-5 exhibits a comparable decline after only 6 hours. This doubling of operational lifetime is attributed to a marked reduction in the rate of coke formation, which is a direct consequence of the modified acid site distribution and the suppression of ethylene polymerization pathways. The coke that does form is less graphitic and more readily removable, as evidenced by the full recovery of catalytic activity following regeneration at 500 °C in air for one hour. Regeneration at 450 °C, while sufficient to restore partial activity in V-ZSM-5, results in only 94% recovery for the heterobimetallic catalyst, suggesting that the coke deposits on this material are more thermally stable and require higher temperatures for complete oxidation. This observation further supports the notion that the nature of the coke is altered by the presence of indium, which may promote the formation of less condensed, more hydrogen-rich carbonaceous species rather than the highly condensed polyaromatic structures typical of ethylene polymerization on strong acid sites.

A critical innovation of the invention lies in its tolerance for aqueous ethanol feeds containing fermentation impurities. Unlike previous catalyst systems, which exhibit reduced aromatic selectivity or accelerated deactivation in the presence of water, the heterobimetallic catalyst performs identically whether fed with pure ethanol or with a 40% aqueous solution containing trace levels of acetaldehyde, methanol, diacetyl, ethyl acetate, and acetic acid at a combined concentration of less than 0.1%. Gas chromatographic profiles of the hydrocarbon products remain unchanged, and no new byproducts are observed, indicating that the impurities do not interfere with the catalytic mechanism or poison active sites. This tolerance is unprecedented and has profound implications for process economics, as it eliminates the need for expensive distillation or molecular sieve dehydration units, which typically consume 20–30% of the total energy input in ethanol upgrading processes. The catalyst’s ability to function effectively with low-grade, partially concentrated ethanol streams derived directly from fermentation reactors enables a simplified, integrated biorefinery architecture wherein ethanol undergoes catalytic upgrading immediately after primary separation, without intermediate purification steps.

The synthesis of the catalyst is carried out via a sequential ion-exchange procedure that ensures the formation of a true heterobimetallic structure rather than a mixture of monometallic domains. Commercial NH₄-ZSM-5 with a silica-to-alumina ratio of 23 is first converted to the H-form by calcination at 500 °C for four hours. Vanadium is then introduced by soaking the zeolite in a 0.05 M aqueous solution of V(III)Cl₃ at 80 °C under reflux for 16 hours, followed by filtration, washing, drying, and calcination at 500 °C to yield V-ZSM-5 with a vanadium loading of 0.6–0.7 wt%. The V-ZSM-5 material is then subjected to a second ion-exchange step with a 0.015 M aqueous solution of indium(III) nitrate under identical reflux conditions, resulting in the incorporation of indium at a loading of 1.6 wt%. The use of indium nitrate as the precursor ensures that the indium species are introduced in a form compatible with zeolite ion exchange, and the sequential nature of the process—where indium is added to a pre-formed V-ZSM-5 material—ensures that indium cations occupy sites adjacent to or in proximity with vanadium species, facilitating the formation of heterobimetallic centers. The final catalyst is calcined at 500 °C with a controlled ramp rate of 1 °C/min to ensure complete decomposition of nitrate residues and stabilization of the metal species within the zeolite framework. The resulting material is stable under reaction conditions, with no leaching of metals observed during extended operation, and retains its structural integrity as confirmed by post-reaction XRD and nitrogen physisorption measurements.

The mechanism by which the catalyst operates is fundamentally distinct from previously proposed pathways for ethanol conversion. Deuterium labeling experiments provide compelling evidence against the conventional dehydration mechanism as the primary initiation step. When deuterated ethanol (C₂H₅OD) is used as feedstock, deuterium is incorporated into all hydrocarbon products except ethylene, including ethane, propane, butanes, and aromatic compounds. This observation is inconsistent with a mechanism wherein ethanol first dehydrates to ethylene, as the deuterium would be lost as water and not retained in the hydrocarbon products. Similarly, when a mixture of 70% ethanol and 30% D₂O is employed, deuterium is again incorporated into all hydrocarbons except ethylene, further confirming that the source of deuterium is not the zeolite’s hydroxyl groups or cationic sites, as control experiments with D₂O and ethylene show no deuterium incorporation. The absence of deuterium in ethylene, despite its presence in the product stream, suggests that ethylene is not a primary intermediate but rather a secondary product formed via dehydrogenation or cracking of larger hydrocarbon pool intermediates. These findings strongly support a hydrocarbon pool mechanism, wherein ethanol molecules directly add to olefinic or aromatic species already present within the zeolite pores, forming ethoxy intermediates that undergo dehydration, rearrangement, and cyclization without passing through a free ethylene intermediate. The vanadium species likely facilitate the initial activation of ethanol by weakening the C–O bond, while the indium species promote dehydrogenation and hydrogen transfer reactions that drive the formation of unsaturated cyclic intermediates. The cooperative action of these two metals enables a reaction network that bypasses the high-energy barrier of ethylene formation and instead favors direct C–C coupling, resulting in higher selectivity to liquid hydrocarbons and reduced coking.

In-situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) provides further mechanistic insight. Upon exposure of the catalyst to ethanol at 350 °C, a transient band at 1690 cm⁻¹ appears within the first 30 seconds, which is assigned to linear alkenes such as propene or butene. This band is rapidly supplanted by a dominant band at 1567 cm⁻¹, corresponding to C=C stretches in alkylated aromatics, indicating that linear olefins are short-lived intermediates that quickly undergo cyclization and aromatization. Concurrently, the C–H stretching region evolves from a dominant peak at 2987 cm⁻¹, associated with terminal methyl groups, to a peak at 2911 cm⁻¹, indicative of methylene groups in alkyl side chains on aromatic rings. This spectral evolution confirms that the hydrocarbon pool consists of growing aromatic molecules with alkyl substituents, which are formed directly from ethanol addition rather than from ethylene oligomerization. The persistence of these features over time, coupled with the absence of ethoxy or diethyl ether signatures above 200 °C, further supports the conclusion that ethanol is activated directly on the metal sites rather than on Brønsted acid sites.

The catalyst’s performance is consistent across a broad range of operating conditions. At space velocities between 1.0 and 6.0 h⁻¹, complete ethanol conversion is maintained, with only minor variations in product distribution. The optimal temperature window is 300–400 °C, where C3+ selectivity is maximized and ethylene production is minimized. Below 250 °C, conversion is incomplete, while above 450 °C, cracking reactions become more prominent, leading to increased methane and ethane yields. The catalyst is stable under repeated regeneration cycles, with no measurable loss in activity or selectivity after five consecutive regeneration cycles involving air calcination at 500 °C for one hour. The zeolite framework remains intact, as confirmed by XRD and nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms, with no evidence of dealumination or collapse of the pore structure. The catalyst can be produced at scale using standard ion-exchange equipment and commercial zeolite precursors, making it economically viable for industrial deployment.

The invention thus provides a catalyst and method that fundamentally redefines the pathway for ethanol upgrading, enabling the direct conversion of low-grade, aqueous ethanol streams into high-octane, low-vapor-pressure hydrocarbon blend-stock with exceptional efficiency, durability, and economic advantage. The heterobimetallic structure of vanadium and indium in ZSM-5 is not merely an incremental improvement over existing catalysts but represents a new class of materials with unique structural, spectroscopic, and functional properties that enable a previously unattainable combination of performance characteristics. The invention eliminates the need for feed purification, extends catalyst lifetime, enhances product quality, and reduces energy consumption, thereby unlocking the full potential of biomass-derived ethanol as a sustainable source of transportation fuels.

### Example 1

In a representative embodiment of the invention, a heterobimetallic zeolite catalyst was prepared by first calcining commercial NH₄-ZSM-5 (CBV2314, SiO₂/Al₂O₃ = 23) at 500 °C for four hours under a flow of dry nitrogen to convert ammonium ions to protons, yielding H-ZSM-5. The resulting material was then subjected to ion exchange with a 0.05 M aqueous solution of vanadium(III) chloride (VCl₃) at 80 °C under reflux for 16 hours. The solid was collected by vacuum filtration, washed thoroughly with deionized water until the filtrate showed no chloride ions upon silver nitrate testing, dried at 105 °C overnight, and subsequently calcined at 500 °C for four hours under air to oxidize vanadium to the +5 oxidation state, yielding V-ZSM-5. Elemental analysis confirmed a vanadium loading of 0.68 wt%. This V-ZSM-5 material was then subjected to a second ion-exchange step using a 0.015 M aqueous solution of indium(III) nitrate nonahydrate (In(NO₃)₃·9H₂O) under identical reflux conditions at 80 °C for 16 hours. After filtration, washing, and drying, the material was calcined at 500 °C for four hours with a heating rate of 1 °C/min to ensure complete decomposition of nitrate ligands and stabilization of indium species. Final elemental analysis indicated a vanadium loading of 0.62 wt% and an indium loading of 1.58 wt%, confirming successful incorporation of both metals into the zeolite framework. The catalyst was ground to a particle size of 250–500 μm and loaded into a fixed-bed quartz reactor for catalytic testing.

Ethanol conversion was evaluated at a weight hourly space velocity of 4.0 h⁻¹ and a reactor inlet temperature of 350 °C. Pure ethanol was delivered via a syringe pump at a rate of 0.04 mL/h into a stream of 50 sccm helium, which was preheated to the reaction temperature. The reactor was held at temperature for 30 minutes prior to sampling to ensure steady-state conditions. Product gases were analyzed by online gas chromatography equipped with a HP-PLOT Q capillary column and a flame ionization detector, with nitrogen as an internal standard. Liquid hydrocarbons were trapped in a liquid nitrogen condenser, separated from water by phase separation, dried over anhydrous magnesium sulfate, and sent for comprehensive analysis by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry and SGS Oil, Gas, and Chemicals. The catalyst achieved 100% ethanol conversion with no detectable oxygenates in the product stream. The hydrocarbon product distribution consisted of 12.3% ethylene, 6.7% propylene, 5.1% isobutene, 17.2% n-butane, 17.8% iso-butane, 19.1% C3–C4 olefins, 34.6% C3–C4 paraffins, and 33.1% C5+ liquid fraction. The liquid fraction had an average molecular weight of 98.1 g/mol, a specific gravity of 0.808, a Reid vapor pressure of 5.09 psi, and calculated research and motor octane numbers of 106.2 and 91.1, respectively. The liquid product contained 60.5% aromatics, 5.3% olefins, 3.9% paraffins, 5.6% naphthenes, and 4.1% unidentified compounds by volume. The catalyst operated continuously for 12.5 hours before the C3+ yield dropped below 80% of its initial value, at which point it was regenerated by heating in air to 500 °C at a rate of 2 °C/min, holding for 15 minutes at 450 °C, then holding for one hour at 500 °C. Post-regeneration, the catalyst recovered 100% of its initial C3+ yield, with no change in product distribution or selectivity. The catalyst was subjected to five consecutive regeneration cycles with no measurable degradation in performance.

To evaluate feedstock flexibility, a 40% aqueous ethanol solution containing 0.08% acetaldehyde, 0.01% methanol, 0.005% diacetyl, 0.003% ethyl acetate, and 0.002% acetic acid was fed to the catalyst under identical conditions. The product distribution, including the relative intensities of all chromatographic peaks and the composition of the liquid fraction, was statistically indistinguishable from that obtained with pure ethanol. No new byproducts were detected, and the catalyst maintained its durability profile, operating for 12.2 hours before requiring regeneration. This demonstrated that the catalyst is not poisoned by fermentation impurities and that water does not interfere with the catalytic mechanism.

Deuterium labeling experiments were conducted using C₂H₅OD and a 70:30 mixture of C₂H₅OH and D₂O. In both cases, deuterium was detected in ethane, propane, butanes, and all aromatic compounds by mass spectrometry, but not in ethylene. Control experiments with D₂O and ethylene showed no deuterium incorporation into ethylene or other hydrocarbons, confirming that deuterium was not transferred via zeolite hydroxyl groups or cationic sites. These results are inconsistent with a dehydration-initiated mechanism and support a hydrocarbon pool pathway wherein ethanol adds directly to olefinic or aromatic intermediates, with deuterium being incorporated via hydrogen exchange during dehydrogenation and cyclization steps.

### Example 2

In a second embodiment, the heterobimetallic catalyst was synthesized using the same sequential ion-exchange protocol, but with a modified vanadium precursor. Instead of VCl₃, vanadium oxytrichloride (VOCl₃) was used to prepare V-ZSM-5. The VOCl₃ solution was prepared at 0.05 M in distilled water, and the ion-exchange procedure was carried out identically to Example 1. Subsequent indium exchange was performed with In(NO₃)₃·9H₂O as before. Elemental analysis revealed a vanadium loading of 0.65 wt% and an indium loading of 1.61 wt%. The UV-Vis spectrum of the resulting catalyst exhibited the same absorption features: a band at 286 nm attributable to tetrahedral V⁵⁺, a band at 300 nm assigned to In³⁺, and the absence of the 402 nm band indicative of octahedral vanadium. Raman spectroscopy again revealed the two characteristic peaks at 484 cm⁻¹ and 919 cm⁻¹, confirming structural similarity to the catalyst prepared with VCl₃. Catalytic testing under identical conditions (350 °C, WHSV 4.0 h⁻¹) yielded a product distribution of 11.9% ethylene, 6.5% propylene, 5.3% isobutene, 16.9% n-butane, 18.0% iso-butane, 19.4% C3–C4 olefins, 34.8% C3–C4 paraffins, and 33.0% C5+ liquid fraction. The liquid fraction had a molecular weight of 97.4 g/mol, a specific gravity of 0.812, a Reid vapor pressure of 5.11 psi, and octane numbers of 105.9 and 90.8. The catalyst demonstrated a 12.1-hour operational lifetime before C3+ yield declined below 80%, and full activity was restored after regeneration at 500 °C. These results confirm that the choice of vanadium precursor does not significantly alter the catalyst’s structure or performance, provided that the final vanadium loading and oxidation state are maintained.

To further validate the uniqueness of the heterobimetallic structure, a physical mixture of V-ZSM-5 (0.62 wt% V) and In-ZSM-5 (1.58 wt% In) was prepared by grinding the two materials in a 1:1 mass ratio and loading the mixture into the reactor under identical conditions. The physical mixture exhibited a product distribution nearly identical to that of V-ZSM-5 alone, with ethylene selectivity of 39.2% and C3+ yield only 12% higher than that of V-ZSM-5. The Raman spectrum of the mixture was indistinguishable from that of V-ZSM-5, lacking the 484 cm⁻¹ and 919 cm⁻¹ peaks. The catalyst deactivated after 6.3 hours, consistent with the behavior of V-ZSM-5. These results conclusively demonstrate that the enhanced performance of the heterobimetallic catalyst is not due to the mere presence of both metals, but to their structural integration within a single zeolite framework.

In a third variation, the catalyst was tested with a 60% aqueous ethanol feed containing 0.1% total impurities. The catalyst maintained 100% conversion and identical product distribution for 11.8 hours before regeneration was required. No signs of hydrolysis, dealumination, or metal leaching were detected by post-reaction XRD, ICP-MS, or BET surface area measurements. The catalyst was then subjected to 200 hours of continuous operation under alternating feed conditions—pure ethanol for 10 hours, followed by 40% aqueous ethanol for 10 hours—with no cumulative degradation in performance. This demonstrates the robustness of the catalyst under dynamic, real-world operating conditions.

The invention, as demonstrated in these examples, provides a catalyst and method that are not only technologically superior to all prior art but are also economically transformative. By enabling the use of low-grade, aqueous ethanol streams without purification, extending catalyst lifetime, enhancing product quality, and eliminating the need for downstream refining, the invention unlocks a viable pathway for the commercial production of renewable gasoline from biomass. The heterobimetallic structure of vanadium and indium in ZSM-5 is a novel material with unique properties that cannot be predicted from the behavior of its monometallic components, and its synthesis, characterization, and application represent a significant advancement in the field of catalytic biomass conversion.
