# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of trapped ion quantum computing has long been constrained by the inherent instability of conventional experimental architectures, which rely on manually assembled optical tables and open-air laser paths exposed to environmental perturbations such as thermal drift, acoustic noise, and air turbulence. While trapped ions offer some of the most coherent and controllable qubits known to modern physics—leveraging hyperfine states in isotopes such as 171Yb+ for long-lived quantum memory—their operational fidelity is critically dependent on the stability of the electromagnetic fields used to manipulate them. These fields, typically delivered via free-space laser beams, must maintain sub-wavelength positional and phase stability over extended durations to enable high-fidelity quantum logic gates, particularly those mediated by motional modes of the ion chain. Traditional setups, though flexible in design, suffer from cumulative misalignment due to minute thermal expansions in optical mounts, vibration coupling from cryogenic cooling systems, and pressure fluctuations in the vacuum environment that compromise ion lifetime and gate fidelity. The challenge is exacerbated when attempting to scale such systems into multi-qubit processors, where the number of optical paths increases exponentially and the cumulative effect of drift becomes prohibitive.

To achieve the vacuum conditions necessary for long-term ion confinement, experiments typically operate under ultrahigh vacuum (UHV) pressures below 10−9 torr, which requires extensive pumping and careful material selection to minimize outgassing. Conventional UHV chambers are large, complex, and often constructed from stainless steel with copper gasket seals, necessitating bake-out procedures and high-power vacuum pumps that are incompatible with cryogenic operation. Moreover, the integration of cryogenic cooling—essential for reducing thermal noise and enhancing cryopumping via getter materials—introduces additional mechanical instability through the motion of cryocooler expanders and thermal contraction of components. These mechanical perturbations couple directly into the ion trap, inducing motional heating and degrading the coherence of quantum operations. Existing cryogenic systems, while capable of reaching low temperatures, lack the mechanical decoupling and thermal management required to maintain both cryogenic stability and optical alignment simultaneously.

Furthermore, the delivery of laser light to the trapped ions has historically been accomplished through a patchwork of mirrors, lenses, and beam splitters mounted on adjustable posts, each introducing a potential point of misalignment. Even minor temperature fluctuations of a few degrees Celsius can cause micron-scale displacements in beam paths, which, when multiplied across multiple optical elements, result in phase errors exceeding the tolerance required for quantum gate operations. The use of optical fibers to route laser light has been explored as a solution to reduce free-space complexity, but prior implementations have failed to address the critical issue of mechanical coupling between the fiber output and the ion location. Without a rigid, monolithic, and thermally stabilized platform to anchor the final optical elements, fiber-coupled systems still suffer from drift and vibration-induced phase noise.

The need for a fundamentally new architectural approach becomes evident when considering the requirements for scalable quantum computing: reproducibility, modularity, long-term stability, and compatibility with cryogenic environments. Existing systems are bespoke, one-off constructions that cannot be replicated or upgraded without extensive realignment and recalibration. This lack of modularity impedes progress toward standardized quantum hardware platforms. There exists no prior art that integrates a compact, cryogenically cooled, hermetically sealed trap assembly with a monolithic optomechanical framework where all optical functions are implemented as precision-machined, interchangeable modules, each thermally anchored to a common substrate and aligned via dowel-pin referencing. Nor has any prior system combined differential pumping via a cryopumped trap enclosure with a fully fiber-coupled optical architecture that maintains sub-nanometer beam stability over extended operational periods in a closed-cycle cryostat.

The present invention addresses these longstanding challenges by introducing a novel integrated system architecture that redefines the physical implementation of trapped ion quantum processors. It combines a compact trap cryopackage—a hermetically sealed, copper-lidded, ceramic-pin-grid-array-based enclosure containing the ion trap, an ablation target, and activated carbon getter material—with a monolithic optomechanical structure composed of MIC-6 cast aluminum optical blocks, each housing a single optical function and mounted with sub-micron precision onto a common baseplate via dowel pins. The entire system operates within a closed-cycle cryostat whose sample space is rigidly anchored to the laboratory frame, eliminating the relative motion between the ion trap and the optical delivery system that has plagued previous designs. The optical blocks are temperature-stabilized using water-cooled baseplates, and all laser beams are delivered via single-mode optical fibers that terminate at fixed, alignment-critical points on each block. This design ensures that once assembled and aligned, the system remains stable for over a year without requiring manual intervention. The trap cryopackage itself is cooled to below 10 K, enabling cryopumping by the getter material to achieve UHV conditions within the sealed volume, while the external cryostat chamber operates at a higher temperature, thereby creating a differential pumping regime that isolates the trap from ambient pressure fluctuations. The system further incorporates a helical RF resonator mounted directly on the 90-K stage to minimize inductance and RF losses, and a custom imaging lens with infinite conjugate design to maximize collection efficiency while minimizing aberrations across thermal gradients. Together, these innovations form a self-contained, scalable, and reproducible platform for trapped ion quantum computing that achieves unprecedented levels of mechanical, thermal, and optical stability.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a novel integrated system for the stable manipulation of trapped ion qubits under ultrahigh vacuum and cryogenic conditions, designed specifically to enable high-fidelity quantum logic operations at scale. The system comprises three core innovations: a compact trap cryopackage, a monolithic optomechanical framework composed of precision-machined optical blocks, and a differential pumping architecture that enables ultrahigh vacuum within a cryogenic environment without compromising mechanical stability. The trap cryopackage is a hermetically sealed assembly consisting of a copper lid mechanically bonded to a 100-pin ceramic pin grid array (CPGA) package that supports the ion trap, an ablation target for ion loading, and a distributed array of activated carbon getter material. The lid is sealed to the CPGA via indium wires that provide both thermal conduction and mechanical retention, while the windows are fabricated from materials selected for their thermal expansion compatibility with copper and low gas permeability—sapphire for the imaging aperture and N-BK7 glass for the side viewports, all anti-reflection coated and bonded with cryogenic-grade epoxy. The top surface of the copper lid features multiple tapped holes for direct attachment to the cryostat’s cold finger, ensuring efficient thermal transfer and minimizing thermal gradients within the sealed volume. Upon cooldown to below 10 K, the getter material cryopumps residual gases, achieving a pressure in the trap region that is orders of magnitude lower than the surrounding cryostat chamber, thereby enabling long-term ion storage without contamination or loss.

The optomechanical framework is constructed around a rigid, monolithic foundation composed of a 3.5-centimeter-thick MIC-6 cast aluminum baseplate, which serves as the primary mechanical and thermal substrate for the entire system. Mounted atop this baseplate is a secondary optical breadboard, also fabricated from MIC-6 aluminum, featuring a grid of precisely positioned tapped holes and dowel pin receptacles that allow for the repeatable, sub-micron alignment of optical blocks. Each optical block is a self-contained module machined from a single piece of MIC-6 aluminum, housing all optical components necessary for a specific function—such as Doppler cooling, state detection, Raman beam delivery, or fluorescence imaging—mounted directly onto the block using dowel pins for absolute positioning. These blocks are thermally stabilized via water-cooled baseplates that maintain a constant temperature to within ±0.1 K, effectively eliminating thermal drift in optical alignment. Laser light is delivered to each block via single-mode optical fibers, which terminate at fixed input ports on the block, ensuring that the beam path from fiber to ion is entirely contained within a rigid, non-moving structure. The entire optomechanical assembly is enclosed within a metallic housing that suppresses air turbulence and acoustic noise, further enhancing stability. The system employs a closed-cycle cryostat with a uniquely anchored sample space, eliminating the relative motion between the cryocooler and the laboratory frame that typically introduces mechanical vibrations into the trap region. The cryostat’s 5-K sample mount is directly coupled to the trap cryopackage, while the 90-K stage supports ancillary components including a helical RF resonator, a cryogenic imaging lens, and a Raman focusing lens, all mounted with kinematic precision to minimize thermal stress and maximize optical performance.

The system further includes a frequency-stabilized laser architecture, wherein the 370-nm, 935-nm, and 399-nm lasers used for cooling, repumping, and photoionization are locked to high-finesse optical cavities via the Pound-Drever-Hall technique, with the cavity length itself stabilized against a rubidium vapor reference using Doppler-free spectroscopy. The 355-nm Raman laser, used for quantum gate operations, is generated by a mode-locked Nd:YAG laser frequency-tripled and split into a global beam and individually addressable beams, each modulated by acousto-optic modulators driven by programmable RF sources. These beams are coupled into photonic crystal fibers and delivered to the ion chain via downstream optical blocks that include MEMS-based beam steering mirrors for single-ion addressing, with elliptical beam shaping to match the numerical aperture constraints of the surface trap. The entire optical path, from laser source to ion, is fiber-coupled except for the final millimeters, which are fixed within the optical blocks, thereby decoupling the laser systems from the cryogenic environment and enabling independent testing and replacement of modules without realignment of the entire system. The system’s performance is validated through direct measurements of vacuum pressure via ion chain dynamics, motional heating rates, and optical coherence times. The pressure within the trap cryopackage is inferred from the flipping rate of a seven-ion zig-zag chain between degenerate configurations, revealing an exponential pressure-temperature dependence consistent with cryopumping by activated carbon, with estimated pressures below 5×10−11 torr at 8 K. The radial motional heating rate is measured at 13±5.1 quanta per second, which, when normalized for ion mass and trap frequency, yields an electric field noise spectral density of 3.2×10−7 V²/m²/Hz—significantly below the average of prior cryogenic surface traps. The optical coherence time of the Raman beams, measured via Ramsey interferometry on the carrier transition, is found to be 330 milliseconds, demonstrating that phase fluctuations are negligible over the duration of entangling gates. Together, these metrics confirm that the system achieves a level of mechanical, thermal, and optical stability previously unattainable in trapped ion platforms, enabling quantum gate fidelities approaching the fault-tolerant threshold without requiring active feedback or real-time alignment correction.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE DRAWINGS

The system of the present invention is composed of a tightly integrated suite of mechanical, thermal, optical, and vacuum subsystems, each designed to function in concert to achieve unprecedented stability in a cryogenic trapped ion environment. The architecture is best understood through its constituent components, each of which is illustrated in the accompanying figures and described herein with reference to their functional role, material composition, and interconnection with the broader system.

The foundational element of the system is the closed-cycle cryostat, which provides the low-temperature environment necessary for cryopumping and thermal noise suppression. As depicted in Figure 2, the cryostat is a Montana Instruments Cryostation s200, modified to anchor its sample space rigidly to the laboratory tabletop rather than suspending it from the top, as is conventional in prior designs. This modification eliminates the mechanical coupling between the cryocooler’s expander motion and the ion trap, a primary source of vibration in traditional systems. The cryostat’s internal structure includes a large, flat 90-K stage with a grid of tapped holes for mounting ancillary components, and a central 5-K base plate upon which the trap cryopackage is mounted. The 5-K stage is thermally isolated from the 90-K stage by a vacuum gap and radiation shields, ensuring that the trap region remains at the lowest possible temperature. The cryostat housing is sealed by two o-ring seals—one at the base and one at the lid—creating a primary vacuum chamber that operates at approximately 10−8 torr. This chamber is not intended to achieve ultrahigh vacuum; rather, it serves as a buffer zone that isolates the trap cryopackage from ambient pressure fluctuations. Temperature sensors are distributed throughout the system, including on the 5-K base plate, the sample mount, and the trap cryopackage lid, providing real-time feedback for thermal control and diagnostics.

The trap cryopackage, illustrated in detail in Figure 4, is the heart of the vacuum system and represents a radical departure from conventional UHV trap enclosures. It consists of a copper lid, machined from high-purity oxygen-free copper, sealed to a 100-pin alumina ceramic pin grid array (CPGA) package that houses the ion trap. The lid is not hermetically sealed in the traditional sense; instead, it is mechanically retained by indium wires pressed between a ringframe brazed to the CPGA and a matching groove in the lid. This design provides sufficient adhesion to withstand handling and installation while allowing for thermal expansion mismatch between the copper and alumina materials. The indium wires serve a dual purpose: they provide a low-resistance thermal path from the lid to the CPGA, ensuring efficient heat transfer to the getter material, and they act as a breakable mechanical bond, enabling the cryopackage to be removed and replaced without damaging the CPGA socket. The top surface of the copper lid is machined with eight tapped holes for direct attachment to the 5-K sample mount, ensuring that the entire assembly is thermally anchored to the coldest point in the system. The lid contains an internal cavity filled with activated carbon getter material, encapsulated in a fine copper mesh to prevent particulate release. Upon cooldown, the getter material adsorbs residual gases, primarily hydrogen and water vapor, achieving a pressure in the sealed volume below 10−10 torr. The lid features two optical windows: a central sapphire window, 15 mm in diameter and 2 mm thick, for fluorescence imaging, and two side N-BK7 glass windows, 5 mm in diameter and 2 mm thick, for laser delivery. The sapphire window is chosen for its coefficient of thermal expansion closely matched to that of copper and titanium, minimizing stress during thermal cycling, and for its low permeability to helium and hydrogen. The side windows are made of N-BK7 to avoid the birefringence-induced astigmatism inherent in sapphire, as the laser beams traversing these windows are not polarization-sensitive. All windows are anti-reflection coated and bonded to the lid using Epotek T7110, a cryogenic-compatible epoxy with low outgassing and high adhesion strength at low temperatures. An oblong conical ground shield is machined beneath the sapphire window to extend the field of view of the imaging optics while shielding the ion from electric fields arising from charge buildup on the dielectric surface. An internal ablation target holder is positioned opposite one of the side windows, allowing for orthogonal illumination by the 399-nm photoionization laser, which enables isotope-selective loading of 171Yb atoms into the trap.

The ion trap itself is a Sandia HOA 2.0 surface electrode trap, mounted on a ceramic interposer that elevates the trap surface to be coplanar with the optical windows. The interposer contains trench capacitors that filter high-frequency noise from the DC electrodes, preventing unwanted coupling into the trap potential. The trap surface is coated with a 0.5-micron layer of gold to enhance conductivity and reduce resistive heating. The entire trap cryopackage is assembled in a class-100 cleanroom to minimize particulate contamination, and then pressed into the 100-pin socket of a printed circuit board (PCB) that provides electrical connectivity to the trap electrodes. The PCB is thermally lagged at the 90-K stage using phosphor bronze flexi-PCB ribbon cables, which have low thermal conductivity and are insulated with Kapton to minimize heat flow from room temperature to the 5-K stage. The RF signal is delivered via a coaxial cable connected to a helical resonator mounted on the 90-K stage, as shown in Figure 5. The helical resonator is a compact, 6.9-cm-long copper coil with a thick copper wire output that terminates directly at the trap PCB’s RF pin. A grounded copper shielding tube bridges the length of the cable to suppress electromagnetic interference and reduce inductance. Measurements of the resonator’s Q-factor during cooldown show a steady increase from 40 at room temperature to 120 at base temperature, indicating a significant reduction in resistive losses and confirming the effectiveness of the cryogenic design.

The optical delivery system is organized around a monolithic optomechanical framework, as shown in Figures 1, 3, and 5. The primary substrate is a 3.5-cm-thick MIC-6 cast aluminum baseplate, measuring 0.89 meters on each side, which serves as the mechanical foundation for the entire system. MIC-6 is selected for its low internal stress, excellent flatness, and granular microstructure that allows for high-speed machining without distortion. Mounted atop this baseplate is a secondary optical breadboard, also fabricated from MIC-6, with a grid of 1/4-20 tapped holes spaced 2 inches apart and two precision 2-mm dowel pin holes adjacent to each tapped hole. This configuration allows for the repeatable, sub-micron alignment of optical blocks via dowel pins, eliminating the need for manual adjustment after initial assembly. Each optical block is a self-contained module, machined from a single piece of MIC-6 aluminum, and contains all components necessary for a specific optical function. For example, the imaging block (shown in red in Figure 3) contains a high-numerical-aperture (0.6 NA) cryogenic imaging lens, a multimode fiber array, and a set of mirrors to collect fluorescence from individual ions and direct it to the detection system. The CW beam delivery block (green in Figure 3) contains a periscope system that redirects the 370-nm and 935-nm laser beams from the fiber input to a downward 45-degree angle, allowing them to pass through the top viewport of the cryostat and illuminate the ion chain. The Raman beam delivery block contains a pair of MEMS tilting mirrors, each capable of steering a 1.5-micron beam waist to individual ions in the chain with minimal crosstalk, and a set of elliptical shaping optics to match the numerical aperture of the trap’s surface electrodes. All optical components within each block are mounted using dowel pins referenced to a common datum on the block, ensuring that the relative positions of lenses, mirrors, and fibers are fixed to within 1 micron. Each block is thermally stabilized by a water-cooled baseplate that circulates chilled water at a constant temperature, maintaining the block within ±0.1 K of its setpoint. This thermal control eliminates drift due to ambient temperature fluctuations, a primary source of misalignment in traditional systems. The entire optomechanical structure is enclosed within a metallic housing that suppresses air turbulence and acoustic noise, further enhancing stability. The system has been operated continuously for over a year without requiring any manual realignment of the optical components, a feat unattainable in any prior trapped ion system.

The laser systems are divided into three functional categories: continuous wave (CW) lasers for cooling and detection, frequency-stabilized reference lasers, and the Raman laser for quantum gates. The CW lasers include a 399-nm laser for photoionizing neutral 171Yb atoms, a 370-nm laser for Doppler cooling and state detection, and a 935-nm laser for repumping population from the metastable D3/2 state. These lasers are mounted on separate optical blocks, each of which is temperature-stabilized and fiber-coupled. The 370-nm and 935-nm lasers are stabilized to optical cavities using the Pound-Drever-Hall technique, with the cavity length locked to a 780-nm reference laser that is itself stabilized against a rubidium vapor cell using Doppler-free spectroscopy. This ensures that the frequency of the cooling and repumping lasers remains stable to within a few kilohertz over extended periods. The 355-nm Raman laser is generated by a mode-locked Nd:YAG laser frequency-tripled using nonlinear crystals, and its output is split into a global beam and two individually addressable beams. Each beam passes through a series of acousto-optic modulators (AOMs) driven by programmable RF sources, allowing for precise control of frequency, phase, and amplitude. The beams are then coupled into single-mode photonic crystal fibers, which deliver them to the downstream optical blocks. The global beam is shaped into an elongated elliptical profile to uniformly illuminate the entire ion chain, while the individual beams are focused to 1.5-micron waists using a cryogenic Raman projection lens mounted on the 90-K stage. The use of photonic crystal fiber ensures single-mode propagation and minimizes modal noise, while the fixed termination points on the optical blocks ensure that the final beam path is entirely rigid and stable.

The system’s vacuum performance is characterized indirectly through the dynamics of a seven-ion zig-zag chain, as shown in Figure 8. In the absence of an ion gauge within the sealed trap cryopackage, pressure is inferred from the rate at which the ion chain flips between two degenerate zig-zag configurations. The energy barrier between these configurations is estimated to be approximately 0.02 meV, and the probability of a collision-induced flip is modeled using a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution of background gas molecules. By measuring the flip rate as a function of temperature, and applying a Langevin model that accounts for the polarizability of hydrogen molecules and the reduced mass of the ion-molecule system, the pressure inside the cryopackage is calculated. The resulting pressure-temperature curve exhibits an exponential dependence, consistent with the desorption of hydrogen from the activated carbon getter. At 8 K, the estimated pressure is below 5×10−11 torr, demonstrating that the cryopumping mechanism is highly effective. This level of vacuum enables ion chains to be stored for hours without loss due to collisions, a critical requirement for multi-qubit operations.

The motional heating rate is measured by first cooling the ions to an average of 0.09 quanta of excitation using resolved sideband cooling, then allowing them to heat for variable durations before measuring the resulting excitation via sideband spectroscopy. The heating rate is extracted from the linear slope of excitation versus wait time, yielding a mean value of 13 quanta per second with a standard deviation of 5.1 quanta per second. When normalized for ion mass and trap frequency, this corresponds to an electric field noise spectral density of 3.2×10−7 V²/m²/Hz, which is an order of magnitude lower than the average of prior cryogenic surface traps. This low noise level is attributed to the combination of cryogenic operation, the use of gold-coated electrodes, and the absence of moving parts or high-voltage feedthroughs near the trap.

Finally, the optical coherence time of the Raman beams is measured using Ramsey interferometry on the carrier transition. A π/2 pulse prepares the qubit in a superposition state, followed by a variable delay and a second π/2 pulse with a phase shift. The resulting interference fringe contrast decays exponentially with delay time, yielding a coherence time of 330 milliseconds. This value exceeds the duration of any entangling gate in the system, confirming that phase fluctuations in the Raman beams are not a limiting factor for gate fidelity. The stability is maintained by the rigid, fiber-coupled, temperature-stabilized optical blocks, which prevent any relative motion between the laser source and the ion.

In summary, the system described herein represents a complete, integrated architecture for trapped ion quantum computing that achieves a level of mechanical, thermal, and optical stability previously unattainable. By combining a cryopumped trap enclosure, a monolithic optomechanical framework with precision-machined optical blocks, and a fully fiber-coupled laser delivery system, the invention eliminates the primary sources of instability in prior systems and enables the reliable, long-term operation of high-fidelity quantum gates. This architecture is not merely an incremental improvement but a fundamental rethinking of how trapped ion systems are constructed, paving the way for scalable, reproducible, and commercially viable quantum processors.
