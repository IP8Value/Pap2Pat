# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to novel methods and compositions for the modulation of chronic bacterial infection, particularly those involving Helicobacter pylori, through targeted structural modification of the urease enzyme complex. More specifically, the invention encompasses genetically engineered variants of the H. pylori urease protein that retain full enzymatic activity for urea hydrolysis but exhibit altered surface topography at discrete epitope regions, thereby impairing the bacterium’s ability to establish long-term persistence within the gastric mucosa of a mammalian host. These engineered urease variants are not merely attenuated strains but are precisely defined molecular constructs that decouple the acid-neutralizing function of urease from its immunomodulatory role in host-pathogen interaction. The invention further provides for the use of such modified urease proteins as components of therapeutic vaccines, diagnostic reagents, or as molecular tools for probing host immune recognition pathways, particularly those involving CD74-mediated signaling, dendritic cell modulation, and T-helper cell polarization. The utility of the invention extends to the development of next-generation anti-H. pylori interventions that target persistent colonization rather than acute infection, offering a strategic departure from conventional antimicrobial approaches that have largely failed due to bacterial resistance and immune evasion.

## BACKGROUND

Helicobacter pylori is a Gram-negative, microaerophilic bacterium that chronically colonizes the gastric epithelium of more than half the global human population. Its persistence is associated with a spectrum of gastroduodenal pathologies, including chronic gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, gastric adenocarcinoma, and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma. Despite decades of research and the availability of effective antibiotic regimens, eradication rates remain suboptimal due to rising antimicrobial resistance, poor patient compliance, and the bacterium’s remarkable capacity to evade host immune clearance. The failure of prior vaccine candidates to confer durable protection underscores a critical gap in understanding the molecular mechanisms that enable H. pylori to establish and maintain lifelong infection. While the bacterium’s ability to neutralize gastric acid via urease activity has long been recognized as essential for initial colonization, recent evidence suggests that urease serves additional, non-enzymatic roles in immune modulation that are indispensable for chronic persistence. Urease, a nickel-containing enzyme composed of a dodecameric assembly of UreA and UreB subunits arranged as four alpha/beta trimers, constitutes up to ten percent of the total bacterial proteome and is among the most abundant and immunogenic antigens expressed by H. pylori. Its surface-exposed regions have been implicated in direct interactions with host receptors such as CD74, a non-polymorphic MHC class II invariant chain expressed on gastric epithelial cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages. Binding of urease to CD74 triggers intracellular signaling cascades that result in the upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-8, and modulates the balance between Th1 and Th2 immune responses. This immunomodulatory function appears to be critical for the bacterium’s ability to subvert host defenses and establish a state of controlled inflammation that supports its long-term survival without triggering sterilizing immunity. Prior attempts to exploit urease as a vaccine antigen have focused on its enzymatic activity or its overall immunogenicity, often resulting in immune responses that fail to prevent colonization or even exacerbate pathology. The discovery that discrete surface regions of urease, distinct from the catalytic site, are required for persistence but dispensable for acid neutralization represents a paradigm shift in the design of anti-H. pylori therapeutics. The present invention builds upon this insight by identifying and characterizing specific structural motifs on the urease surface that, when modified, abrogate the bacterium’s ability to persist in the host while preserving its enzymatic function. These modifications do not impair bacterial viability or urease activity under acidic conditions, nor do they affect the structural integrity of the enzyme’s catalytic core. Instead, they selectively disrupt interactions with host immune cells, thereby rendering the bacterium vulnerable to clearance by adaptive immune mechanisms that are normally suppressed or misdirected during wild-type infection. This targeted disruption of immune evasion, rather than direct bacterial killing, represents a novel therapeutic strategy with broad implications for the treatment of chronic infections where immune tolerance is a key barrier to eradication.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for attenuating Helicobacter pylori persistence in a mammalian host by introducing specific, non-disruptive modifications to the surface of the urease enzyme complex, thereby impairing its ability to modulate host immune responses without compromising its ureolytic function. The invention is based on the unexpected discovery that certain surface-exposed regions of the urease dodecamer—particularly those located at the interface between alpha/beta trimers—are critical for long-term colonization but not for acid resistance or enzymatic activity. By introducing in-frame epitope insertions at these sites, the inventors generated H. pylori strains that colonize the gastric mucosa initially but are subsequently cleared during the chronic phase of infection, despite maintaining wild-type levels of urease expression and acid-neutralizing capacity. The invention encompasses genetically modified urease proteins comprising amino acid insertions at positions corresponding to site 8 of the UreB subunit, which is located within a beta-meander region proximal to the trimer-trimer rotational axis. These modifications do not alter the catalytic site, the nickel-binding pocket, or the overall quaternary structure of the enzyme, yet they significantly reduce the bacterium’s ability to induce sustained Th2 polarization, impair dendritic cell modulation, and diminish CD74-mediated signaling. The invention further provides for the use of such modified urease proteins as immunogens in vaccines designed to elicit protective immune responses against H. pylori, as well as for their use in diagnostic assays to detect strain-specific immune signatures associated with chronic infection. The modified urease variants of the invention are characterized by their ability to retain full enzymatic activity under acidic conditions, as demonstrated by their capacity to hydrolyze urea at pH levels as low as 3.0, while simultaneously failing to elicit the immune tolerance mechanisms that permit wild-type H. pylori to persist for months or years. The invention further includes compositions comprising purified recombinant urease proteins containing the site 8 modification, as well as nucleic acid constructs encoding such proteins, for use in gene therapy, DNA vaccination, or recombinant protein delivery systems. The invention also provides for the use of these modified urease variants in high-throughput screening assays to identify small molecules or biologics that mimic their immunomodulatory effects, thereby enabling the development of novel host-directed therapies for chronic H. pylori infection. The invention further encompasses methods for generating transgenic bacterial strains, viral vectors, or synthetic nanoparticles displaying the modified urease surface, for targeted delivery to gastric epithelial cells or antigen-presenting cells. The utility of the invention lies in its ability to transform a key virulence factor into a tool for immune re-education, converting a pathogen’s own mechanism of persistence into a vulnerability that can be exploited for therapeutic benefit.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS OF THE INVENTION

### Isolation of Clinical Strains

The isolation of clinical strains of Helicobacter pylori for the purposes of this invention was conducted under strict biosafety conditions using gastric biopsy specimens obtained from patients diagnosed with chronic gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, or gastric adenocarcinoma following endoscopic examination. All specimens were collected in accordance with institutional review board-approved protocols and with informed consent from patients. Biopsy samples were immediately transported in sterile, anaerobic transport medium containing brain heart infusion supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 0.1% sodium bicarbonate to maintain physiological pH and osmolarity. Upon arrival in the laboratory, tissues were homogenized using a sterile tissue grinder in a laminar flow hood under microaerobic conditions (5% oxygen, 10% carbon dioxide, 85% nitrogen) to preserve bacterial viability. The homogenates were serially diluted in phosphate-buffered saline and plated onto selective agar media composed of brain heart infusion supplemented with 5% defibrinated horse blood, 10 µg/mL of erythromycin, and 10 µg/mL of streptomycin to inhibit the growth of contaminating flora. Plates were incubated at 37°C for five to seven days under microaerobic conditions, and colonies exhibiting characteristic spiral morphology, positive oxidase and catalase activity, and urease positivity as determined by rapid urease test were selected for further characterization. Isolates were confirmed as H. pylori by amplification and sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene, the ureA and ureB loci, and the vacA and cagA virulence genes. A total of 120 clinical isolates were obtained from diverse geographic regions, including North America, Europe, East Asia, and South America, to ensure broad genetic representation. Each isolate was cryopreserved in glycerol at −80°C and passaged no more than three times in vitro to minimize selection bias. The genomic DNA of each isolate was extracted using a commercial phenol-chloroform method followed by ethanol precipitation and resuspension in TE buffer. The ureA and ureB genes were amplified by polymerase chain reaction using high-fidelity DNA polymerase and primers designed to flank the entire coding sequence, including upstream and downstream regulatory regions. The resulting amplicons were sequenced using Sanger sequencing technology, and the amino acid sequences of UreA and UreB were aligned against the reference strain 26695 to identify conserved and variable regions. Structural modeling of the urease dodecamer was performed using the crystallographic coordinates of the H. pylori urease complex (PDB ID: 1E9Y), and surface accessibility was calculated using the NACCESS algorithm to identify solvent-exposed loops with high thermal mobility, as indicated by B-factor values exceeding the mean for the entire protein. Regions with average B-factors above 60 Å² were prioritized for mutagenesis, as these were deemed most likely to tolerate structural perturbations without disrupting the enzyme’s catalytic core. Eight distinct surface-exposed loops were identified as candidate sites for epitope insertion, including the N- and C-termini of UreA and UreB, as well as six internal loops located at positions corresponding to residues 37–42, 128–133, 172–177, 220–225, 280–285, and 310–315 of UreB. These sites were selected based on their location on the exterior surface of the dodecamer, their proximity to the trimer-trimer interface, and their absence of conserved residues across phylogenetically diverse H. pylori strains. The clinical isolates were then subjected to natural transformation with PCR-generated DNA cassettes encoding HA and FLAG epitopes flanked by semi-random six-amino-acid linkers designed to minimize structural strain. Transformants were selected on acidified agar plates containing urea and phenol red, and only those colonies exhibiting a color change from yellow to red, indicative of urease activity at pH 5.0, were retained for further analysis. Western blot analysis confirmed the presence of epitope-tagged urease in all transformants, and the integrity of the enzyme’s quaternary structure was verified by native PAGE and size-exclusion chromatography. The clinical isolates that successfully incorporated the epitope insertions without loss of urease function were designated as candidate strains for in vivo colonization studies. These strains were then subjected to rigorous genetic characterization to ensure that the observed phenotypes were attributable solely to the introduced mutations and not to secondary chromosomal alterations. Whole-genome sequencing of each transformant was performed using Illumina technology, and the resulting reads were mapped against the reference genome to confirm the absence of off-target mutations. Only strains with a single, clean insertion at the intended site and no additional polymorphisms were carried forward for murine infection experiments. The successful isolation and characterization of these clinical strains established a robust platform for the generation of genetically defined urease variants, each with a precisely engineered surface topology, enabling the systematic dissection of the relationship between urease structure and immune evasion in chronic H. pylori infection.

### Example 2

In this example, the functional integrity of the urease enzyme in the genetically modified H. pylori strains was rigorously assessed to confirm that the introduced epitope insertions did not compromise the enzyme’s catalytic activity or its remarkable acid stability. Bacterial cultures of wild-type and mutant strains were grown to mid-log phase in brain heart infusion broth supplemented with 7% horse serum and harvested by centrifugation. The bacterial pellets were washed twice in cold saline solution to remove residual media components and then resuspended to a standardized optical density at 600 nm of 4.0 to ensure equal bacterial load across all samples. Each suspension was divided into two aliquots: one was immediately neutralized with phosphate-buffered saline (pH 6.8) and served as the control, while the other was exposed to an acidic environment by addition of potassium chloride buffer adjusted to pH 3.0 and supplemented with 0.2% Tween 20 to enhance membrane permeability. The samples were incubated at room temperature for ten minutes with continuous shaking at 300 rpm to simulate the transient acidic stress encountered by H. pylori during gastric transit. Following acid exposure, the suspensions were neutralized by the addition of an equal volume of phosphate-buffered saline (pH 6.8), and the urease activity of each sample was quantified using a colorimetric assay based on the pH-dependent conversion of phenol red. A volume of 150 µL of each neutralized sample was mixed with 25 µL of phenol red sodium salt (80 mg/mL) and pre-incubated at 37°C for five minutes to equilibrate temperature. The reaction was initiated by the addition of 75 µL of 0.5 M urea, and the change in absorbance at 560 nm was monitored every 70 seconds for a total of 10 minutes using a spectrophotometer equipped with a temperature-controlled cuvette holder. The rate of color change, which corresponds directly to the production of ammonia and carbonate from urea hydrolysis, was calculated as the slope of the linear phase of the absorbance curve. All mutant strains, including those with insertions at sites 1, 3, 4, and 8, exhibited urease activity comparable to that of the wild-type strain, with no statistically significant difference in the rate of pH change (p > 0.05, Student’s t-test). Notably, the strain carrying the insertion at site 1, which had previously been shown to exhibit reduced acid stability in preliminary assays, demonstrated no significant difference in urease activity following acid exposure compared to the wild-type, indicating that the mutation did not impair the enzyme’s ability to function under low pH conditions. Similarly, the strain with the insertion at site 8, which was later shown to be defective in chronic colonization, maintained urease activity indistinguishable from that of the wild-type, confirming that the loss of persistence was not due to a failure in acid neutralization. To further validate these findings, a second assay was performed using intact bacterial cells to assess urease activity under conditions that more closely mimic the gastric lumen. Bacterial suspensions were incubated in artificial gastric juice containing pepsin (1 mg/mL), hydrochloric acid (pH 2.5), and mucin (0.5%) for 30 minutes at 37°C, followed by neutralization and urease activity measurement as described above. Again, all mutant strains retained full enzymatic capacity, demonstrating that the epitope insertions did not interfere with the enzyme’s structural resilience in the presence of gastric proteases or mucus barriers. Additionally, the stability of the urease complex was assessed by subjecting bacterial lysates to thermal denaturation. Samples were heated at 65°C for 30 minutes and then cooled on ice before being analyzed by native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. The dodecameric structure of urease was preserved in all strains, with no evidence of subunit dissociation or aggregation, indicating that the insertions did not destabilize the quaternary assembly. Furthermore, the nickel content of purified urease from each strain was quantified using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry, and no significant differences were detected between wild-type and mutant enzymes, confirming that the catalytic metal center remained intact. These results collectively demonstrate that the epitope insertions introduced at the surface of urease do not impair its enzymatic function, its resistance to acidic denaturation, or its structural integrity under physiologically relevant conditions. The preservation of these critical properties confirms that any observed phenotypic differences in host colonization must arise from alterations in host-pathogen interactions rather than from a loss of ureolytic capacity.

### Example 3

In this example, the ability of the genetically modified H. pylori strains to colonize the gastric mucosa of C57BL/6 mice was evaluated over both acute and chronic time points to determine whether the surface modifications of urease affected the bacterium’s capacity to establish and maintain infection. Eight-week-old, Helicobacter-free C57BL/6 mice were obtained from a specific pathogen-free facility and acclimated for one week under controlled environmental conditions. Each mouse was orogastrically inoculated with a pool of three independent clonal isolates expressing either wild-type urease or one of the eight mutant variants, ensuring that any observed phenotype was not attributable to a single genetic background or secondary mutation. The inoculum contained approximately 1.0 × 10⁹ colony-forming units suspended in 100 µL of brain heart infusion broth and was delivered via oral gavage using a sterile feeding needle. Mice were monitored daily for signs of distress, weight loss, or gastrointestinal symptoms, and no adverse effects were observed in any group. At three months post-inoculation, a subset of mice from each group was euthanized, and their stomachs were harvested for bacterial quantification. The gastric tissue was homogenized in 500 µL of phosphate-buffered saline using a tissue lyser with stainless steel beads, and serial dilutions were plated on selective agar media containing antibiotics to inhibit commensal flora. After five to seven days of incubation under microaerobic conditions, colony-forming units per gram of gastric tissue were counted. All mutant strains, including those with insertions at sites 1, 3, 4, and 8, colonized the stomachs of mice at levels indistinguishable from the wild-type strain, with mean bacterial loads ranging from 1.2 × 10⁶ to 2.5 × 10⁶ CFU/g. These results confirmed that the surface modifications did not impair the bacterium’s ability to survive gastric transit, adhere to the mucosal epithelium, or initiate acute infection. However, when the infection was extended to ten months, a striking divergence in colonization dynamics became evident. While wild-type H. pylori and the strains carrying insertions at sites 1, 3, and 4 maintained stable bacterial loads throughout the chronic phase, the strain expressing urease with the site 8 insertion exhibited a dramatic decline in gastric colonization, with mean bacterial titers falling below the limit of detection in 80% of the mice (less than 10³ CFU/g). The remaining 20% of mice in the site 8 group showed only transient, low-level persistence, with bacterial counts never exceeding 10⁴ CFU/g, in stark contrast to the sustained colonization observed in all other groups. To confirm that this reduction in persistence was not due to the loss of the inserted DNA cassette, bacterial colonies recovered from the stomachs of infected mice at ten months were subjected to PCR amplification using primers flanking the insertion site. In every case, the expected epitope-containing urease gene was still present, indicating that the loss of colonization was not the result of genetic reversion or deletion. Furthermore, Western blot analysis of bacterial lysates confirmed the continued expression of the tagged urease protein at levels comparable to those observed in the wild-type strain, ruling out transcriptional or translational silencing as a contributing factor. The humoral immune response to infection was also assessed by measuring serum IgG antibodies specific for total H. pylori antigens and for the UreB subunit. Mice infected with the site 8 mutant strain exhibited normal levels of total anti-H. pylori IgG, indicating that the immune system was fully capable of recognizing and responding to the bacterium. However, antibody titers specific for UreB were significantly reduced in these mice compared to those infected with wild-type or other mutant strains, suggesting a selective impairment in the recognition of the modified urease epitope. This finding was corroborated by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays performed using purified recombinant UreB protein as the coating antigen, which revealed a 70% reduction in UreB-specific IgG in the site 8-infected group. Importantly, the total IgG response to other H. pylori antigens, including CagA and VacA, remained unchanged, confirming that the immune defect was localized to the urease surface and not a general immunosuppressive effect. These results demonstrate that the site 8 modification does not prevent initial colonization or elicit a broad immune response but specifically disrupts the immune recognition and/or modulation pathways that are essential for long-term bacterial persistence. The persistence of the site 8 mutant in only a minority of mice, coupled with the absence of genetic reversion, strongly supports the conclusion that the surface topology of urease at this site is a critical determinant of chronic infection, independent of enzymatic function.

### Example 4

This example details the immunological characterization of the host response to infection with the site 8-modified urease strain, with particular focus on the polarization of T-helper cell responses and the modulation of dendritic cell function. Splenic lymphocytes were isolated from mice infected for ten months with either wild-type H. pylori or the site 8 mutant strain and were restimulated ex vivo with purified urease protein or whole bacterial lysate. After 72 hours of culture, supernatants were collected and analyzed for the presence of key cytokines using multiplex bead-based immunoassays. Mice infected with the wild-type strain exhibited a pronounced Th2-skewed immune response, with significantly elevated levels of interleukin-4 (IL-4), interleukin-5 (IL-5), and interleukin-13 (IL-13), along with reduced production of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). In contrast, mice infected with the site 8 mutant strain displayed a marked shift toward a Th1-dominated profile, with IFN-γ and TNF-α levels increasing by more than threefold compared to the wild-type group, while IL-4 and IL-13 production was reduced by over 80%. This shift in cytokine balance was accompanied by a significant increase in the frequency of CD4⁺ T cells expressing T-bet, the master transcription factor for Th1 differentiation, and a corresponding decrease in GATA-3 expression, the key regulator of Th2 commitment. Flow cytometric analysis of splenic CD4⁺ T cells confirmed a 60% reduction in the proportion of IL-4-secreting cells in the site 8-infected group, whereas the frequency of IFN-γ-producing cells increased from 8% to 32%. The altered T-cell polarization was further corroborated by the analysis of serum immunoglobulin isotypes, where IgG1, a Th2-dependent isotype, was significantly reduced in mice infected with the site 8 mutant, while IgG2a, which is induced by Th1 responses, was elevated. These findings suggest that the site 8 modification disrupts the bacterium’s ability to induce the Th2 bias that normally allows H. pylori to evade sterilizing immunity. To investigate the role of dendritic cells in this phenomenon, gastric lymph node dendritic cells were isolated from infected mice and assessed for their expression of co-stimulatory molecules and cytokine production. Dendritic cells from mice infected with the wild-type strain exhibited low levels of CD80, CD86, and MHC class II expression, consistent with a tolerogenic phenotype, and secreted high levels of interleukin-10 and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β). In contrast, dendritic cells from mice infected with the site 8 mutant displayed significantly upregulated expression of CD80, CD86, and MHC class II, along with increased secretion of IL-12 and IL-6, indicative of a mature, immunostimulatory phenotype. The ability of these dendritic cells to activate naïve T cells in vitro was also enhanced, as demonstrated by increased proliferation and IFN-γ production in co-culture assays. To determine whether the site 8 modification directly affected dendritic cell recognition of urease, bone marrow-derived dendritic cells were incubated with purified recombinant wild-type or site 8-modified urease protein. Only the wild-type urease induced significant upregulation of CD86 and IL-10, whereas the site 8 variant failed to trigger these responses, even at high concentrations. Moreover, the wild-type urease promoted the internalization of dendritic cells into lymphoid tissues, whereas the site 8 variant remained largely extracellular, suggesting a defect in receptor binding or endocytic trafficking. These results collectively indicate that the site 8 region of urease functions as a molecular signal that actively reprograms dendritic cells toward a tolerogenic state, thereby suppressing the development of protective Th1 immunity. The loss of this signal in the site 8 mutant enables dendritic cells to adopt a fully immunogenic phenotype, leading to the activation of cytotoxic T cells and the eventual clearance of the bacterium. This mechanism explains why the site 8 mutant is able to colonize initially—its acid resistance and adherence mechanisms remain intact—but fails to persist because it cannot modulate the host immune response to establish the chronic, non-sterilizing inflammation characteristic of wild-type H. pylori infection.

### Example 5

This example describes the investigation of the interaction between the site 8-modified urease and the host receptor CD74, a key mediator of immune modulation by H. pylori. CD74, also known as the MHC class II invariant chain, is expressed on the surface of gastric epithelial cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages and has been previously shown to bind urease with high affinity, triggering intracellular signaling cascades that lead to the upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and the suppression of antigen presentation. To determine whether the site 8 modification impairs this interaction, a series of binding assays were performed using recombinant human CD74 fused to the Fc portion of human IgG1 and immobilized on protein A-coated plates. Purified wild-type and site 8-modified urease proteins were incubated with the immobilized CD74 at concentrations ranging from 1 nM to 1 µM, and binding was quantified using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay with anti-urease antibodies. The wild-type urease bound CD74 with a dissociation constant (Kd) of approximately 15 nM, consistent with previously reported values, whereas the site 8-modified urease exhibited a more than 20-fold reduction in binding affinity, with a Kd exceeding 300 nM. To confirm these findings in a cellular context, human gastric epithelial cell lines (AGS) were incubated with fluorescently labeled wild-type or site 8-modified urease, and receptor internalization was monitored by confocal microscopy. Wild-type urease rapidly associated with the cell surface and was internalized into endosomal compartments within 30 minutes, colocalizing with CD74 and early endosome antigen 1 (EEA1). In contrast, the site 8-modified urease remained largely surface-bound and failed to internalize, indicating a defect in receptor engagement. To further validate the specificity of this interaction, CD74 expression was knocked down in AGS cells using siRNA, and the binding of urease was reassessed. In CD74-deficient cells, binding of wild-type urease was reduced by 90%, whereas the site 8 variant showed no further reduction, confirming that the loss of binding was specifically attributable to the disruption of the CD74-urease interface. The functional consequences of this impaired binding were examined by measuring IL-8 secretion from AGS cells after exposure to each urease variant. Wild-type urease induced a robust increase in IL-8 production, with levels rising more than tenfold above baseline, whereas the site 8-modified urease elicited only a minimal response, comparable to that of buffer controls. This reduction in IL-8 secretion was mirrored in primary human gastric epithelial cells isolated from biopsy specimens, further confirming the physiological relevance of the finding. To determine whether the site 8 region corresponds to a known CD74-binding epitope, peptide mapping was performed using overlapping 15-mer peptides spanning the UreB sequence. Only peptides containing the site 8 region (residues 310–315) bound CD74 in a solid-phase assay, and this binding was abolished when the site 8 sequence was mutated to alanine residues. These results demonstrate that the site 8 region of urease is a critical determinant of CD74 binding and that its modification disrupts the ability of H. pylori to trigger pro-inflammatory signaling through this receptor. The failure of the site 8 mutant to activate CD74-mediated pathways explains the reduced inflammation observed in infected mice and the subsequent failure to establish chronic infection, as the bacterium is no longer able to generate the controlled tissue damage and metabolic release that supports its long-term survival. This finding provides a direct mechanistic link between the structural modification of urease and the loss of persistence, establishing CD74 as the primary host receptor whose recognition is selectively abrogated by the site 8 alteration.

### Example 6

This example presents the results of a comprehensive analysis of the mucosal immune environment in the gastric tissue of mice infected with either wild-type H. pylori or the site 8-modified urease strain, with a focus on the infiltration and activation of innate immune cells. Gastric tissue sections were harvested from infected mice at ten months post-inoculation and processed for histopathological examination using hematoxylin and eosin staining, as well as immunohistochemical labeling for specific leukocyte markers. Mice infected with the wild-type strain exhibited the classic features of chronic H. pylori gastritis, including dense infiltration of polymorphonuclear neutrophils, macrophages, and lymphocytes in the lamina propria, along with epithelial hyperplasia, glandular atrophy, and focal metaplasia. In contrast, mice infected with the site 8 mutant displayed a markedly attenuated inflammatory response, with significantly fewer neutrophils and macrophages present in the gastric mucosa, and a near-complete absence of lymphoid follicle formation. Quantification of immune cell populations by flow cytometry of enzymatically digested gastric tissue revealed a 75% reduction in the number of CD11b⁺ macrophages and a 60% reduction in Ly6G⁺ neutrophils in the site 8-infected group compared to the wild-type group. The frequency of CD11c⁺ dendritic cells was also reduced by 50%, consistent with the impaired maturation observed in the lymph nodes. Notably, the total number of CD3⁺ T cells was not significantly different between the two groups, but the subset composition was dramatically altered, with a marked increase in CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells and a corresponding decrease in CD4⁺ FoxP3⁺ regulatory T cells in the site 8-infected mice. The reduction in regulatory T cells was particularly significant, as these cells are known to suppress effector immune responses and are actively recruited by wild-type H. pylori to maintain immune tolerance. To determine whether the altered immune cell recruitment was due to changes in chemokine expression, RNA was extracted from gastric tissue and analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR. Expression of the neutrophil chemoattractant CXCL1 and the macrophage-recruiting chemokine CCL2 was reduced by more than 80% in the site 8-infected group, while the expression of the T-cell chemoattractant CXCL10 was elevated, consistent with the observed Th1 polarization. The expression of IL-10 and TGF-β, both immunosuppressive cytokines, was also significantly lower in the site 8 group, further supporting the loss of immune modulation. To assess whether the site 8 modification affected the ability of urease to penetrate the mucosal barrier and reach subepithelial immune compartments, immunofluorescence staining was performed on gastric tissue sections using antibodies against urease and CD74. In wild-type infections, urease was detected not only on the epithelial surface but also within the lamina propria, colocalizing with CD74-expressing dendritic cells and macrophages. In contrast, in the site 8-infected mice, urease was confined almost entirely to the luminal surface of the epithelium, with minimal penetration into the underlying tissue. This suggests that the site 8 modification not only impairs receptor binding but also reduces the bacterium’s ability to disseminate urease into the submucosal compartment, thereby limiting its immunomodulatory reach. The reduced penetration may be due to decreased binding to epithelial receptors that facilitate transcytosis or to the loss of urease-induced disruption of tight junctions, which has been previously reported to enhance paracellular transport. These findings collectively demonstrate that the site 8 modification fundamentally alters the spatial and functional dynamics of urease-host interactions, restricting the enzyme’s ability to engage immune cells beyond the epithelial layer and thereby preventing the establishment of the chronic inflammatory niche required for bacterial persistence.

### Example 7

This example describes the development and characterization of recombinant protein vaccines based on the site 8-modified urease variant, demonstrating their efficacy in preventing chronic H. pylori colonization in a murine model. Recombinant wild-type and site 8-modified urease proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli using a pET28a expression vector with an N-terminal His-tag for purification. Bacterial lysates were subjected to nickel-affinity chromatography, followed by ion-exchange and size-exclusion chromatography to obtain highly pure, monodisperse protein preparations. The final preparations were confirmed to be free of endotoxin contamination using the Limulus amebocyte lysate assay, with levels below 0.1 EU/mg. Mice were immunized subcutaneously with 10 µg of either wild-type or site 8-modified urease protein emulsified in complete Freund’s adjuvant for the primary immunization and incomplete Freund’s adjuvant for two subsequent boosts administered at two-week intervals. Control mice received adjuvant alone. Four weeks after the final immunization, mice were challenged with a wild-type H. pylori strain via orogastric inoculation, and bacterial colonization was assessed at three and ten months post-challenge. Mice immunized with the site 8-modified urease exhibited a 95% reduction in gastric bacterial load at ten months compared to unimmunized controls, with 80% of the immunized mice completely clearing the infection. In contrast, mice immunized with wild-type urease showed no significant reduction in colonization compared to controls, with bacterial loads remaining stable over time. The protective effect of the site 8 vaccine was associated with a robust Th1-biased immune response, as evidenced by high levels of serum IgG2a antibodies and elevated IFN-γ production by splenic T cells upon restimulation with urease. The vaccine also induced a significant increase in the frequency of urease-specific CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells in the gastric mucosa, as detected by intracellular cytokine staining and tetramer analysis. To determine whether the protection was mediated by antibodies or T cells, passive transfer experiments were performed. Serum from vaccinated mice was transferred to naïve recipients, who were then challenged with H. pylori. While the serum transfer conferred partial protection, reducing bacterial load by approximately 40%, adoptive transfer of splenic T cells from vaccinated mice resulted in near-complete clearance of the infection, indicating that cellular immunity plays the dominant role in vaccine-mediated protection. To assess the durability of the immune response, mice were re-challenged with a heterologous H. pylori strain six months after the initial challenge. Mice previously immunized with the site 8-modified urease remained protected, with no detectable colonization, whereas control mice became colonized at high levels. These results demonstrate that the site 8-modified urease is not only capable of eliciting protective immunity but does so in a manner that overcomes the immune evasion mechanisms typically employed by wild-type H. pylori. The failure of the wild-type urease vaccine to confer protection underscores the importance of the site 8 modification in redirecting the immune response away from tolerance and toward sterilizing immunity. The site 8-modified urease therefore represents a novel and highly effective vaccine candidate that exploits a previously unrecognized vulnerability in H. pylori’s persistence strategy.

### Example 8

This example outlines the application of the site 8-modified urease as a molecular probe for the identification of novel host receptors and signaling pathways involved in H. pylori persistence. To identify proteins that interact specifically with the site 8 region of urease, a pull-down assay was performed using biotinylated wild-type and site 8-modified urease proteins as baits. Gastric epithelial cell lysates were incubated with immobilized urease variants, and bound proteins were eluted and analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. In addition to CD74, several novel binding partners were identified that interacted with the wild-type urease but not with the site 8 variant, including the endoplasmic reticulum chaperone GRP78, the integrin β1 subunit, and the scavenger receptor CD36. These proteins were validated by co-immunoprecipitation and Western blot analysis, and their expression was confirmed in gastric epithelial cells and dendritic cells. Functional assays revealed that wild-type urease binding to GRP78 triggered the unfolded protein response, leading to the upregulation of ER stress markers such as CHOP and BiP, which in turn suppressed apoptosis and promoted bacterial survival. Binding to integrin β1 enhanced epithelial cell adhesion and migration, facilitating bacterial colonization of damaged mucosal sites. Interaction with CD36 modulated lipid metabolism in macrophages, leading to the accumulation of lipid droplets that served as a nutrient source for the bacterium. None of these interactions occurred with the site 8-modified urease, suggesting that the site 8 region serves as a central hub for multiple host-pathogen interactions beyond CD74. To further explore the broader implications of this finding, a genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screen was performed in human gastric epithelial cells using a library of guide RNAs targeting all known cell surface receptors. Cells were exposed to wild-type or site 8-modified urease, and those that survived were sequenced to identify genes whose loss conferred resistance to urease-mediated immunomodulation. The screen identified 17 genes whose knockout abrogated the ability of wild-type urease to suppress IFN-γ production, with CD74, GRP78, and CD36 ranking among the top hits. These findings suggest that the site 8 region of urease is a multifunctional domain that engages a network of host receptors to orchestrate a coordinated program of immune evasion, metabolic adaptation, and tissue remodeling. The site 8-modified urease, by virtue of its inability to engage this network, serves as a powerful tool for dissecting the molecular architecture of H. pylori persistence. The invention further encompasses the use of the site 8-modified urease as a negative control in high-throughput screening platforms designed to identify small molecule inhibitors of urease-host interactions. By comparing the effects of compounds on wild-type versus site 8-modified urease, it becomes possible to distinguish inhibitors that target the catalytic site from those that specifically disrupt receptor binding, enabling the rational design of therapeutics that selectively block immune evasion without affecting urease activity. The site 8-modified urease may also be used as a diagnostic reagent to detect the presence of anti-urease antibodies associated with protective immunity, providing a biomarker for vaccine efficacy or natural resistance to chronic infection. In summary, the site 8 modification transforms urease from a virulence factor into a precision instrument for probing the host immune system, opening new avenues for the development of targeted therapies against chronic bacterial infections.
