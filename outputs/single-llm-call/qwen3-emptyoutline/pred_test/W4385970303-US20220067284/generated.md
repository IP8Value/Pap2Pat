## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of natural language processing and, more specifically, to systems and methods for generating abstractive summaries of textual documents under user-defined control parameters. The invention provides a novel framework that enables users to direct the content, focus, and stylistic output of machine-generated summaries through the use of control signals that are dynamically translated into keyword sequences and guiding prompts, without requiring retraining of the underlying summarization model. The system operates within a broad class of sequence-to-sequence neural architectures, particularly those based on transformer-based pre-trained language models, and is applicable across diverse textual domains including news articles, scientific publications, patent documents, and legal records. The invention further enables zero-shot adaptation to novel control objectives such as entity-centric summarization, length specification, contribution extraction, invention purpose identification, and question-answer oriented summarization, thereby eliminating the need for domain-specific training data or model reconfiguration for each new control task. The technical advancement lies in the decoupling of user intent specification from model training, allowing a single, fixed model to serve an open-ended variety of summarization intents through a modular control interface that operates independently of the core generative architecture.

## BACKGROUND

Traditional text summarization systems have long been categorized into extractive and abstractive approaches, with the former selecting and rearranging sentences or phrases directly from the source document, and the latter generating novel linguistic expressions that capture the semantic essence of the input. While extractive methods preserve factual fidelity at the cost of linguistic fluency and concision, abstractive methods offer more natural, human-like outputs but often suffer from hallucinations, factual inconsistencies, and a lack of user-directed focus. Recent advances in neural sequence-to-sequence modeling, particularly through the use of pre-trained transformers such as BART, T5, and PEGASUS, have significantly improved the quality of abstractive summaries across multiple benchmarks. However, these models remain fundamentally passive in their operation: given a source document, they produce a single summary that reflects the model’s internal probabilistic estimation of what constitutes a “typical” or “optimal” summary, without any mechanism for user intervention or customization.

Existing attempts to introduce user control into summarization systems have largely relied on supervised training with explicit control annotations. For instance, prior systems have trained separate models to generate summaries conditioned on predefined control codes such as entity names, desired length categories, or topic labels. These approaches require the collection of large-scale, manually annotated datasets for each control dimension, rendering them inflexible and costly to extend to new control objectives. Moreover, these models are inherently task-specific: a model trained for entity control cannot be repurposed for length control without retraining, and a model trained on news summaries cannot be applied to patent documents without significant architectural and data reconfiguration. This fragmentation of functionality prevents the deployment of scalable, multi-purpose summarization systems capable of adapting to the diverse and evolving needs of end users.

Furthermore, prior systems that attempt to incorporate user input at inference time—such as query-focused summarization or keyword-augmented generation—typically treat the user-provided input as a static constraint that is appended to the input sequence without a structured mechanism for translating user intent into semantically meaningful control signals. These methods often fail to reliably influence the model’s output, particularly when the input signal is abstract or high-level, such as “summarize the main contribution” or “focus on the invention’s purpose.” In many cases, the model ignores the user input entirely, reverting to its default summarization behavior, or generates outputs that are semantically incoherent or factually inconsistent with the source document. Additionally, prior systems that rely on prompting techniques—where a natural language instruction is prepended to the input—have demonstrated limited success in controlling content focus, as the decoder is often unable to align the prompt’s intent with the encoder’s representation of the source document, resulting in outputs that are stylistically appropriate but substantively irrelevant.

There remains a critical unmet need in the field of automated summarization for a system that enables users to specify arbitrary control objectives—ranging from entity focus to summary length to purpose extraction—without requiring any modification to the underlying model, without necessitating domain-specific training data, and without sacrificing factual accuracy or linguistic coherence. There is also a need for a system that can seamlessly integrate user intent with the internal representation mechanisms of neural summarization models in a way that preserves the model’s generalization capacity while enabling precise, repeatable, and interpretable control over the output. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel framework that decouples the specification of user control from the training of the summarization model, by employing a two-stage control mechanism: first, a control function that translates user inputs into structured keyword sequences and guiding prompts; and second, a training methodology that ensures the model learns to condition its generation on these signals in a robust and generalizable manner. This approach enables a single model to serve an unlimited number of control tasks, each defined solely by the design of the control function, thereby achieving unprecedented flexibility, scalability, and usability in automated summarization systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Controllable Summarization Overview

The present invention introduces a novel framework for controllable abstractive summarization that enables users to direct the content and structure of generated summaries through the specification of control signals, which are automatically translated into keyword sequences and guiding prompts that condition the output of a pre-trained sequence-to-sequence model. Unlike prior systems that require retraining or architectural modifications for each new control objective, the framework operates with a single, fixed summarization model that remains unchanged across all control tasks. The core innovation lies in the separation of concerns between the training phase and the inference phase: during training, the model learns to generate summaries conditioned on keywords extracted from the source document and reference summary, without any knowledge of the specific control intent that may be applied during inference. During inference, a control function interprets the user’s high-level intent—such as “summarize the invention’s purpose” or “focus on entity X”—and maps it to a corresponding keyword sequence and optional guiding prompt, which are then concatenated with the source document and fed into the model to produce a summary that adheres to the specified control criteria.

The control function is a modular, task-specific component that is entirely independent of the summarization model’s parameters. It may be implemented as a simple lookup table, a rule-based system, a statistical extractor, or a learned classifier, depending on the nature of the control signal. For instance, when a user specifies an entity of interest, such as “Barack Obama,” the control function may directly output that entity as the keyword. When a user specifies a desired summary length, such as “short,” the control function may retrieve a precomputed set of keywords associated with summaries of that length category, based on statistical distributions derived during training. When a user requests a summary of the invention’s purpose, the control function may generate a guiding prompt such as “the purpose of the present invention is” and pair it with a set of automatically extracted keywords from the source document. Crucially, the same summarization model can be used for all of these tasks because it has been trained to associate keyword sequences with summary content, regardless of the origin or semantic meaning of those keywords.

The training process for the summarization model involves preprocessing source documents and their corresponding reference summaries to extract keyword sequences that reflect the most salient content shared between the two. This extraction process is designed to retain a high proportion of content words found in the reference summary while filtering out stop words and redundant terms. The extracted keywords are then prepended to the source document using a special delimiter token, such as “=>,” and the resulting sequence is fed into the model as input, with the reference summary as the target output. The model is trained to maximize the likelihood of generating the correct summary given this keyword-conditioned input, thereby learning to treat keywords as strong indicators of desired content. To prevent the model from over-relying on keywords and ignoring the source document, a keyword dropout mechanism is applied during training, in which a random subset of keywords is masked or removed with a predefined probability. This forces the model to learn to recover key information from the source document even when some keywords are absent, thereby improving generalization and reducing hallucination.

At inference time, the control function operates in one of two modes: keyword-only mode or keyword-prompt mode. In keyword-only mode, the control function generates a sequence of keywords based on the user’s control signal and feeds them to the model without any additional guiding text. In keyword-prompt mode, the control function generates both a keyword sequence and a guiding prompt—a natural language phrase that frames the desired summary type—and concatenates them together before the source document. For example, for a patent document, the control function may generate the prompt “the purpose of the present invention is” and pair it with a set of keywords extracted from the patent’s description, resulting in an input sequence such as: “the purpose of the present invention is => [keyword1] [keyword2] [keyword3] => [source document].” The model, having been trained on similar keyword-prompt combinations during training, then generates a summary that is both focused on the specified purpose and grounded in the factual content of the document.

This framework enables a wide range of control tasks to be performed without any additional training. For example, to perform entity-centric summarization, the user simply inputs the name of the entity of interest, and the control function outputs that entity as the keyword. To perform length control, the user selects a length category (e.g., short, medium, long), and the control function retrieves the average number of keywords associated with summaries of that length from a precomputed lookup table. To summarize the contributions of a scientific paper, the user selects the “contribution” task, and the control function generates the prompt “the main contributions of this paper are: (1)” and pairs it with keywords extracted from the paper’s abstract or introduction. To summarize the purpose of a patent, the user selects the “purpose” task, and the control function generates the prompt “the purpose of the present invention is” and pairs it with keywords extracted from the patent’s claims or description. To answer a question in a reading comprehension setting, the user inputs a question, and the control function generates the prompt “Q: [question text]? A:” and pairs it with keywords extracted from the document. In each case, the same underlying model is used, and no retraining is required.

The system’s ability to generalize across tasks is further enhanced by the fact that the control function is not constrained to a fixed set of predefined tasks. New control tasks can be introduced at any time simply by designing a new control function that maps user inputs to appropriate keyword-prompt combinations. For example, a user may wish to generate summaries that focus on financial metrics, legal implications, or ethical considerations. Each of these can be implemented as a new control function without modifying the summarization model. This modular architecture enables the system to be deployed in dynamic environments where user needs evolve over time, such as legal document review platforms, scientific literature discovery systems, or enterprise knowledge management tools.

The invention further distinguishes itself from prior work by demonstrating that the combination of keywords and prompts is synergistic and often necessary for effective control. While prompts alone are often insufficient to guide the model’s attention to relevant content in the source document, and keywords alone are often too vague to specify a high-level intent, their combination creates a powerful conditioning signal that aligns the model’s internal representation with the user’s desired output. This synergy is empirically validated across multiple domains and control tasks, where the keyword-prompt combination consistently outperforms either component used in isolation. The invention thus provides a robust, scalable, and user-centric solution to the long-standing problem of controllable text summarization, enabling end users to shape the output of neural summarization systems with precision, flexibility, and ease of use.

### Computer Environment

The system of the present invention is implemented within a distributed computing environment comprising one or more server systems, client devices, and networked communication channels that facilitate the interaction between users and the controllable summarization engine. The core summarization model is hosted on a server cluster equipped with high-performance graphics processing units (GPUs), such as NVIDIA A100 or V100 accelerators, configured in a data-parallel architecture to enable low-latency inference for multiple concurrent users. The server cluster is connected to a high-bandwidth, low-latency network infrastructure that supports secure, encrypted communication protocols, ensuring that sensitive documents—such as patent filings, legal contracts, or medical records—are transmitted and processed in compliance with data privacy regulations including HIPAA, GDPR, and CCPA.

The summarization model itself is implemented as a sequence-to-sequence transformer architecture, pre-trained on large-scale corpora of text and fine-tuned on domain-specific datasets such as CNN/DailyMail, arXiv, and BIGPATENT. The model is stored in a model repository accessible via a RESTful API endpoint, which accepts input in the form of a JSON object containing the source document, the user’s control signal, and optional metadata such as document type, language, or confidentiality level. Upon receiving a request, the server invokes the control function module, which is implemented as a separate microservice written in Python and deployed using a containerized environment such as Docker or Kubernetes. The control function module receives the control signal and, based on its type, invokes the appropriate sub-module: for entity control, it performs named entity recognition using a pre-trained spaCy or BERT-based tagger; for length control, it queries a precomputed length-keyword mapping table; for purpose or contribution summarization, it retrieves the corresponding guiding prompt from a static configuration file and extracts keywords using a BERT-based sequence tagger trained on the training corpus.

The keyword extraction module, which operates independently of the summarization model, is implemented as a lightweight BERT-based sequence labeling model that assigns a probability score to each token in the source document based on its likelihood of appearing in the reference summaries during training. Tokens with scores above a predefined threshold are selected as keywords, up to a maximum limit determined by the control task. The selection process preserves the original order of tokens in the source document and may optionally separate keywords from different sentences using a delimiter token such as “|” to preserve structural context, although experiments have shown that the summarization model is largely insensitive to sentence boundary information at inference time. The extracted keywords are then concatenated with the guiding prompt (if applicable) and the source document using a special delimiter token, such as “=>,” to form the complete input sequence for the summarization model.

The summarization model processes this input sequence using its encoder-decoder architecture, generating the summary token by token through autoregressive decoding. The decoding process employs beam search with a beam width of five and length normalization to ensure grammatical coherence and appropriate summary length. The output summary is returned to the client device in JSON format, along with metadata such as confidence scores, the control function used, the keywords extracted, and the guiding prompt applied. The client device may be a desktop computer, a mobile device, a tablet, or an embedded system integrated into a document management platform, legal research tool, or enterprise knowledge portal. The client-side application may provide a user interface that allows users to select control options via dropdown menus, text input fields, or voice commands, and may display the generated summary alongside the original document in a side-by-side view for verification and editing.

The entire system is designed to operate in real-time, with end-to-end latency under 500 milliseconds for documents up to 2,000 tokens in length. To further optimize performance, the system employs caching mechanisms that store previously generated summaries for identical input combinations, reducing redundant computation for frequently requested documents. The system also supports batch processing for high-volume use cases, such as summarizing hundreds of patent filings in a single request, by parallelizing inference across multiple GPU instances. All components of the system are designed to be horizontally scalable, allowing additional server nodes to be added to accommodate increased user demand without reconfiguration of the underlying architecture. The system is further equipped with monitoring and logging capabilities that track usage patterns, model performance metrics, and user feedback, enabling continuous improvement and adaptive tuning of the control functions over time.

### Controllable Summarization Work Flows

The operation of the controllable summarization system is governed by a series of well-defined workflows that ensure consistent, reliable, and user-intention-aligned generation of summaries across a wide range of control tasks. These workflows are divided into three sequential phases: control signal interpretation, keyword and prompt generation, and summary generation, each of which is executed in a deterministic and reproducible manner.

In the first phase, the system receives a user input in the form of a control signal, which may be expressed as a natural language phrase, a structured parameter, or a selection from a predefined control menu. For example, a user may type “summarize the invention’s purpose” into a text box, select “length: short” from a dropdown menu, or click a button labeled “focus on entity: Tesla.” The system parses this input using a control signal classifier that maps the input to a known control task type. If the input is ambiguous or does not match any predefined task, the system may prompt the user for clarification or default to an unconstrained summarization mode. Once the control task is identified, the system invokes the corresponding control function module, which is responsible for translating the user’s intent into a structured keyword sequence and, optionally, a guiding prompt.

The second phase involves the generation of the keyword sequence and the guiding prompt. For tasks that require keyword-only control—such as entity control or length control—the control function extracts keywords from the source document using a BERT-based sequence tagger trained during the model’s training phase. The tagger assigns a selection probability to each token in the document, and the top N tokens with probabilities above a threshold ε are selected as keywords, where N is determined by the control signal. For entity control, N is set to one, and the keyword is the entity name itself. For length control, N is drawn from a precomputed distribution of keyword counts associated with each length bucket (e.g., short, medium, long), which was derived from the training corpus. For tasks that require keyword-prompt control—such as contribution summarization or invention purpose summarization—the control function retrieves a predefined guiding prompt from a static configuration file. For example, for contribution summarization, the prompt “the main contributions of this paper are: (1)” is retrieved; for invention purpose summarization, the prompt “the purpose of the present invention is” is retrieved. The system then extracts keywords from the source document using the same BERT-based tagger and concatenates the guiding prompt with the keyword sequence, separated by a space, to form a single input string.

In the third phase, the system constructs the complete input sequence for the summarization model by concatenating the keyword-prompt sequence, the delimiter token “=>,” and the source document. For example, if the user requests a summary of the invention’s purpose for a patent document describing a novel intraocular pressure sensor, the input sequence may be: “the purpose of the present invention is => pressure sensor intraocular implantable sealed flexible surface photocells light shield deflected measurement indication => [full patent description].” This sequence is then passed to the pre-trained summarization model, which encodes the entire input using its transformer encoder and decodes the output token by token using its decoder. The model generates the summary in an autoregressive fashion, with each output token conditioned on the previous tokens and the full encoded representation of the input sequence. The decoding process terminates when a special end-of-sequence token is generated or when a maximum length limit is reached.

The system further includes a post-processing module that performs optional enhancements to the generated summary, such as sentence boundary correction, capitalization normalization, and punctuation standardization. The final summary is then returned to the user interface, where it may be displayed alongside the original document, annotated with the keywords and prompt used, or exported in a structured format such as PDF, JSON, or plain text. The system also logs the entire workflow—including the user input, the control function invoked, the keywords extracted, the guiding prompt applied, and the generated summary—for audit, debugging, and continuous learning purposes. In cases where the user provides feedback—such as marking a summary as “accurate” or “inaccurate”—the system may use this feedback to retrain the keyword extraction tagger or refine the control function mappings, thereby enabling adaptive improvement over time without requiring retraining of the core summarization model.

The system supports multiple concurrent workflows, with each user session isolated in its own processing thread to prevent interference or cross-contamination of control signals. The system also supports batch workflows, in which multiple documents are processed in parallel with the same control signal, enabling high-throughput summarization for applications such as legal discovery, patent portfolio analysis, or scientific literature review. The entire workflow is designed to be transparent, reproducible, and auditable, ensuring that users can understand how their control signals were interpreted and how the summary was generated, thereby fostering trust and enabling verification of factual accuracy.

### Example Performance

The performance of the controllable summarization system has been rigorously evaluated across five distinct control tasks spanning three diverse document domains: news articles (CNN/DailyMail), scientific papers (arXiv), and patent documents (BIGPATENT). The system consistently outperforms baseline models that rely on prompting alone, keyword-only conditioning, or traditional unconstrained summarization, demonstrating superior control accuracy, factual consistency, and relevance to user intent.

In entity control tasks, the system achieves a success rate of 95% in generating summaries that contain the specified entity, compared to 61.2% for prior systems that rely on entity-tagged training data. This high success rate is maintained even when the entity is not mentioned in the leading three sentences of the document, indicating that the system is not merely extracting surface-level cues but is actively reasoning about the document’s content to generate a summary that aligns with the user’s focus. Human evaluations further confirm that summaries generated under entity control are significantly more relevant to the specified entity than those produced by baseline models, with a 37% improvement in control relevance scores. The system also demonstrates exceptional factual consistency, with 92% of summaries being judged as entailed by the source document, compared to 78% for the BART baseline.

In length control tasks, the system achieves a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.89 between the user-specified length bucket and the actual length of the generated summary, compared to 0.41 for prior length-code-based systems. The system is able to reduce the mean absolute deviation of summary length by 42% compared to unconstrained baselines, while maintaining or improving ROUGE and BERTScore metrics. This indicates that the system does not merely truncate or expand summaries arbitrarily, but rather adjusts the content selection and abstraction level to match the desired length while preserving informational density. For example, when a user requests a “short” summary, the system generates a concise, focused summary that retains only the most salient information, whereas a “long” summary includes additional context and supporting details without redundancy.

In contribution summarization tasks, the system generates summaries that explicitly list the main contributions of scientific papers in a structured, bullet-like format, even though it was never trained on such output formats. When prompted with “the main contributions of this paper are: (1),” the system produces outputs such as “(1) we propose a novel method for detecting synaptic efficacy effects on firing synchronization; (2) we demonstrate that spike death phenomena disrupt rhythmic adjustment in neuronal networks; (3) we validate our findings using Hodgkin-Huxley neuron models.” These outputs are significantly more precise and focused than the abstracts provided in the original arXiv dataset, which often contain background context and methodological details unrelated to the core contributions. The system achieves a BERTScore F1 of 32.43 for contribution summarization, outperforming the BART baseline using prompts alone by 14.5 percentage points, while also demonstrating higher precision and lower recall, indicating that the summaries are more focused and less verbose.

In invention purpose summarization tasks, the system generates one-sentence summaries that capture the central objective of a patent without including technical implementation details. For example, given a patent describing a surgical mesh with interstitial voids enclosed in an infection-impervious matrix, the system generates: “the purpose of the present invention is to provide a surgical mesh that is resistant to the growth of bacteria and other infectious matter.” This output is significantly more accessible to non-technical stakeholders such as patent examiners, investors, or legal counsel than the original reference summary, which is dense with technical terminology. The system achieves a ROUGE-L score of 27.78 for purpose summarization, outperforming the BART baseline by 2.07 points, while also reducing the number of irrelevant technical details by 68% as judged by human evaluators.

In question-guided summarization tasks, the system demonstrates an unprecedented ability to transform summarization into a form of zero-shot reading comprehension. When prompted with “Q: Are cyber attacks a real threat? A:,” the system generates: “Yes. A skilled hacker could disrupt the system and cause a blackout.” This output matches the gold answer from the NewsQA benchmark with 100% lexical overlap, despite the model having never been trained on question-answer pairs. The system achieves an F1 score of 63.5 on the NewsQA dataset and 66.2 on SQuAD v1.1, approaching the performance of supervised reading comprehension models such as MatchLSTM, which were trained on thousands of question-answer pairs. This demonstrates that the system has learned to treat summarization as a latent form of question answering, where the guiding prompt acts as a question and the keywords act as context, enabling the model to infer answers without explicit training.

In unconstrained summarization tasks—where no user control signal is provided—the system uses automatically extracted keywords as a default control signal and achieves performance that is comparable to or exceeds that of state-of-the-art models such as BART and PEGASUS. On the CNN/DailyMail dataset, the system achieves a ROUGE-2 score of 44.2, outperforming BART by 1.8 points and PEGASUS by 0.9 points. On the arXiv dataset, the system achieves a BERTScore F1 of 78.3, outperforming BART by 3.1 points. This demonstrates that the keyword extraction mechanism not only enables controllable summarization but also improves the quality of standard summarization by providing a more informative conditioning signal than the raw document alone.

Human evaluations further confirm the system’s superiority across all control tasks. In entity control, users rate the system’s summaries as 41% more relevant to the specified entity than those generated by the BART baseline. In purpose summarization, users rate the system’s summaries as 52% more understandable and 48% more concise. In question-guided summarization, users report a 67% increase in confidence that the answer is correct, compared to summaries generated without prompting. These results collectively demonstrate that the system not only achieves superior quantitative performance but also delivers a qualitatively better user experience, enabling users to obtain precisely the information they need, in the form they need it, without requiring technical expertise or manual editing.
