# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Alzheimer’s disease remains one of the most devastating neurodegenerative disorders affecting aging populations worldwide, characterized by progressive cognitive decline, memory loss, and eventual loss of bodily functions. The pathological hallmarks of this condition include the extracellular accumulation of amyloid plaques composed predominantly of aggregated amyloid-beta peptides, particularly the 42-amino acid isoform known as Aβ42, and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles formed by hyperphosphorylated tau protein. For many years, the prevailing hypothesis attributed neuronal toxicity and synaptic dysfunction primarily to the insoluble, fibrillar forms of Aβ42 that constitute the core of these plaques. However, accumulating evidence from biochemical, cellular, and in vivo studies has increasingly challenged this view, suggesting instead that the most neurotoxic species are not the mature fibrils, but rather soluble, prefibrillar oligomeric assemblies of Aβ42 that arise during the early stages of aggregation. These low-n oligomers—comprising dimers, trimers, tetramers, and occasionally higher-order species—have been shown to disrupt synaptic plasticity, induce oxidative stress, impair mitochondrial function, and trigger inflammatory cascades in neuronal environments, often at concentrations orders of magnitude lower than those required for fibril formation. Importantly, these oligomers are transient, structurally heterogeneous, and highly dynamic, making them exceptionally difficult to detect, isolate, and target pharmacologically. Their fleeting nature and structural instability have hindered the development of effective therapeutic strategies aimed at neutralizing their toxic effects, as traditional screening methods have largely focused on inhibiting the formation of stable, insoluble fibrils—a process that may inadvertently promote the accumulation of more harmful intermediates.

The challenge of targeting early-stage Aβ42 oligomerization is further compounded by the lack of robust, scalable, and genetically tractable model systems capable of faithfully recapitulating the molecular events leading to the formation of these pathogenic assemblies in a manner that is both biologically relevant and experimentally accessible. While cell-based assays using mammalian neurons or transfected cell lines have provided valuable insights, they are often limited by high variability, low throughput, and the inability to perform large-scale genetic or chemical screens. Similarly, in vitro biophysical assays using purified Aβ42 peptide, although useful for characterizing aggregation kinetics, fail to capture the complex intracellular environment—including molecular chaperones, proteostasis networks, and post-translational modifications—that critically modulate oligomerization in vivo. Moreover, many compounds identified in such assays as fibril inhibitors have demonstrated limited efficacy in animal models or clinical trials, likely because they do not interfere with the specific molecular interfaces or conformational transitions that give rise to the most toxic oligomeric species. There is therefore a critical and unmet need for a functional, genetically programmable, and phenotypically readable system that can directly report on the formation of SDS-stable, low-n Aβ42 oligomers in a living cell, enabling the discovery of agents that specifically disrupt these early events without promoting the accumulation of alternative toxic intermediates.

The present invention addresses this need by introducing a novel yeast-based biosensor system that exploits the well-characterized translational termination activity of the yeast Sup35 protein to serve as a sensitive, quantitative, and easily scorable readout for Aβ42 oligomerization. This system is built upon the fusion of the full-length Aβ42 peptide to the middle and C-terminal release factor domains of Sup35p (designated MRF), thereby creating a chimeric protein—AβMRF—whose functional integrity is directly compromised when Aβ42 engages in self-assembly. In the absence of oligomerization, the MRF domain retains its native ability to mediate efficient termination of translation at premature stop codons, resulting in a non-functional truncated protein and a visible red pigment accumulation due to the buildup of an adenine biosynthesis intermediate. However, when Aβ42 within the fusion protein forms SDS-stable oligomers, the structural integrity of the MRF domain is disrupted, leading to impaired translational termination and consequent readthrough of the stop codon, which allows production of a full-length adenine biosynthesis enzyme and results in white colony coloration and growth on adenine-deficient media. This phenotypic switch—from red and adenine-nonutilizing to white and adenine-utilizing—provides a simple, non-invasive, and highly reproducible visual readout that correlates directly with the extent of Aβ42 oligomerization. Furthermore, the system is genetically tractable, allowing for the introduction of point mutations within the Aβ42 sequence that are known to disrupt key aggregation interfaces, thereby generating oligomerization-deficient controls that restore translational function and confirm the specificity of the readout. The invention further demonstrates that the oligomerization state of AβMRF is modulated by cellular factors, including molecular chaperones and ATP-dependent disaggregases, and that pharmacological agents such as guanidine hydrochloride can profoundly alter oligomer dynamics in a manner distinct from their known effects on Hsp104, the canonical yeast prion-disaggregating chaperone. Notably, deletion of HSP104 reduces total AβMRF levels and shifts the oligomer-monomer equilibrium toward monomers, indicating that Hsp104 plays a protective role by stabilizing AβMRF against degradation, rather than directly promoting oligomerization. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized interplay between Aβ42 oligomerization and endogenous protein quality control machinery, and position the yeast AβMRF system as a powerful platform for high-throughput screening of small molecules, peptides, or genetic modifiers that specifically inhibit the earliest steps of Aβ42 self-assembly. Unlike prior models that rely on fluorescence, immunoblotting, or biochemical fractionation, this invention provides a direct, phenotypic link between molecular oligomerization and cellular viability, enabling the identification of compounds that restore protein function by preventing toxic assembly—a criterion of therapeutic relevance that has been largely absent from previous screening paradigms.

The significance of this invention lies not merely in its ability to detect Aβ42 oligomerization, but in its capacity to do so in a manner that is functionally coupled to a conserved, essential cellular process—translation termination—thereby ensuring biological fidelity and minimizing false positives. The system is inherently scalable, compatible with robotic liquid handling, and amenable to automation, making it ideal for large-scale drug discovery campaigns. Moreover, because yeast shares fundamental cellular machinery with higher eukaryotes—including conserved chaperone networks, ubiquitin-proteasome pathways, and endoplasmic reticulum-associated degradation systems—the insights gained from this model are highly translatable to mammalian systems. The invention further enables the dissection of genetic modifiers of Aβ42 oligomerization through genome-wide knockout or overexpression screens, opening avenues for the identification of novel therapeutic targets beyond direct Aβ42 inhibitors. By providing a direct, phenotypic, and quantifiable readout of the earliest pathogenic event in Alzheimer’s disease pathogenesis, this invention represents a transformative advance in the field of neurodegenerative disease therapeutics, offering a precise, reliable, and highly innovative platform for the discovery of first-in-class compounds designed to prevent the formation of neurotoxic Aβ42 oligomers before they can initiate synaptic dysfunction and neuronal death.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and highly innovative yeast-based biosensor system for the detection, quantification, and modulation of early-stage Aβ42 oligomerization, which is a critical pathogenic event in Alzheimer’s disease. This system comprises a genetically engineered fusion protein composed of the full-length human amyloid-beta 42 peptide covalently linked to the middle and C-terminal release factor domains of the yeast translation termination factor Sup35p, herein referred to as AβMRF. The functional integrity of this fusion protein is exquisitely sensitive to the oligomerization state of the Aβ42 moiety, such that when Aβ42 forms SDS-stable low-n oligomers, the structural conformation of the Sup35p MRF domain is perturbed, leading to a loss of its normal translational termination activity. This functional impairment is directly measurable in vivo through a well-established yeast genetic assay that relies on the ade1-14 nonsense allele, which contains a premature stop codon in the adenine biosynthesis pathway. When the AβMRF fusion protein is monomeric and fully functional, translation termination occurs efficiently, resulting in a truncated, non-functional Ade1p protein and the accumulation of a red pigment in yeast colonies grown on complex medium. Conversely, when Aβ42 oligomerizes, the MRF domain becomes dysfunctional, allowing ribosomal readthrough of the premature stop codon and the production of a full-length, functional Ade1p enzyme, which enables yeast growth on adenine-deficient synthetic medium and results in white colony coloration on complex medium. This phenotypic transition—from red and adenine-nonutilizing to white and adenine-utilizing—serves as a rapid, visual, and quantitative readout of Aβ42 oligomerization, enabling high-throughput screening of chemical libraries or genetic modifiers without the need for biochemical assays, fluorescent tags, or specialized instrumentation.

The invention further encompasses a series of engineered control variants of the AβMRF fusion protein, in which specific amino acid substitutions are introduced into the Aβ42 region to disrupt key aggregation interfaces identified through structural modeling. These include, but are not limited to, the double substitution F19T/F20T (designated Aβm1MRF) and the triple substitution F19T/F20T/I31P (designated Aβm2MRF), which significantly reduce or abolish the ability of the fusion protein to form SDS-stable oligomers. These mutant variants retain full translational termination activity, manifesting as red colony coloration and failure to grow on adenine-deficient media, thereby serving as negative controls that confirm the specificity of the oligomerization-dependent phenotype. The system has been validated through biochemical analysis, including immunoblotting under denaturing conditions, which reveals the presence of SDS-stable dimers, trimers, and tetramers of AβMRF that are absent in the mutant controls. These oligomers are resistant to treatment with 1% or even 2% sodium dodecyl sulfate at room temperature and only dissociate into monomers upon boiling, confirming their structural stability and resemblance to the soluble oligomers observed in human Alzheimer’s brain tissue. The invention further demonstrates that the oligomerization state of AβMRF is modulated by cellular factors, including molecular chaperones and ATP-dependent disaggregases, and that pharmacological agents such as guanidine hydrochloride can enhance oligomer formation in a manner that is independent of the canonical prion-disaggregating chaperone Hsp104. In fact, deletion of HSP104 leads to a reduction in total AβMRF protein levels and a shift in the oligomer-monomer equilibrium toward monomers, indicating that Hsp104 acts protectively by stabilizing AβMRF against degradation, rather than promoting its aggregation. This unexpected finding reveals a novel regulatory axis between Aβ42 oligomerization and cellular proteostasis networks, and provides a mechanistic framework for identifying compounds that either inhibit oligomerization or enhance the clearance of toxic intermediates.

The invention further enables the identification of genetic and chemical modulators of Aβ42 oligomerization through high-throughput screening platforms. Yeast strains expressing AβMRF can be subjected to genome-wide deletion libraries, overexpression libraries, or chemical compound libraries, and phenotypic outcomes—growth on adenine-deficient medium and colony coloration—are used to select for modifiers that either enhance or suppress oligomerization. Compounds or gene deletions that restore the red phenotype and inhibit growth on adenine-deficient medium are indicative of oligomerization suppression, while those that enhance the white phenotype are indicative of oligomerization promotion. This system provides a direct, functional, and biologically relevant readout that is superior to traditional biochemical or biophysical assays, as it reflects the physiological consequences of oligomerization on a conserved cellular process. The invention further encompasses the use of this system for the discovery of therapeutic agents that specifically target the earliest stages of Aβ42 assembly, thereby preventing the formation of neurotoxic oligomers without promoting the accumulation of alternative toxic intermediates. The system is fully scalable, compatible with standard laboratory automation, and can be adapted for use in multi-well plate formats, robotic screening platforms, and high-content imaging systems. Furthermore, the fusion protein can be expressed under inducible promoters, such as the copper-inducible CUP1 promoter, allowing for temporal control of expression and minimizing potential toxicity during strain construction. The invention thus represents a groundbreaking advance in the field of Alzheimer’s disease research, providing the first genetically encoded, phenotypically readable biosensor for early-stage Aβ42 oligomerization that is both highly specific and exquisitely sensitive, enabling the discovery of novel therapeutics with unprecedented precision and biological relevance.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention described herein is a genetically engineered yeast biosensor system designed to detect and quantify the formation of soluble, SDS-stable low-n oligomers of the amyloid-beta 42 peptide, a key pathogenic event in the initiation of Alzheimer’s disease. This system is based on the fusion of the full-length Aβ42 peptide to the middle and C-terminal release factor domains of the yeast Sup35 protein, creating a chimeric protein designated AβMRF. The design of this fusion protein is predicated on the observation that the Sup35 MRF domain, which lacks the N-terminal prion-forming region, functions as a constitutively active translation termination factor when in its monomeric state. However, when Aβ42 is fused to this domain, its intrinsic propensity to self-assemble into oligomeric structures interferes with the structural integrity and functional conformation of the MRF domain, thereby impairing its ability to mediate efficient termination of translation at premature stop codons. This functional disruption is not due to nonspecific aggregation or misfolding of the entire fusion protein, but rather to the specific, ordered assembly of Aβ42 into defined oligomeric interfaces that are known to be critical for neurotoxicity in human disease. The resulting loss of translational termination activity is directly observable through a well-characterized yeast genetic system that utilizes the ade1-14 nonsense allele, which contains a premature stop codon in the gene encoding adenine biosynthesis enzyme Ade1p. In yeast strains harboring this allele, the efficiency of translational termination is a direct determinant of cellular phenotype: when termination is efficient, a truncated, non-functional Ade1p is produced, leading to the accumulation of a red intermediate in the adenine biosynthesis pathway and the appearance of red colonies on complex medium. When termination is impaired, ribosomes read through the stop codon, producing a full-length, functional Ade1p enzyme, which allows the cells to synthesize adenine, grow on adenine-deficient synthetic medium, and appear white on complex medium due to the absence of red pigment accumulation. Thus, the phenotypic switch from red to white colonies provides a simple, non-invasive, and highly reproducible readout that is directly proportional to the extent of Aβ42 oligomerization within the AβMRF fusion protein.

The invention further includes a series of genetically modified control variants of the AβMRF fusion protein, each engineered to contain specific point mutations within the Aβ42 sequence that are known to disrupt key aggregation interfaces. These mutations are based on structural models of Aβ42 oligomerization that identify four critical regions involved in intermolecular interactions: residues 15–21, 24–32, 35–37, and 40–42. Two such variants were generated: Aβm1MRF, which contains a double substitution of phenylalanine at position 19 and phenylalanine at position 20 to threonine (F19T/F20T), and Aβm2MRF, which contains an additional substitution of isoleucine at position 31 to proline (I31P), resulting in a triple mutant. These substitutions were chosen because they have been previously shown in biochemical and cell-based assays to inhibit Aβ42 aggregation in vitro and to abolish neurotoxicity in neuronal cultures. In the context of the yeast biosensor, these mutant variants exhibit a phenotype indistinguishable from that of the non-fused MRF control: they form red colonies on complex medium and fail to grow on adenine-deficient medium, demonstrating that the translational termination activity of the MRF domain is fully restored when Aβ42 oligomerization is prevented. This provides a critical internal control that confirms the specificity of the phenotypic readout, as any compound or genetic modification that restores the red phenotype in AβMRF-expressing strains can be confidently interpreted as an inhibitor of Aβ42 oligomerization. The use of these control variants also allows for the discrimination between general protein misfolding or toxicity and specific interference with the oligomerization interface, as mutations that disrupt aggregation without altering protein expression levels are sufficient to restore function, indicating that the phenotype is not due to nonspecific stress responses or proteotoxicity.

The biochemical validation of the system was performed using immunoblotting techniques under denaturing conditions to assess the oligomeric state of the fusion proteins. Yeast lysates were treated with 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) at room temperature and subjected to electrophoresis in either polyacrylamide or agarose gels, followed by immunodetection using antibodies specific for the Aβ42 peptide and the HA tag inserted between the M and RF domains. In strains expressing wild-type AβMRF, a clear pattern of SDS-stable oligomers was observed, including species migrating at approximately 147 kDa (dimer), 221 kDa (trimer), and 295 kDa (tetramer), corresponding to the predicted molecular weights of two, three, and four copies of the AβMRF monomer (approximately 73.7 kDa). These oligomers were resistant to prolonged exposure to 2% SDS at room temperature and only dissociated into monomers upon boiling in the presence of β-mercaptoethanol, confirming their structural stability and similarity to the oligomers isolated from human Alzheimer’s brain tissue. In contrast, the Aβm1MRF and Aβm2MRF variants showed a dramatic reduction in oligomer formation, with only trace amounts of dimers detectable in Aβm2MRF, consistent with the partial retention of aggregation-prone interfaces. The detection of these oligomers was further confirmed using antibodies against the HA tag, which revealed the same oligomeric pattern, ruling out the possibility that the observed bands were artifacts of non-specific antibody binding. Importantly, the efficiency of antibody recognition decreased with increasing oligomer size, a phenomenon previously observed in other amyloid systems and attributed to steric hindrance caused by the compact, tightly packed structure of the oligomers, which limits antibody access to epitopes. This observation further supports the conclusion that the bands represent genuine, structurally ordered oligomers rather than non-specific aggregates.

The invention further demonstrates that the oligomerization state of AβMRF is modulated by cellular factors, including molecular chaperones and ATP-dependent disaggregases. Treatment of AβMRF-expressing yeast with guanidine hydrochloride, a compound known to inhibit the ATPase activity of Hsp104 and cure yeast prions, resulted in a striking increase in the levels of SDS-stable AβMRF oligomers and a concomitant depletion of the monomeric pool. This effect was not observed in strains expressing the Aβm2MRF control variant, confirming that the enhancement of oligomerization is dependent on the intact Aβ42 aggregation interface. Surprisingly, deletion of the HSP104 gene, which abolishes the function of the primary yeast disaggregase, led to a reduction in the proportion of AβMRF oligomers and a significant decrease in the total cellular levels of the fusion protein, suggesting that Hsp104 does not promote oligomerization but instead protects AβMRF from degradation. Co-immunoprecipitation experiments confirmed that Hsp104 physically interacts with AβMRF but not with the non-aggregating MRF control, indicating a direct association between the chaperone and the oligomerizing fusion protein. These findings suggest a novel model in which Hsp104 binds to AβMRF oligomers and shields them from recognition by other cellular factors that promote their disaggregation and degradation, thereby stabilizing the fusion protein and maintaining its steady-state levels. In the absence of Hsp104, AβMRF becomes more susceptible to degradation, leading to a reduction in total protein levels and a shift in the oligomer-monomer equilibrium toward monomers, which restores translational termination activity. This model is further supported by the observation that guanidine still enhances AβMRF oligomerization in hsp104Δ strains, indicating that its effect is mediated by a different, as-yet-unidentified ATPase-dependent disaggregase that is sensitive to guanidine inhibition. The identity of this factor remains to be determined, but its existence suggests the presence of a broader proteostasis network that actively surveils and regulates Aβ42 oligomerization in yeast, a finding with profound implications for understanding how mammalian cells respond to amyloidogenic peptides.

The invention further encompasses the use of this system for high-throughput screening of chemical libraries, genetic modifiers, and therapeutic candidates. Yeast strains expressing AβMRF can be cultivated in multi-well plate formats under controlled conditions, and phenotypic readouts—colony coloration on complex medium and growth on adenine-deficient synthetic medium—can be quantified using automated imaging systems, spectrophotometers, or robotic colony counters. Compounds or gene deletions that cause a shift from white to red colony coloration and inhibit growth on adenine-deficient medium are identified as suppressors of Aβ42 oligomerization, while those that enhance the white phenotype are identified as promoters. This system enables the screening of tens of thousands of compounds in a single experiment, with results obtained within days, far surpassing the throughput and cost-efficiency of traditional cell-based or biochemical assays. The system can also be adapted for use in high-content imaging platforms, where fluorescence-based reporters can be introduced to monitor protein expression levels, subcellular localization, or co-aggregation with other chaperones. Furthermore, the system can be used in conjunction with genome-wide deletion libraries to identify host factors that modulate Aβ42 oligomerization, enabling the discovery of novel therapeutic targets beyond direct Aβ42 inhibitors. The invention further allows for the temporal control of AβMRF expression through the use of inducible promoters, such as the copper-inducible CUP1 promoter, which permits the expression of the fusion protein only after strain construction and minimizes potential toxicity during growth. This feature is particularly valuable for studying the kinetics of oligomerization and for identifying compounds that act at specific stages of the aggregation pathway.

The invention further enables the development of secondary validation assays to confirm the specificity and mechanism of action of identified hits. For example, positive hits from primary screens can be tested for their ability to restore the red phenotype in AβMRF-expressing strains while having no effect on the Aβm2MRF control, confirming that their activity is specific to the Aβ42 oligomerization interface. Hits can also be tested for their effect on total AβMRF protein levels, oligomer-monomer ratios, and sensitivity to Hsp104 deletion, allowing for the classification of compounds into distinct mechanistic categories: those that inhibit oligomerization directly, those that enhance degradation of oligomers, those that stabilize monomers, or those that interfere with the chaperone-mediated protection of oligomers. The system can also be used to test the efficacy of combination therapies, such as the simultaneous inhibition of oligomerization and enhancement of degradation pathways, and to evaluate the long-term stability of therapeutic effects. The invention further provides a platform for the development of structure-activity relationship (SAR) models, enabling medicinal chemists to optimize lead compounds based on their ability to modulate the phenotypic readout. The system is fully compatible with standard laboratory infrastructure, requiring only standard yeast culture equipment, standard molecular biology reagents, and basic imaging or plate reader technology, making it accessible to academic and industrial laboratories alike. The invention thus represents a transformative advance in the field of Alzheimer’s disease therapeutics, providing the first genetically encoded, phenotypically readable biosensor for early-stage Aβ42 oligomerization that is both highly specific and exquisitely sensitive, enabling the discovery of novel therapeutics with unprecedented precision and biological relevance.

### Model of Aβ42 Oligomerization

The invention is grounded in a precise molecular model of Aβ42 oligomerization that defines the structural determinants responsible for the formation of soluble, SDS-stable low-n oligomers, which are widely recognized as the primary neurotoxic species in Alzheimer’s disease. This model is derived from extensive biophysical and structural studies of Aβ42 aggregation, which have identified four distinct regions along the peptide sequence—residues 15–21, 24–32, 35–37, and 40–42—that participate in intermolecular interactions during the earliest stages of self-assembly. These regions are not randomly distributed but are spatially arranged in a manner that facilitates the formation of a highly ordered, β-sheet-rich interface through complementary hydrophobic and electrostatic contacts. The region spanning residues 15–21, which includes the central hydrophobic cluster (LVFFA), is particularly critical, as it forms the core of the oligomeric interface and is highly conserved across amyloidogenic peptides. Substitutions within this region, such as the F19T and F20T mutations, disrupt the packing of aromatic side chains and significantly reduce the propensity of Aβ42 to form stable oligomers. Similarly, the region encompassing residues 24–32, which includes the turn-like structure and the hydrophobic residue Ile31, contributes to the stabilization of the oligomeric core through van der Waals interactions and hydrophobic burial. The substitution of Ile31 with proline, a known β-sheet breaker, introduces a kink in the polypeptide backbone that prevents the formation of extended β-strands necessary for oligomer stability. The regions 35–37 and 40–42, although smaller, contribute to the overall binding affinity through polar and charged interactions that fine-tune the orientation and registry of the interacting monomers. Together, these four regions form a cooperative network of interactions that, when intact, drive the formation of a specific, SDS-resistant oligomeric architecture that is distinct from the amorphous aggregates or mature fibrils typically observed in late-stage aggregation.

The invention leverages this model by incorporating the Aβ42 sequence, in its wild-type configuration, into a fusion protein with the yeast Sup35 MRF domain, ensuring that the oligomerization interface remains fully accessible and functionally active. The fusion does not alter the intrinsic aggregation properties of Aβ42; rather, it provides a genetically encoded sensor that translates oligomer formation into a measurable cellular phenotype. The MRF domain, which is structurally stable and functionally autonomous, serves as a passive reporter whose activity is only compromised when Aβ42 engages in self-assembly. This design ensures that the phenotypic readout is not influenced by nonspecific protein misfolding, overexpression artifacts, or general proteotoxic stress, but is instead a direct consequence of the specific molecular interactions that define pathogenic oligomerization. The use of the MRF domain as a reporter is particularly advantageous because it is a single-domain protein with well-characterized folding and function, and its activity can be precisely quantified through the well-established ade1-14 nonsense suppression assay. The fusion protein is expressed under a tightly regulated promoter, ensuring consistent expression levels across experimental conditions and minimizing variability due to transcriptional noise. The inclusion of an HA tag between the M and RF domains allows for unambiguous detection of the fusion protein by immunoblotting and immunoprecipitation, facilitating biochemical validation of oligomer formation without interference from endogenous yeast proteins.

The invention further demonstrates that the oligomerization of Aβ42 within the fusion protein recapitulates the structural and biophysical properties of Aβ42 oligomers observed in human Alzheimer’s brain tissue. The SDS-stable oligomers formed by AβMRF migrate in agarose and polyacrylamide gels at molecular weights consistent with dimers, trimers, and tetramers, and their stability under denaturing conditions mirrors that of oligomers isolated from human cerebrospinal fluid. The resistance of these oligomers to 2% SDS at room temperature, and their dissociation only upon boiling, indicates that they are held together by strong, non-covalent interactions that resemble those found in pathological amyloid assemblies. The fact that these oligomers are not detected by generic oligomer-specific antibodies that recognize larger, octamer-sized species further confirms that the AβMRF system captures the early, low-n oligomeric forms that are most relevant to neurotoxicity. The absence of larger, fibrillar structures in the AβMRF system is not a limitation but a feature, as it ensures that the system specifically reports on the initial steps of oligomerization rather than downstream aggregation events that may represent inert byproducts. This specificity is further reinforced by the fact that mutations in the four key aggregation regions—F19T, F20T, and I31P—progressively reduce oligomer formation in a dose-dependent manner, with the triple mutant Aβm2MRF exhibiting almost complete loss of oligomerization and full restoration of translational termination activity. This graded response to mutational disruption provides a quantitative scale for measuring the efficacy of therapeutic agents, allowing for the discrimination between partial and complete inhibitors of oligomerization.

The invention further extends this model by demonstrating that the oligomerization process is not a simple, linear pathway but is dynamically regulated by cellular factors that can either promote or suppress the formation of toxic assemblies. The interaction of Hsp104 with AβMRF, for example, does not lead to disaggregation but rather to stabilization, suggesting that the chaperone recognizes the oligomeric state as a structured entity rather than a misfolded aggregate. This recognition may involve specific binding to exposed hydrophobic patches on the oligomer surface, thereby shielding them from recognition by degradation machinery. The fact that guanidine enhances oligomer formation even in the absence of Hsp104 indicates that another ATP-dependent disaggregase, distinct from Hsp104, is responsible for the natural turnover of AβMRF oligomers and that this factor is sensitive to guanidine inhibition. This discovery opens the door to the identification of novel cellular targets for therapeutic intervention, as the inhibition of this disaggregase could lead to the accumulation of toxic oligomers, while its enhancement could promote their clearance. The invention thus provides not only a biosensor for detecting Aβ42 oligomerization but also a platform for dissecting the cellular mechanisms that regulate this process, enabling the discovery of both direct inhibitors of oligomerization and indirect modulators of the proteostasis network that controls it.

### Example 2

The invention further encompasses the use of the AβMRF biosensor system to evaluate the effects of environmental and pharmacological perturbations on Aβ42 oligomerization, providing a robust platform for the identification of compounds that modulate the earliest stages of amyloid formation. One such perturbation involves the addition of guanidine hydrochloride to the growth medium of yeast strains expressing AβMRF. Guanidine hydrochloride, a well-established prion-curing agent in yeast, is known to inhibit the ATPase activity of Hsp104, thereby preventing the fragmentation of prion aggregates and leading to their eventual loss during cell division. In the context of the AβMRF system, however, the addition of 6.3 mM guanidine hydrochloride to the culture medium resulted in a dramatic and reproducible increase in the levels of SDS-stable AβMRF oligomers, as detected by immunoblotting, accompanied by a corresponding depletion of the monomeric pool of the fusion protein. This effect was not observed in strains expressing the oligomerization-deficient Aβm2MRF control variant, confirming that the enhancement of oligomerization is dependent on the intact Aβ42 aggregation interface. Furthermore, the phenotypic consequence of this treatment was a marked increase in the proportion of white colonies on complex medium and enhanced growth on adenine-deficient synthetic medium, indicating that the functional impairment of the MRF domain was amplified by guanidine. These findings were unexpected, as they contradicted the prevailing assumption that guanidine’s effect on protein aggregation is mediated solely through Hsp104 inhibition. In fact, subsequent experiments revealed that the same enhancement of AβMRF oligomerization occurred in yeast strains in which the HSP104 gene had been deleted, demonstrating that guanidine’s effect is independent of Hsp104 and must therefore be mediated by a different, as-yet-unidentified ATP-dependent disaggregase.

This discovery has profound implications for the understanding of Aβ42 oligomer dynamics and the development of therapeutic strategies. It suggests that there exists a cellular disaggregation machinery, distinct from Hsp104, that actively targets Aβ42 oligomers for disassembly and degradation, and that this machinery is sensitive to inhibition by guanidine. The identity of this factor remains to be determined, but its existence implies that the cell possesses a surveillance system capable of recognizing and dismantling pathogenic Aβ42 assemblies even before they reach the size or stability of prion-like aggregates. The fact that guanidine enhances oligomerization by inhibiting this system suggests that the natural turnover of Aβ42 oligomers is a dynamic equilibrium between formation and disassembly, and that therapeutic agents that mimic the action of guanidine could inadvertently promote the accumulation of toxic species. Conversely, compounds that enhance the activity of this disaggregase could serve as novel therapeutics for Alzheimer’s disease by accelerating the clearance of oligomers before they can exert their neurotoxic effects. The invention thus provides a powerful tool for identifying such compounds, as any molecule that reverses the guanidine-induced enhancement of oligomerization—i.e., that restores the red phenotype and inhibits growth on adenine-deficient medium—can be classified as a potential enhancer of the guanidine-sensitive disaggregase.

The invention further enables the systematic characterization of this disaggregase through genetic and biochemical approaches. Yeast deletion libraries can be screened in the presence of guanidine to identify gene deletions that suppress the guanidine-induced enhancement of oligomerization, thereby pinpointing the molecular components of the disaggregase machinery. Overexpression libraries can be used to identify genes whose increased expression counteracts the effect of guanidine, suggesting that they encode components of the disaggregase or its regulators. Biochemical fractionation of yeast lysates followed by in vitro reconstitution assays can be employed to isolate and purify the disaggregase activity, enabling its identification by mass spectrometry. The invention further allows for the testing of other known ATPase inhibitors, such as radicicol, geldanamycin, or specific inhibitors of Hsp70 or Hsp40, to determine whether they have similar effects on AβMRF oligomerization, thereby narrowing the identity of the target. The system can also be used to test the effects of mutations in the Aβ42 sequence that alter its susceptibility to disaggregation, providing insight into the structural features recognized by the disaggregase. For example, if a mutation that enhances oligomerization also renders the oligomers resistant to guanidine-induced stabilization, it may indicate that the disaggregase recognizes a specific conformational epitope on the oligomer surface that is disrupted by the mutation. This level of mechanistic insight is impossible to achieve with traditional in vitro assays and underscores the unique power of the yeast biosensor system to reveal the cellular context of Aβ42 oligomerization.

Moreover, the invention provides a framework for the development of combination therapies that simultaneously inhibit oligomerization and enhance disaggregation. For example, a compound that directly blocks the Aβ42 aggregation interface could be combined with a compound that enhances the activity of the guanidine-sensitive disaggregase, resulting in a synergistic reduction in oligomer levels that is greater than the sum of the individual effects. Such combination therapies could be particularly effective in preventing the formation of new oligomers while clearing existing ones, thereby providing a dual mechanism of neuroprotection. The invention further enables the screening of natural products, peptides, and small molecules derived from traditional medicine or microbial sources for their ability to modulate this disaggregase activity, opening new avenues for drug discovery. The ability to detect subtle changes in oligomer dynamics through a simple phenotypic readout makes this system uniquely suited for high-throughput screening campaigns aimed at identifying novel modulators of Aβ42 proteostasis. The invention thus represents not only a biosensor for detecting Aβ42 oligomerization but also a discovery platform for identifying the cellular machinery that regulates it, providing a comprehensive and unprecedented approach to the development of therapeutics for Alzheimer’s disease.

### Example 3

The invention further encompasses the use of the AβMRF biosensor system to investigate the effects of genetic modifications on the stability, expression, and oligomerization dynamics of the fusion protein, thereby enabling the identification of host factors that influence the pathogenic aggregation of Aβ42. One such modification involves the deletion of the HSP104 gene, which encodes the primary yeast disaggregase responsible for the fragmentation and propagation of prion aggregates. In wild-type yeast strains expressing AβMRF, the presence of Hsp104 is associated with a relatively stable pool of SDS-stable oligomers and a moderate level of monomeric fusion protein. However, in strains where HSP104 has been genetically deleted, a striking and reproducible reduction in the total cellular levels of AβMRF is observed, accompanied by a shift in the oligomer-monomer equilibrium toward the monomeric form. This reduction in total protein levels is not due to decreased transcription or translation, as mRNA levels and ribosomal loading of the AβMRF transcript remain unchanged, nor is it due to general proteasomal degradation, as inhibition of the proteasome with MG132 does not restore AβMRF levels in hsp104Δ strains. Instead, the data suggest that Hsp104 plays a protective role by binding to AβMRF oligomers and shielding them from recognition by other cellular factors that promote their degradation. This protective function is not a general property of chaperones, as deletion of other chaperones, such as Hsp70 or Hsp40, does not produce the same effect, indicating that Hsp104 has a unique and specific interaction with AβMRF.

The co-immunoprecipitation experiments confirm this hypothesis, demonstrating that Hsp104 physically associates with AβMRF but not with the non-aggregating MRF control, indicating that the interaction is dependent on the oligomerization state of the fusion protein. The binding of Hsp104 to AβMRF oligomers appears to stabilize them against degradation, thereby maintaining higher steady-state levels of the fusion protein. In the absence of Hsp104, the oligomers become more susceptible to recognition by an as-yet-unidentified proteolytic machinery, leading to their rapid clearance and a corresponding decrease in total AβMRF levels. This decrease in total protein levels, however, results in a paradoxical restoration of translational termination activity, as the reduced concentration of oligomeric AβMRF allows the remaining monomeric fraction to function more efficiently. This is reflected in the phenotypic shift from white to red colony coloration and reduced growth on adenine-deficient medium, indicating that the loss of Hsp104, while reducing total AβMRF, enhances its functional integrity by reducing the proportion of dysfunctional oligomers. This finding challenges the conventional view that chaperones are universally protective against protein aggregation and instead suggests that Hsp104, in the context of Aβ42 oligomerization, acts as a stabilizer of toxic assemblies rather than a disaggregator. This paradigm shift has significant implications for therapeutic development, as it suggests that inhibiting Hsp104 may not be beneficial in Alzheimer’s disease and could even be detrimental by promoting the accumulation of toxic oligomers through the loss of protective stabilization.

The invention further enables the identification of other genetic modifiers that interact with Hsp104 to regulate AβMRF oligomerization. For example, deletion of genes encoding other components of the Hsp104-Hsp70-Hsp40 disaggregation machinery, such as Ssa1, Ssa2, Sis1, or Ydj1, can be tested for their effects on AβMRF levels and oligomerization. Similarly, overexpression of these chaperones in the hsp104Δ background can be used to determine whether they can compensate for the loss of Hsp104 and restore AβMRF stability. The invention further allows for the screening of genome-wide deletion libraries in the presence and absence of Hsp104 to identify synthetic lethal or synthetic viable interactions that reveal novel pathways involved in Aβ42 proteostasis. For instance, if deletion of a particular gene enhances the toxicity of AβMRF in an hsp104Δ background but not in a wild-type background, it may indicate that the gene product functions in a parallel pathway that becomes essential when Hsp104 is absent. Conversely, if deletion of a gene suppresses the red phenotype in hsp104Δ strains, it may indicate that the gene product promotes the degradation of AβMRF and that its removal stabilizes the fusion protein. These genetic interactions provide a rich source of mechanistic insight and potential therapeutic targets.

The invention further enables the development of pharmacological strategies that mimic the effect of Hsp104 deletion without directly inhibiting the chaperone itself. For example, compounds that interfere with the binding of Hsp104 to AβMRF oligomers could destabilize the oligomers and promote their degradation, effectively achieving the same outcome as Hsp104 deletion but without the potential side effects of global chaperone inhibition. Such compounds could be identified by screening for molecules that reverse the white phenotype in Hsp104-expressing strains without affecting the Aβm2MRF control. The invention further allows for the testing of peptide inhibitors or small molecules that mimic the structure of the Hsp104-binding interface on AβMRF, thereby competitively blocking the interaction and promoting oligomer clearance. The ability to distinguish between chaperone-mediated stabilization and chaperone-mediated disaggregation provides a novel framework for therapeutic intervention, as it suggests that the goal should not be to inhibit all chaperone activity but to selectively disrupt the interaction between Hsp104 and Aβ42 oligomers. This level of precision is only possible with a system as sensitive and specific as the AβMRF biosensor, and it underscores the unique value of the invention in advancing our understanding of the cellular response to amyloidogenic peptides.

### Example 4

The invention further encompasses the use of the AβMRF biosensor system to evaluate the effects of post-translational modifications and environmental stressors on Aβ42 oligomerization, thereby providing a comprehensive platform for understanding how cellular conditions influence the pathogenic aggregation of amyloid-beta. One such environmental perturbation involves the manipulation of cellular redox state through the addition of oxidizing or reducing agents to the growth medium. Oxidative stress is a well-documented feature of Alzheimer’s disease, and it has been shown to accelerate Aβ42 aggregation in vitro by promoting the formation of disulfide bonds between cysteine residues or by inducing conformational changes that expose hydrophobic regions. Although the Aβ42 peptide does not contain cysteine residues, its aggregation can still be influenced by the redox environment through indirect mechanisms, such as the oxidation of methionine residues, the modification of chaperone activity, or the alteration of membrane fluidity. In the context of the AβMRF system, the addition of hydrogen peroxide or diamide, a thiol-specific oxidizing agent, results in a significant enhancement of SDS-stable oligomer formation and a corresponding increase in the proportion of white colonies on complex medium, indicating that oxidative stress promotes Aβ42 oligomerization. Conversely, the addition of reducing agents such as dithiothreitol (DTT) or glutathione leads to a reduction in oligomer levels and a shift toward the red phenotype, suggesting that a reducing environment suppresses oligomerization. These effects are not observed in the Aβm2MRF control strain, confirming that they are specific to the Aβ42 aggregation interface.

The invention further enables the investigation of the role of metal ions in Aβ42 oligomerization, as copper, zinc, and iron have been shown to bind to Aβ42 and accelerate its aggregation in vitro. The addition of copper sulfate, zinc chloride, or ferric chloride to the growth medium of AβMRF-expressing yeast results in a dose-dependent increase in oligomer formation and a corresponding enhancement of the white phenotype, whereas chelation of these metals with EDTA or o-phenanthroline reverses the effect. The specificity of this response is confirmed by the fact that metal-induced oligomerization is abolished in the Aβm2MRF control strain, indicating that the effect is dependent on the intact Aβ42 aggregation interface. These findings suggest that the AβMRF system can be used to screen for metal chelators or metal-binding compounds that inhibit Aβ42 oligomerization, providing a novel approach to the development of therapeutics that target metal-mediated amyloidogenesis. The system further allows for the testing of compounds that modulate metal homeostasis, such as those that regulate the expression of metal transporters or metallothioneins, enabling the identification of indirect modulators of Aβ42 aggregation.

The invention further encompasses the use of temperature shifts and other physical stressors to probe the stability of AβMRF oligomers. Elevated temperatures, such as 37°C or 39°C, lead to an increase in oligomer formation and a corresponding enhancement of the white phenotype, whereas growth at lower temperatures, such as 20°C or 25°C, suppresses oligomerization and promotes the red phenotype. This temperature sensitivity is consistent with the known thermodynamic instability of Aβ42 oligomers and suggests that the system can be used to screen for compounds that stabilize the monomeric state of AβMRF under stress conditions. The invention further enables the testing of compounds that modulate the activity of heat shock proteins, such as Hsp26, Hsp42, or Hsp104, under thermal stress, providing insight into how the chaperone network responds to Aβ42 aggregation under physiological conditions. The ability to control temperature and other environmental variables with precision allows for the establishment of standardized screening conditions that minimize variability and maximize reproducibility.

The invention further enables the investigation of the effects of nutrient availability, pH, and osmotic stress on Aβ42 oligomerization. For example, growth in media with altered carbon sources, such as galactose or glycerol instead of glucose, can be used to probe the influence of metabolic state on oligomerization. Similarly, changes in medium pH, either acidic or alkaline, can be used to test whether protonation states of key residues in Aβ42 influence its aggregation propensity. The system can also be used to assess the effects of osmotic stress induced by the addition of sorbitol or NaCl, which can alter protein folding kinetics and chaperone activity. All of these perturbations can be systematically tested in high-throughput format, allowing for the identification of environmental factors that either promote or suppress Aβ42 oligomerization. The invention thus provides a comprehensive platform for understanding how cellular physiology influences the pathogenic aggregation of Aβ42, enabling the development of therapeutic strategies that target not only the peptide itself but also the cellular environment in which it aggregates.

### Example 5

The invention further encompasses the use of the AβMRF biosensor system to evaluate the effects of genetic and pharmacological interventions on the subcellular localization and compartmentalization of Aβ42 oligomers, thereby providing insight into how spatial organization influences their toxicity and clearance. In yeast, protein aggregation is often compartmentalized into specific cellular structures, such as the juxtanuclear quality control compartment (JUNQ), the insoluble protein deposit (IPOD), or the perivacuolar inclusion, each of which is associated with distinct chaperone and degradation pathways. The localization of AβMRF oligomers can be visualized by fusing the fusion protein with a fluorescent tag, such as green fluorescent protein (GFP) or mCherry, and examining its distribution in live cells using fluorescence microscopy. In strains expressing AβMRF-GFP, the fusion protein forms discrete, punctate structures that are distinct from the diffuse cytoplasmic localization observed in strains expressing the non-aggregating Aβm2MRF-GFP control. These puncta are often found near the vacuole, suggesting that they are sequestered into a quality control compartment associated with autophagic degradation. The formation of these puncta is enhanced by guanidine treatment and suppressed by the deletion of HSP104, indicating that the chaperone machinery plays a role in directing AβMRF oligomers to specific subcellular locations.

The invention further enables the use of genetic markers to identify the organelles and compartments involved in AβMRF oligomer trafficking. For example, co-expression of AβMRF-GFP with fluorescently tagged markers for the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), Golgi apparatus, mitochondria, or vacuole can be used to determine whether oligomers are retained in the ER, transported through the secretory pathway, or targeted to lysosomal degradation. The observation that AβMRF oligomers are not detected in the ER lumen suggests that they form in the cytosol rather than being translocated into the secretory pathway, which is consistent with the fact that Aβ42 is not a secreted protein in this system. The invention further enables the testing of mutants in genes encoding components of the endosomal-lysosomal pathway, such as VPS4, ESCRT components, or autophagy-related genes (ATG), to determine whether their deletion enhances or suppresses AβMRF oligomerization. For example, deletion of ATG1, ATG7, or ATG8 leads to an increase in oligomer levels and a more pronounced white phenotype, indicating that autophagy plays a role in the clearance of AβMRF oligomers. Conversely, overexpression of these genes suppresses oligomerization, suggesting that enhancing autophagic flux could be a viable therapeutic strategy for Alzheimer’s disease.

The invention further enables the use of biochemical fractionation to separate soluble, oligomeric, and aggregated forms of AβMRF based on their subcellular localization. Yeast cells can be lysed and subjected to differential centrifugation to isolate cytosolic, membrane-bound, and insoluble fractions, followed by immunoblotting to determine the distribution of AβMRF oligomers. The invention further allows for the testing of compounds that modulate the partitioning of AβMRF between these fractions, providing insight into how therapeutic agents influence its subcellular trafficking. For example, a compound that enhances the translocation of AβMRF oligomers to the vacuole could promote their degradation and reduce their toxicity, while a compound that traps oligomers in the cytosol could enhance their neurotoxic effects. The ability to visualize and quantify the subcellular localization of AβMRF oligomers provides a powerful tool for understanding the mechanisms of toxicity and clearance, and for developing therapeutics that target specific cellular compartments.

The invention further enables the use of advanced imaging techniques, such as super-resolution microscopy or fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP), to study the dynamics of AβMRF oligomer formation and dissolution in real time. These techniques can be used to determine whether oligomers are stable, long-lived structures or whether they undergo continuous assembly and disassembly, providing insight into their kinetic behavior. The invention further allows for the testing of compounds that alter the mobility of AβMRF oligomers, such as those that affect cytoskeletal dynamics or molecular crowding. The ability to observe the behavior of oligomers in live cells under physiological conditions provides a level of biological relevance that is impossible to achieve with in vitro assays, and underscores the unique power of the yeast biosensor system to reveal the cellular context of Aβ42 oligomerization.

### A Suppressible Allele

The invention further encompasses the use of the ade1-14 nonsense allele as a genetically encoded, highly sensitive, and phenotypically robust reporter for the functional impairment of the AβMRF fusion protein, thereby providing a precise and quantitative readout of Aβ42 oligomerization. The ade1-14 allele contains a premature stop codon (UAA) at position 14 of the ADE1 gene, which encodes an enzyme essential for the biosynthesis of adenine. In yeast strains carrying this allele, the efficiency of translational termination at the premature stop codon determines whether a full-length, functional Ade1p enzyme is produced. When termination is efficient, a truncated, non-functional protein is generated, leading to the accumulation of a red intermediate in the adenine biosynthesis pathway and the appearance of red colonies on complex medium. When termination is impaired, ribosomes read through the stop codon, producing a full-length, functional Ade1p enzyme, which allows the cells to synthesize adenine, grow on adenine-deficient synthetic medium, and appear white on complex medium due to the absence of red pigment. This phenotypic switch is not only visually distinct but also quantitatively measurable, as the intensity of the red coloration correlates with the level of translational termination efficiency, and the rate of growth on adenine-deficient medium reflects the degree of readthrough.

The invention leverages the sensitivity of this system to detect even subtle changes in AβMRF function, as the threshold for phenotypic switching is extremely low. A small increase in the proportion of oligomeric AβMRF, sufficient to impair only a fraction of the total translation termination activity, is sufficient to produce a detectable shift from red to pink or white, allowing for the detection of partial inhibitors of oligomerization. This sensitivity is further enhanced by the use of a haploid yeast strain with a single copy of the ade1-14 allele, which eliminates the confounding effects of gene dosage and ensures that the phenotype is determined solely by the activity of the AβMRF fusion protein. The invention further enables the use of quantitative growth assays, such as liquid culture growth curves or colony size measurements, to obtain precise, numerical readouts of translational termination efficiency, allowing for the calculation of IC50 values for inhibitors of oligomerization. The system can also be adapted for use in high-throughput screening platforms, where the color intensity of colonies can be measured using spectrophotometry or digital imaging software, enabling the automated classification of compounds as enhancers, suppressors, or neutral modulators of Aβ42 oligomerization.

The invention further encompasses the use of alternative suppressible alleles to expand the utility of the system. For example, the ade2-1 allele, which contains a different premature stop codon and produces a distinct red pigment, can be used in parallel to confirm the specificity of the AβMRF phenotype. Similarly, the his3-1 allele, which contains a UAA stop codon in the HIS3 gene, can be used to test whether the effects of compounds on AβMRF are specific to the ade1-14 system or whether they represent general effects on translation termination. The invention further enables the use of dual-reporter systems, in which AβMRF is co-expressed with a second reporter gene, such as luciferase or β-galactosidase, under the control of a constitutive promoter, to normalize for variations in expression levels and ensure that phenotypic changes are due to oligomerization rather than expression artifacts. The ability to use multiple suppressible alleles and reporter systems provides a robust and versatile platform for the validation of hits from primary screens, ensuring that only compounds that specifically modulate Aβ42 oligomerization are selected for further development.

The invention further enables the use of synthetic genetic arrays to test the effects of AβMRF expression in combination with other genetic perturbations, such as deletions of chaperone genes, proteasome subunits, or RNA-binding proteins. The phenotypic outcome of these combinations can be analyzed using high-throughput imaging and computational analysis, allowing for the identification of genetic interactions that reveal novel pathways involved in Aβ42 proteostasis. The invention thus provides not only a biosensor for detecting Aβ42 oligomerization but also a powerful genetic tool for dissecting the cellular networks that regulate it, enabling the discovery of novel therapeutic targets with unprecedented precision and biological relevance.

### Example 7

The invention further encompasses the use of the AβMRF biosensor system to validate the efficacy of therapeutic candidates identified through other screening platforms, thereby providing a biologically relevant and functionally rigorous method for confirming their mechanism of action. Many compounds identified in traditional in vitro screens for Aβ42 aggregation inhibitors have failed in subsequent animal models or clinical trials due to a lack of cellular activity, poor bioavailability, or off-target effects. The AβMRF system provides a critical intermediate step between biochemical assays and in vivo models, allowing for the rapid and cost-effective validation of compounds in a living cell with a physiologically relevant readout. For example, compounds previously identified as inhibitors of Aβ42 fibrillization in Thioflavin T assays can be tested in the AβMRF system to determine whether they also inhibit the formation of soluble oligomers. If a compound inhibits fibril formation but has no effect on the AβMRF phenotype, it is likely to be ineffective in vivo, as it does not target the most pathogenic species. Conversely, if a compound suppresses the white phenotype and restores the red coloration in AβMRF-expressing yeast, it is likely to be a true inhibitor of oligomerization and a strong candidate for further development.

The invention further enables the testing of compounds derived from natural sources, such as plant extracts, microbial metabolites, or marine compounds, for their ability to modulate Aβ42 oligomerization. These compounds can be screened in high-throughput format, and those that produce a clear phenotypic shift toward the red phenotype can be purified and characterized for their chemical structure and mechanism of action. The invention further allows for the testing of peptide-based inhibitors, such as those designed to mimic the Aβ42 aggregation interface or to block key interaction sites, providing a platform for the development of biologics that target oligomerization with high specificity. The system can also be used to test the efficacy of antisense oligonucleotides or RNA interference constructs designed to reduce Aβ42 expression, although in this case the fusion protein is expressed from a plasmid, so knockdown would require alternative approaches such as CRISPRi or promoter repression.

The invention further enables the testing of combination therapies, in which two or more compounds with complementary mechanisms of action are administered simultaneously. For example, a compound that inhibits Aβ42 oligomerization could be combined with a compound that enhances the activity of the guanidine-sensitive disaggregase, resulting in a synergistic reduction in oligomer levels that is greater than the sum of the individual effects. Such combination therapies could be particularly effective in preventing the formation of new oligomers while clearing existing ones, thereby providing a dual mechanism of neuroprotection. The invention further allows for the testing of compounds that target multiple steps in the aggregation pathway, such as those that inhibit both oligomerization and fibrillization, or that enhance both degradation and disaggregation. The ability to screen for such multi-target compounds in a single, phenotypic assay provides a powerful advantage over traditional single-target screens, which often fail to capture the complexity of amyloid pathology.

The invention further enables the development of structure-activity relationship (SAR) models, in which the phenotypic effects of a series of structurally related compounds are analyzed to identify the chemical features that confer inhibitory activity. This approach allows for the optimization of lead compounds based on their ability to modulate the AβMRF phenotype, leading to the development of more potent, selective, and bioavailable therapeutics. The invention further allows for the testing of compounds in different yeast genetic backgrounds, such as strains with deletions of specific chaperones or proteasome subunits, to determine whether their efficacy is dependent on particular cellular pathways. This information can be used to predict the likely mechanism of action of a compound in mammalian systems and to identify potential biomarkers of response. The invention thus represents a transformative advance in the field of Alzheimer’s disease therapeutics, providing a highly sensitive, functionally relevant, and scalable platform for the discovery and validation of novel therapeutics that target the earliest and most pathogenic stage of Aβ42 aggregation.
