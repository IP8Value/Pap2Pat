# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to systems and methods for the identification, quantification, and spatial mapping of wavefront discontinuities during atrial fibrillation using intracardiac electrograms. More specifically, the invention provides a novel computational framework for estimating regional dominant frequency and wave break rate from sequentially acquired electrogram data, enabling the localization of arrhythmogenic substrates with high spatial and temporal precision. The invention is particularly useful in the context of catheter-based electrophysiological mapping and ablation procedures for persistent and paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, where conventional metrics such as complex fractionated electrogram duration or average dominant frequency have demonstrated limited predictive value for procedural success. The disclosed method leverages short-time Fourier transform analysis of averaged, preprocessed electrogram signals to extract transient fluctuations in regional frequency content, which are then correlated with the occurrence of wave breaks—disruptions in coherent wavefront propagation that are mechanistically linked to rotor dynamics and arrhythmia perpetuation. By introducing a robust, computationally efficient algorithm that does not rely on precise activation time detection, the invention overcomes longstanding limitations in signal interpretation associated with fragmented, non-isoelectric, or low-amplitude electrograms commonly encountered in advanced atrial fibrillation. The resulting regional dominant frequency and wave break rate metrics can be rendered as color-coded overlays on three-dimensional electroanatomic maps, providing clinicians with an intuitive, real-time guidance tool for targeted ablation. The invention further establishes empirically validated thresholds for minimum recording duration required to reliably estimate these metrics, ensuring reproducibility across clinical settings and enabling standardized implementation in routine practice. This technology represents a paradigm shift from passive, morphology-based mapping to dynamic, wavefront-kinetic characterization of atrial substrate, offering a path toward improved ablation outcomes in patients with persistent atrial fibrillation.

## BACKGROUND

Atrial fibrillation remains the most prevalent sustained cardiac arrhythmia and a leading cause of stroke, heart failure, and cardiovascular morbidity. Despite decades of clinical research and technological advancement, the mechanistic underpinnings of its perpetuation remain incompletely understood, particularly in persistent forms of the disease. Conventional ablation strategies, primarily centered on pulmonary vein isolation, have demonstrated limited efficacy in persistent atrial fibrillation, with recurrence rates exceeding 50% in many cohorts. This suboptimal outcome has prompted extensive investigation into alternative ablation targets beyond the pulmonary veins, including regions of complex fractionated electrograms, areas of high dominant frequency, and sites of localized electrical instability. However, the clinical translation of these concepts has been hindered by significant methodological limitations. Existing approaches to mapping atrial fibrillation often rely on either panoramic, multi-electrode basket catheters that suffer from poor contact stability and limited spatial resolution, or sequential mapping techniques that lack real-time analytical capability. Furthermore, many algorithms depend on the accurate detection of local activation times, a task rendered unreliable in the presence of fractionated, low-amplitude, or overlapping electrograms that are characteristic of advanced atrial substrate. The concept of the rotor—a self-sustaining spiral wave of electrical activation—has been proposed as a key driver of persistent atrial fibrillation, yet its direct visualization in human tissue remains contentious due to technical constraints and conflicting evidence. While some studies have attempted to identify rotors using phase mapping or frequency-based methods, these approaches frequently conflate the presence of high-frequency activity with the actual location of the rotor core, failing to distinguish between drivers and passive conduction barriers. Moreover, the spatial heterogeneity of atrial tissue, the dynamic nature of wavefront interactions, and the influence of fibrosis, conduction slowing, and anisotropy further complicate the interpretation of electrogram morphology. As a result, current clinical mapping systems often generate maps based on averaged or static metrics such as complex fractionated electrogram duration or mean dominant frequency, which do not capture the transient, non-stationary behavior of wavefronts during fibrillation. These limitations have led to a persistent disconnect between the theoretical understanding of atrial fibrillation mechanisms and the practical tools available to clinicians during ablation procedures. There exists, therefore, a critical unmet need for a robust, clinically feasible method that can objectively quantify the spatiotemporal dynamics of wavefront propagation, identify regions of wavebreak activity, and correlate these features with sites of arrhythmia termination. The present invention addresses this gap by introducing a novel signal processing paradigm that transforms raw intracardiac electrograms into quantitative, mechanistically interpretable metrics—regional dominant frequency and wave break rate—that reflect the underlying kinematics of atrial activation. Unlike prior methods, this approach does not require precise timing of individual activations, is resilient to signal noise and morphology variability, and operates on short-duration segments that are compatible with clinical workflow. By linking these metrics to procedural outcomes, the invention provides a new paradigm for substrate-based ablation that moves beyond empirical lesion sets toward mechanism-guided intervention.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a system and method for identifying and quantifying wavefront discontinuities during atrial fibrillation through the analysis of regional dominant frequency and wave break rate derived from intracardiac electrograms. The method comprises the steps of acquiring sequential intracardiac electrogram data from a catheter with multiple electrode pairs positioned within the atria, preprocessing each electrogram signal to convert complex morphologies into smooth, pulse-like waveforms, averaging the preprocessed signals across all electrode pairs to generate a composite regional signal, applying a two-sided exponential finite impulse response filter to the averaged signal to attenuate high-frequency noise while preserving transient discontinuities, computing the instantaneous regional dominant frequency by performing a short-time Fourier transform on the filtered signal over a one-second sliding window, determining the upper quartile of the instantaneous regional dominant frequency values over the recording duration to define a stable regional dominant frequency threshold, identifying wave breaks as transient drops in the instantaneous regional dominant frequency exceeding three hertz below the regional dominant frequency threshold and persisting for more than one hundred milliseconds, calculating the wave break rate as the number of identified wave breaks per second, and rendering the regional dominant frequency and wave break rate as spatially resolved color-coded maps overlaid on a three-dimensional electroanatomic reconstruction of the atrial endocardium. The invention further includes the determination of minimum recording durations required for reliable estimation of these metrics, establishing that a four-second segment is sufficient for accurate regional dominant frequency estimation and a twenty-five-second segment is required for reliable wave break rate quantification. The system is implemented using a computer-readable medium storing executable instructions that, when executed by a processor, perform the above steps in real-time or offline, and is configured to interface with standard electrophysiology mapping systems to display the resulting maps during clinical procedures. The invention enables clinicians to identify regions of high regional dominant frequency and low wave break rate as potential arrhythmogenic drivers, with such regions demonstrating significant correlation with sites of atrial fibrillation termination during ablation. The method is applicable to both paroxysmal and persistent atrial fibrillation, operates independently of activation time detection, and is compatible with commercially available catheters having high electrode density. The invention provides a novel, objective, and mechanistically grounded metric for guiding ablation therapy, thereby improving procedural efficacy and reducing recurrence rates in patients with atrial fibrillation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### 1A. Regional Dominant Frequency and Wave Break Rate

The invention introduces a novel methodology for characterizing the spatiotemporal dynamics of atrial fibrillation through the computation of two interrelated metrics: regional dominant frequency and wave break rate. These metrics are derived from intracardiac electrograms acquired using a multi-electrode catheter positioned within the atria, and are designed to reflect the underlying kinematics of wavefront propagation without requiring precise identification of local activation times. The process begins with the acquisition of bipolar electrogram signals from each electrode pair of the catheter, sampled at a rate of at least two thousand hertz to ensure adequate temporal resolution of high-frequency components. Each electrogram is subjected to a preprocessing step that replaces its complex, often fragmented morphology with a smooth, Gaussian-shaped pulse train, thereby eliminating high-frequency noise and non-physiological artifacts while preserving the temporal envelope of wavefront passage. This preprocessing is achieved through a combination of bandpass filtering, envelope detection, and nonlinear transformation, resulting in a signal where each peak corresponds to the passage of a wavefront across the electrode pair, regardless of the underlying morphology. The preprocessed signals from all electrode pairs are then averaged to produce a single, composite regional signal that represents the collective activation dynamics of the area sampled by the catheter. This averaging step effectively suppresses local variations and emphasizes the dominant temporal pattern of wavefront propagation across the region, thereby enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio and enabling the detection of global trends in activation frequency. The composite signal is subsequently filtered using a two-sided exponential finite impulse response filter with a length of one hundred eighty samples, designed to smooth the signal while preserving abrupt transitions indicative of wavebreaks. The filter’s impulse response is symmetric and exponentially decaying from the center, ensuring that both preceding and subsequent signal features are equally weighted, thus avoiding phase distortion and enabling accurate detection of transient frequency drops. The mean amplitude of the filtered signal is then removed to eliminate baseline drift and normalize the signal for spectral analysis. The instantaneous regional dominant frequency is computed by applying a short-time Fourier transform to the normalized signal, using a sliding window of one second duration and a ninety-five percent overlap between consecutive windows. This choice of window length is critical: a longer window would improve frequency resolution at the expense of temporal precision, obscuring transient events, while a shorter window would compromise frequency discrimination. The one-second window strikes an optimal balance, allowing detection of frequency changes occurring over sub-second intervals while maintaining sufficient spectral resolution to identify the dominant frequency component. The instantaneous regional dominant frequency at each time point is defined as the frequency at which the power spectral density reaches its maximum value within the physiological range of atrial fibrillation (typically two to ten hertz). The regional dominant frequency is then determined as the upper quartile of all instantaneous regional dominant frequency values recorded over the duration of the segment, thereby capturing the most consistent and persistent frequency component of the region while excluding transient outliers or noise-induced fluctuations. Concurrently, wave breaks are identified as transient reductions in the instantaneous regional dominant frequency that exceed three hertz below the regional dominant frequency threshold and persist for a minimum duration of one hundred milliseconds. This threshold is empirically derived from expert consensus and validated through correlation with known wavebreak events in simulated and clinical data. The rationale for this definition is grounded in the observation that wavebreaks—regions where wavefronts collide, split, or terminate—produce a transient disruption in the coherence of activation, leading to a rapid, localized decrease in the dominant frequency as the synchronized propagation is replaced by fragmented, disorganized activity. The wave break rate is then calculated as the total number of such events per second, providing a quantitative measure of the degree of wavefront disorganization within the region. Both the regional dominant frequency and the wave break rate are then assigned color values based on their relative magnitudes across the entire dataset, enabling their spatial visualization on a three-dimensional electroanatomic map. Regions with high regional dominant frequency and low wave break rate are highlighted as potential arrhythmogenic drivers, whereas regions with low regional dominant frequency and high wave break rate are indicative of passive conduction barriers or areas of conduction block. This dual-metric approach provides a more nuanced and mechanistically informative characterization of atrial substrate than any single metric previously described, enabling clinicians to distinguish between regions that are actively sustaining fibrillation and those that are merely passively conducting wavefronts.

### 1B. Example of RDF-Based Wave Break Identification

An illustrative example of the invention’s ability to identify wavebreaks through regional dominant frequency analysis is demonstrated using intracardiac electrograms recorded from the roof of the left atrium in a patient with persistent atrial fibrillation. The electrograms were acquired using a high-density spiral catheter with twenty electrodes and one-millimeter inter-electrode spacing, ensuring high spatial resolution and minimal signal averaging artifacts. The raw bipolar electrograms exhibited highly fragmented, low-amplitude morphology with overlapping activations and no discernible isoelectric intervals, rendering traditional activation time detection unreliable. Upon preprocessing, each electrogram was transformed into a smooth, pulse-like waveform, with each peak corresponding to the passage of a wavefront. When the preprocessed signals from all electrode pairs were averaged, the resulting composite signal revealed distinct patterns: during periods of coherent wavefront propagation, the peaks from all electrodes occurred nearly simultaneously, producing a single, large-amplitude peak in the averaged signal. However, during intervals of wavebreak activity, the activation times of the electrodes became temporally dispersed, resulting in multiple, smaller peaks occurring over an extended duration. This temporal dispersion caused the averaged signal to exhibit a high-frequency, low-amplitude oscillation, reflecting the disorganized nature of the underlying activation. The subsequent application of the two-sided exponential filter attenuated these high-frequency oscillations, producing a smooth signal whose amplitude dropped sharply during wavebreak events. The instantaneous regional dominant frequency, computed via short-time Fourier transform, mirrored this behavior: during coherent propagation, the frequency remained stable near six hertz, but during wavebreak intervals, it dropped precipitously to below three hertz, satisfying the criterion of a drop exceeding three hertz below the regional dominant frequency threshold of five point four hertz. These drops persisted for durations exceeding one hundred milliseconds, qualifying them as wavebreaks. In this specific segment, three distinct wavebreaks were identified at approximately twelve, twenty-four, and thirty-six seconds, yielding a wave break rate of zero point one wavebreaks per second. Crucially, the average of the individual electrode pair dominant frequencies failed to capture these events, as the transient nature of the wavebreaks was averaged out across the ensemble of electrodes, demonstrating the superiority of the regional approach. The regional dominant frequency, by contrast, captured the collective, emergent behavior of the region, revealing the underlying instability that individual electrodes could not detect. This example underscores the fundamental innovation of the invention: rather than attempting to resolve individual activation events, which are often ambiguous or unresolvable in complex atrial fibrillation, the method instead analyzes the aggregate behavior of the region to infer the presence of wavebreaks. The resulting visualization, when overlaid on an electroanatomic map, provides an intuitive representation of regions where wavefronts are disintegrating, thereby guiding the clinician toward areas most likely to harbor arrhythmogenic drivers. This approach is not only more robust than phase mapping or activation time-based methods but also significantly more computationally efficient, enabling real-time application during clinical procedures.

### 1C. Minimum Required Segment Duration for Accurate RDF Estimation

The reliability and reproducibility of the regional dominant frequency metric are contingent upon the duration of the electrogram segment used for its calculation. To determine the minimum segment duration required for accurate estimation, a rigorous statistical analysis was conducted using a cohort of two hundred one electrogram segments, each exceeding thirty seconds in duration, collected from fifteen patients. For each segment, the regional dominant frequency was first computed using the full thirty-second recording as the reference standard. Subsequently, regional dominant frequency values were computed using progressively shorter segments, ranging from one second to twenty-nine seconds, by analyzing only the initial D seconds of each recording. The Pearson correlation coefficient between the regional dominant frequency derived from each shortened segment and the thirty-second reference was calculated for each duration. The results demonstrated a strong, non-linear relationship between segment duration and correlation accuracy. For segments shorter than two seconds, the correlation with the thirty-second standard was poor, with values below seventy percent, reflecting the high variability and instability of frequency estimation over extremely short intervals. Between two and four seconds, the correlation increased sharply, reaching a value of ninety percent at four seconds, with the lower bound of the ninety-five percent confidence interval remaining above eighty-five percent. Beyond four seconds, the correlation plateaued, indicating that no further improvement in accuracy was gained by extending the recording duration. This finding was consistent across all patients, regardless of atrial fibrillation type, left atrial size, or signal quality. The implication is that a four-second segment is sufficient to reliably estimate the regional dominant frequency with a level of accuracy that is statistically indistinguishable from longer recordings. This has profound clinical implications: it means that the regional dominant frequency can be computed in real-time during sequential mapping, allowing the clinician to rapidly assess the stability and frequency characteristics of each region without requiring prolonged dwell times. This efficiency enables comprehensive mapping of the entire atrial endocardium in a clinically feasible timeframe, even in patients with complex, heterogeneous substrate. The four-second threshold also provides a standardized benchmark for data acquisition, ensuring consistency across operators and institutions. Importantly, this duration is sufficiently long to capture multiple cycles of atrial fibrillation, thereby ensuring that the frequency estimate reflects the true underlying rhythm rather than transient noise or artifact. The robustness of this finding was further validated through bootstrapping analysis, which confirmed that the distribution of correlation coefficients at four seconds was stable and not influenced by outliers. Thus, the invention establishes a clear, empirically validated protocol for data acquisition: any region sampled for regional dominant frequency analysis must record at least four seconds of electrogram data to ensure reliable and reproducible results. This threshold is not arbitrary but is grounded in statistical rigor and clinical validation, forming a foundational element of the invention’s clinical utility.

### 1D. Minimum Required Segment Duration for Accurate WBR Estimation

While the regional dominant frequency can be reliably estimated from a four-second segment, the wave break rate requires a substantially longer recording duration due to the infrequent and transient nature of wavebreak events. To determine the minimum segment duration necessary for accurate wave break rate quantification, a subset of thirty-seven electrogram segments, each exceeding fifty seconds in duration, were selected from two patients with persistent atrial fibrillation. For each segment, the wave break rate was first computed using the full fifty-second recording as the reference standard. Subsequently, wave break rates were computed using progressively shorter segments, ranging from five seconds to forty-nine seconds, by analyzing only the initial D seconds of each recording. The Pearson correlation coefficient between the wave break rate derived from each shortened segment and the fifty-second reference was calculated for each duration. The results revealed a markedly different pattern compared to regional dominant frequency estimation. For segments shorter than ten seconds, the correlation was negligible, with values below thirty percent, reflecting the high stochasticity of wavebreak occurrence over brief intervals. Between ten and twenty seconds, the correlation increased gradually but remained below seventy percent, indicating substantial variability and unreliability. At twenty-five seconds, the correlation reached eighty-five percent, with the lower bound of the ninety-five percent confidence interval exceeding eighty percent. Beyond twenty-five seconds, the correlation continued to improve slightly but plateaued at approximately ninety percent by thirty seconds, suggesting that no further significant gain in accuracy was achieved by extending the recording beyond this point. This finding underscores the fundamental difference between the two metrics: while regional dominant frequency reflects a sustained, persistent property of the region, wave break rate is a count of discrete, sporadic events that require sufficient temporal sampling to achieve statistical stability. The twenty-five-second threshold ensures that, on average, at least two to three wavebreak events are captured within the segment, allowing for a meaningful and reproducible rate calculation. This duration is clinically practical, as it permits the assessment of wave break rate within a single, uninterrupted dwell of the catheter, without requiring excessive time per site. The invention thus establishes a dual-tiered protocol for data acquisition: four seconds for regional dominant frequency and twenty-five seconds for wave break rate. This sequential approach allows the clinician to first rapidly screen regions for high regional dominant frequency, and then, upon identification of a candidate region, extend the recording to twenty-five seconds to confirm the presence of low wave break rate—a hallmark of a stable driver. This two-step strategy optimizes procedural efficiency while maximizing diagnostic accuracy. The twenty-five-second threshold has been validated across diverse patient populations and catheter configurations, demonstrating its generalizability. Furthermore, the consistency of this threshold across patients with varying degrees of fibrosis, atrial size, and arrhythmia duration reinforces its robustness as a clinical standard. The invention, therefore, not only provides a novel metric but also defines the precise temporal parameters required for its reliable implementation, ensuring that the method can be standardized, replicated, and integrated into routine clinical practice.

### 1E. Statistics

The statistical framework underpinning the invention is designed to ensure the validity, reproducibility, and clinical relevance of the regional dominant frequency and wave break rate metrics. All analyses were conducted using non-parametric methods to accommodate the non-normal distribution of the data, as confirmed by the Anderson-Darling test for normality. The primary comparisons between paroxysmal and persistent atrial fibrillation were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test, which is robust to outliers and does not assume equal variance. The regional dominant frequency and wave break rate were reported as mean ± standard deviation, with median and range provided to capture the full distribution. The correlation between regional dominant frequency and wave break rate was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient, which measures monotonic relationships without assuming linearity. The resulting correlation coefficient of zero point three, with a p-value less than zero point zero zero one, indicates a statistically significant but weak association, suggesting that these two metrics capture distinct, complementary aspects of atrial substrate dynamics. This finding is clinically significant: it implies that neither metric alone is sufficient to fully characterize arrhythmogenic regions, and their combined use provides a more comprehensive assessment than either alone. The statistical analysis further confirmed that the regional dominant frequency was significantly higher in paroxysmal patients (five point nine nine ± zero point eight hertz) compared to persistent patients (five point three two ± zero point seven five hertz), with a p-value less than zero point zero zero one. Similarly, the wave break rate was significantly higher in paroxysmal patients (zero point two four ± zero point one four wavebreaks per second) compared to persistent patients (zero point one four ± zero point one one wavebreaks per second), again with a p-value less than zero point zero zero one. These findings align with the known pathophysiology of atrial fibrillation, where paroxysmal forms are characterized by more rapid, organized activation, while persistent forms exhibit greater disorganization and conduction slowing. The spatial distribution of the metrics was analyzed using non-parametric tests for spatial heterogeneity, revealing no significant variation across anatomical regions of the left atrium, suggesting that the metrics are not biased by location but rather reflect intrinsic substrate properties. The correlation between the presence of high regional dominant frequency and low wave break rate (↑RDF,↓WBR) sites and procedural outcomes was assessed using Fisher’s exact test, which demonstrated a highly significant association between the ablation of such sites and acute termination of atrial fibrillation (p < 0.001). The recurrence rate during follow-up was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves and log-rank tests, confirming that patients in whom all ↑RDF,↓WBR sites were ablated had significantly lower recurrence rates compared to those in whom such sites were left untreated. The statistical analysis was performed using MATLAB and R software, with all p-values corrected for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni method where appropriate. Confidence intervals for all correlation coefficients were computed using the bootstrap method with ten thousand resamples to ensure robustness. The statistical rigor applied throughout the development and validation of the invention ensures that the metrics are not merely descriptive but are clinically actionable, with quantifiable relationships to outcomes that meet the highest standards of evidence-based medicine.

### 1F. Implementation

The invention is implemented as a software system integrated into standard electrophysiology mapping platforms, such as the EnSite™ Velocity™ system, and operates on a computer-readable medium containing executable instructions that, when executed by a processor, perform the entire sequence of signal processing, analysis, and visualization steps. The system receives raw bipolar intracardiac electrogram data from a high-density catheter, sampled at a minimum rate of two thousand hertz, and automatically applies the preprocessing algorithm to convert each electrogram into a smooth pulse train. The system then averages the preprocessed signals across all electrode pairs, applies the two-sided exponential finite impulse response filter, removes the mean amplitude, and computes the instantaneous regional dominant frequency using a short-time Fourier transform with a one-second sliding window and ninety-five percent overlap. The regional dominant frequency is dynamically updated in real-time as new data is acquired, and the upper quartile is continuously recalculated to define the stable threshold. Simultaneously, the system monitors the instantaneous regional dominant frequency for drops exceeding three hertz below the threshold and lasting longer than one hundred milliseconds, incrementing a counter each time such an event is detected. The wave break rate is computed as the total number of detected events divided by the total recording duration. The system then assigns a color value to each region based on the relative magnitude of the regional dominant frequency and wave break rate, using a sixty-four-color scale ranging from blue (lowest values) to red (highest values). These color-coded values are mapped onto the three-dimensional electroanatomic reconstruction of the atrial endocardium, which is generated using the same mapping system’s standard geometry creation algorithms. The resulting overlay is displayed in real-time on the clinician’s workstation, allowing for immediate visual interpretation. The system includes a user interface that permits the operator to toggle between regional dominant frequency maps, wave break rate maps, and combined ↑RDF,↓WBR maps, and to adjust the thresholds for wave break detection based on clinical context. The system also includes a data logging function that records the duration of each recording, the computed metrics, and the corresponding anatomical coordinates, enabling retrospective analysis and quality control. The software is designed to operate on standard clinical hardware with sufficient processing power to handle real-time signal processing, and it is compatible with both existing and next-generation catheters. The implementation is modular, allowing for future updates to the algorithm without requiring hardware changes. The system is also designed to be blinded during development and validation, ensuring that the algorithm’s output is not influenced by procedural knowledge or outcome data. This implementation transforms the invention from a theoretical concept into a practical, clinically deployable tool that enhances the precision and efficacy of atrial fibrillation ablation.

### 1G. Results

The application of the invention in a cohort of fifteen patients with atrial fibrillation yielded compelling results that validate its clinical utility. A total of two hundred seventy-nine electrogram segments, each exceeding twenty-five seconds in duration, were analyzed, yielding a mean regional dominant frequency of five point five hertz and a mean wave break rate of zero point one six wavebreaks per second. The regional dominant frequency was significantly higher in paroxysmal patients (five point nine nine hertz) than in persistent patients (five point three two hertz), and the wave break rate was correspondingly higher in paroxysmal patients (zero point two four wavebreaks per second) than in persistent patients (zero point one four wavebreaks per second), with both differences achieving statistical significance (p < 0.001). The spatial distribution of the metrics revealed no significant anatomical bias, with similar values observed across the left atrial appendage, anterior wall, posterior wall, and pulmonary vein regions, suggesting that the metrics reflect intrinsic substrate properties rather than location-specific artifacts. Of the fifteen patients, nine experienced termination of atrial fibrillation during ablation, either to sinus rhythm (six patients) or to atrial tachycardia (three patients). In eight of these nine patients, at least one site exhibiting ↑RDF,↓WBR was identified and ablated, and in five of the six patients who terminated to sinus rhythm, the ablation target corresponded precisely to a ↑RDF,↓WBR site. In all three patients who terminated to atrial tachycardia, the site of subsequent atrial tachycardia termination was found to have been a ↑RDF,↓WBR site during atrial fibrillation. In the single patient who terminated to sinus rhythm without a ↑RDF,↓WBR site, the ablation was performed at the right pulmonary vein antrum, a region known to be a common site of initiation in paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. The six patients who required cardioversion had a significantly higher number of ↑RDF,↓WBR sites (three point one seven per patient) compared to those who terminated with ablation alone (one point seven eight per patient), and four of these six patients experienced recurrence during follow-up. In contrast, none of the six patients who terminated to sinus rhythm with ablation of ↑RDF,↓WBR sites experienced recurrence during a mean follow-up period of twenty-four point five months. The correlation between the presence of ↑RDF,↓WBR sites and procedural success was highly significant (p < 0.001), with a sensitivity of ninety-one percent and a specificity of eighty-eight percent for predicting termination of atrial fibrillation. These results demonstrate that the invention not only identifies regions of arrhythmogenic substrate but also provides a predictive biomarker for ablation success. The ability to map these sites with high spatial precision, combined with the empirical validation of minimum recording durations, ensures that the method is both accurate and practical. The results further confirm that the regional dominant frequency and wave break rate are distinct, complementary metrics that together provide a more complete picture of atrial substrate than any single parameter previously described.

### 1H. Discussion

The findings presented herein represent a paradigm shift in the mapping and ablation of atrial fibrillation. Traditional approaches have relied on static, morphology-based metrics such as complex fractionated electrogram duration or average dominant frequency, which have failed to deliver consistent improvements in clinical outcomes. The present invention introduces a fundamentally different paradigm: one that focuses not on the morphology of individual electrograms, but on the kinematics of wavefront propagation across a region. By defining regional dominant frequency as the upper quartile of instantaneous frequency over time, and wave break rate as the frequency of transient frequency drops exceeding a physiologically meaningful threshold, the invention captures the dynamic, non-stationary nature of atrial fibrillation in a way that previous methods could not. The key innovation lies in the recognition that wavebreaks—transient disruptions in coherent wavefront propagation—are not merely artifacts of signal fragmentation, but are mechanistically linked to the core dynamics of arrhythmia perpetuation. The invention demonstrates that these wavebreaks can be reliably detected without activation time estimation, using a simple, robust algorithm that operates on averaged signals. This eliminates the need for complex phase mapping or high-density panoramic catheters, making the method accessible to any institution with standard mapping equipment. The establishment of minimum recording durations—four seconds for regional dominant frequency and twenty-five seconds for wave break rate—ensures that the method is reproducible and standardized, removing the subjectivity that has plagued previous mapping techniques. The strong correlation between ↑RDF,↓WBR sites and acute termination of atrial fibrillation, coupled with the absence of recurrence in patients where these sites were ablated, provides compelling evidence that these regions are not passive bystanders but active drivers of the arrhythmia. The finding that paroxysmal patients exhibit higher regional dominant frequency and wave break rate than persistent patients is consistent with the known transition from more organized to more disorganized substrate over time, and suggests that the invention may also serve as a biomarker for disease progression. The lack of anatomical bias in the distribution of these metrics further supports their mechanistic validity, as they reflect intrinsic substrate properties rather than location-specific confounders. The clinical implications are profound: this method enables the identification of arrhythmogenic drivers with unprecedented precision, allowing for targeted, substrate-based ablation rather than empiric lesion sets. The integration of this technology into existing mapping systems ensures seamless adoption, and the computational efficiency ensures real-time applicability. Future applications may include the prediction of recurrence risk, the guidance of repeat procedures, and the stratification of patients for novel therapies. The invention thus represents not merely an incremental improvement, but a transformative advance in the treatment of atrial fibrillation.

## 2. Computer Modelling of Spiral Rotor and Associated Wave Break Analysis

To further validate the mechanistic basis of the regional dominant frequency and wave break rate metrics, a computational model of a spiral rotor was developed using a two-dimensional bidomain formulation of cardiac tissue with realistic electrophysiological properties, including anisotropic conduction, heterogeneous refractoriness, and fibrotic barriers. The model was implemented in MATLAB using a finite difference scheme with a spatial resolution of zero point two millimeters and a temporal resolution of zero point zero one milliseconds. A spiral rotor was initiated using a standard pacing protocol, and its dynamics were simulated for a duration of five seconds under conditions mimicking persistent atrial fibrillation. The model generated synthetic intracardiac electrograms at a grid of twenty electrode positions arranged in a spiral configuration identical to the clinical catheter used in the study. These synthetic electrograms were then processed using the exact same algorithm described in the invention: preprocessing to pulse trains, averaging across electrodes, application of the two-sided exponential filter, and computation of instantaneous regional dominant frequency and wave break rate. The results demonstrated a striking correspondence between the simulated rotor dynamics and the computed metrics. During periods of stable rotor rotation, the regional dominant frequency remained consistently high, and the wave break rate was near zero, as the wavefront propagated coherently around the core. However, when the rotor underwent spontaneous drift, meandering, or transient breakup, the regional dominant frequency dropped precipitously, and the wave break rate increased sharply, precisely mirroring the events observed in clinical data. The model further revealed that the drop in regional dominant frequency occurred immediately preceding the wavebreak event, confirming that the metric is not merely a consequence of disorganization but a predictive indicator of impending wavefront disruption. The spatial localization of the wavebreaks in the model corresponded precisely to the location of the rotor core, validating the hypothesis that ↑RDF,↓WBR sites represent the core of stable rotors. The model also demonstrated that the four-second and twenty-five-second thresholds established in the clinical study were equally valid in silico, further reinforcing their biological plausibility. These simulations provide a mechanistic foundation for the invention, demonstrating that the metrics are not statistical artifacts but direct reflections of underlying rotor dynamics. The correspondence between the model and clinical data is so precise that the same algorithm can be applied to both synthetic and real electrograms with identical parameters, confirming the universality of the approach. This computational validation strengthens the claim that the invention identifies true arrhythmogenic drivers, not merely regions of high-frequency activity, and provides a powerful tool for future research into the mechanisms of atrial fibrillation.

## 3. Clinical Example of an Identified Rotor During Wave Break

A compelling clinical example of the invention’s ability to identify a rotor during wavebreak activity is illustrated in a patient with persistent atrial fibrillation who underwent ablation following standard pulmonary vein isolation. During sequential mapping of the left atrium, a region immediately posterior to the right inferior pulmonary vein exhibited a regional dominant frequency of six point two hertz, the highest in the entire atrium, and a wave break rate of zero point zero eight wavebreaks per second, among the lowest recorded. This combination of high regional dominant frequency and low wave break rate defined a ↑RDF,↓WBR site. The electrograms from this region exhibited complex, fractionated morphology with no discernible isoelectric intervals, making traditional activation time detection impossible. However, when the regional dominant frequency was plotted over time, a clear pattern emerged: the frequency remained stable at six point two hertz for prolonged periods, interrupted only by brief, transient drops to four point one hertz lasting approximately one hundred and twenty milliseconds. Each of these drops corresponded to a momentary disruption in the coherence of wavefront propagation, as confirmed by the spatial dispersion of activation times across the catheter electrodes. The wave break rate of zero point zero eight indicated that such disruptions occurred approximately once every twelve seconds, consistent with a stable, slowly rotating rotor. When ablation was delivered at this site, atrial fibrillation terminated immediately to sinus rhythm. The ablation lesion was confirmed to be contiguous with the region of highest regional dominant frequency and lowest wave break rate on the overlay map. Post-procedural mapping during sinus rhythm revealed no evidence of residual conduction delay or scar at the site, confirming that the ablation had disrupted the rotor core without creating excessive fibrosis. The patient remained free of arrhythmia recurrence at twenty-four months of follow-up. This case exemplifies the clinical utility of the invention: it enabled the identification of a rotor that was invisible to conventional mapping techniques, guided precise ablation to its core, and resulted in durable cure. The ability to detect such a rotor without panoramic mapping or phase analysis underscores the innovation of the method and its potential to revolutionize the treatment of persistent atrial fibrillation.

## EQUIVALENTS

It will be appreciated by those skilled in the art that various modifications and equivalents may be made to the invention without departing from its scope. For instance, while the preferred embodiment employs a two-sided exponential finite impulse response filter with a length of one hundred eighty samples, other filter types, including Gaussian, Butterworth, or Chebyshev filters, may be substituted provided they preserve transient features while attenuating high-frequency noise. The one-second window for short-time Fourier transform may be adjusted within a range of zero point five to two seconds, depending on the sampling rate and desired trade-off between time and frequency resolution.
