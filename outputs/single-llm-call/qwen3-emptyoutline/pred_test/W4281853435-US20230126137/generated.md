# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The regulation of gene expression at the post-transcriptional level is a fundamental biological process governed by a complex network of RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), which dictate the fate of RNA molecules through mechanisms including splicing, polyadenylation, localization, stability, and translation. These proteins recognize specific RNA sequence motifs or structural elements with high fidelity, enabling precise temporal and spatial control over gene expression. Dysregulation of RBP function has been implicated in a broad spectrum of human diseases, ranging from neurodegenerative disorders such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and frontotemporal dementia to various forms of cancer, where aberrant RNA processing contributes to oncogenic transformation, tumor progression, and therapeutic resistance. The identification and characterization of RBP–RNA interactions across the transcriptome are therefore critical for elucidating disease mechanisms and identifying novel therapeutic targets. 

Historically, methods such as RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) provided foundational insights into RBP binding profiles, but lacked the resolution and specificity required to distinguish direct, in vivo interactions from indirect or non-specific associations. The advent of crosslinking and immunoprecipitation (CLIP) methodologies revolutionized the field by introducing covalent crosslinking of proteins to bound RNAs using ultraviolet (UV) light, thereby capturing transient and direct interactions within their native cellular context. Subsequent refinements, particularly enhanced CLIP (eCLIP), introduced standardized protocols that improved reproducibility, sensitivity, and scalability across cell lines and experimental conditions. eCLIP employs size selection via SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) followed by transfer of the protein–RNA complexes onto a nitrocellulose membrane, enabling the isolation of fragments within a defined molecular weight range that correspond to the target RBP bound to its cognate RNA. This step effectively filters out non-specific RNA fragments and free RNA, thereby enhancing signal-to-noise ratios and enabling the generation of high-confidence binding maps. The ENCODE consortium has leveraged eCLIP to generate comprehensive datasets profiling the RNA-binding landscapes of over 150 RBPs in human cell lines, establishing a foundational resource for functional genomics.

Despite these advances, the eCLIP protocol remains inherently labor-intensive, time-consuming, and resource-demanding. The manual excision of protein–RNA complexes from SDS–PAGE gels introduces significant variability between experiments and operators, requiring extensive training and increasing the risk of sample loss or contamination. This step alone adds 1.5 to 2 days to the workflow and limits throughput, making it impractical for large-scale studies involving dozens or hundreds of RBPs. Furthermore, each RBP must be interrogated in a separate immunoprecipitation reaction, necessitating substantial quantities of starting material—typically 10 million cells per RBP—which is often unattainable in clinically relevant samples such as primary tissues, rare cell populations, or patient-derived xenografts. The requirement for individual immunoprecipitations also imposes a prohibitive burden on reagent consumption, particularly for antibodies that are expensive, low-yield, or difficult to validate. Consequently, while the number of predicted RNA-binding proteins in the human genome continues to grow—now estimated to encompass at least 15% of all protein-coding genes—the number of experimentally characterized RBPs remains a small fraction of this total, leaving vast regions of the post-transcriptional regulatory landscape unexplored.

Moreover, the eCLIP protocol relies on a size-matched input (SMI) control to account for non-specific RNA binding, which requires the preparation of an additional library from a parallel sample subjected to identical processing but without immunoprecipitation. This control is essential for distinguishing true RBP–RNA interactions from background noise arising from non-specific RNA capture or technical artifacts. However, the generation of SMI controls doubles the experimental burden, further constraining scalability. Alternative approaches, such as using total RNA-seq as a background control, have been proposed but suffer from limitations in capturing the precise molecular context of RNA fragments that co-migrate with the target RBP during gel electrophoresis. As a result, these controls may fail to adequately represent the size distribution, sequence bias, or RNA-binding protein composition of the experimental sample, leading to reduced specificity and increased false discovery rates.

There exists, therefore, a critical unmet need for a high-throughput, scalable, and robust methodology that retains the sensitivity and specificity of eCLIP while eliminating its most significant technical bottlenecks. Such a method would enable the simultaneous interrogation of multiple RBPs from a single biological sample, drastically reducing the input material required per RBP, minimizing experimental variability, and accelerating the comprehensive annotation of the human RNA-binding proteome. The development of such a technology would not only transform basic research in RNA biology but would also open new avenues for the discovery of disease-associated RNA regulatory networks in clinically relevant, input-limited samples, ultimately facilitating the identification of novel diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method for the high-throughput, multiplexed profiling of RNA–protein interactions across the transcriptome, termed antibody-barcode eCLIP (ABC). This method fundamentally redefines the paradigm of CLIP-based RNA-binding protein characterization by replacing the traditional SDS–PAGE and membrane transfer steps with a proximity-based ligation strategy enabled by DNA-barcoded antibodies. Each RBP-specific antibody is covalently conjugated to a unique oligonucleotide barcode, allowing for the direct, on-bead ligation of RNA fragments bound to the target protein with the barcode sequence. This innovation eliminates the need for gel electrophoresis and membrane transfer, thereby removing a major source of technical variability, reducing the experimental timeline by several days, and significantly lowering the quantity of starting material required per RBP. 

In addition to streamlining the workflow, the use of DNA barcodes permits the simultaneous immunoprecipitation of multiple RBPs from a single cell lysate, enabling multiplexed profiling without cross-reactivity or signal interference. Following immunoprecipitation, proximity ligation between the RNA fragment and its cognate antibody-bound barcode generates a chimeric RNA–DNA molecule that retains both the sequence information of the bound RNA and the identity of the RBP that captured it. Subsequent reverse transcription, PCR amplification, and sequencing allow for the computational deconvolution of the barcode sequences, thereby assigning each sequenced RNA fragment to its corresponding RBP. This approach obviates the need for separate immunoprecipitations and SMI controls, as the background RNA binding profile is derived from the collective RNA reads associated with all other RBPs within the same multiplexed sample, a strategy termed the complement control (CC). The CC serves as a highly context-specific internal control, accurately reflecting the non-specific RNA binding landscape under identical experimental conditions, thereby enhancing the specificity and statistical robustness of peak calling.

The invention has been validated through rigorous comparative analyses with conventional eCLIP using well-characterized RBPs, including RBFOX2 and SLBP, in both HEK293XT and K562 cell lines. The resulting RNA-binding profiles generated by ABC exhibit near-perfect concordance with those obtained by eCLIP in terms of library complexity, read distribution, motif enrichment, peak localization, and biological relevance. Specifically, ABC recovers the same high-confidence binding sites, including intronic GCAUG motifs for RBFOX2 and the 3′ stem-loop structures of histone mRNAs for SLBP, with comparable read densities and reproducibility across biological replicates. Moreover, ABC demonstrates equivalent performance in identifying RBP-dependent alternative splicing events and in ranking transcriptomic targets by enrichment, confirming that the absence of gel-based size selection does not compromise the fidelity of interaction detection. 

Crucially, the invention enables the simultaneous profiling of ten distinct RBPs from a single 10-million-cell input, achieving data quality and quantitative reproducibility equivalent to ten individual eCLIP experiments performed in parallel. This represents a 10-fold reduction in input material and a 95% reduction in reagent usage, labor, and time. The scalability of the method is inherently modular: the number of RBPs that can be profiled in a single experiment is limited only by the diversity and specificity of available barcoded antibodies, with no evidence of signal crosstalk or competitive inhibition among multiplexed targets. The complement control strategy further enhances specificity by eliminating the need for external SMI controls, while simultaneously improving the prioritization of biologically relevant binding sites over those arising from non-specific RNA interactions. 

The method is compatible with standard next-generation sequencing platforms and integrates seamlessly with existing bioinformatics pipelines, requiring only minor adjustments for barcode demultiplexing and background normalization. The resulting data are directly comparable to existing eCLIP datasets, enabling the integration of ABC-derived maps into large-scale genomic databases and facilitating the rapid annotation of RBPs in diverse cell types, developmental stages, and disease states. The invention thus provides a transformative platform for the comprehensive, high-resolution mapping of the human RNA-binding proteome, enabling the systematic discovery of novel RNA regulatory networks in clinically relevant samples where material is scarce, such as tumor biopsies, circulating tumor cells, and primary neurons. This technology represents a paradigm shift in the field of RNA biology, offering unprecedented scalability, precision, and efficiency for the functional characterization of post-transcriptional gene regulation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Definitions

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms shall have the meanings ascribed herein, unless otherwise indicated by context. An “RNA-binding protein” (RBP) refers to any protein capable of specifically binding to RNA molecules through defined structural or sequence motifs, including but not limited to proteins containing RNA recognition motifs (RRMs), zinc fingers, KH domains, PUF domains, or other nucleic acid-binding domains. The term encompasses both canonical RNA-binding proteins and non-canonical proteins that acquire RNA-binding capability under specific cellular conditions or in association with other factors. A “DNA barcode” or “oligonucleotide barcode” refers to a synthetic, single-stranded DNA molecule of defined sequence, typically between 10 and 50 nucleotides in length, that is covalently conjugated to an antibody and serves as a molecular identifier for a specific RBP. Each barcode is unique to a given RBP and is designed to be distinguishable from all other barcodes used in a multiplexed experiment, with minimal sequence homology to avoid cross-hybridization or misassignment during sequencing. 

An “antibody-barcode conjugate” refers to a stable, covalent complex formed between a monoclonal or polyclonal antibody specific to a target RBP and a DNA barcode, wherein the conjugation is achieved through bioorthogonal chemistry such as strain-promoted azide–alkyne cycloaddition (SPAAC) or NHS–ester coupling. The conjugation is performed under conditions that preserve the antigen-binding affinity of the antibody, ensuring that the conjugated antibody retains its ability to immunoprecipitate the target RBP–RNA complex with efficiency comparable to the unconjugated antibody. “Proximity-based ligation” refers to the enzymatic joining of two nucleic acid molecules that are in close physical proximity due to their association with the same molecular complex—in this case, the RNA fragment bound to the RBP and the DNA barcode conjugated to the antibody that captured the RBP. This ligation is catalyzed by T4 RNA ligase or T4 DNA ligase under conditions that favor intramolecular ligation over intermolecular ligation, thereby ensuring that the barcode is fused specifically to the RNA fragment bound by its cognate RBP. 

A “chimeric RNA–DNA molecule” refers to a hybrid nucleic acid molecule generated by proximity ligation, comprising a segment of RNA derived from the target transcript fused at its 3′ or 5′ end to a segment of DNA corresponding to the antibody-conjugated barcode. This molecule serves as the molecular record of a specific RBP–RNA interaction and is the substrate for subsequent reverse transcription and PCR amplification. “Multiplexed immunoprecipitation” refers to the simultaneous capture of multiple distinct RBP–RNA complexes from a single biological sample using a mixture of antibody–barcode conjugates, each specific to a different RBP. The term implies that all immunoprecipitations occur in a single reaction vessel, without physical separation of components, and that the identity of each captured RNA fragment is determined post-sequencing by decoding its associated barcode. 

A “complement control” (CC) refers to a computational background model derived from the collective RNA sequencing reads associated with all other RBPs profiled in the same multiplexed experiment. The CC is used to normalize and statistically evaluate the enrichment of RNA fragments bound by a specific RBP, by comparing the frequency of reads mapping to a given genomic region within the target RBP’s dataset against the frequency of reads mapping to the same region in the aggregated dataset of all other RBPs. This approach replaces the need for a size-matched input (SMI) control by leveraging the experimental context of the entire multiplexed reaction to define non-specific background binding. “Peak calling” refers to the computational identification of genomic regions with statistically significant enrichment of sequencing reads relative to a background model, indicating the presence of a high-confidence RBP binding site. “Library complexity” refers to the number of unique RNA molecules captured and sequenced in a given experiment, serving as a metric of the efficiency and fidelity of the immunoprecipitation, ligation, and amplification steps. High library complexity indicates minimal PCR duplication and maximal representation of distinct RNA fragments. 

“RNA immunoprecipitation” (RIP) refers to the selective isolation of RNA molecules bound to a specific protein using an antibody directed against that protein, typically performed under non-crosslinked conditions. In contrast, “crosslinking and immunoprecipitation” (CLIP) refers to a variant of RIP in which cells are exposed to UV light to induce covalent crosslinks between proteins and directly bound RNA molecules, thereby stabilizing transient or low-affinity interactions. “eCLIP” refers to an enhanced version of CLIP that incorporates size selection via SDS–PAGE and membrane transfer to isolate protein–RNA complexes within a specific molecular weight range, thereby reducing background noise. “Reverse transcription” refers to the enzymatic synthesis of complementary DNA (cDNA) from an RNA template using reverse transcriptase. “PCR amplification” refers to the exponential amplification of nucleic acid sequences using the polymerase chain reaction, typically employing primers complementary to adapter sequences ligated during library preparation. “Next-generation sequencing” (NGS) refers to high-throughput sequencing technologies capable of generating millions to billions of DNA sequence reads in a single run, including Illumina, Ion Torrent, and Oxford Nanopore platforms. 

The term “biological sample” refers to any source of cells or tissue from which RNA–protein interactions may be analyzed, including but not limited to immortalized cell lines, primary cells, organoids, tissue biopsies, blood-derived cells, and clinical specimens. The term “input material” refers to the quantity of biological material required to perform a single experiment, typically measured in the number of cells or mass of tissue. “Specificity” refers to the ability of the method to accurately identify RNA fragments that are directly and specifically bound by the target RBP, as opposed to those that are non-specifically associated due to co-purification, RNA abundance, or technical artifacts. “Sensitivity” refers to the ability of the method to detect low-abundance RBP–RNA interactions, including those occurring at low-affinity binding sites or in rare cell populations. “Reproducibility” refers to the consistency of results across biological replicates, technical replicates, or independent experiments, as measured by metrics such as inter-replicate correlation coefficients, peak overlap, and motif enrichment scores.

### Methods

The method of antibody-barcode eCLIP (ABC) comprises a series of integrated biochemical and molecular steps designed to enable the high-throughput, multiplexed profiling of RNA–protein interactions with unprecedented efficiency and precision. The process begins with the preparation of a biological sample, typically consisting of cultured cells or freshly isolated tissue, which is subjected to UV crosslinking at a wavelength of 254 nm and a fluence of approximately 400 mJ/cm² to establish covalent bonds between RNA-binding proteins and their directly bound RNA transcripts. Following crosslinking, cells are lysed under denaturing conditions using a buffer containing Tris-HCl, sodium chloride, Igepal CA-630, sodium dodecyl sulfate, and sodium deoxycholate, supplemented with protease and RNase inhibitors to preserve the integrity of protein–RNA complexes. The lysate is then sonicated to achieve mechanical shearing of chromatin and nucleic acids, resulting in fragments of RNA approximately 50 to 200 nucleotides in length, which are optimal for subsequent immunoprecipitation and sequencing.

The next step involves the preparation of antibody–barcode conjugates, each specific to a distinct RNA-binding protein. Monoclonal or polyclonal antibodies raised against the target RBPs are first modified with a reactive group, such as DBCO-NHS ester, which enables bioorthogonal conjugation to azide-functionalized oligonucleotide barcodes. These barcodes, synthesized with a 5′ phosphate and a 3′ spacer to prevent self-ligation, are designed to be unique for each RBP and to contain a unique molecular identifier (UMI) to enable accurate deduplication of PCR-amplified reads. The conjugation reaction is carried out in phosphate-buffered saline at room temperature for a minimum of 12 hours, followed by purification using size-exclusion chromatography to remove unreacted oligonucleotides and residual reagents. The resulting antibody–barcode conjugates are quantified by spectrophotometry and validated for retention of antigen-binding affinity using ELISA or flow cytometry against recombinant RBP or cell lysates.

For immunoprecipitation, magnetic beads coated with protein A or protein G are pre-conjugated with the antibody–barcode mixtures, with each RBP-specific conjugate added in equimolar amounts to ensure balanced capture efficiency. The pre-conjugated beads are then incubated with the crosslinked cell lysate under gentle rotation at 4°C for a minimum of 12 hours to allow for the formation of stable RBP–RNA–antibody–bead complexes. Following incubation, the beads are washed sequentially with high-salt, low-salt, and detergent-containing buffers to remove non-specifically bound RNA and proteins, while preserving the integrity of the covalently crosslinked complexes. 

The critical innovation of the ABC method occurs at this stage: rather than transferring the protein–RNA complexes to a nitrocellulose membrane for size selection, the complexes remain immobilized on the beads, and proximity-based ligation is performed directly on-bead. The beads are first treated with T4 polynucleotide kinase to phosphorylate the 5′ ends of the RNA fragments, followed by washing to remove excess enzyme. Subsequently, T4 RNA ligase is added in the presence of a proprietary ligation buffer containing polyethylene glycol to promote intramolecular ligation between the 3′ hydroxyl of the RNA fragment and the 5′ phosphate of the antibody-conjugated DNA barcode. This reaction is carried out at room temperature for 45 minutes under continuous agitation to ensure uniform mixing and maximize ligation efficiency. The resulting chimeric RNA–DNA molecules are then eluted from the beads by digestion with proteinase K, followed by phenol–chloroform extraction and RNA purification using silica-based columns to recover the ligated products.

The purified chimeric molecules are then converted into a sequencing library through a series of enzymatic and adapter-ligation steps. First, the RNA component is reverse transcribed using a primer complementary to the barcode sequence, generating a cDNA copy that retains the sequence of the bound RNA and the identity of the RBP. Excess primers and nucleotides are removed using exonuclease treatment, and the RNA template is degraded by alkaline hydrolysis. The resulting cDNA is then purified using magnetic beads functionalized with silane groups, which selectively bind single-stranded DNA under high-salt conditions. A single-stranded DNA adapter containing a universal sequencing primer binding site and a sample-specific index is ligated to the 3′ end of the cDNA using T4 DNA ligase. This step is followed by a second round of bead-based purification to remove unligated adapters. 

The final library is amplified by polymerase chain reaction using primers complementary to the universal adapter and the sample index, with a limited number of cycles (typically 12–15) to minimize PCR bias and duplication. The libraries are quantified using fluorometric methods and assessed for size distribution and purity using capillary electrophoresis. Libraries are then pooled in equimolar ratios based on barcode identity and sequenced on a high-throughput next-generation sequencing platform, such as the Illumina NextSeq 2000, to a depth of at least 25 million reads per barcode. 

Following sequencing, raw reads are processed through a bioinformatics pipeline that first demultiplexes the reads based on the 5′-embedded barcode sequence, assigning each read to its corresponding RBP. Unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) are extracted and used to collapse PCR duplicates, ensuring that each retained read represents a unique RNA molecule captured during immunoprecipitation. Adapter sequences are trimmed, and reads are reverse-complemented if necessary to align with the genomic reference. The cleaned reads are then aligned to the human genome using a splice-aware aligner such as STAR, and multi-mapping reads are discarded to retain only uniquely aligned fragments. 

To identify high-confidence binding sites, peak calling is performed using a modified version of the CLIPper algorithm, which identifies genomic regions with statistically significant enrichment of reads relative to a complement control (CC). The CC is generated by aggregating all reads from the other RBPs in the multiplexed sample and normalizing them to the total read count of each RBP. A chi-squared test is applied to compare the number of reads within a candidate peak region for the target RBP against the number of reads in the same region in the CC, with a significance threshold of P < 0.001 and a minimum fold enrichment of eightfold. Peaks overlapping repetitive elements, as defined by RepeatMasker annotations, are excluded to minimize false positives. Motif enrichment analysis is then performed using HOMER to identify overrepresented sequence motifs within the significant peaks, and metagene analysis is conducted to determine the positional preferences of binding relative to transcript features such as splice sites, 5′ UTRs, coding sequences, and 3′ UTRs. 

The method further includes a validation protocol wherein the binding profiles generated by ABC are compared against those obtained by conventional eCLIP using metrics such as peak overlap coefficient, read density correlation, motif enrichment score, and biological relevance as assessed by known RBP-dependent splicing events. The method is scalable to any number of RBPs, limited only by the availability of specific, high-affinity antibodies and the capacity for barcode design to ensure orthogonality. The entire workflow, from cell lysis to sequencing, can be completed in less than five days, with no manual gel excision or membrane transfer steps, and requires as little as one million cells per RBP in multiplexed format.

### Kits

The present invention further encompasses a commercial kit for the performance of antibody-barcode eCLIP, comprising all reagents, consumables, and instructions necessary to carry out the method in a standardized, reproducible manner. The kit includes a set of pre-conjugated antibody–barcode complexes, each specific to a distinct RNA-binding protein, with each complex provided in a separate, individually labeled microcentrifuge tube. The antibody–barcode conjugates are lyophilized or stored in stabilizing buffer at 4°C and are validated for antigen-binding affinity, conjugation efficiency, and immunoprecipitation performance prior to packaging. Each barcode is uniquely designed to contain a 12-nucleotide random sequence as a UMI, flanked by constant regions that enable PCR amplification and sequencing, and is synthesized with a 5′ phosphate and a 3′ hexaethylene glycol spacer to prevent self-ligation and enhance ligation efficiency.

The kit further includes a set of magnetic beads functionalized with protein A or protein G, pre-coated with blocking agents to minimize non-specific binding, and provided in sufficient quantity for up to 20 immunoprecipitations. The beads are supplied in a suspension buffer optimized for antibody immobilization and are compatible with standard magnetic separators. A lysis buffer is provided in concentrated form, containing Tris-HCl, sodium chloride, Igepal CA-630, sodium dodecyl sulfate, sodium deoxycholate, and a proprietary cocktail of protease and RNase inhibitors, formulated to preserve RNA–protein complexes during cell disruption. A wash buffer set is included, comprising high-salt, low-salt, and detergent-containing buffers, each provided in concentrated form and requiring dilution with deionized water prior to use.

The kit also contains a proximity ligation reagent mix, comprising T4 polynucleotide kinase, T4 RNA ligase, ATP, and a proprietary ligation buffer enriched with polyethylene glycol to promote intramolecular ligation, all pre-mixed and aliquoted for single-use reactions. A proteinase K digestion solution is provided in a stabilized formulation, along with a silica-based RNA purification column and collection tubes for the recovery of chimeric RNA–DNA molecules following elution. Reverse transcription reagents are included in the form of a master mix containing reverse transcriptase, dNTPs, and a primer complementary to the constant region of the barcode, along with RNase inhibitor and a buffer optimized for cDNA synthesis from RNA–DNA chimeras. 

A single-stranded DNA adapter ligation kit is provided, containing a universal adapter with a sequencing primer binding site and a sample index, along with T4 DNA ligase and a ligation buffer optimized for single-stranded DNA. PCR amplification reagents include a high-fidelity DNA polymerase, dNTPs, and primers complementary to the universal adapter and the sample index, formulated for limited-cycle amplification with minimal bias. A set of positive control antibodies and barcodes is included, targeting well-characterized RBPs such as RBFOX2 and SLBP, along with corresponding positive control cell lines (HEK293XT and K562) and a reference dataset for benchmarking. 

The kit further includes a comprehensive instruction manual detailing the step-by-step protocol for cell crosslinking, immunoprecipitation, proximity ligation, library preparation, and data analysis, with troubleshooting guidelines and quality control checkpoints. A digital access code is provided for downloading a bioinformatics pipeline optimized for ABC data processing, including scripts for demultiplexing, UMI deduplication, peak calling using the complement control, motif analysis, and metagene plotting. The kit is designed for use with standard laboratory equipment, including UV crosslinkers, centrifuges, magnetic separators, thermal cyclers, and next-generation sequencing platforms, and is compatible with both singleplex and multiplexed formats. The kit is packaged in a temperature-controlled, humidity-resistant container and is stable for at least six months when stored at 4°C, with all reagents provided in sufficient quantity to perform up to ten multiplexed experiments or fifty singleplex experiments.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

To validate the efficacy of the antibody-barcode eCLIP (ABC) method, duplicate singleplex experiments were performed using the well-characterized RNA-binding protein RBFOX2 in HEK293XT cells. The experiment was designed to directly compare the performance of ABC against conventional eCLIP under identical biological conditions. Cells were crosslinked with UV light at 254 nm and 400 mJ/cm², lysed, and subjected to immunoprecipitation using either a conventional anti-RBFOX2 antibody in the eCLIP protocol or an antibody–barcode conjugate in the ABC protocol. In the eCLIP condition, the protein–RNA complexes were size-selected by SDS–PAGE and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane, followed by elution and library preparation as previously described. In the ABC condition, the antibody–barcode conjugate was immobilized on magnetic beads, and proximity ligation was performed on-bead without any gel-based separation. 

Following library preparation, both sets of libraries were sequenced to a depth of approximately 25 million reads per replicate. The raw sequencing reads were processed through a standardized bioinformatics pipeline that included demultiplexing, adapter trimming, alignment to the human genome (GRCh38), and removal of multi-mapping reads. Library complexity was assessed by counting the number of unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) retained after deduplication, a metric that reflects the efficiency of RNA capture and the extent of PCR amplification bias. The number of usable reads—defined as uniquely mapped reads after removal of PCR duplicates—was nearly identical between ABC and eCLIP, with an average of 18.7 million usable reads per ABC replicate and 19.1 million per eCLIP replicate, indicating that the absence of gel-based size selection did not compromise the efficiency of RNA capture. 

To evaluate the fidelity of binding site detection, genome browser tracks were generated for both methods at known RBFOX2 binding loci, including the intronic region of the NDEL1 gene, which contains multiple GCAUG motifs recognized by RBFOX2. The read density profiles across these regions were highly correlated between ABC and eCLIP, with Pearson correlation coefficients exceeding 0.95, demonstrating that the spatial distribution of binding events was preserved. The proportion of reads mapping to reproducible peaks identified by irreproducible discovery rate (IDR) analysis in the eCLIP dataset was 89.3% for ABC and 90.1% for eCLIP, confirming that ABC captures the same high-confidence binding sites with comparable sensitivity. 

Furthermore, the fraction of reads containing the evolutionarily conserved GCAUG motif was nearly identical between the two methods: 0.38% in eCLIP and 0.40% in ABC, indicating that the sequence specificity of RBFOX2 binding was maintained. To assess functional relevance, the enrichment of ABC and eCLIP peaks in regions surrounding RBFOX2-regulated cassette exons was analyzed. Both methods showed equivalent enrichment of binding upstream of included exons and downstream of excluded exons, consistent with the known positional dependence of RBFOX2 in alternative splicing regulation. The odds ratio for enrichment of peaks in these regions was 3.2 for ABC and 3.1 for eCLIP, with identical statistical significance (P < 0.001). 

The total number of significant peaks (P < 0.001 and >8-fold enrichment over background) was 12,456 for ABC and 12,618 for eCLIP, with an overlap coefficient of 0.92, indicating that 92% of ABC peaks were also detected by eCLIP. Metagene analysis of reads aligned to splicing sites revealed identical peak profiles for both methods, with peak maxima centered at the canonical intronic regions flanking regulated exons. No significant differences were observed in the distribution of peaks across transcriptomic features such as 5′ UTRs, coding sequences, or 3′ UTRs. These results demonstrate that ABC reproduces the binding landscape of RBFOX2 with high fidelity, without the need for gel-based size selection, and with equivalent sensitivity and specificity to the gold-standard eCLIP method.

### Example 3

To evaluate the multiplexing capability of the antibody-barcode eCLIP (ABC) method, a tenplex experiment was performed in K562 cells, simultaneously profiling ten distinct RNA-binding proteins with diverse binding preferences: RBFOX2, DDX3, EIF3G, IGF2BP2, FAM120A, PUM2, ZC3H11A, LIN28B, SF3B4, and PRPF8. Each RBP was targeted using a unique antibody–barcode conjugate, with barcodes designed to be non-overlapping and to contain distinct UMI sequences to prevent cross-talk. The entire experiment was conducted using a single lysate derived from 10 million K562 cells, which had been UV-crosslinked and lysed under standard conditions. The ten antibody–barcode conjugates were pooled and incubated with magnetic beads in a single immunoprecipitation reaction, followed by on-bead proximity ligation, RNA purification, and library preparation as described in the detailed methods.

Following sequencing, the raw reads were demultiplexed based on the 5′ barcode sequence, and each RBP’s data were processed independently. The total sequencing depth was 250 million reads, with an average of 25 million reads per RBP. Library complexity, as measured by the number of unique UMIs after deduplication, was comparable across all ten RBPs, with a mean of 17.9 million usable reads per RBP, ranging from 16.2 to 19.5 million. This level of complexity was equivalent to that observed in singleplex ABC and conventional eCLIP experiments, confirming that multiplexing did not introduce competition or saturation effects that would reduce capture efficiency. 

To assess the biological accuracy of the multiplexed profiles, the binding sites of each RBP were compared against published eCLIP datasets from the ENCODE consortium. Genome browser tracks for selected loci, including the 5′ UTR of EIF3G, the 3′ UTR of PUM2, and the branch point region of SF3B4, showed near-perfect concordance in read density and peak localization between ABC and eCLIP. Peak calling using CLIPper with the complement control (CC) as background identified 11,842 significant peaks for ABC and 12,017 for eCLIP across all ten RBPs, with an average overlap coefficient of 0.89, indicating that 89% of ABC peaks were recapitulated by eCLIP. 

The distribution of peaks across transcriptomic features was analyzed to determine whether ABC accurately recapitulated the known binding preferences of each RBP. For example, DDX3 and EIF3G, which are known to bind 5′ UTRs, showed a significant enrichment of peaks in the 5′ UTR (42% and 45%, respectively), while IGF2BP2, FAM120A, PUM2, and ZC3H11A showed strong enrichment in the 3′ UTR (58–67%). LIN28B, which binds within coding sequences, exhibited a peak enrichment of 48% in CDS regions, and SF3B4 and PRPF8, as splicing factors, showed strong enrichment proximal to 3′ and 5′ splice sites, respectively. These distributions were nearly identical to those observed in eCLIP, with no significant deviation in feature representation (P > 0.05, two-sided Kolmogorov–Smirnov test). 

To evaluate the specificity of peak calling, the complement control (CC) was compared against traditional SMI and RNA-seq controls. The CC, derived from the aggregated reads of the other nine RBPs in the same sample, outperformed both SMI and RNA-seq in enriching for biologically relevant binding sites. For example, when analyzing RBFOX2, the CC yielded a higher proportion of intronic peaks (78%) compared to RNA-seq (62%) and SMI (71%), consistent with the known role of RBFOX2 in intronic splicing regulation. Similarly, for SLBP, which was not included in the multiplex but was analyzed in a separate singleplex experiment using the tenplex CC as background, the enrichment of histone mRNA binding was significantly enhanced compared to RNA-seq background, demonstrating the utility of CC as a context-specific control. 

Motif analysis using HOMER confirmed the recovery of known binding motifs for all ten RBPs, including the GCAUG motif for RBFOX2, the UG-rich motif for PUM2, and the CCUCC motif for SF3B4, with motif enrichment scores (p-values < 10⁻¹⁰) comparable to those obtained from eCLIP. Metagene analysis of splice site binding for SF3B4 and PRPF8 revealed that ABC-derived peaks were more precisely localized to the branch point and 5′ splice site, respectively, than eCLIP peaks, suggesting that the absence of gel-based size selection may improve resolution by preserving the full length of bound RNA fragments. 

Finally, the correlation of read coverage between ABC and eCLIP across all peaks was assessed, with a mean Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.93 across all RBPs. No significant differences were observed in peak intensity, number, or distribution between singleplex and multiplexed ABC experiments, confirming that multiplexing does not compromise data quality. These results demonstrate that ABC enables the simultaneous, high-fidelity profiling of ten distinct RBPs from a single biological sample, with data quality and biological accuracy equivalent to ten individual eCLIP experiments, thereby achieving a 10-fold increase in throughput with a 95% reduction in input material and reagent usage.

### Example 4

To further validate the robustness and scalability of the antibody-barcode eCLIP (ABC) method, a series of experiments were conducted to assess the method’s performance under suboptimal conditions and to evaluate its applicability to low-input samples. First, the sensitivity of ABC was tested by performing serial dilutions of K562 cell lysates, starting from 10 million cells and reducing to 1 million, 500,000, and 100,000 cells. Each dilution was subjected to a tenplex ABC experiment using the same set of ten antibody–barcode conjugates as in Example 3. Despite the reduction in input material, the number of usable reads per RBP remained above 5 million even at the 100,000-cell input level, with library complexity declining only marginally (mean UMI count of 4.8 million at 100,000 cells vs. 17.9 million at 10 million cells). The overlap coefficient between the 10 million-cell and 100,000-cell datasets was 0.81, indicating that 81% of high-confidence binding sites were retained even at a 100-fold reduction in input. 

To evaluate reproducibility, triplicate ABC experiments were performed at the 1 million-cell input level, and inter-replicate correlations were calculated. The mean Pearson correlation coefficient between replicates was 0.96, with a coefficient of variation (CV) of 8.3% for peak counts and 7.1% for motif enrichment scores, demonstrating exceptional technical reproducibility. In contrast, conventional eCLIP performed at the same input level exhibited a CV of 15.2% for peak counts and 14.8% for motif enrichment, indicating that ABC provides greater consistency, likely due to the elimination of manual gel excision and membrane transfer steps. 

To test the method’s compatibility with clinical samples, frozen primary tumor biopsies from three patients with acute myeloid leukemia were thawed and processed using the ABC protocol. Each biopsy contained approximately 500,000 viable cells, insufficient for conventional eCLIP but sufficient for ABC. The tenplex ABC experiment was performed, and binding profiles were generated for RBFOX2, PUM2, and SF3B4, all of which have been implicated in hematopoietic malignancies. Despite the low input and heterogeneous cell composition, the method successfully recovered known binding motifs and peak distributions, with enrichment of RBFOX2 binding in intronic regions and PUM2 binding in 3′ UTRs consistent with published literature. The number of significant peaks per RBP ranged from 3,200 to 4,500, comparable to levels observed in cell line models at similar input levels. 

To assess the impact of antibody quality on performance, a subset of the ten antibody–barcode conjugates was replaced with lower-affinity or non-validated antibodies. In these cases, the number of significant peaks dropped by 60–80%, and motif enrichment was significantly reduced, confirming that the method’s fidelity is contingent upon antibody specificity. However, even with suboptimal antibodies, the complement control (CC) effectively filtered out non-specific peaks, reducing false positives by 70% compared to using RNA-seq as a background. 

Finally, the method was extended to profile 20 RBPs in a single experiment using a new set of 20 uniquely designed barcodes, each with a minimum Hamming distance of 4 to prevent misassignment. The experiment was performed in HEK293XT cells with 20 million cells as input, yielding an average of 12.5 million usable reads per RBP. Peak overlap with eCLIP data was maintained at 0.87, and motif recovery remained robust for all 20 RBPs. These results demonstrate that the ABC method is not only scalable to higher multiplexing levels but also maintains high fidelity and reproducibility even under challenging conditions, including low input, heterogeneous samples, and variable antibody quality. The method’s ability to generate high-quality RBP–RNA interaction maps from clinically relevant samples with minimal material represents a transformative advance in the field of RNA biology and precision medicine.
