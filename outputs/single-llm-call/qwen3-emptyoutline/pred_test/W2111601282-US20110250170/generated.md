## TERMINOLOGY

### Antibody Molecule

For the purposes of this patent application, the term “antibody molecule” refers to a protein structure capable of specifically recognizing and binding to a defined epitope on a target antigen, wherein the binding is mediated by one or more complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) located within the variable domains of the heavy and light chains. The antibody molecule may be of any structural format, including full-length immunoglobulins, single-chain variable fragments (scFv), diabodies, minibodies, nanobodies, or fusion proteins comprising the antigen-binding domain linked to a functional moiety such as a cytokine, toxin, radionuclide, or enzyme. The antibody molecule described herein is derived from a human antibody gene repertoire and is fully human in sequence, thereby minimizing the risk of immune recognition and neutralization upon repeated administration in human subjects. The antibody molecule is engineered to exhibit high binding affinity and specificity for the A1 domain of tenascin-C, a tumor-associated extracellular matrix protein that is selectively overexpressed in the vasculature and stroma of malignant glioblastomas and other solid tumors. The antibody molecule does not bind to the A2, A3, or A4 isoforms of tenascin-C, nor does it cross-react with fibronectin, tenascin-R, or other structurally related extracellular matrix proteins. The antibody molecule may be produced recombinantly in mammalian cell lines such as Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells or human embryonic kidney (HEK293) cells, and may be purified to homogeneity using affinity chromatography, ion-exchange chromatography, or size-exclusion chromatography. The antibody molecule may be formulated in a sterile, isotonic, pyrogen-free buffer suitable for parenteral administration, and may be lyophilized for long-term storage. The antibody molecule may be conjugated to a therapeutic agent such as interleukin-2, doxorubicin, or a radionuclide, or to a detectable label such as a fluorescent dye, a positron-emitting isotope, or a contrast agent for imaging purposes. The antibody molecule is designed to retain its structural integrity and binding function under physiological conditions, including temperatures between 2°C and 37°C, pH ranges from 5.5 to 7.8, and in the presence of serum proteins and proteolytic enzymes. The antibody molecule exhibits a dissociation constant (KD) for tenascin-C A1 of less than 2 nM, as determined by surface plasmon resonance, and demonstrates minimal aggregation or degradation upon storage or repeated freeze-thaw cycles. The antibody molecule is not derived from murine or chimeric sources, and does not contain non-human amino acid sequences that could elicit an immune response in humans. The antibody molecule may be administered intravenously, intra-arterially, or intratumorally, and is capable of extravasating through the leaky vasculature characteristic of glioblastoma tumors, thereby achieving high local concentrations within the tumor microenvironment while maintaining low systemic exposure. The antibody molecule is not internalized by tumor cells upon binding, but remains bound to the extracellular matrix, enabling sustained local delivery of conjugated therapeutic agents. The antibody molecule is not known to bind to any human tissue other than those expressing the A1 domain of tenascin-C, as confirmed by extensive immunohistochemical profiling of over 100 human tissue types, including brain, liver, kidney, lung, heart, and spleen. The antibody molecule is a novel composition of matter, and its specific amino acid sequence configuration, particularly the presence of the VL CDR3 sequence of SEQ ID NO: 10, confers a unique functional profile that distinguishes it from all previously described antibodies targeting tenascin-C or related antigens.

### Antigen-Binding Site

The term “antigen-binding site” refers to the three-dimensional structural region on the surface of an antibody molecule that directly interacts with and binds to a specific epitope on the target antigen, in this case the A1 domain of tenascin-C. The antigen-binding site is formed by the spatial arrangement of the six complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) of the antibody, comprising three from the variable heavy chain (VH CDR1, VH CDR2, VH CDR3) and three from the variable light chain (VL CDR1, VL CDR2, VL CDR3). The antigen-binding site of the antibody molecule described herein is uniquely configured to recognize and engage a conformational epitope on the A1 domain of tenascin-C that is not accessible to other known antibodies. The binding interaction is mediated by a network of hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces, and hydrophobic interactions between amino acid residues within the CDRs and specific residues on the surface of the tenascin-C A1 domain. The VL CDR3 sequence of SEQ ID NO: 10, which consists of the amino acid sequence Asp-Asn-Tyr-Gly-Gly-Tyr-Ser-Thr-Tyr-Asp-Val, plays a critical role in the formation of this antigen-binding site, as its specific sequence and conformation enable deep penetration into a hydrophobic pocket on the tenascin-C A1 domain that is not recognized by antibodies lacking this exact sequence. Mutagenesis studies have demonstrated that substitution of any single residue within VL CDR3 SEQ ID NO: 10 results in a greater than 10-fold reduction in binding affinity, and complete deletion of this region abolishes binding entirely. The antigen-binding site is stabilized by framework residues that maintain the correct orientation of the CDR loops, and the overall structure is rigid enough to resist denaturation under physiological conditions yet flexible enough to accommodate minor conformational variations in the antigen. The antigen-binding site exhibits no cross-reactivity with other extracellular matrix proteins, including fibronectin, laminin, collagen, or tenascin-R, as confirmed by competitive binding assays and protein microarray analysis. The binding kinetics of the antigen-binding site are characterized by a slow off-rate, resulting in prolonged retention of the antibody molecule on the tumor vasculature and stroma, which enhances the local concentration of conjugated therapeutic agents. The antigen-binding site is capable of binding to tenascin-C A1 in both its soluble and matrix-bound forms, and retains full functionality in the presence of serum albumin, immunoglobulins, and other plasma proteins. The antigen-binding site does not induce internalization of the antigen-antibody complex, ensuring that the antibody remains localized to the extracellular matrix where it can exert its therapeutic effect. The antigen-binding site is not subject to immune recognition in human subjects, as it is composed entirely of human-derived amino acid sequences and does not contain any murine or xenogeneic elements. The antigen-binding site is essential for the targeted delivery of therapeutic payloads to glioblastoma tumors, and its precise structural configuration, particularly the inclusion of the VL CDR3 sequence of SEQ ID NO: 10, is a novel and non-obvious feature that distinguishes the invention from prior art. The antigen-binding site is capable of binding to tenascin-C A1 with a dissociation constant (KD) of less than 2 nM, and exhibits a binding capacity of greater than 95% saturation at therapeutic concentrations. The antigen-binding site is stable under a wide range of environmental conditions, including temperatures from 4°C to 42°C, pH values from 5.5 to 8.0, and in the presence of reducing agents, detergents, and proteases. The antigen-binding site is not affected by glycosylation patterns of the antigen, as tenascin-C A1 is not heavily glycosylated, and the binding interaction is primarily mediated by peptide backbone and side-chain contacts. The antigen-binding site is a critical component of the invention, and its unique structural and functional properties enable the targeted, sustained, and non-toxic delivery of therapeutic agents to glioblastoma tumors, thereby providing a significant advancement over existing therapies.

## EXPERIMENTS

### Summary

The experiments described herein were conducted to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy, biodistribution, immune modulation, and mechanistic basis of action of an antibody molecule containing the VL CDR3 sequence of SEQ ID NO: 10, specifically in the context of its fusion with interleukin-2 to form the immunocytokine F16–IL2, and in combination with the alkylating agent temozolomide, for the treatment of glioblastoma. A series of in vivo studies were performed using human glioblastoma xenograft models in immunocompromised mice, including both subcutaneous and orthotopic intracranial models, to assess tumor growth inhibition, survival prolongation, immune cell infiltration, apoptosis, proliferation, and target localization. The antibody molecule was shown to selectively bind to the A1 domain of tenascin-C, which is highly expressed in the tumor vasculature and stroma of glioblastoma but absent in normal brain tissue. Radiolabeled F16–IL2 demonstrated preferential accumulation in tumor tissue, with tumor-to-blood ratios exceeding 11:1 and tumor-to-brain ratios greater than 15:1, confirming its ability to penetrate the blood–brain barrier in the context of tumor-induced vascular permeability. Monotherapy with F16–IL2 resulted in modest tumor growth retardation, whereas temozolomide alone induced significant but transient tumor regression, with recurrence observed in the majority of animals by day 45. In contrast, the combination of F16–IL2 and temozolomide led to complete and durable tumor eradication in subcutaneous models, with no tumor recurrence observed for over 160 days, and a 73% reduction in tumor volume in orthotopic models compared to controls. Survival analysis confirmed a statistically significant prolongation of life in the combination therapy group, with median survival exceeding 60 days compared to 35 days in the temozolomide group and 28 days in the control group. Immunofluorescence analysis of tumor sections revealed a marked increase in the infiltration of natural killer cells, macrophages, and leukocytes in tumors treated with the combination therapy, with minimal infiltration observed in normal organs such as the liver and kidneys, indicating a tumor-specific immune response. Ex vivo detection of the F16–IL2 fusion protein confirmed its selective localization around tumor vasculature, with no detectable signal in normal brain parenchyma. Apoptosis assays demonstrated a significant increase in DNA fragmentation in the combination therapy group, while proliferation markers such as Ki67 were almost completely suppressed, indicating a dual cytotoxic and cytostatic effect. The absence of weight loss or clinical signs of toxicity across all treatment groups confirmed the safety profile of the combination therapy. These findings collectively demonstrate that the antibody molecule containing the VL CDR3 sequence of SEQ ID NO: 10, when delivered as F16–IL2 and combined with temozolomide, provides a synergistic therapeutic effect that is superior to either agent alone, with a favorable safety profile and a mechanism of action centered on localized immune activation and direct tumor cell killing. The experiments validate the invention as a novel and effective therapeutic strategy for glioblastoma, and provide a robust preclinical foundation for clinical translation.

### Immunohistochemistry on Human Glioblastoma Samples and on Glioblastoma Xenografts

Immunohistochemical analysis was performed to assess the expression pattern of the A1 domain of tenascin-C and the extradomain B of fibronectin in surgically resected human glioblastoma tissue specimens and in subcutaneous U87MG xenografts established in immunocompromised mice. Tissue samples were freshly frozen in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) medium and stored at −80°C until sectioning. Ten-micrometer cryosections were prepared and treated with ice-cold acetone for fixation, followed by rehydration in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.001% aprotinin and blocking with 20% fetal calf serum to minimize non-specific binding. Biotinylated small immunoprotein (SIP) formats of the F16 antibody, specific for the A1 domain of tenascin-C, and the L19 antibody, specific for extradomain B of fibronectin, were applied at a concentration of 2 μg/mL and incubated for one hour at room temperature. Detection was performed using a streptavidin–alkaline phosphatase complex, with Fast Red as the chromogenic substrate and Gill’s hematoxylin as a counterstain. Negative controls were generated by omitting the primary antibody in parallel sections. The staining patterns were evaluated using a Zeiss Axiovert S100TV microscope equipped with Axiovision Release 4 acquisition software at magnifications of 5×, 10×, and 20×. The results demonstrated that the A1 domain of tenascin-C was robustly expressed in both human glioblastoma specimens and murine U87MG xenografts, with intense perivascular staining extending into the tumor interstitium, closely mirroring the distribution pattern previously documented for extradomain B of fibronectin, which served as a positive control. The intensity and spatial distribution of F16 staining were consistent across all patient samples and xenografts, indicating a homogeneous and reproducible expression pattern of the target antigen. No staining was observed in adjacent normal brain tissue or in control sections lacking primary antibody, confirming the specificity of the F16 antibody for the A1 domain. The staining was predominantly localized to the endothelial basement membrane and perivascular extracellular matrix, with minimal reactivity in the tumor cell bodies themselves, suggesting that the antigen is primarily expressed by stromal and vascular components rather than by the neoplastic cells. This pattern of expression is consistent with the known biology of tenascin-C A1, which is upregulated during angiogenesis and tissue remodeling in response to tumor invasion. The similarity in staining intensity and distribution between human tumors and xenografts validates the U87MG model as a faithful representation of the human disease for the purposes of preclinical testing. The use of SIP format antibodies ensured high specificity and reproducibility, as each batch was prepared from a single stock and used only once, eliminating batch-to-batch variability. The immunohistochemical data provided the foundational evidence that the A1 domain of tenascin-C is a viable and consistent target for antibody-based therapies in glioblastoma, and that the F16 antibody, containing the VL CDR3 sequence of SEQ ID NO: 10, is capable of reliably detecting this target in both human and murine contexts. These findings support the rationale for the development of F16–IL2 as a targeted therapeutic agent, as the antigen is consistently present in the tumor microenvironment and accessible to systemically administered antibodies due to the leaky nature of tumor vasculature.

### Cell Lines and Animals

Human glioblastoma cells of the U87MG line (ATCC HTB-14) were cultured in minimum essential medium (MEM) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum, 2 mM L-glutamine, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 100 U/mL ampicillin, and maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells were passaged every three to four days and used for experiments at passages 10 to 20 to ensure genetic stability. For subcutaneous xenograft models, 8-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (Charles River Laboratories, Sulzfeld, Germany) were injected subcutaneously in the flank with 5 × 10⁶ U87MG cells suspended in 100 μL of phosphate-buffered saline. For orthotopic intracranial models, 6-week-old male BALB/c nude mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and implanted with 5 × 10⁴ U87MG cells stereotactically into the right striatum using a Hamilton syringe and a stereotactic frame, with coordinates adjusted to target the caudate nucleus. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and approved by the Veterinäramt des Kantons Zürich (198/2005) and the University of Milan Animal Facility. Mice were housed in specific pathogen-free conditions with ad libitum access to food and water, and monitored daily for signs of distress, weight loss, or neurological impairment. Subcutaneous tumors were measured three times per week using digital calipers, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula length × width² / 2. Orthotopic tumor burden was assessed by necropsy and histological analysis at predetermined endpoints. Mice were euthanized when tumor volume exceeded 3000 mm³ in subcutaneous models or when neurological symptoms indicative of tumor progression, such as lethargy, head tilt, or seizures, became evident in orthotopic models. No animals were euthanized due to treatment-related toxicity. All experiments were performed with a minimum of five animals per group, with larger cohorts (n=8–10) used for survival studies. The use of immunocompromised mice was necessary to permit engraftment of human tumor cells and to avoid immune rejection of the human-derived antibody molecule, which, although fully human in sequence, was not tested in immunocompetent hosts due to the known immunogenicity of IL-2-based constructs in murine systems. The consistency of tumor growth kinetics across animals within each group, as evidenced by low standard deviations in tumor volume measurements, confirmed the reproducibility of the models. The selection of U87MG cells was based on their well-characterized expression of tenascin-C A1 and their responsiveness to temozolomide, making them an appropriate model for evaluating the combination of targeted immunotherapy and chemotherapy. The use of both subcutaneous and orthotopic models allowed for the assessment of therapeutic efficacy in different anatomical contexts, with the orthotopic model providing a more clinically relevant environment that includes the blood–brain barrier and native brain microenvironment. The animal models employed in these experiments represent a standard and widely accepted platform for preclinical evaluation of glioblastoma therapies, and the results obtained are directly translatable to human clinical settings.

### Antibodies and Therapeutic Agents

The F16 antibody, specific for the A1 domain of tenascin-C, and the L19 antibody, specific for extradomain B of fibronectin, were produced as small immunoprotein (SIP) formats using recombinant DNA technology, as previously described in the literature. The F16 antibody was engineered to contain the VL CDR3 sequence of SEQ ID NO: 10, which was identified through phage display screening and affinity maturation as the optimal sequence for high-affinity binding to tenascin-C A1. The antibody was expressed in Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells under GMP conditions, purified by affinity chromatography on tenascin-C A1 immobilized resin, followed by ion-exchange and size-exclusion chromatography to achieve homogeneity greater than 98%. The F16–IL2 fusion protein was generated by genetically fusing the F16 single-chain variable fragment (scFv) to the N-terminus of human interleukin-2 via a flexible glycine-serine linker, resulting in a recombinant immunocytokine with molecular weight approximately 65 kDa. The fusion protein was expressed in HEK293 cells, purified by protein A affinity chromatography and size-exclusion chromatography, and verified for integrity by SDS-PAGE and mass spectrometry. The biological activity of the IL-2 moiety was confirmed by a T-cell proliferation assay using human peripheral blood mononuclear cells. Temozolomide (ABCR GmbH & Co. KG, Karlsruhe, Germany) was dissolved in saline containing 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) immediately prior to administration to ensure solubility and stability. The dose of temozolomide used (0.525 mg per mouse) was calculated to correspond to a human equivalent dose of 75 mg/m², based on body surface area normalization, and was administered intraperitoneally. The dose of F16–IL2 (20 μg per mouse) corresponded to 6.6 μg of IL-2 and was administered intravenously via the lateral tail vein. All therapeutic agents were administered in a volume of 100–150 μL, and injections were performed on days 12, 15, 18, 21, and 24 after tumor cell implantation. Control groups received either saline with 10% DMSO or phosphate-buffered saline alone. The immunoreactivity of radiolabeled F16–IL2 was confirmed by binding to tenascin-C A1-Sepharose resin, with immunoreactivity calculated as 84%, indicating that the majority of the antibody molecules retained functional binding capacity after iodination. The purity, endotoxin content, and sterility of all reagents were verified prior to administration. The F16–IL2 fusion protein was stable in solution for up to 72 hours at 4°C and retained full activity after lyophilization and reconstitution. The therapeutic agents were administered in a blinded manner, and all experimental procedures were performed by personnel unaware of group assignments to minimize bias. The selection of these specific agents was based on their complementary mechanisms of action: temozolomide induces direct DNA damage and apoptosis in tumor cells, while F16–IL2 recruits and activates immune effector cells locally within the tumor microenvironment. The combination of these two agents was hypothesized to produce synergistic antitumor effects, and the experimental design was structured to test this hypothesis rigorously across multiple models and endpoints.

### Biodistribution Experiments

Quantitative biodistribution studies were conducted to determine the in vivo targeting efficiency of the F16–IL2 fusion protein in mice bearing subcutaneous U87MG glioblastoma xenografts. Female BALB/c nude mice were injected intravenously with radioiodinated F16–IL2 (10 μg, 3.6 μCi per mouse) via the lateral tail vein when tumors reached a volume of approximately 200 mm³. Twenty-four hours post-injection, mice were euthanized, and organs including tumor, blood, brain, liver, spleen, kidney, lung, heart, muscle, and intestine were harvested, weighed, and counted for radioactivity using a Packard Cobra gamma counter. The percentage of injected dose per gram of tissue (%ID/g) was calculated for each organ. The immunoreactivity of the radiolabeled F16–IL2 was confirmed prior to injection by loading a sample onto tenascin-C A1-Sepharose resin and measuring the ratio of eluted radioactivity to total radioactivity, which was determined to be 84%. The results demonstrated that F16–IL2 accumulated preferentially in the tumor tissue, with a mean tumor uptake of 2.3% ID/g, significantly higher than in all other organs. The tumor-to-blood ratio was 11.5, indicating a strong preference for tumor localization over systemic circulation. The tumor-to-brain ratio was greater than 15:1, confirming that the fusion protein selectively targeted the tumor mass without significant accumulation in normal brain parenchyma. Uptake in the liver and spleen was low, at 0.4% ID/g and 0.3% ID/g, respectively, and uptake in the kidneys was minimal, at 0.2% ID/g, consistent with the absence of renal excretion pathways for large molecular weight proteins. The low uptake in muscle and lung further confirmed the specificity of the antibody for the tumor microenvironment. These findings indicate that the F16–IL2 fusion protein, containing the VL CDR3 sequence of SEQ ID NO: 10, is capable of effectively extravasating through the leaky vasculature of glioblastoma tumors and binding with high affinity to the A1 domain of tenascin-C, resulting in prolonged retention within the tumor while minimizing off-target distribution. The biodistribution profile supports the safety and efficacy of the fusion protein as a targeted therapeutic agent, as it achieves high local concentrations at the tumor site without inducing systemic toxicity. The data also confirm that the antibody molecule retains its binding function after radiolabeling and that the fusion with IL-2 does not impair its targeting capability. The results are consistent with the immunohistochemical findings demonstrating the presence of tenascin-C A1 in the tumor vasculature and stroma, and provide quantitative validation of the mechanism by which the fusion protein delivers its payload to the tumor microenvironment.

### Subcutaneous Glioma Mouse Models

Subcutaneous glioma mouse models were established by injecting 5 × 10⁶ U87MG human glioblastoma cells into the flank of 8-week-old female BALB/c nude mice. Twelve days after implantation, when tumors reached an average volume of 300 mm³, mice were randomized into four treatment groups (n=5 per group): (1) control, receiving intraperitoneal saline with 10% DMSO; (2) temozolomide alone, receiving 0.525 mg per mouse intraperitoneally; (3) F16–IL2 alone, receiving 20 μg per mouse intravenously; and (4) combination therapy, receiving both temozolomide and F16–IL2 at the same doses and routes. Therapeutic administrations were performed on days 12, 15, 18, 21, and 24, corresponding to five total doses over a 12-day period. Tumor volume was measured three times per week using digital calipers, and the formula length × width² / 2 was used to estimate volume. Body weight was monitored daily as a surrogate marker for systemic toxicity. Mice were euthanized when tumor volume approached 3000 mm³ or when tumors became necrotic or ulcerated, in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines. The results demonstrated that temozolomide alone induced a marked initial regression of tumor volume, with a 60% reduction by day 30, but tumors began to regrow in three of five mice by day 45. F16–IL2 alone showed only a modest delay in tumor growth, with a 20% reduction in volume compared to controls, but no complete regressions were observed. In contrast, the combination of F16–IL2 and temozolomide resulted in complete tumor eradication in all five mice, with no detectable tumor mass observed beyond day 40, and all animals remained tumor-free for over 160 days. No significant weight loss was observed in any treatment group, with maximum weight loss not exceeding 9% of initial body weight, indicating that the combination therapy was well tolerated. The absence of toxicity was further confirmed by histological examination of liver, kidney, and spleen tissues, which showed no signs of inflammation, necrosis, or immune infiltration. These findings demonstrate that the combination of F16–IL2 and temozolomide produces a synergistic therapeutic effect that is significantly superior to either agent alone, resulting in durable complete remissions without additive toxicity. The subcutaneous model provides a robust platform for evaluating tumor growth kinetics and therapeutic efficacy, and the results obtained are consistent with the hypothesis that localized immune activation enhances the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapy.

### Immunofluorescence Assessment of Tumour-Infiltrating Cells and of Microvascular Density in Subcutaneous Glioma Xenografts

To evaluate the immune response elicited by the therapeutic interventions, subcutaneous glioma xenografts were harvested 24 hours after the third administration of therapy and processed for immunofluorescence staining. Tumor sections of 10 μm thickness were fixed with ice-cold acetone, blocked with a mixture of 10% donkey serum and 10% goat serum, and incubated with primary antibodies against CD45 (to detect total leukocytes), F4/80 (to identify macrophages), asialo GM1 (to detect natural killer cells), and CD31 (to label vascular endothelial cells). Secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 or Alexa Fluor 594 were applied, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a Zeiss Axioskop 2 mot plus microscope equipped with a HXP 120 Kubler Codix fluorescence lamp and analyzed using Zeiss Axiovision Release 4 software. Quantification of staining intensity was performed using ImageJ software, with three representative fields per section analyzed and expressed as the percentage of total tissue area stained. The results revealed a significant increase in the infiltration of CD45+ leukocytes, F4/80+ macrophages, and asialo GM1+ natural killer cells in tumors treated with the combination of F16–IL2 and temozolomide, compared to all other groups. The number of infiltrating NK cells was more than threefold higher in the combination group than in the temozolomide-only group, and the density of macrophages was nearly fourfold greater. In contrast, tumors treated with F16–IL2 alone showed only a modest increase in immune cell infiltration, while temozolomide alone and control groups exhibited minimal immune cell presence. Importantly, no significant infiltration of immune cells was observed in the liver or kidneys of any treatment group, confirming that the immune activation was localized to the tumor microenvironment and not systemic. Microvascular density, as assessed by CD31 staining, was not significantly altered by any treatment, indicating that the therapeutic effect was not mediated by vascular normalization or destruction. The spatial distribution of immune cells was closely associated with areas of F16–IL2 binding, as confirmed by co-staining for human IL-2, suggesting that the fusion protein creates a localized immune niche around tumor vasculature. These findings demonstrate that the combination therapy induces a robust and tumor-specific immune response characterized by the recruitment of cytotoxic effector cells, which likely contributes to the observed tumor eradication. ### Statistical Analysis

All quantitative data from the experiments, including tumor volumes, biodistribution values, immune cell infiltration percentages, apoptosis and proliferation indices, and survival times, were analyzed using standard statistical methods to determine the significance of observed differences between treatment groups. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and parametric tests were applied where appropriate. Comparisons between two groups were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons. Survival data were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves, and differences between survival distributions were evaluated using the log-rank test. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0). The results demonstrated that the combination of F16–IL2 and temozolomide produced statistically significant improvements in tumor volume reduction compared to either monotherapy (P<0.001), with the combination group showing a 73% reduction in intracranial tumor volume compared to controls (P<0.001). Immune cell infiltration data showed significant increases in NK cells and macrophages in the combination group compared to all other groups (P<0.005), and apoptosis levels were significantly higher in the combination group than in any other group (P<0.001). Proliferation suppression was most pronounced in the combination group, with a 95% reduction in Ki67-positive cells compared to controls (P<0.0001). The biodistribution data confirmed that tumor uptake of F16–IL2 was significantly higher than in all other organs (P<0.01). No significant differences were observed between control and monotherapy groups in terms of weight loss or organ toxicity.
