# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of superconducting quantum computing has experienced rapid advancement over the past two decades, driven by continuous improvements in coherence times, gate fidelities, and the scalability of qubit architectures. Among the various circuit designs developed for this purpose, the transmon qubit has emerged as the dominant platform in nearly all modern quantum processors due to its robustness against charge noise and relatively straightforward fabrication and control protocols. However, despite its widespread adoption, the transmon’s inherent limitations—particularly its weak anharmonicity and consequent susceptibility to leakage errors during gate operations—have imposed fundamental constraints on the speed and fidelity of single-qubit operations. These constraints arise because the anharmonicity of the transmon is typically only a few percent of its qubit frequency, forcing gate pulses to be shaped over many Larmor periods to avoid population transfer to non-computational states. As a result, even state-of-the-art transmon systems operate with gate infidelities approaching 10⁻⁴, a value that is fundamentally tied to the ratio of qubit frequency to quality factor and anharmonicity. While material improvements and error mitigation techniques have pushed transmon performance closer to theoretical limits, further gains are increasingly difficult to achieve without a paradigm shift in qubit design.

In parallel, alternative qubit architectures such as the flux qubit and its more advanced variant, the fluxonium, have long been recognized for their superior anharmonicity, which theoretically permits faster and more robust gate operations. The flux qubit, composed of a superconducting loop interrupted by Josephson junctions, exhibits a double-well potential structure that supports two low-energy states separated by a frequency on the order of gigahertz, with energy splittings to higher levels that are orders of magnitude larger. This large anharmonicity makes it inherently less prone to leakage during driving, and its flux-tunable nature allows for precise control via magnetic fields. However, the flux qubit’s extreme sensitivity to magnetic flux noise has historically rendered it impractical for scalable quantum computing, as even minute fluctuations in the ambient magnetic environment induce rapid dephasing. To mitigate this, the fluxonium was developed by shunting the Josephson junction with a large inductance, effectively suppressing the sensitivity to flux noise while preserving the desired level structure. Subsequent refinements, including the introduction of a large shunting capacitor, led to the heavy-fluxonium architecture, which further reduces coupling to charge noise and enhances energy relaxation times. Recent experiments have demonstrated that heavy-fluxonium qubits in three-dimensional packaging can achieve coherence times exceeding those of transmons, suggesting a promising path forward.

Yet, despite these advances in coherence, the heavy-fluxonium has remained largely uncompetitive in terms of gate speed. The primary obstacle lies in the suppression of charge matrix elements due to the large shunting capacitor, which renders conventional microwave-driven control schemes—reliant on dipole transitions between computational states—inefficient and slow. Attempts to circumvent this limitation through Raman transitions, which utilize higher-lying excited states as intermediate levels, have been hindered by the need for high drive powers and the associated risk of increased decoherence from the very states used for manipulation. Moreover, the low qubit frequency of the heavy-fluxonium, typically below 20 MHz, places it in a regime where thermal populations are nearly equal between ground and excited states, making initialization and readout challenging. Conventional dispersive readout methods, which depend on a large frequency detuning between the qubit and the readout resonator to minimize heating, become ineffective when the qubit frequency is so low that the dispersive shift is negligible. Consequently, while the heavy-fluxonium possesses the ideal structural properties for high-fidelity, high-speed quantum operations, the absence of a viable control mechanism has prevented it from becoming a practical alternative to the transmon.

This invention addresses these longstanding challenges by introducing a novel operational framework for the heavy-fluxonium qubit that simultaneously enables ultrafast single-qubit gates, high-fidelity initialization, and efficient readout—all within a single, coherent architecture. The core innovation lies in the realization that the low qubit frequency, previously considered a disadvantage, can be leveraged as an asset. By operating the qubit at approximately 14 MHz, well below the ambient thermal energy scale, decoherence processes are inherently slowed, resulting in unprecedented coherence times exceeding 300 microseconds. This extended lifetime, combined with the large energy gap to higher excited states (several gigahertz), creates a unique regime in which the qubit can be manipulated using strong, nonadiabatic flux drives that act within a single Larmor period. Unlike conventional approaches that rely on weak, resonant microwave pulses, this method exploits the full anharmonicity of the system by applying flux pulses with amplitudes far exceeding the qubit frequency, thereby inducing rapid Landau-Zener transitions between the computational states without populating lossy intermediate levels. Furthermore, the large frequency separation between the computational subspace and the higher plasmon states enables a novel reset protocol that utilizes the readout resonator to rapidly cool the qubit to an effective temperature of 190 microkelvin, achieving 97% initialization fidelity. Readout is accomplished through a plasmon-assisted scheme that transfers population from the computational states to higher-energy levels with larger dispersive shifts, thereby amplifying the measurement signal without requiring direct coupling between the qubit and the resonator. Together, these innovations transform the heavy-fluxonium from a theoretically promising but practically constrained architecture into a fully functional, high-performance quantum processor element that outperforms the transmon in both coherence and gate speed, while remaining compatible with existing fabrication and control infrastructure.

## SUMMARY

This invention discloses a superconducting quantum circuit architecture and associated control protocols that enable ultrafast, high-fidelity quantum operations in a heavy-fluxonium qubit operating at a qubit frequency below the ambient thermal energy scale. The qubit is realized through a circuit configuration comprising a small-area Josephson junction shunted by a large superinductor and a large capacitor, resulting in a double-well potential with a low-energy splitting of approximately 14 megahertz and a large energy gap to higher excited states exceeding several gigahertz. This design achieves coherence times on the order of hundreds of microseconds, surpassing those of state-of-the-art transmon qubits, by suppressing both charge and flux noise through the combined effects of the large shunting elements and operation in a low-frequency regime where decoherence rates are inherently reduced. A novel initialization protocol is implemented using the readout resonator and higher circuit levels to rapidly transfer entropy from the qubit, achieving a state preparation fidelity of 97% by driving transitions between the ground state and a higher plasmon state, followed by a photon decay mechanism that preferentially populates the computational ground state. Readout is performed through a plasmon-assisted dispersive measurement scheme, wherein a π pulse is applied to transfer population from the excited computational state to a higher-energy plasmon state that exhibits a significantly larger dispersive shift with the readout resonator, thereby amplifying the measurement contrast and enabling single-shot readout fidelity of 50% without requiring direct coupling between the computational states and the resonator. The most significant innovation lies in the implementation of single-qubit gates via nonadiabatic flux pulses that induce Landau-Zener transitions within a single Larmor period, circumventing the limitations imposed by suppressed charge matrix elements. These gates are generated by applying triangular flux pulses with amplitudes exceeding 300 megahertz, far above the qubit frequency, which drive the system through avoided crossings in the energy landscape, resulting in coherent rotations about the x and z axes of the Bloch sphere. The pulse shapes are designed to be antisymmetric, ensuring zero net flux and thereby minimizing the effects of pulse distortions and low-frequency noise, while the total gate duration for all computational operations remains under 70 nanoseconds, matching the Larmor period of the qubit and rendering them ten to thirty times faster than conventional microwave-driven gates. The fidelity of these gates is characterized through randomized benchmarking, yielding average single-qubit gate fidelities exceeding 99.8%, with individual gate infidelities as low as 1×10⁻⁴, limited primarily by calibration errors rather than decoherence. The entire system is fabricated using conventional superconducting circuit techniques on a sapphire substrate with niobium layers and Dolan-bridge Josephson junctions, ensuring compatibility with existing quantum processor fabrication pipelines. The invention enables the first practical realization of a heavy-fluxonium qubit that combines the long coherence times of protected qubits with the gate speeds of high-frequency architectures, establishing a new paradigm for scalable quantum computing that operates efficiently at temperatures significantly higher than those required by conventional systems. The control scheme is broadly applicable to any low-frequency superconducting qubit with a large anharmonicity and can be extended to multi-qubit systems with inductive coupling, enabling two-qubit gates that are as fast as the single-qubit gates demonstrated herein, without requiring participation of lossy excited states.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention pertains to a superconducting quantum circuit architecture and a suite of control protocols that enable high-fidelity, ultrafast quantum operations in a heavy-fluxonium qubit operating in a previously unexplored regime of low qubit frequency. The qubit is constructed from a superconducting loop interrupted by a single small-area Josephson junction, which is shunted by a large inductance and a large capacitance, forming a circuit whose Hamiltonian supports a double-well potential with two low-energy states separated by a transition frequency of approximately 14 megahertz. The large inductance is realized through an array of three hundred Josephson junctions, each designed to operate in the limit of high Josephson energy relative to charging energy, ensuring that the array behaves as a linear inductor with negligible nonlinearities. The large shunting capacitor, fabricated from a low-loss dielectric material, suppresses the coupling to charge noise and reduces the dipole matrix elements between the computational states, which in turn suppresses relaxation via dielectric loss. This design results in an energy landscape where the computational subspace is separated from higher excited states by gaps exceeding three gigahertz, a feature that is critical for both the stability of the qubit and the implementation of novel control protocols. The qubit is operated near the flux-frustration point, where the external magnetic flux threading the loop is tuned to half a flux quantum, causing the wave functions of the two lowest states to become symmetric and antisymmetric combinations across the two potential wells. At this point, the qubit frequency is minimized, and the system becomes first-order insensitive to low-frequency flux noise, enabling the longest coherence times. The qubit is capacitively coupled to a readout resonator with a resonance frequency of approximately 5.7 gigahertz, which is chosen to be far detuned from the qubit transition to minimize heating during measurement, while still allowing for strong coupling to higher plasmon states.

The qubit’s low frequency, while initially a challenge for initialization and readout, is exploited as a key advantage in this invention. At ambient temperatures of approximately 15 millikelvin, the thermal energy scale is on the order of one gigahertz, meaning that the qubit’s 14-megahertz transition frequency is significantly below the thermal energy, resulting in a nearly equal population distribution between the ground and excited states. To overcome this, a reset protocol is implemented that utilizes the readout resonator and the higher-energy plasmon states of the circuit to rapidly cool the qubit to an effective temperature of 190 microkelvin. This is achieved by simultaneously driving two transitions: the transition from the ground state to a higher plasmon state, and the transition from that plasmon state to the excited computational state. The high frequency of the readout resonator ensures that any photon emitted during the decay from the excited plasmon state is rapidly lost to the environment, effectively removing entropy from the system. The large matrix element between the intermediate plasmon state and the excited computational state ensures a rapid and efficient transfer of population, resulting in 97% of the qubit population being prepared in the excited state within 15 microseconds. A subsequent π pulse on the computational transition then transfers the population to the ground state, completing the initialization. This protocol is fundamentally distinct from conventional reset methods, which rely on parametric amplification or active cooling of the resonator, and instead leverages the intrinsic level structure of the heavy-fluxonium to achieve rapid, high-fidelity initialization without requiring additional hardware or cryogenic infrastructure.

Readout of the qubit state is performed using a plasmon-assisted dispersive measurement scheme. Because the qubit frequency is so low, the dispersive shift induced by the computational states on the readout resonator is negligible, on the order of 60 kilohertz, making direct dispersive readout impractical. To circumvent this limitation, the invention employs a two-step protocol: first, a π pulse is applied to transfer population from the excited computational state to a higher plasmon state whose transition frequency is closer to that of the readout resonator. This plasmon state exhibits a dispersive shift that is five times larger than that of the computational states, thereby amplifying the measurement signal. The population transfer is performed in approximately 80 nanoseconds, a duration that is short compared to the qubit’s coherence time, ensuring minimal decoherence during the readout process. Following this transfer, a standard dispersive measurement is performed on the resonator, and the resulting signal is proportional to the difference in dispersive shifts between the plasmon state and the ground state. This method effectively decouples the readout fidelity from the small dispersive shift of the computational states and instead relies on the larger shifts of the higher-energy levels, which are more readily accessible due to the rich spectrum of the heavy-fluxonium. The resulting single-shot readout fidelity is approximately 50%, a value that is limited primarily by the current design of the resonator and the quality factor of the readout line, but which can be significantly improved through optimization of the circuit parameters, such as increasing the coupling strength between the plasmon states and the resonator or employing a parametric amplifier to enhance signal-to-noise ratio.

The most significant innovation of this invention lies in the implementation of single-qubit gates using nonadiabatic flux pulses that induce Landau-Zener transitions within a single Larmor period. Conventional microwave-driven gates are rendered ineffective in the heavy-fluxonium due to the suppression of charge matrix elements, which drastically reduces the Rabi frequency of transitions between the computational states. Instead, this invention employs direct modulation of the external magnetic flux to drive the system through avoided crossings in the energy landscape. The Hamiltonian governing the qubit near the flux-frustration point can be approximated as a two-level system with a Hamiltonian proportional to σₓ and σ_z, where the σₓ term is proportional to the deviation of the external flux from the frustration point and the σ_z term corresponds to the qubit frequency. By applying a triangular flux pulse with a peak amplitude of approximately 300 megahertz, the system is rapidly driven away from the flux-frustration point, where the σₓ term dominates, and then returned, resulting in a coherent rotation about the x-axis. The duration of the pulse is kept short—approximately 4.76 nanoseconds—so that the system does not have time to adiabatically follow the energy levels, ensuring that the transition occurs in a nonadiabatic manner. The total rotation angle is determined by the area under the flux pulse, which is proportional to the integral of the flux offset over time. A subsequent idle period at the flux-frustration point allows for a rotation about the z-axis, governed by the qubit frequency. A second, antisymmetric flux pulse is then applied to complete the gate sequence, ensuring that the net flux change over the entire pulse is zero. This antisymmetric design is critical, as it cancels out low-frequency noise and minimizes the impact of pulse distortions that are common in flux-bias lines. The entire sequence for a Y/2 gate, for instance, takes approximately 20 nanoseconds, while the X/2 gate, which requires two such pulses and a z-rotation, takes approximately 60 nanoseconds. All computational gates are completed within one Larmor period of 70 nanoseconds, making them ten to thirty times faster than conventional microwave-driven gates, which typically require durations exceeding 700 nanoseconds. The fidelity of these gates is characterized through randomized benchmarking, yielding an average single-qubit gate fidelity of 99.8%, with individual gate infidelities as low as 1×10⁻⁴. The dominant source of error is not decoherence, which is negligible over these short timescales, but rather residual miscalibration of the pulse parameters, indicating that further improvements are possible through refined pulse shaping and feedback control.

The coherence properties of the qubit are characterized through measurements of energy relaxation time T₁ and dephasing time T₂e. At the flux-frustration point, T₁ is measured to be 315 microseconds, while T₂e reaches 300 microseconds, both exceeding the performance of state-of-the-art transmon qubits. The relaxation time increases to over four milliseconds as the flux is tuned away from the frustration point, consistent with the suppression of tunneling and the reduction of dielectric loss. The dominant loss mechanism near the frustration point is identified as dielectric loss in the shunting capacitor, which scales inversely with the qubit frequency and is consistent with a loss tangent of approximately 4×10⁻⁶, a value that is within a factor of two of the best reported in similar devices. The dephasing is dominated by low-frequency flux noise, as evidenced by the spin-echo decay envelope, which follows a Gaussian profile consistent with 1/f noise. The amplitude of this noise is determined to be 5.21 microflux quanta, a value that is comparable to the best flux qubits and significantly lower than in conventional transmons. The T₂e decreases rapidly as the flux is tuned away from the frustration point, consistent with the increasing flux sensitivity of the qubit frequency, and is projected to remain above 10 microseconds even far from the optimal point. The combination of long coherence times and ultrafast gates ensures that the number of operations that can be performed within the coherence window is among the highest reported for any superconducting qubit.

The fabrication of the device is carried out using standard superconducting circuit techniques on a 430-micrometer-thick C-plane sapphire substrate. The base layer of the circuit is formed from a 150-nanometer-thick film of niobium, deposited via electron-beam evaporation and patterned using optical lithography and reactive ion etching. The large inductance array is fabricated using the same process, with each junction in the array designed to have a Josephson energy much larger than its charging energy to ensure linear behavior. The small-area Josephson junction is fabricated using electron-beam lithography and the Dolan bridge technique, with double-angle evaporation to ensure uniform and reproducible junction characteristics. The entire device is mounted on a printed circuit board and wire-bonded for electrical access. The system is housed in a dilution refrigerator operating at a base temperature of 15 millikelvin, with extensive filtering and shielding to minimize electromagnetic interference. The flux and charge lines are attenuated at multiple stages, and the qubit is surrounded by a multi-layer magnetic shield composed of mu-metal and copper to suppress external magnetic noise. The readout resonator is coupled to a low-noise amplifier, and all control signals are generated using high-speed digital-to-analog converters capable of producing the required flux pulses with nanosecond precision.

The control electronics are designed to generate the triangular flux pulses with arbitrary amplitude and duration, and the system is calibrated using Rabi oscillations and Ramsey interferometry to determine the precise pulse parameters required for each gate. The antisymmetric nature of the pulses ensures that the system is robust against low-frequency drift and distortion in the flux lines, and the total gate duration is independent of the exact shape of the pulse, depending only on the integrated area. This property makes the control scheme highly resilient to imperfections in the pulse generation hardware, a critical advantage for scalability. The entire system is compatible with existing quantum processor architectures and can be integrated into multi-qubit arrays using capacitive or inductive coupling schemes. The invention thus provides a complete, self-contained quantum computing platform that combines the long coherence times of protected qubits with the gate speeds of high-frequency architectures, enabling a new class of superconducting quantum processors that operate efficiently at temperatures significantly higher than those required by conventional systems.

### Experimental Demonstration

The experimental realization of the heavy-fluxonium qubit and its associated control protocols was carried out using a fully integrated superconducting circuit fabricated on a sapphire substrate and operated in a dilution refrigerator at a base temperature of 15 millikelvin. The device was characterized using a suite of microwave and flux control lines, each equipped with multiple stages of attenuation and filtering to ensure minimal thermal and electromagnetic noise coupling to the qubit. The flux bias line was modified to combine both dc and rf components using a custom bias tee with a shorted capacitor, allowing for the application of fast flux pulses without introducing additional parasitic elements. The charge drive line, used for initialization and readout, was terminated with a low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 8 gigahertz to suppress high-frequency noise, and additional infrared filters were placed along the signal path to reduce thermal photon loading. The qubit was coupled to a readout resonator with a quality factor of 600 and a resonance frequency of 5.7 gigahertz, chosen to be far detuned from the qubit transition to minimize heating while still allowing for strong coupling to higher plasmon states. The resonator output was amplified using a high-electron-mobility transistor amplifier and digitized using a high-speed data acquisition system, enabling single-shot readout with sufficient signal-to-noise ratio to distinguish between the ground and excited states.

The initialization protocol was first demonstrated by applying simultaneous microwave pulses to drive the jg0i → jh0i and jh0i → je1i transitions, with pulse durations of 15 microseconds and power levels calibrated to achieve the maximum transition rates. The population dynamics were monitored by measuring the excited state population after the reset sequence using a Rabi oscillation between the jei and jfi levels. The contrast of the Rabi oscillation doubled following the reset, confirming that approximately 50% of the population had been in the excited state prior to initialization, consistent with thermal equilibrium at the ambient temperature. After the reset, the population in the excited state was reduced to 3%, corresponding to a state preparation fidelity of 97%. The effective temperature of the qubit following reset was inferred from the steady-state population distribution and found to be 190 microkelvin, a reduction of more than two orders of magnitude compared to the ambient temperature. This result was further corroborated by measuring the thermal population of the jfi state, which, due to its higher frequency, exhibited a thermal population consistent with the ambient temperature, confirming that the reset protocol selectively cooled only the computational subspace.

Readout was performed by first applying a π pulse to transfer population from jei to jfi, followed by a dispersive measurement of the resonator frequency shift. The resulting histogram of measurement outcomes for the four states—jgi, jei, jfi, and jhi—showed partial separation, with the jgi and jei states overlapping due to the small dispersive shift of the computational states. However, the difference between the jfi and jgi states was clearly resolvable, with a separation of approximately 1.5 standard deviations, yielding a single-shot readout fidelity of 50%. This fidelity is limited by the current design of the resonator and the coupling strength, but the protocol itself is fundamentally scalable. By engineering the circuit parameters to bring other plasmon states closer to the resonator frequency, or by employing a parametric amplifier to enhance the signal, the readout fidelity can be improved to exceed 90%, as demonstrated in other architectures. The key advantage of this approach is that it does not require direct coupling between the computational states and the resonator, thereby decoupling readout fidelity from the intrinsic limitations of the qubit’s dispersive shift.

The performance of the flux-driven gates was characterized using Rabi oscillations and Ramsey interferometry. By sweeping the amplitude and duration of the triangular flux pulses, the system was driven through a range of rotation angles, and the resulting population in the computational states was measured. The data revealed clear oscillations in both the x and z projections of the Bloch sphere, with a Larmor frequency of 14 megahertz, confirming that the qubit was being coherently manipulated. The gate durations for the Y/2 and Z/2 operations were measured to be 20 and 14 nanoseconds, respectively, while the X/2 gate, which required two flux pulses and a z-rotation, took 60 nanoseconds. All gates were completed within one Larmor period of 70 nanoseconds, demonstrating that the system operates in the ultrafast regime. The fidelity of these gates was quantified using randomized benchmarking, in which sequences of random Clifford gates were applied to the qubit, followed by a recovery gate to return the state to the excited state. The decay of the excited state population as a function of sequence length was fitted to an exponential, yielding an average gate fidelity of 99.8%. Interleaved randomized benchmarking was then performed by inserting individual computational gates—Z/2, Y/2, and X/2—into the random sequences, allowing for the isolation of their individual fidelities. The results showed infidelities of 1×10⁻⁴, 8×10⁻⁴, and 24×10⁻⁴ for the Z/2, Y/2, and X/2 gates, respectively. The higher infidelity of the X/2 gate was attributed to the cumulative effect of two flux pulses and a z-rotation, each contributing small calibration errors, rather than any fundamental limitation of the protocol. The decoherence-limited error for the Y/2 gate was calculated to be 6.67×10⁻⁵, indicating that the majority of the observed infidelity arises from imperfections in pulse calibration, not from intrinsic decoherence. This conclusion was further supported by the fact that the gate fidelity remained constant even when the pulse duration was varied, confirming that the system is robust against timing jitter and pulse distortion.

The coherence times of the qubit were measured using standard T₁ and T₂e protocols. The T₁ time was found to be 315 microseconds at the flux-frustration point, with a maximum value of 4.3 milliseconds achieved at a flux offset of 0.4 flux quanta. The increase in T₁ away from the frustration point was attributed to the suppression of tunneling, which reduces the rate of relaxation via dielectric loss. The T₂e time was measured to be 300 microseconds at the flux-frustration point, consistent with the T₁ time, indicating that the qubit is limited by depolarization rather than pure dephasing. Away from the frustration point, the T₂e time decreased rapidly, following the predicted dependence on the flux slope, and was projected to remain above 10 microseconds even at large flux offsets. The dephasing was found to be dominated by 1/f flux noise, with an amplitude of 5.21 microflux quanta, a value that is comparable to the best flux qubits and significantly lower than in transmons. The combination of long coherence times and ultrafast gates results in a gate coherence product of over 10⁷, which is among the highest reported for any superconducting qubit.

The entire system was fabricated using standard superconducting circuit techniques, with niobium layers deposited via electron-beam evaporation and patterned using optical lithography and reactive ion etching. The Josephson junctions were fabricated using electron-beam lithography and the Dolan bridge technique, ensuring high reproducibility and uniformity. The device was mounted on a printed circuit board and wire-bonded for electrical access. The entire setup was shielded with multiple layers of mu-metal and copper to suppress external magnetic and electromagnetic noise. The control electronics were based on high-speed digital-to-analog converters capable of generating flux pulses with nanosecond precision, and all signals were filtered to remove high-frequency noise. The experimental results demonstrate that the heavy-fluxonium qubit, when operated with the protocols described herein, achieves performance metrics that surpass those of state-of-the-art transmon qubits in both coherence and gate speed, establishing it as a viable and superior alternative for scalable quantum computing.

## CONCLUSION

This invention represents a fundamental advancement in superconducting quantum computing by demonstrating a fully functional heavy-fluxonium qubit architecture that simultaneously achieves record-long coherence times, ultrafast single-qubit gate operations, and high-fidelity initialization and readout—all within a single, integrated system. The core innovation lies in the deliberate operation of the qubit in a low-frequency regime, where decoherence processes are inherently suppressed, and the large anharmonicity of the circuit is leveraged to enable nonadiabatic flux-driven gates that occur within a single Larmor period. This approach overcomes the longstanding limitations of both the transmon and conventional fluxonium architectures, which are constrained by weak anharmonicity and suppressed charge matrix elements, respectively. By replacing microwave-driven control with direct flux modulation, the invention eliminates the need for high-power, resonant drives and avoids the decoherence associated with populating lossy intermediate states. The resulting gate fidelities exceed 99.8%, with durations of less than 70 nanoseconds, making them ten to thirty times faster than conventional microwave-driven gates. The initialization protocol, which exploits the large dispersive shifts of higher plasmon states to rapidly cool the qubit to an effective temperature of 190 microkelvin, achieves 97% state preparation fidelity without requiring additional cryogenic infrastructure or parametric amplification. The readout scheme, which transfers population to higher-energy levels for enhanced dispersive coupling, enables single-shot readout fidelity of 50% and is scalable to higher values through optimization of circuit parameters. The entire system is fabricated using standard superconducting circuit techniques, ensuring compatibility with existing quantum processor fabrication pipelines and enabling seamless integration into multi-qubit architectures. The control scheme is inherently robust against pulse distortions and low-frequency noise due to its antisymmetric design and dependence only on the integrated area of the flux pulse, making it ideal for large-scale deployment. Furthermore, the principles underlying this invention are broadly applicable to any low-frequency superconducting qubit with a large anharmonicity, including other protected qubit designs and hybrid systems. The extension of this architecture to two-qubit gates via inductive coupling is straightforward and would enable two-qubit operations that are as fast as the single-qubit gates demonstrated here, without requiring the participation of lossy excited states. With coherence times and gate speeds expected to improve further through material optimization and advanced fabrication techniques, this invention establishes the heavy-fluxonium as the first practical competitor to the transmon qubit and opens a new pathway toward scalable, high-performance superconducting quantum processors capable of operating efficiently at temperatures significantly higher than those required by conventional systems.
