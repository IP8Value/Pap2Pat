# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### 1. Field of the Invention

The present invention relates to a rapid, wireless, and label-free method for determining antimicrobial susceptibility in bacterial populations, particularly in clinical and high-throughput diagnostic environments. More specifically, the invention encompasses a passive inductively coupled LC resonant sensor system integrated into standard microtiter plate formats, enabling the detection of bacterial growth dynamics and antibiotic response within thirty minutes of inoculation without the need for sample enrichment, species-specific surface modifications, or complex data processing. The system operates by measuring changes in the complex permittivity of a bacterial suspension through non-contact electromagnetic coupling between a miniaturized sensor embedded within each well of a 96-well plate and an external impedance analyzer. The sensor’s resonant frequency shifts in response to dielectric property alterations induced by bacterial proliferation and metabolic activity, allowing for the quantitative assessment of antimicrobial efficacy based on the slope of permittivity change over a short, early time window. This technology is particularly suited for use in intensive care units, emergency departments, and clinical microbiology laboratories where rapid decision-making regarding antibiotic therapy is critical to patient survival and antimicrobial stewardship.

### 2. Description of the Related Art

The global rise in antimicrobial resistance has precipitated a public health crisis of unprecedented scale, with millions of infections and hundreds of thousands of deaths annually attributable to drug-resistant pathogens. In hospital settings, particularly within intensive care units, the prevalence of resistant organisms exceeds thirty percent in high-income nations and is two to three times higher in low- and middle-income countries. The mortality risk associated with delayed or inappropriate antibiotic administration in sepsis is well documented; studies have demonstrated that a delay of merely three hours in initiating effective antimicrobial therapy can reduce survival rates by more than ten percent, with survival dropping from eighty percent to less than fifteen percent when treatment is withheld beyond fifteen hours post-inoculation in experimental models of sepsis. These findings underscore the urgent need for diagnostic tools capable of delivering actionable antimicrobial susceptibility results within a clinically relevant timeframe—ideally within one to two hours of sample receipt.

Traditional phenotypic methods for antimicrobial susceptibility testing, such as broth microdilution, agar disk diffusion, and gradient diffusion techniques, remain the gold standard in many clinical laboratories due to their reliability and broad applicability. However, these methods require extended incubation periods, typically ranging from sixteen to twenty-four hours, to achieve sufficient bacterial growth for reliable interpretation. This delay forces clinicians to initiate empiric antibiotic regimens, often broad-spectrum and potentially inappropriate, which contribute to the selection pressure driving further resistance development and increase the risk of adverse patient outcomes. Although automated systems such as VITEK and Phoenix have improved throughput and reduced turnaround time to approximately six to eight hours, they still fall short of the ideal window for early intervention in critical care scenarios.

Genotypic approaches, including polymerase chain reaction (PCR), whole-genome sequencing, and matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF), offer faster results—sometimes within hours—by detecting known resistance genes or protein signatures. However, these methods are inherently limited by their reliance on pre-existing knowledge of resistance mechanisms. Novel or emerging resistance determinants, such as newly evolved beta-lactamases, efflux pumps, or membrane modifications not yet cataloged in genomic databases, remain undetectable by such techniques. Consequently, genotypic assays may yield false-negative results in cases involving previously uncharacterized resistance pathways, rendering them unreliable as standalone diagnostic tools in dynamic clinical environments where novel resistance variants are continually emerging.

Recent innovations in biosensing have sought to bridge this gap by leveraging physical, optical, or electrochemical signals to detect bacterial metabolic activity in real time. Optical imaging techniques using microfluidic chambers have enabled the visualization of single-cell growth dynamics, while pH sensors have tracked acidification byproducts of bacterial metabolism. Bioluminescent assays based on adenosine triphosphate (ATP) quantification and magnetic sensors coated with species-specific antibodies have also demonstrated sensitivity to low bacterial concentrations. Electrochemical biosensors employing peptide or antibody-functionalized electrodes have achieved detection limits as low as thirty colony-forming units per milliliter. Despite their technical sophistication, these approaches suffer from significant limitations that hinder their translation into routine clinical practice. Many require elaborate sample preparation, including cell washing, lysis, or purification steps that are impractical in high-volume or resource-constrained settings. Others necessitate expensive instrumentation, specialized reagents, or highly trained personnel for operation and data interpretation. Furthermore, several of these methods are compromised by the presence of host proteins, cellular debris, or other biological matrices commonly encountered in clinical specimens such as blood, urine, or wound exudates, which can cause signal interference, sensor fouling, or nonspecific binding.

A critical shortcoming shared by many of these emerging technologies is their dependence on single-cell resolution or population-level measurements that are sensitive to microbial heterogeneity. In polymicrobial infections—a common occurrence in clinical isolates from catheter-related bloodstream infections, diabetic foot ulcers, or intra-abdominal abscesses—current systems often fail to distinguish between competing species or to accurately quantify the relative susceptibility of each constituent organism. This limitation renders them unsuitable for comprehensive antimicrobial susceptibility profiling in complex clinical samples. Moreover, many of these platforms are not compatible with standardized laboratory infrastructure, such as 96-well microtiter plates, which are universally used in clinical microbiology for high-throughput screening, storage, and automation. The inability to integrate with existing workflows imposes a significant barrier to adoption, requiring costly reconfiguration of laboratory equipment and protocols.

Another class of technologies that has garnered attention is based on wireless passive sensors employing inductively coupled LC resonant circuits. These sensors operate without an internal power source, relying instead on electromagnetic induction to transmit energy and receive data from an external reader. Their inherent simplicity, low cost, and potential for long-term stability make them attractive for implantable or embedded biomedical applications. Early iterations of LC sensors for microbial detection were developed in the 1990s and 2000s, primarily for monitoring bacterial growth in large-volume agitated cultures. However, these systems suffered from prolonged response times exceeding eight hours, rendering them unsuitable for rapid diagnostics. Additionally, they lacked robustness against protein adsorption, a phenomenon that occurs when serum proteins or other macromolecules bind to sensor surfaces and alter their dielectric properties, thereby masking or distorting the signal attributable to bacterial growth. Furthermore, these sensors were not designed for integration into microtiter plate formats, nor were they optimized for the detection of antimicrobial susceptibility, focusing instead on bulk growth kinetics in homogeneous environments.

Impedance spectroscopy has also been explored for microbial detection, with some studies reporting high sensitivity to low bacterial concentrations. However, these systems typically require complex electrode coatings, such as conductive polymers or nanomaterials, to enhance signal transduction, and are often incompatible with the presence of biological fluids. The coatings degrade over time, necessitate frequent recalibration, and are not easily scalable for mass production. Moreover, impedance-based systems generally require direct electrical contact with the sample, limiting their utility in sealed or disposable formats and introducing risks of cross-contamination and electrode fouling.

Thus, despite decades of research and numerous technological advances, there remains a critical unmet need for a rapid, broadly applicable, cost-effective, and clinically compatible antimicrobial susceptibility testing platform that can deliver reliable results within thirty minutes of sample inoculation, without requiring prior knowledge of the pathogen’s identity, without the need for complex sample preparation or specialized reagents, and without being compromised by the presence of host proteins or polymicrobial complexity. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel LC sensor architecture that exploits the intrinsic capacitive properties of bacterial cells and their metabolic byproducts to detect antimicrobial susceptibility through wireless, non-contact, label-free measurement of permittivity changes in a standardized microtiter plate format. The system is uniquely configured to operate in the presence of biological matrices, is scalable to high-throughput screening, and provides a quantitative, slope-based metric for susceptibility that correlates directly with established clinical benchmarks such as minimum inhibitory concentrations, thereby enabling immediate clinical decision-making without the delays inherent in conventional methods.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel system and method for the rapid determination of antimicrobial susceptibility in bacterial populations through the use of a passive, wireless, inductively coupled LC resonant sensor integrated into a standard 96-well microtiter plate. The sensor consists of a flexible polyimide-based circuit board patterned with a spiral inductor coil and an interdigitated capacitor structure, both of which are coated with a thin, biocompatible polyurethane layer to prevent nonspecific fouling while permitting the passage of dielectric changes induced by bacterial growth. The sensor is inserted into individual wells of a 96-well plate, each containing a diluted bacterial suspension in a low-salt culture medium supplemented with varying concentrations of antimicrobial agents. An external impedance analyzer, connected to a receiver coil positioned beneath the plate, wirelessly interrogates each sensor by sweeping a range of frequencies and detecting the resonant frequency shift caused by changes in the complex permittivity of the bacterial suspension. The resonant frequency is determined by identifying the point at which the imaginary component of the total impedance is minimized, and the corresponding real component is used to calculate the sensor’s equivalent capacitance and resistance. These parameters are then employed to compute the complex permittivity of the medium over time using a derived mathematical model that accounts for the geometric configuration of the interdigitated capacitor, the substrate properties, and the known electromagnetic coupling characteristics between the sensor and the receiver coil. The rate of change in permittivity during the initial thirty-minute period following inoculation is quantified as the slope of a linear regression fit to the time-series data, and this slope serves as a direct indicator of bacterial metabolic activity and, by extension, antimicrobial susceptibility. A positive slope indicative of increasing permittivity signifies bacterial growth and thus resistance to the administered antimicrobial, whereas a near-zero or negative slope indicates growth inhibition and susceptibility. The system is capable of detecting susceptibility across a broad spectrum of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial species, including Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and is effective against multiple classes of antibiotics, including beta-lactams, fluoroquinolones, glycopeptides, and aminoglycosides. Crucially, the method requires no prior knowledge of the pathogen’s identity, does not necessitate species-specific surface modifications, and functions reliably in the presence of up to two percent fetal bovine serum, simulating the protein-rich environment of clinical specimens such as blood plasma or serum. The entire process—from sample loading to result generation—can be fully automated using a programmable interface that triggers frequency sweeps at five-minute intervals and processes the data in real time using a simple algorithm to compute slopes and classify susceptibility. The system is compatible with existing laboratory infrastructure, requires no specialized reagents beyond standard culture media, and can be manufactured at low cost using conventional printed circuit board fabrication techniques. The resulting platform enables high-throughput antimicrobial susceptibility testing with a turnaround time of thirty minutes, significantly outperforming conventional methods and providing a practical solution for rapid clinical decision-making in critical care settings.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Example

The present invention is directed to a system and method for the rapid, label-free, and high-throughput determination of antimicrobial susceptibility in bacterial populations through the use of a passive, wireless, inductively coupled LC resonant sensor integrated into a standard 96-well microtiter plate format. The system operates without the need for sample enrichment, species-specific surface modifications, or complex data analysis, and delivers clinically actionable results within thirty minutes of inoculation. The core innovation lies in the design and application of a miniaturized LC sensor that detects changes in the complex permittivity of a bacterial suspension through non-contact electromagnetic coupling with an external impedance analyzer, enabling the quantification of bacterial metabolic activity as a function of antimicrobial exposure. The sensor comprises a flexible polyimide substrate upon which a spiral inductor coil and an interdigitated capacitor structure are fabricated using conventional photolithographic and etching techniques. The inductor coil, consisting of fifty turns of copper trace with a width of sixty micrometers and a gap of sixty micrometers between adjacent traces, is patterned on both sides of the substrate to maximize inductance while minimizing footprint. The interdigitated capacitor, positioned adjacent to the coil, consists of a series of parallel finger electrodes, each with a width of seventy-five micrometers and a spacing of seventy-five micrometers, extending radially from the center of the sensor to form a circular array of one hundred and twenty digits. The total outer diameter of the sensor is designed to fit precisely within the internal circumference of a standard 96-well plate, ensuring uniform positioning and optimal electromagnetic coupling. The entire sensor surface is coated with a thin, uniform layer of oil-based polyurethane spray, approximately two micrometers in thickness, which serves to protect the metallic traces from corrosion, prevent direct adhesion of bacterial cells or host proteins to the conductive surfaces, and create a dielectric interface that allows the permittivity of the surrounding medium to influence the capacitance of the interdigitated structure without impeding signal transduction. The sensor is mounted onto a double-sided adhesive backing, cured in a desiccator for forty-eight hours to ensure complete solvent evaporation and polymer cross-linking, and then individually cut and rolled into a cylindrical sleeve that is inserted vertically into each well of the microtiter plate, with the sensing region aligned along the inner wall of the well to minimize interference from sedimentation and to maximize exposure to the liquid phase of the suspension.

The microtiter plate containing the sensors is placed atop a receiver coil, which is connected to a high-precision impedance analyzer capable of sweeping frequencies from one megahertz to twelve megahertz with a resolution of sixteen hundred data points per sweep. The receiver coil, identical in geometry to the sensor coil but larger in diameter and fixed in position beneath the plate, generates a time-varying magnetic field that induces a current in the sensor circuit through mutual inductance. The magnetic coupling between the two coils is optimized by positioning the sensor and receiver coils at a separation distance of one point two millimeters, with a polystyrene well bottom of standard thickness serving as a magnetically transparent spacer that does not attenuate the field. The impedance analyzer measures the total impedance of the combined system, which includes the intrinsic impedance of the receiver coil, the mutual inductance between the coils, and the complex impedance of the sensor circuit. The sensor’s impedance is modeled as a parallel combination of a resistor and a capacitor representing the interdigitated capacitor structure, in series with the inductance of the coil. The total measured impedance is then mathematically deconvoluted to isolate the sensor’s equivalent resistance and capacitance by subtracting the background impedance of the system and solving for the unknown parameters using a set of derived equations that relate the resonant frequency and zero-reactance frequency to the permittivity of the medium. The zero-reactance frequency is identified as the point at which the imaginary component of the total impedance is minimized, and the resonant frequency is determined as the frequency at which the derivative of the real component of the impedance reaches its maximum. These two frequencies are then substituted into a permittivity calculation model that incorporates the geometric constants of the interdigitated capacitor, the permittivity of the polyimide substrate, and the permittivity of free space to compute the complex permittivity of the bacterial suspension at each time point. The permittivity values are recorded at five-minute intervals over a thirty-minute period, and a linear regression is performed on the time-series data to determine the slope of the permittivity change. This slope is normalized against a baseline established from sterile medium and antibiotic-free controls, and a threshold value is applied to classify the bacterial population as susceptible or resistant to the administered antimicrobial agent. A slope exceeding a predetermined positive threshold indicates active bacterial growth and thus resistance, whereas a slope near zero or negative indicates growth inhibition and susceptibility. The system has been validated across multiple bacterial species, including Escherichia coli MG1655, Escherichia coli MG1655 ASV (a genetically engineered ampicillin-resistant strain), Staphylococcus aureus ALC2085, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1, and has demonstrated consistent performance with antibiotics from diverse chemical classes, including ampicillin, ofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, vancomycin, and tobramycin. The method has been shown to correlate strongly with traditional broth microdilution minimum inhibitory concentration assays, with sensitivity and specificity exceeding ninety-eight percent when compared against reference standards. Importantly, the system retains functionality in the presence of up to two percent fetal bovine serum, a concentration sufficient to simulate the protein content of human plasma or serum, demonstrating its potential for use with clinical specimens that have undergone minimal preparation such as centrifugation or dilution. The coating thickness and composition of the sensor surface can be further optimized to extend the upper limit of protein tolerance, and the geometric parameters of the interdigitated capacitor can be adjusted to increase the saturation thickness of the dielectric interface, thereby enabling compatibility with even more complex biological matrices. The entire process is fully automatable, with a custom MATLAB script interfacing directly with the impedance analyzer to trigger frequency sweeps, record data, compute slopes, and generate binary susceptibility reports without human intervention. The system requires no specialized reagents beyond standard low-salt Luria-Bertani broth, which is selected for its high resistivity to minimize background noise and enhance the detectability of permittivity shifts. The sensors are manufactured using commercially available printed circuit board fabrication facilities, enabling mass production at low cost, and the entire platform is compatible with existing laboratory automation systems, including robotic liquid handlers and plate readers. The method is not limited by the identity of the bacterial species, does not require prior knowledge of resistance genes, and is equally effective in polymicrobial suspensions, where the aggregate permittivity slope reflects the combined growth dynamics of all viable organisms present. This makes the system uniquely suited for use in clinical settings where the identity of the pathogen is unknown at the time of testing and where mixed infections are common. The system’s ability to provide a quantitative, slope-based metric for antimicrobial susceptibility within thirty minutes represents a paradigm shift in diagnostic microbiology, offering a practical, scalable, and clinically viable alternative to traditional methods that require overnight incubation and to genotypic methods that are limited by their dependence on pre-existing genomic knowledge. The invention enables rapid antibiotic stewardship, reduces the time to effective therapy in septic patients, and provides a platform for real-time surveillance of emerging resistance patterns in hospital environments.

The underlying principle of the invention is rooted in the dielectric properties of bacterial cells and their interaction with electromagnetic fields. Bacterial cells, particularly during active growth, exhibit distinct capacitive characteristics due to the presence of charged membrane components, intracellular electrolytes, and metabolic byproducts that alter the local permittivity of the surrounding medium. As cells proliferate, their increasing density and metabolic activity cause measurable changes in the dielectric constant and loss factor of the suspension, which are detected by the interdigitated capacitor structure of the LC sensor. The sensor’s design is optimized to maximize sensitivity to these subtle permittivity shifts by employing a high-density interdigitated capacitor with narrow finger spacing and a large number of digits, which increases the surface area exposed to the medium and enhances the signal-to-noise ratio. The spiral inductor coil, with its high number of turns and precise geometry, ensures strong inductive coupling with the receiver coil, enabling efficient energy transfer and reliable signal detection even through the polystyrene well bottom. The polyurethane coating plays a critical role in the system’s functionality by creating a thin, uniform dielectric barrier that prevents direct adsorption of proteins or cells onto the conductive surfaces while allowing the permittivity of the medium to modulate the capacitance of the interdigitated structure. This design overcomes the primary limitation of prior LC sensor systems, which failed in protein-rich environments due to nonspecific fouling that masked the bacterial signal. The mathematical model used to compute permittivity from the measured resonant and zero-reactance frequencies is derived from first principles of electromagnetic theory and circuit analysis, incorporating the geometric constants of the sensor, the known properties of the substrate, and the mutual inductance between the coils. The model has been validated through finite element simulations using COMSOL Multiphysics, which confirmed the accuracy of the predicted permittivity values under varying bacterial densities and media compositions. The slope-based classification algorithm, which relies on the rate of permittivity change rather than absolute values, provides robustness against minor variations in sensor calibration, temperature fluctuations, and slight differences in inoculum size, making the system highly reproducible across multiple batches and operators. The threshold for susceptibility classification is determined empirically through extensive testing across multiple strains and antibiotics, and is set to ensure a high degree of concordance with established clinical breakpoints. The system’s performance has been confirmed through direct comparison with colony-forming unit counts, which demonstrate a strong linear correlation with optical density measurements, validating the assumption that permittivity changes are directly proportional to viable bacterial biomass. The ability to detect susceptibility within thirty minutes is made possible by the system’s high sensitivity to early metabolic activity, which manifests as a detectable permittivity shift long before the population reaches the exponential growth phase detectable by conventional methods. This early detection capability is critical for clinical utility, as it allows for the initiation of appropriate antimicrobial therapy during the critical window in which patient outcomes are most sensitive to treatment timing. The system’s compatibility with standard 96-well plates ensures seamless integration into existing laboratory workflows, eliminating the need for costly infrastructure changes or specialized cartridges. The passive nature of the sensor, requiring no internal power source or battery, ensures long-term stability, eliminates the risk of electronic failure, and enables the production of single-use, disposable sensors at low cost. The entire platform can be deployed in point-of-care settings, including emergency departments and intensive care units, where rapid diagnostic information is essential for guiding antimicrobial therapy. The invention represents a significant advancement in the field of antimicrobial susceptibility testing, providing a solution that is not only faster and more accurate than existing methods but also simpler, more robust, and more adaptable to the complexities of real-world clinical samples.
