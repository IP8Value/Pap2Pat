# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF INVENTION

The present invention relates to a non-invasive, multi-modal biosignal monitoring system and associated machine learning-based predictive algorithm for the early detection and forecasting of surgical site infections, particularly superficial incisional surgical site infections, in post-operative patients. More specifically, the invention encompasses a wearable sensor array capable of continuously and simultaneously measuring peri-wound tissue oxygen saturation, temperature, and bioimpedance, coupled with a robust ensemble machine learning model trained to interpret the temporal dynamics of these physiological parameters in order to identify subtle, pre-clinical deviations indicative of developing infection. The system is designed for deployment in both inpatient and outpatient settings, enabling remote, real-time surveillance of surgical wounds without reliance on subjective clinical assessment or scheduled follow-up visits. By leveraging continuous, objective digital biomarkers derived from tissue-level physiological changes, the invention provides a novel and clinically actionable method for predicting the onset of surgical site infection hours before traditional diagnostic criteria become apparent, thereby enabling timely therapeutic intervention and significantly improving patient outcomes while reducing healthcare expenditures.

## BACKGROUND

Surgical site infections represent one of the most prevalent and consequential complications following surgical procedures, affecting millions of patients globally each year and imposing a substantial burden on healthcare systems. These infections, which manifest in the incisional area following surgical intervention, are classified into three categories: superficial incisional, deep incisional, and organ/space infections, with superficial incisional infections being the most frequently encountered. Despite decades of advances in aseptic technique, prophylactic antibiotic administration, and perioperative care protocols, the incidence of surgical site infections remains stubbornly high, with estimates suggesting that over half a million such infections occur annually in the United States alone. A significant proportion—nearly seventy percent—of these infections are not detected until after the patient has been discharged from the hospital, rendering traditional surveillance methods inadequate and often delayed. The consequences of such delayed diagnosis are severe: prolonged hospitalization, increased risk of mortality, heightened risk of antibiotic resistance, and an estimated additional cost of over twenty-five thousand dollars per case. The economic toll across the healthcare system exceeds ten billion dollars annually, underscoring the urgent need for more effective, proactive, and objective monitoring tools.

Current methods for detecting post-discharge surgical site infections rely heavily on patient self-reporting, scheduled outpatient visits, telephone interviews, or visual inspection by healthcare professionals, all of which are inherently subjective, intermittent, and prone to human error. Clinical signs such as erythema, swelling, purulent drainage, and localized warmth typically emerge only after microbial proliferation has progressed to a point where the host inflammatory response is visibly manifest. By this stage, the infection has often already triggered a cascade of tissue damage and systemic immune activation, limiting the efficacy of early therapeutic intervention. Moreover, the variability in clinical presentation, the subtlety of early signs, and the lack of standardized protocols for post-discharge monitoring contribute to inconsistent detection rates and frequent underreporting. The absence of a reliable, continuous, and non-invasive monitoring system means that critical windows for intervention—particularly those occurring within the first 24 to 48 hours after the initiation of pathophysiological changes—are routinely missed.

While prior research has explored the use of single-modality sensors to monitor wound conditions, such as temperature or pH, these approaches have been limited in their predictive power due to the multifactorial nature of infection. The physiological response to infection is not characterized by a single parameter but rather by a complex, dynamic interplay of metabolic, vascular, and immunological processes that manifest as concurrent alterations in tissue oxygenation, thermal conductivity, and electrical impedance. For instance, the inflammatory response increases local metabolic demand, leading to transient increases in tissue oxygen consumption followed by hypoxia; the influx of immune cells and exudative fluid alters tissue conductivity and bioimpedance; and the release of pyrogenic mediators elevates local temperature. These changes occur in a temporally coordinated but non-linear fashion, often preceding visible clinical signs by many hours. Existing technologies have failed to integrate these multiple physiological dimensions into a unified, real-time diagnostic framework capable of extracting predictive patterns from noisy, high-dimensional data streams.

Machine learning has demonstrated considerable promise in medical diagnostics, particularly in identifying subtle patterns in complex datasets. However, prior applications in the context of surgical site infection have been constrained by reliance on static, discrete clinical variables—such as patient demographics, laboratory values, or surgical duration—rather than continuous, dynamic physiological signals captured directly from the wound environment. Furthermore, many existing models suffer from poor generalizability due to small sample sizes, lack of temporal resolution, or insufficient handling of class imbalance inherent in the low prevalence of infection relative to the vast number of non-infected wounds. There remains a critical gap in the literature and in clinical practice for a system that can continuously acquire multi-modal biosignals from the peri-wound region, filter and process these signals in real time, and apply a validated, ensemble-based machine learning algorithm to predict the onset of infection with sufficient lead time to enable effective clinical intervention. The present invention addresses this unmet need by introducing a novel, integrated hardware-software platform that captures, processes, and interprets the physiological signatures of early infection with unprecedented accuracy and temporal resolution, thereby transforming the paradigm of post-operative wound surveillance from reactive to predictive.

## SUMMARY

The present invention comprises a non-invasive, wearable biosignal monitoring system and a machine learning-based predictive algorithm designed to detect and forecast the onset of superficial incisional surgical site infections prior to the emergence of overt clinical signs. The system integrates a multi-sensor array capable of simultaneously and continuously measuring three distinct physiological parameters—tissue oxygen saturation, peri-wound temperature, and bioimpedance—across the surgical incision site and its immediate periphery. These sensors are embedded within a flexible, lightweight, and clinically safe wearable module that adheres to the skin surrounding the incision without requiring sutures or invasive fixation, thereby enabling long-term, uninterrupted monitoring in both hospital and home settings. The sensor array is coupled with a portable data acquisition unit that performs real-time signal conditioning, artifact removal, and temporal segmentation, storing high-fidelity data for subsequent analysis or wireless transmission to a remote monitoring platform.

The core innovation of the invention lies in the application of a sophisticated ensemble machine learning model trained to interpret the dynamic, multi-dimensional patterns of the acquired biosignals. This model employs a bagged, stacked, and balanced logistic regression architecture that integrates feature extraction from multiple temporal windows, accounts for class imbalance inherent in infection data, and fuses predictions from multiple sub-classifiers to generate a probabilistic estimate of infection status. The model is trained to predict three distinct clinical endpoints: the current presence of a superficial incisional surgical site infection, the likelihood of infection developing within the next 24 hours, and the likelihood of infection developing within the next 48 hours. Through rigorous validation using cross-validation techniques and statistical comparison against clinician-based diagnosis, the model demonstrates superior diagnostic performance, achieving an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve greater than 0.80 for prediction 24 hours in advance of clinical diagnosis, with sensitivity and specificity exceeding 80% and 75%, respectively.

The invention further includes a user interface and clinical decision support system that translates the model’s probabilistic output into actionable alerts for clinicians, nurses, or patients, enabling timely initiation of diagnostic evaluation or therapeutic intervention such as targeted antibiotic therapy, wound debridement, or enhanced monitoring. The system operates autonomously, requiring minimal user input after initial placement, and is designed to integrate seamlessly with existing electronic health record systems, telehealth platforms, and hospital monitoring infrastructure. By providing continuous, objective, and predictive insight into the physiological state of the surgical wound, the invention fundamentally shifts the paradigm of post-operative care from passive observation to active prediction, thereby reducing the incidence of delayed diagnosis, minimizing unnecessary hospital readmissions, lowering healthcare costs, and ultimately improving patient survival and recovery outcomes. The invention is applicable to a broad range of surgical procedures involving superficial incisions, including but not limited to cardiothoracic, orthopedic, gynecologic, vascular, and general surgical interventions, and represents a transformative advancement in the field of digital wound care.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present invention is directed to a comprehensive system for the continuous, non-invasive monitoring of surgical wounds and the predictive detection of superficial incisional surgical site infections through the integration of multi-modal biosignal acquisition and ensemble machine learning analysis. The system is composed of two primary components: a wearable biosignal sensor array and a data processing and predictive analytics unit, both of which function in concert to capture, interpret, and act upon physiological changes occurring in the peri-wound tissue prior to the manifestation of clinical symptoms. The sensor array is engineered to measure three distinct, physiologically relevant parameters—tissue oxygen saturation, peri-wound temperature, and bioimpedance—with high temporal resolution and signal fidelity, while the analytics unit employs a robust, multi-layered machine learning architecture to extract predictive patterns from these signals and generate clinically actionable predictions regarding the likelihood of infection onset.

The wearable biosignal sensor array is constructed as a compact, flexible module designed for secure, non-invasive attachment to the skin surrounding a surgical incision. The module incorporates three integrated sensing subsystems, each optimized for the continuous, real-time measurement of a specific physiological parameter. The first subsystem employs near-infrared spectroscopy to determine tissue oxygen saturation, or StO2, by emitting light at two distinct wavelengths—730 nanometers and 850 nanometers—through a pair of light-emitting diodes positioned at a fixed source-detector separation distance of 50 millimeters. The emitted light penetrates the subcutaneous tissue layers, where it is absorbed and scattered by hemoglobin in the microvasculature. The reflected light is captured by a silicon photodiode with a large active area of 7.5 square millimeters, enabling high signal-to-noise ratio acquisition even under conditions of minor motion artifact. The analog signals from the photodiode are processed by an AFE4420 analog front-end integrated circuit, which performs amplification, filtering, and analog-to-digital conversion to yield a continuous stream of oxygen saturation values expressed as a percentage of baseline tissue oxygenation. This measurement reflects the dynamic balance between oxygen delivery and consumption in the tissue, which is profoundly altered during the inflammatory cascade associated with infection.

The second sensing subsystem utilizes a high-precision digital temperature sensor, specifically the TMP117 manufactured by Texas Instruments, to measure the surface temperature of the skin immediately adjacent to the incision. This sensor provides temperature readings with a resolution of 0.001 degrees Celsius and an accuracy of ±0.1 degrees Celsius across the physiological range of 30 to 45 degrees Celsius. The sensor is mounted in direct contact with the skin via a thermally conductive, biocompatible silicone interface that ensures consistent thermal coupling while minimizing the influence of ambient environmental fluctuations. Temperature data is sampled at a rate of 50 hertz and continuously logged, allowing for the detection of minute, transient increases in local heat production that precede visible signs of inflammation. These increases are attributed to elevated metabolic activity of infiltrating immune cells, increased blood flow due to vasodilation, and the exothermic nature of enzymatic reactions involved in microbial killing and tissue repair.

The third sensing subsystem is a bioimpedance measurement system based on the AD5941 integrated circuit from Analog Devices, capable of generating and measuring high-frequency alternating current signals up to 200 kilohertz. This system applies a small, imperceptible electrical current across two electrodes positioned on either side of the incision and measures the resulting voltage differential to calculate the tissue’s opposition to current flow, or bioimpedance. The bioimpedance value is influenced by the composition and structure of the tissue, including the concentration of extracellular fluid, the integrity of cell membranes, and the presence of inflammatory exudates. During the early stages of infection, the infiltration of immune cells, increased vascular permeability, and accumulation of interstitial fluid lead to a measurable decrease in bioimpedance, reflecting a shift in tissue conductivity. The system employs a multi-frequency sweep protocol to enhance signal robustness and mitigate the effects of electrode-skin interface variability, ensuring consistent measurements across diverse patient populations and skin types.

All three sensing subsystems are integrated onto a single printed circuit board, which is encased in a medical-grade silicone molding that conforms to the curvature of the body and provides mechanical stability, moisture resistance, and protection against mechanical trauma. The entire sensor module measures 10 centimeters in length, 6 centimeters in width, and 3 centimeters in thickness, and weighs 121 grams, making it lightweight and comfortable for prolonged wear. The module is secured to the skin using a hypoallergenic, pressure-sensitive adhesive layer that is compatible with extended wear (up to seven days) and does not require sutures, staples, or other invasive fixation methods. This design allows for seamless integration into standard post-operative care pathways without disrupting wound dressing protocols or patient mobility.

The sensor array is connected via a low-power, shielded cable to a portable data acquisition unit known as the CrelyPRO, which serves as the central hub for signal processing, storage, and communication. The CrelyPRO unit is built around the Feather nRF52840 microcontroller, a low-power Bluetooth-enabled system-on-chip that supports real-time data streaming and wireless communication with external devices. The unit incorporates a PCF8523 real-time clock to timestamp all sensor readings with microsecond precision, ensuring accurate temporal alignment of multi-modal data streams. Data is acquired in burst-mode sampling at a frequency of 50 hertz, with each burst lasting one minute and occurring every ten minutes, resulting in a total of six data points per hour per sensor. This sampling strategy balances the need for high-resolution temporal data with the constraints of power consumption and data storage capacity. All acquired data is stored locally on an onboard flash memory chip, which can retain up to 14 days of continuous monitoring data, allowing for uninterrupted operation even in the absence of wireless connectivity.

The CrelyPRO unit is powered by a rechargeable 3.7-volt lithium polymer battery, which provides sufficient energy to operate the system continuously for at least seven days without requiring recharging. The unit is designed with a rugged, waterproof casing suitable for use in clinical and home environments and includes a status indicator light and a physical button for manual data export or system reset. Communication with external systems is established via Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) protocol, enabling secure, low-bandwidth transmission of aggregated data to a smartphone application, hospital monitoring station, or cloud-based analytics platform. The system is compatible with standard healthcare interoperability protocols, including HL7 and FHIR, allowing for seamless integration into electronic health record systems.

The data collected by the sensor array and processed by the CrelyPRO unit is subsequently analyzed by a machine learning model specifically designed to detect the subtle, multi-dimensional signatures of early surgical site infection. The model is trained on a dataset comprising thousands of time-series observations derived from both infected and non-infected surgical wounds, with each observation representing a 10-minute window of continuous sensor data. Prior to model training, the raw biosignal data undergoes a multi-stage preprocessing pipeline designed to remove noise, correct for motion artifacts, and normalize the signals across patients and anatomical locations. First, a bandpass filter is applied to eliminate high-frequency electrical interference and low-frequency drift caused by respiration or patient movement. Second, a wavelet-based artifact detection algorithm identifies and removes transient spikes in the signal attributable to patient motion, skin displacement, or sensor loosening. Third, each biosignal is normalized to its individual baseline value, calculated as the mean of the first 24 hours of data collected post-surgery, to account for inter-individual variability in baseline physiology.

Following preprocessing, a feature extraction engine generates 90 distinct numerical descriptors from each 10-minute window of data. These features include statistical measures such as mean, median, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis; temporal features such as slope, rate of change, and acceleration; frequency domain features derived from Fourier and wavelet transforms; and nonlinear dynamic features such as approximate entropy and sample entropy, which quantify the complexity and predictability of the signal over time. Each feature is computed across multiple time lags, allowing the model to capture both immediate and delayed responses in the physiological signals. For example, the rate of change in tissue oxygen saturation over the preceding 30 minutes is calculated alongside the cumulative deviation from baseline over the past four hours, enabling the model to distinguish between transient fluctuations and sustained pathological trends.

To address the inherent class imbalance in the dataset—where the number of infected wounds is significantly smaller than the number of non-infected wounds—a balanced ensemble training strategy is employed. The minority class (infected wounds) is up-sampled using a synthetic minority oversampling technique that generates synthetic data points by interpolating between existing infected observations in the feature space, thereby preventing the model from being biased toward the majority class. Additionally, the model incorporates a cost-sensitive learning component that assigns higher misclassification penalties to false negatives—instances where infection is predicted as absent when it is actually present—thereby prioritizing sensitivity over specificity in clinical decision-making.

The core of the machine learning architecture is a stacked, bagged, and balanced logistic regression ensemble. This architecture combines the strengths of multiple modeling techniques to enhance predictive performance and robustness. First, a set of 10 individual logistic regression classifiers is trained on bootstrap samples of the training data, each drawn with replacement to create diverse training subsets. This bagging procedure reduces variance and improves generalization by averaging predictions across multiple models, each exposed to slightly different data distributions. Second, the outputs of these 10 base classifiers are fed into a meta-classifier, which is itself a logistic regression model trained to weigh and combine the predictions of the base models based on their individual performance across validation folds. This stacking approach allows the meta-classifier to learn which base models are most reliable under specific physiological conditions, such as high motion artifact or low signal-to-noise ratio, thereby improving overall accuracy. Third, the ensemble is trained to predict three distinct clinical endpoints: the current presence of infection, the likelihood of infection developing within the next 24 hours, and the likelihood of infection developing within the next 48 hours. Each prediction is output as a probability score between 0 and 1, with a threshold of 0.5 used to generate binary classifications, although clinicians may adjust this threshold based on clinical context and risk tolerance.

The model is rigorously validated using leave-one-out cross-validation, a technique in which each individual wound observation is sequentially held out as a test set while the model is trained on all remaining observations. This approach ensures that the model’s performance is evaluated on data it has never encountered during training, thereby minimizing the risk of overfitting and providing a conservative estimate of real-world accuracy. Performance metrics—including area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value—are computed for each fold and averaged across all iterations. The model demonstrates exceptional performance for predicting infection 24 hours in advance of clinical diagnosis, achieving an AUC of 0.80, an accuracy of 78%, a sensitivity of 81%, and a specificity of 76%. For prediction 48 hours in advance, the model maintains acceptable discriminability with an AUC of 0.74, while for current infection detection, the AUC is 0.77, indicating that the system is capable of detecting infection both before and at the time of clinical manifestation, with superior predictive power for early detection.

The system is further enhanced by a clinical decision support interface that translates the model’s probabilistic outputs into intuitive, actionable alerts for healthcare providers. The interface, accessible via web portal or mobile application, displays a real-time dashboard showing the temporal evolution of each biosignal alongside the model’s infection probability curve. When the probability of infection exceeds a predefined threshold—such as 0.7 for high-risk patients or 0.6 for standard-risk patients—the system triggers an automated alert to the responsible clinician, nurse, or care coordinator, accompanied by a recommendation for clinical evaluation, laboratory testing, or initiation of empiric antibiotic therapy. The interface also includes a historical trend viewer that allows clinicians to review the entire monitoring period, compare the patient’s trajectory to population-based norms, and assess the consistency of the signal changes over time. In addition, the system generates a confidence interval for each prediction, enabling clinicians to gauge the reliability of the alert and make more informed decisions.

The system is designed for scalability and adaptability across diverse clinical settings. In the inpatient environment, the CrelyPRO unit can be integrated into the hospital’s wireless monitoring network, with data automatically uploaded to the electronic health record and flagged for review by the surgical team. In the outpatient or home setting, the system can be paired with a patient-owned smartphone, which transmits data to a secure cloud server and notifies the care team if an alert is triggered. The adhesive sensor module is disposable and intended for single-use per surgical site, ensuring sterility and eliminating cross-contamination risks. The data acquisition unit is reusable and can be reprogrammed for different surgical procedures or patient populations, making the system cost-effective and sustainable over time.

The invention is applicable to a wide range of surgical procedures in which superficial incisions are made, including but not limited to coronary artery bypass grafting, mastectomy, hernia repair, appendectomy, cesarean section, orthopedic joint replacement, and vascular access procedures. The system is particularly valuable in outpatient surgical centers and ambulatory care facilities, where patients are discharged within hours of surgery and have limited access to immediate clinical follow-up. It is also highly suited for use in resource-limited settings, where access to diagnostic imaging, laboratory testing, or specialist consultation is restricted, as it provides an objective, automated means of identifying infection without requiring advanced infrastructure.

The system’s predictive capability is grounded in a deep understanding of the pathophysiology of surgical site infection. The observed changes in tissue oxygen saturation, temperature, and bioimpedance are not arbitrary signals but are direct manifestations of the biological processes that occur during the early stages of infection. The initial rise in tissue oxygen saturation reflects the hypermetabolic state induced by the recruitment of neutrophils and macrophages to the wound site, which increases local oxygen consumption and triggers compensatory vasodilation. This is followed by a decline as the inflammatory response intensifies, microvascular thrombosis occurs, and tissue hypoxia develops. The rise in temperature is driven by the release of pyrogenic cytokines such as interleukin-1 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, which act on the hypothalamus and increase local metabolic heat production. The decrease in bioimpedance is a consequence of increased extracellular fluid volume due to vascular leakage, the influx of inflammatory cells, and the accumulation of protein-rich exudate, all of which enhance the tissue’s ability to conduct electrical current. The ensemble model learns the precise temporal sequence and magnitude of these changes, allowing it to detect the earliest detectable deviations from normal wound healing trajectories.

The system’s performance has been validated in a porcine model of methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus infection, where it demonstrated the ability to predict infection 24 to 31 hours before clinical diagnosis by a blinded veterinarian. The cross-correlation analysis revealed that tissue oxygen saturation exhibited a significant upward trend 25 hours prior to clinical diagnosis, temperature increased 31 hours in advance, and bioimpedance decreased 24 hours in advance, confirming that the biosignals precede clinical manifestation. The machine learning model leveraged these temporal relationships to generate predictions with high accuracy, demonstrating that the combination of multi-modal signals and ensemble learning provides a level of predictive power unattainable by any single parameter or traditional diagnostic method.

The invention further includes a feedback loop for continuous model improvement. As the system is deployed in clinical practice, anonymized data from new patients is periodically aggregated and used to retrain and refine the machine learning model, ensuring that it adapts to evolving patient populations, surgical techniques, and microbial strains. The model is designed to be updated remotely via secure over-the-air firmware updates, allowing for seamless enhancement without requiring hardware replacement. This adaptive learning capability ensures that the system remains accurate and relevant over time, even as the epidemiology of surgical site infections changes.

In summary, the invention represents a paradigm shift in post-operative wound monitoring by providing a continuous, objective, and predictive tool for the early detection of surgical site infection. By integrating advanced biosensing technology with a sophisticated, clinically validated machine learning algorithm, the system transforms the passive observation of wound healing into an active, data-driven process of infection prediction. The result is a clinically deployable solution that reduces the burden of surgical site infection on patients and healthcare systems alike, enabling earlier intervention, shorter hospital stays, fewer readmissions, and improved survival rates. The invention is not merely an incremental improvement in wound care technology but a foundational advancement in the field of digital surgery, setting a new standard for the monitoring of post-operative recovery.
