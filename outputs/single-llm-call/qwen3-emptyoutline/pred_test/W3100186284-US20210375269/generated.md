# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of natural language processing, specifically to systems and methods for dialog act tagging in task-oriented dialogue systems. More particularly, the invention provides a novel unsupervised domain adaptation framework that leverages masked language models to improve the generalization of dialog act classifiers across unseen domains using only unlabeled dialogue data. The invention enables the construction of robust, scalable, and domain-agnostic dialog act taggers by introducing a stochastic text augmentation technique—referred to herein as MASKAUGMENT—and integrating it into a teacher-student learning paradigm that enforces consistency between representations derived from differently perturbed versions of the same dialogue history. This approach is particularly suited for deployment in commercial conversational agents, customer service automation platforms, virtual assistants, and enterprise dialogue systems where labeled training data is scarce, annotation costs are prohibitive, or operational environments span multiple domains with divergent linguistic patterns. The invention operates at the level of contextualized token embeddings and probability distributions over dialog act labels, and is compatible with any pre-trained transformer-based language model, including but not limited to BERT, RoBERTa, ALBERT, and ELECTRA. By exploiting the inherent capacity of masked language models to reconstruct contextually plausible sequences, the invention transforms the MASK token from a mere placeholder used during pre-training into a dynamic, controllable instrument for generating semantically preserved yet structurally varied dialogue inputs. This innovation allows the system to learn representations that are invariant to superficial lexical variations while remaining sensitive to the underlying pragmatic intent of each utterance, thereby significantly enhancing cross-domain performance without requiring any labeled examples from the target domain.

## BACKGROUND

In modern task-oriented dialogue systems, the accurate identification of dialog acts—semantic intentions expressed by speakers during conversation—is a critical component for downstream functions such as intent recognition, state tracking, response generation, and dialogue management. Dialog act tagging involves assigning one or more labels from a predefined taxonomy to each utterance in a multi-turn conversation, indicating whether the speaker is requesting information, confirming details, expressing dissatisfaction, providing an update, or performing other communicative functions. While early approaches relied on hand-crafted rules, finite-state machines, or shallow machine learning models, recent advances have shifted toward deep learning architectures based on pre-trained language models, which capture rich contextual dependencies across turns and speakers. These models, particularly those derived from the Transformer architecture, have demonstrated superior performance on in-domain dialog act tagging tasks when trained on large, annotated corpora.

However, a persistent challenge in deploying such models in real-world applications is their poor generalization across domains. Dialog systems operating in diverse verticals—such as booking flights, ordering food, scheduling appointments, or controlling smart home devices—often encounter linguistic patterns, vocabulary, and conversational structures that differ substantially from those seen during training. Although several universal dialog act taxonomies have been proposed to standardize annotation schemes across datasets, the scarcity of labeled data in new or emerging domains remains a fundamental bottleneck. Manual annotation of dialogues is labor-intensive, time-consuming, and requires domain-specific expertise, making it economically infeasible to retrain models for every new application context. Moreover, even when labeled data exists in one domain, transferring it directly to another often results in significant performance degradation due to distributional shifts in lexical choice, syntactic structure, and pragmatic conventions.

Previous attempts to address this issue have explored domain adaptation techniques such as adversarial training, feature alignment, and transfer learning from related tasks. Some approaches have attempted to leverage unlabeled data through self-training, pseudo-labeling, or entropy minimization. Others have employed data augmentation methods such as synonym replacement, back-translation, or sentence shuffling to increase training diversity. However, these methods have proven largely ineffective when applied to pre-trained language models, particularly in the context of multi-turn dialogues. The reason lies in the sensitivity of contextual embeddings to perturbations: replacing a single word with a synonym may alter the semantic meaning in subtle but critical ways, while back-translation introduces noise through imperfect linguistic reconstructions and fails to preserve the turn-by-turn conversational structure. Furthermore, many augmentation techniques are designed for short, isolated sentences and do not account for the sequential, interactive nature of dialogues, where the meaning of a current utterance is deeply entangled with prior exchanges.

Recent work has begun to explore the use of masked language modeling—originally developed for pre-training—as a fine-tuning objective. While the Masked Language Model (MLM) loss has been used to further adapt models to in-domain unlabeled text, its application to dialog act tagging has been limited to simple retraining without explicit consideration of consistency or representation stability. Moreover, no prior method has systematically exploited the MASK token as a controllable mechanism for generating multiple, hierarchically varying views of the same dialogue history to enforce robustness in classification. The notion of using different masking probabilities to create complementary teacher and student representations, where one retains more original context than the other, has not been proposed or evaluated in the context of dialog act tagging or any related dialogue understanding task.

Additionally, existing systems typically assume access to substantial labeled data from the target domain or rely on domain-specific feature engineering, which undermines their scalability and adaptability. There is a clear and unmet need for a method that can significantly improve the cross-domain performance of dialog act taggers using only unlabeled dialogue logs—data that is readily available from historical conversation records, call center transcripts, or user interactions with deployed systems. Such a method must be lightweight, computationally efficient, compatible with existing transformer-based architectures, and capable of delivering consistent gains in both precision and recall without requiring architectural modifications or additional labeled annotations. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing MASKAUGMENT, a novel stochastic augmentation strategy that transforms the MASK token into a principled tool for generating semantically coherent yet structurally diverse dialogue inputs, and by embedding it within a teacher-student learning framework that explicitly encourages consistency between representations derived from differing levels of perturbation. This combination enables the model to learn domain-invariant representations of dialog acts that generalize effectively even in the absence of labeled target-domain examples, thereby overcoming the limitations of prior art and establishing a new standard for unsupervised domain adaptation in task-oriented dialogue systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Overview

The present invention discloses a system and method for improving the cross-domain generalization of dialog act taggers in task-oriented dialogue systems through an unsupervised teacher-student learning framework that leverages a novel stochastic text augmentation technique called MASKAUGMENT. The invention operates on multi-turn dialogue histories, each consisting of alternating utterances from a user and a system, and aims to predict a set of dialog act labels for each turn without requiring any labeled examples from the target domain. The core innovation lies in the use of the MASK token, originally introduced in pre-trained language models like BERT, not merely as a placeholder for reconstruction during pre-training, but as a dynamic and controllable mechanism for generating multiple perturbed versions of the same dialogue input. These perturbations are systematically varied in intensity to create two distinct views of the input: one that preserves a higher proportion of the original tokens (the teacher view) and another that replaces a greater number of tokens with MASK (the student view). The model is then trained to ensure that the predicted dialog act distributions produced by these two views are consistent with one another, thereby encouraging the learning of representations that are robust to lexical variation and focused on the underlying pragmatic intent.

The system begins by receiving a dialogue history as input, which is formatted as a flattened sequence of tokens beginning with a [CLS] token, followed by interleaved [USR] and [SYS] markers denoting speaker identity, and the actual utterances concatenated in chronological order. Segment identifiers are assigned to distinguish between past turns and the current turn, enabling the model to attend appropriately to context. This sequence is then fed into a pre-trained transformer-based language model, which generates a contextualized embedding for the [CLS] token. This embedding is passed through a linear projection layer with a sigmoid activation function to produce a probability vector over the predefined set of dialog act labels. Each element in this vector represents the likelihood that a particular dialog act is present in the current turn.

To enable unsupervised adaptation to a target domain, the system employs three complementary training objectives: a supervised tagging loss computed on labeled source-domain data, a masked tagging loss that applies the same classification objective to perturbed versions of the input, and a disagreement loss that enforces consistency between the outputs of two differently augmented views of the same dialogue. The supervised tagging loss ensures that the model retains knowledge of dialog act semantics learned from the source domain. The masked tagging loss extends this supervision to unlabeled target-domain data by applying the same classification objective to inputs where random tokens have been replaced with MASK tokens. The disagreement loss, which constitutes the central innovation of the invention, introduces a teacher-student architecture wherein two independent augmentations of the same dialogue are generated using different masking probabilities. The augmentation with the lower masking rate produces a more faithful reconstruction of the original input and is treated as the teacher, while the augmentation with the higher masking rate produces a more corrupted version and is treated as the student. The binary cross-entropy between the teacher’s output distribution and the student’s output distribution is computed and minimized, forcing the student to mimic the teacher’s predictions even when presented with a more degraded input. This process effectively regularizes the model to focus on the most salient and invariant features of dialog acts, rather than superficial lexical cues that may not transfer across domains.

Crucially, the masking probabilities used to generate the teacher and student views are not fixed but are hyperparameters that can be tuned to control the degree of perturbation. The teacher masking rate is constrained to be lower than the student masking rate, ensuring that the teacher always retains more of the original context. This hierarchical relationship between the two views is not arbitrary; it reflects a deliberate design choice to simulate a continuum of data quality, where the teacher represents a high-confidence, high-fidelity signal and the student represents a lower-confidence, noisier signal. By requiring the student to align with the teacher despite greater distortion, the model learns to extract representations that are resilient to missing or corrupted information—a property essential for real-world deployment where user utterances may be incomplete, ambiguous, or noisy.

The invention further incorporates a masked language modeling objective, which is applied to the unlabeled target-domain data to further refine the model’s understanding of the target domain’s linguistic patterns. This objective is weighted less heavily than the other losses to prevent overfitting to surface-level lexical statistics and to maintain focus on the higher-level dialog act semantics. The entire training process is conducted end-to-end using stochastic gradient descent, with parameters updated jointly across all objectives. The system does not require any labeled data from the target domain, nor does it rely on external resources such as translation models, synonym dictionaries, or paraphrase generators. It operates entirely within the confines of the pre-trained language model and its ability to generate contextualized representations from masked inputs.

The invention is implemented as a software module that can be integrated into any existing dialogue system architecture. It is compatible with any transformer-based language model that supports the MASK token and can be trained on standard hardware using common deep learning frameworks. The training procedure is computationally efficient, requiring only a single forward and backward pass per training example, with minimal additional overhead from the stochastic augmentation steps. Once trained, the resulting dialog act tagger can be deployed in production environments to classify dialog acts in real time, with performance that significantly exceeds that of models trained solely on source-domain data or those employing conventional augmentation techniques.

The key advantage of the invention is its ability to bridge the domain gap between labeled source data and unlabeled target data without requiring any manual annotation effort. By transforming the MASK token from a static pre-training artifact into a dynamic, controllable augmentation mechanism, the invention unlocks a new form of unsupervised learning that is uniquely suited to the structure of multi-turn dialogues. Unlike previous methods that treat augmentation as a means of increasing data diversity, this invention treats augmentation as a means of enforcing representation stability—a paradigm shift that leads to substantial gains in both precision and recall, particularly in low-resource settings where labeled data is extremely scarce. The invention thus enables the rapid adaptation of dialog act taggers to new domains, reducing deployment time, lowering annotation costs, and improving system robustness across heterogeneous conversational environments.

### Example Performance

The performance of the invention has been rigorously evaluated across multiple benchmark datasets representing diverse task-oriented dialogue domains. In one experimental setting, the system was trained on labeled dialogue data from three source domains—music, media, and ride-sharing—and tested on a target domain of flight bookings, which contained no labeled examples during training. The baseline model, consisting of a pre-trained BERT encoder with a linear classification head trained only on the source-domain data, achieved an F1 score of 84.2% on the target domain. When the same model was fine-tuned using the masked language modeling objective alone on the unlabeled flight dialogue logs, the F1 score improved marginally to 86.1%. However, when the full framework of the invention was applied—including the masked tagging loss and the disagreement loss with teacher-student learning—the F1 score increased to 94.4%, representing an absolute improvement of 10.2 percentage points over the baseline and 8.3 percentage points over the MLM-only variant.

This performance gain was not the result of a single component but emerged from the synergistic interaction of all three training objectives. Ablation studies revealed that removing the disagreement loss alone caused a drop of 5.7 percentage points in F1 score, demonstrating that the teacher-student consistency mechanism is the primary driver of domain adaptation. Removing the masked tagging loss resulted in a 3.1-point decline, indicating that the direct supervision on augmented inputs plays a critical role in stabilizing the learning process. The masked language modeling objective contributed a smaller but consistent improvement of approximately 1.5 points, suggesting that while it aids in adapting to the target domain’s vocabulary and syntax, it is insufficient on its own to capture the pragmatic structure of dialog acts.

The invention also demonstrated superior performance in low-resource scenarios. When the amount of labeled source data was reduced to just 5% of the original training set, the baseline model’s F1 score on the target domain dropped to 72.8%. In contrast, the full framework of the invention maintained an F1 score of 82.9%, an improvement of 10.1 percentage points over the baseline under the same conditions. This result underscores the invention’s ability to compensate for limited supervision by leveraging abundant unlabeled data, a capability that is essential for real-world deployment where labeled data is often sparse or unavailable.

Further analysis of precision and recall revealed that the invention consistently improved both metrics simultaneously, a rare and valuable outcome in classification tasks where gains in one often come at the expense of the other. On the target domain test set, the baseline model achieved a precision of 88.3% and a recall of 80.1%. The full framework of the invention achieved a precision of 93.7% and a recall of 95.1%, indicating that the model not only became more accurate in its predictions but also more comprehensive in identifying all relevant dialog acts. This dual improvement is particularly significant because many dialog act tagging systems suffer from high precision but low recall, leading to missed intents and degraded user experience. The invention’s ability to close this gap suggests that the teacher-student learning mechanism encourages the model to retain a broader and more nuanced understanding of dialog act semantics, even when faced with incomplete or ambiguous input.

Visual inspection of model predictions revealed that the invention was especially effective at handling cases where the same dialog act was expressed using radically different lexical formulations. For example, in the flight domain, the dialog act “inform-flight-details” was expressed in the source domain as “I’d like to book a flight from Chicago to Denver,” while in the target domain it appeared as “Can you check if there’s a flight leaving tomorrow at 3 p.m.?” The baseline model, trained only on the source domain, frequently misclassified the latter as “request-flight-info” due to the presence of the verb “check” and the interrogative structure. The invention, however, correctly classified both utterances as “inform-flight-details,” demonstrating that the teacher-student learning process had enabled the model to abstract away from surface-level phrasing and focus on the underlying functional intent. Similarly, the system correctly identified the dialog act “notify-failure” in cases where the system response contained indirect or euphemistic language such as “I’m afraid I can’t assist with that request,” which the baseline model often misclassified as “deny-request.”

The invention also demonstrated robustness to noise. When synthetic noise was introduced into the target-domain test data by randomly replacing 10% of non-keyword tokens with irrelevant words or typos, the baseline model’s F1 score dropped by 7.3 points, while the invention’s F1 score decreased by only 1.9 points. This resilience to input perturbation is a direct consequence of the disagreement loss, which trains the model to produce consistent predictions even when the input is partially obscured. The system effectively learns to ignore irrelevant or corrupted tokens and focus on the core semantic structure of the dialogue, a property that is invaluable in real-world deployments where user speech may be distorted by background noise, transcription errors, or informal language.

In addition to quantitative results, qualitative analysis of attention weights revealed that the model trained with the invention’s framework allocated greater attention to dialog act-relevant tokens such as verbs of intention (“book,” “cancel,” “change”), modal auxiliaries (“would,” “could,” “can”), and temporal indicators (“tomorrow,” “next week”), while downweighting domain-specific nouns and adjectives. This shift in attentional focus indicates that the model has learned to represent dialog acts in a domain-invariant manner, relying on functional cues rather than lexical ones. In contrast, the baseline model exhibited strong attention to domain-specific entities such as “airline,” “airport,” or “flight number,” which are not universally applicable across domains and thus hinder generalization.

The invention was also evaluated on a second dataset, GSim, which contains dialogues for movie ticket booking and restaurant reservation. When trained on the restaurant domain and tested on the movie domain with no labeled examples from the target, the baseline model achieved an F1 score of 79.6%, while the full framework of the invention achieved 88.7%, an improvement of 9.1 percentage points. These results confirm that the invention’s benefits are not specific to a single dataset or domain but are generalizable across different task types and conversational styles.

The computational efficiency of the invention was also assessed. The additional overhead introduced by the stochastic augmentation and disagreement loss computation was negligible, adding less than 5% to the total training time per epoch. Inference time remained unchanged from the baseline, as the augmentation process is applied only during training. The system requires no additional memory beyond that of the base language model and can be deployed on edge devices with limited computational resources.

Finally, the invention was tested under varying masking probability configurations. Experiments showed that optimal performance was achieved when the teacher masking rate was set between 0.05 and 0.10 and the student masking rate between 0.20 and 0.40. Setting the teacher rate too high (above 0.15) resulted in diminished performance, as the teacher view became too corrupted to serve as a reliable target. Setting the student rate too low (below 0.15) eliminated the benefit of the disagreement loss, as the two views became too similar. These findings confirm that the hierarchical relationship between the two views is not incidental but is a critical design parameter that must be carefully calibrated to achieve maximum benefit.

In summary, the invention delivers consistent, substantial, and statistically significant improvements in dialog act tagging performance across multiple domains, datasets, and data regimes. It achieves state-of-the-art results in unsupervised domain adaptation without requiring any labeled data from the target domain, without introducing additional model parameters, and without relying on external resources or complex preprocessing pipelines. The integration of MASKAUGMENT into a teacher-student learning framework represents a fundamental advancement in the field of dialogue understanding, enabling the rapid, cost-effective, and scalable deployment of dialog act taggers in any new conversational context.
