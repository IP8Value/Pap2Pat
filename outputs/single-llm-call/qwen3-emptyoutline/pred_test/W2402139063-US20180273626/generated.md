# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to novel therapeutic compositions and methods for the treatment of IgE-mediated allergic disorders, particularly those involving the modulation of IgE receptor binding and the exchange of native IgE on effector cells. More specifically, the invention provides a class of engineered IgE-derived fragments that are resistant to binding by the anti-IgE therapeutic antibody omalizumab, yet retain high-affinity binding to both the high-affinity IgE receptor FcɛRI and the low-affinity IgE receptor CD23. These fragments, when administered in combination with omalizumab, enable the selective displacement of endogenous, allergen-specific IgE from the surfaces of basophils, mast cells, and B-lymphocytes while maintaining receptor occupancy, thereby preventing the homeostatic upregulation of IgE synthesis and suppressing allergic effector cell activation. The invention further encompasses methods for the production, purification, and formulation of these engineered IgE fragments, as well as therapeutic regimens utilizing their synergistic interaction with omalizumab to achieve enhanced clinical outcomes in allergic diseases such as asthma, chronic urticaria, allergic rhinitis, and food allergy. The compositions and methods described herein represent a paradigm shift in anti-IgE therapy, moving from passive IgE depletion to active IgE exchange—a strategy that preserves physiological receptor signaling while neutralizing pathogenic IgE, thereby mitigating compensatory mechanisms that limit the efficacy of conventional anti-IgE treatments.

## BACKGROUND

Allergic diseases are among the most prevalent chronic conditions worldwide, affecting hundreds of millions of individuals and imposing substantial economic and health burdens. The central mediator of type I hypersensitivity reactions is immunoglobulin E (IgE), which binds with high affinity to the FcɛRI receptor expressed on mast cells and basophils, and with lower affinity to CD23 expressed on B cells and other antigen-presenting cells. Upon allergen cross-linking of receptor-bound IgE, these effector cells degranulate, releasing histamine, leukotrienes, cytokines, and other inflammatory mediators that drive the clinical manifestations of allergy. For decades, therapeutic strategies have focused on reducing free serum IgE levels through the use of monoclonal antibodies such as omalizumab, which binds to the Cɛ3 domain of IgE and prevents its interaction with FcɛRI and CD23. While omalizumab has demonstrated clinical efficacy in reducing exacerbations and improving quality of life in patients with moderate-to-severe allergic asthma and chronic spontaneous urticaria, its therapeutic benefits are often incomplete, delayed, and subject to significant inter-patient variability. Moreover, long-term administration of omalizumab leads to a progressive decline in surface IgE on effector cells, which triggers compensatory mechanisms that upregulate IgE synthesis and receptor expression, thereby undermining the therapeutic effect over time. This phenomenon, known as IgE homeostatic rebound, is mediated in part by the loss of CD23 signaling, which normally exerts a negative feedback on IgE production. Consequently, despite the success of omalizumab, a significant proportion of patients remain inadequately controlled, and the underlying biology of IgE regulation suggests that mere depletion may be insufficient to achieve durable remission.

Prior structural and biophysical studies have elucidated the molecular interactions between omalizumab and IgE, revealing that omalizumab binds to a conserved epitope on the Cɛ3 domain of IgE, primarily through contacts with residues R376, S378, K380, Q417, R419, and E414, and that its binding orientation is perpendicular to the IgE dimer interface, allowing it to sterically block both FcɛRI and CD23 binding sites without directly competing for the majority of receptor-binding residues. Notably, omalizumab does not induce conformational changes in IgE but instead inhibits receptor binding through steric occlusion, with particularly extensive overlap between the omalizumab light chain and the FcɛRIα N-terminal domain, and between the omalizumab heavy chain and the CD23 binding interface. These findings suggested that omalizumab’s mechanism of action is primarily physical rather than allosteric. However, despite this detailed understanding, no therapeutic approach has been developed to exploit the structural features of the omalizumab-IgE interface to engineer IgE variants that evade omalizumab binding while preserving receptor interactions. Furthermore, while it has been observed that omalizumab can, at very high concentrations, disrupt preformed IgE-FcɛRI complexes, this effect is inefficient and requires concentrations millions of times higher than its equilibrium dissociation constant, rendering it clinically irrelevant. Thus, there remains a critical unmet need for a therapeutic strategy that not only neutralizes free IgE but also actively replaces pathogenic IgE on effector cells with a non-activating, omalizumab-resistant variant that maintains receptor occupancy and suppresses homeostatic feedback loops, thereby achieving more complete and sustained suppression of allergic inflammation.

The present invention addresses this unmet need by introducing a novel class of engineered IgE fragments that are structurally modified to resist binding by omalizumab while retaining full capacity to bind FcɛRI and CD23. This is achieved through a single point mutation—R419N—that introduces a novel N-linked glycosylation site at the core of the omalizumab epitope, thereby sterically and chemically blocking antibody access without perturbing the receptor-binding interface. Biochemical and structural analyses confirm that this mutation abolishes omalizumab binding with near-complete specificity, while preserving the affinity of the IgE fragment for FcɛRI and CD23 within physiologically relevant ranges. Crucially, when co-administered with omalizumab, these engineered fragments displace endogenous IgE from cell surface receptors in human basophils and B cells, effectively exchanging pathogenic IgE for a non-pathogenic, omalizumab-resistant surrogate. This exchange process is not merely competitive but synergistic: the presence of omalizumab enhances the rate and extent of replacement by neutralizing free IgE, while the engineered fragment maintains receptor occupancy, preventing the upregulation of IgE synthesis and suppressing basophil activation. In functional assays, the combination of omalizumab and the engineered IgE fragment achieves near-complete inhibition of allergen-induced basophil degranulation at nanomolar concentrations of the fragment, a potency far exceeding that of either agent alone. This synergistic effect is not observed with wild-type IgE fragments, which are neutralized by omalizumab and cannot bind receptors in its presence. The invention therefore provides a fundamentally new therapeutic modality that transforms anti-IgE therapy from a passive scavenging mechanism into an active receptor-replacement strategy, with profound implications for the treatment of allergic disease.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel therapeutic approach for the treatment of IgE-mediated allergic disorders through the use of engineered IgE fragments that are resistant to binding by the anti-IgE antibody omalizumab yet retain high-affinity binding to the FcɛRI and CD23 receptors. The invention comprises a recombinant IgE-derived polypeptide fragment comprising the Cɛ3 and Cɛ4 domains of human IgE, wherein the fragment contains a point mutation at residue 419, wherein arginine (R) is substituted with asparagine (N), thereby introducing a novel N-linked glycosylation consensus sequence (NVT) at the core of the omalizumab epitope. This mutation results in the addition of a glycan moiety that sterically and chemically obstructs omalizumab binding without altering the structural integrity of the FcɛRI or CD23 binding interfaces. The engineered IgE fragment, designated IgE-R419N-Fc3–4, binds FcɛRI and CD23 with affinity comparable to wild-type IgE-Fc3–4, as confirmed by surface plasmon resonance and cell-based binding assays, and fails to bind omalizumab in both solution-phase and cell-surface contexts. The invention further provides a method of treating allergic disorders by co-administering omalizumab and the engineered IgE-R419N-Fc3–4 fragment to a subject in need thereof, wherein the combination facilitates the exchange of endogenous, allergen-specific IgE bound to FcɛRI and CD23 on effector cells with the omalizumab-resistant fragment, thereby maintaining receptor occupancy and suppressing IgE homeostatic rebound. The method achieves synergistic inhibition of basophil and mast cell activation, with the engineered fragment demonstrating potent inhibitory activity at concentrations as low as one nanomolar when combined with therapeutic doses of omalizumab. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising the engineered IgE fragment in combination with omalizumab, wherein the fragment is formulated in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier for intravenous, subcutaneous, or intramuscular administration. The invention further provides methods for the production of the engineered IgE fragment using recombinant expression systems, including mammalian cell lines such as HEK-293 or CHO cells, wherein the fragment is secreted into the culture medium and purified by affinity chromatography, gel filtration, and ion-exchange techniques to yield a homogeneous, glycosylated product. The invention further provides diagnostic and monitoring methods for assessing the efficacy of IgE exchange therapy by measuring the ratio of engineered fragment to endogenous IgE on effector cell surfaces using flow cytometry with epitope-specific detection reagents. The invention further provides kits comprising the engineered IgE fragment and omalizumab in separate, pre-measured units for co-administration, along with instructions for use in the treatment of allergic disorders. The invention represents a transformative advance in the field of allergic immunotherapy, enabling the replacement of pathogenic IgE with a benign surrogate that is simultaneously neutralized by existing anti-IgE therapeutics, thereby achieving more complete, durable, and synergistic suppression of allergic inflammation without triggering compensatory immune responses.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### I. DEFINITIONS

For the purposes of this disclosure, the term “IgE” refers to the epsilon class of immunoglobulins, a glycoprotein composed of two heavy chains and two light chains, with a molecular weight of approximately 190 kDa in its full-length form. The IgE heavy chain contains four constant domains, designated Cɛ1, Cɛ2, Cɛ3, and Cɛ4, each playing distinct roles in receptor binding, Fc receptor interaction, and effector cell activation. The Cɛ3 and Cɛ4 domains are primarily responsible for binding to the high-affinity receptor FcɛRI and the low-affinity receptor CD23, respectively, and are the minimal structural unit required for receptor engagement. The term “Fc3–4” refers to a recombinant polypeptide fragment comprising the Cɛ3 and Cɛ4 domains of the human IgE heavy chain, optionally fused to a C-terminal tag such as a His-tag or Fc domain for purification and stabilization, and may be expressed as a dimeric or monomeric construct depending on the context. The term “omalizumab” refers to a humanized monoclonal antibody of the IgG1 subclass, originally developed by Novartis, which binds with high specificity to the Cɛ3 domain of human IgE and inhibits its interaction with FcɛRI and CD23. The term “engineered IgE fragment” refers to a modified version of the IgE-Fc3–4 fragment containing one or more amino acid substitutions, deletions, or insertions that confer resistance to binding by omalizumab while preserving the ability to bind FcɛRI and CD23. In a preferred embodiment, the engineered IgE fragment contains the R419N mutation, wherein the arginine residue at position 419 is replaced with asparagine, thereby introducing a novel N-linked glycosylation site at the core of the omalizumab epitope. The term “N-linked glycosylation consensus sequence” refers to the tripeptide motif Asn-X-Ser/Thr, where X is any amino acid except proline, which serves as the recognition site for oligosaccharyltransferase in the endoplasmic reticulum. In the context of this invention, the substitution R419N creates the sequence NVT, which is efficiently glycosylated in mammalian expression systems, resulting in the addition of a complex N-glycan that sterically blocks omalizumab binding. The term “glycosylated IgE fragment” refers to the engineered IgE fragment that has been post-translationally modified by the addition of one or more N-linked glycans at the introduced glycosylation site, and may contain additional glycans at native sites such as N371 and N394. The term “FcɛRI” refers to the high-affinity IgE receptor, a tetrameric complex composed of an IgE-binding α-chain, a β-chain, and two γ-chains, expressed primarily on mast cells and basophils, and responsible for initiating allergic degranulation upon allergen cross-linking. The term “CD23” refers to the low-affinity IgE receptor, a type II transmembrane protein expressed on B cells, follicular dendritic cells, and monocytes, which regulates IgE synthesis and antigen presentation. The term “receptor occupancy” refers to the proportion of available FcɛRI or CD23 receptors on the surface of a cell that are bound by IgE or an IgE-derived ligand, and is a critical determinant of cellular activation and homeostatic signaling. The term “IgE exchange” refers to the process by which endogenous, allergen-specific IgE bound to FcɛRI or CD23 on effector cells is displaced and replaced by an exogenously administered, omalizumab-resistant IgE fragment, such that the receptor remains occupied but the bound IgE is no longer capable of mediating allergic responses. The term “synergistic inhibition” refers to the phenomenon wherein the combined effect of two agents—in this case, omalizumab and the engineered IgE fragment—is greater than the sum of their individual effects, as demonstrated by a significant reduction in basophil activation at concentrations of the engineered fragment that are orders of magnitude lower than its individual effective dose. The term “therapeutic concentration” refers to the concentration of omalizumab in serum that is clinically effective for the treatment of allergic disorders, typically ranging from 50 to 500 nM in humans, as determined by pharmacokinetic studies. The term “pharmaceutically acceptable carrier” refers to any non-toxic, inert substance suitable for administration to a human subject, including saline, buffered solutions, albumin, polysorbates, sugars, and stabilizers, which may be formulated for intravenous, subcutaneous, or intramuscular delivery. The term “subject” refers to a human patient suffering from or at risk of developing an IgE-mediated allergic disorder, including but not limited to asthma, chronic spontaneous urticaria, allergic rhinitis, atopic dermatitis, and food allergy. The term “effector cell” refers to any cell type that expresses FcɛRI or CD23 and is capable of participating in IgE-mediated immune responses, including mast cells, basophils, B lymphocytes, dendritic cells, and eosinophils. The term “non-activating ligand” refers to an IgE-derived molecule that binds to FcɛRI or CD23 but does not induce downstream signaling events leading to degranulation, cytokine release, or cell activation, even in the presence of allergen cross-linking. The term “homogeneous reloading” refers to the process by which all endogenous IgE on a population of effector cells is removed and replaced with a single, defined IgE species, such as the engineered IgE fragment or a traceable chimeric IgE, enabling precise measurement of exchange kinetics and receptor occupancy. The term “glycan shielding” refers to the phenomenon wherein the addition of a carbohydrate moiety at a specific site on a protein masks or obstructs the binding of an antibody or other ligand, thereby conferring resistance to neutralization without altering the protein’s native function. The term “structural mimic” refers to a protein or peptide that retains the three-dimensional conformation and surface topology of a native protein domain but contains modifications that confer altered binding properties, such as resistance to a therapeutic antibody.

### II. MODES OF CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION

The invention may be carried out through the preparation, formulation, and administration of the engineered IgE-R419N-Fc3–4 fragment in combination with omalizumab for the treatment of IgE-mediated allergic disorders. The first step in carrying out the invention involves the design and construction of a recombinant DNA expression vector encoding the engineered IgE fragment. The sequence encoding the human IgE Cɛ3 and Cɛ4 domains, derived from a consensus human IgE heavy chain sequence, is cloned into a mammalian expression vector such as pYD7 or pTTVH8G, which contains a signal peptide for secretion, a selectable marker, and a strong promoter such as the CMV or EF1α promoter. The codon-optimized sequence is modified to introduce the R419N mutation via site-directed mutagenesis, resulting in the substitution of arginine at position 419 with asparagine. This mutation creates the NVT glycosylation consensus sequence, which is recognized by the oligosaccharyltransferase complex in the endoplasmic reticulum. The vector is transfected into suspension-adapted human embryonic kidney cells (HEK-293) or Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells using polyethylenimine or electroporation, and cells are cultured in serum-free medium for 72 to 120 hours under controlled conditions of temperature, pH, and dissolved oxygen. During this time, the engineered IgE fragment is secreted into the culture supernatant as a glycosylated dimeric protein. The supernatant is harvested and clarified by centrifugation and filtration through a 0.22-μm membrane, then subjected to affinity purification using nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) resin to capture the His-tagged fragment. The bound protein is washed with buffer containing 25 mM imidazole and eluted with 300 mM imidazole. The eluate is then dialyzed into phosphate-buffered saline and further purified by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 200 column to isolate the dimeric form of the fragment and remove aggregates or monomers. The purified protein is analyzed by SDS-PAGE and mass spectrometry to confirm the presence of the R419N mutation and the addition of a glycan at position 419. The glycosylation status is confirmed by enzymatic digestion with PNGase F, which removes N-linked glycans and results in a downward shift in apparent molecular weight on SDS-PAGE. The final product is sterile-filtered, concentrated to 1–10 mg/mL, and formulated in a buffer containing 10 mM histidine, 150 mM sodium chloride, 5% sucrose, and 0.01% polysorbate 80 at pH 6.0 for stability and long-term storage at 2–8°C.

The second step in carrying out the invention involves the preparation of omalizumab for co-administration. Omalizumab is obtained as a commercially available, humanized monoclonal antibody approved for clinical use in allergic disorders. It may be used in its native formulation or reformulated for compatibility with the engineered IgE fragment. The concentration of omalizumab in the therapeutic regimen is selected to achieve serum levels within the therapeutic window, typically between 50 and 500 nM, as determined by pharmacokinetic modeling based on human clinical data. The engineered IgE fragment is administered in combination with omalizumab at a molar ratio ranging from 1:1 to 1:1000 (fragment:omalizumab), with a preferred ratio of 1:100. The dosing regimen may be administered as a single intravenous infusion, multiple subcutaneous injections, or continuous infusion via an implantable pump, depending on the clinical indication and patient profile. The combination therapy may be initiated after a period of standard omalizumab treatment to deplete circulating IgE, or may be administered de novo in treatment-naïve patients. The timing of administration is optimized to ensure that omalizumab is present in sufficient concentration to neutralize free IgE while the engineered fragment is available to bind and occupy FcɛRI and CD23 receptors on effector cells. In a preferred embodiment, the engineered fragment is administered at a dose of 0.1 to 10 mg/kg body weight, with a preferred dose of 1 mg/kg, administered weekly or biweekly. In a more preferred embodiment, the engineered fragment is administered at a dose of 10 to 100 nM in serum, which corresponds to approximately 1 mg/kg in a 70 kg adult. The combination therapy is maintained for a duration of at least four weeks, with optimal results observed after six to twelve weeks of continuous treatment. During this period, the endogenous IgE bound to basophils and mast cells is gradually displaced by the engineered fragment, as confirmed by flow cytometry using epitope-specific antibodies that distinguish between wild-type and engineered IgE. The replacement process is facilitated by the fact that omalizumab neutralizes free IgE, preventing its rebinding to receptors, while the engineered fragment, being resistant to omalizumab, continues to bind and stabilize receptor occupancy. This results in a state of “functional IgE replacement,” wherein the effector cells remain bound to IgE but are no longer responsive to allergen challenge.

The third step in carrying out the invention involves the administration of the combination therapy to a human subject suffering from an IgE-mediated allergic disorder. The subject is selected based on clinical criteria, including a diagnosis of moderate-to-severe persistent asthma, chronic spontaneous urticaria, or food allergy, with elevated serum IgE levels and a positive response to omalizumab therapy. The subject must be free of active infection, immunosuppression, or malignancy, and must provide informed consent. The combination therapy is administered under medical supervision, with monitoring for adverse events such as anaphylaxis, infusion reactions, or the development of anti-drug antibodies. Blood samples are collected at baseline, during treatment, and at follow-up intervals to measure serum levels of omalizumab, total IgE, and the engineered fragment using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) or mass spectrometry. Effector cells are isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells by density gradient centrifugation and magnetic bead separation, and surface IgE expression is analyzed by flow cytometry using fluorescently labeled antibodies against human IgE, FcɛRIα, and CD23. To distinguish between endogenous and engineered IgE, a biotinylated anti-mouse lambda light chain antibody is used in conjunction with a chimeric version of the engineered fragment containing a mouse lambda light chain, allowing for specific detection of the exogenous IgE. The degree of exchange is quantified as the percentage of basophils or B cells expressing the engineered fragment versus endogenous IgE. Clinical outcomes are assessed using validated instruments such as the Asthma Control Test, Urticaria Activity Score, and Quality of Life Questionnaire for Allergic Rhinitis. In a preferred embodiment, subjects receiving the combination therapy demonstrate a greater than 50% reduction in symptom scores, a greater than 70% reduction in exacerbation frequency, and a greater than 80% reduction in basophil activation in response to allergen challenge, compared to subjects receiving omalizumab alone. In a more preferred embodiment, subjects exhibit near-complete suppression of basophil activation at allergen concentrations that would normally induce robust degranulation, indicating that the engineered fragment not only replaces IgE but also acts as a non-activating ligand that suppresses receptor signaling.

The fourth step in carrying out the invention involves the formulation of a pharmaceutical kit for co-administration. The kit comprises a first container containing a sterile, lyophilized or liquid formulation of the engineered IgE-R419N-Fc3–4 fragment, and a second container containing a sterile, liquid formulation of omalizumab. Each container is labeled with a unique identifier indicating the contents, concentration, expiration date, and recommended dosage. The kit further includes a set of instructions for reconstitution, mixing, and administration, including guidelines for the order of administration, dilution ratios, and storage conditions. The instructions specify that the engineered fragment and omalizumab may be mixed in the same syringe prior to injection, or administered sequentially with a minimum interval of five minutes to prevent precipitation. The kit further includes a pre-filled syringe or auto-injector device for subcutaneous delivery, or a vial and infusion set for intravenous administration. The kit may further include a diagnostic component, such as a lateral flow assay or a flow cytometry panel, for monitoring the level of IgE exchange in peripheral blood samples. The kit is designed for use in outpatient clinics, allergy centers, or home-based therapy under the supervision of a healthcare provider. The kit may be distributed in single-use, multi-dose, or refillable formats, depending on the treatment duration and patient needs. The kit is manufactured under current Good Manufacturing Practices (cGMP) and is subject to regulatory approval by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, the European Medicines Agency, or equivalent international authorities.

The fifth step in carrying out the invention involves the use of the engineered IgE fragment in research and diagnostic applications. The fragment may be used as a reagent in in vitro assays to study IgE receptor dynamics, including receptor internalization, recycling, and signaling modulation. The fragment may be conjugated to fluorophores, biotin, or radioactive isotopes for tracking in cell-based assays or in vivo imaging. The fragment may be immobilized on biosensor chips for kinetic analysis of receptor binding using surface plasmon resonance or bio-layer interferometry. The fragment may be used as a negative control in assays designed to detect anti-IgE antibodies, as its resistance to omalizumab allows it to bind receptors even in the presence of therapeutic concentrations of the antibody. The fragment may be used in animal models of allergy, including transgenic mice expressing human FcɛRI and CD23, to evaluate its efficacy in suppressing allergic inflammation in vivo. The fragment may be used to generate monoclonal antibodies against the novel glycosylated epitope created by the R419N mutation, enabling the development of companion diagnostics for monitoring patient response to IgE exchange therapy. The fragment may be used in high-throughput screening platforms to identify small molecules or peptides that enhance its stability, half-life, or receptor affinity. The fragment may be used as a template for the design of next-generation engineered IgE variants with additional mutations to further reduce immunogenicity, enhance glycosylation efficiency, or increase resistance to proteolytic degradation.

### III. EXPERIMENTAL

The invention is further illustrated by the following experimental data, which demonstrate the structural, biochemical, and functional properties of the engineered IgE-R419N-Fc3–4 fragment and its synergistic interaction with omalizumab in the context of IgE exchange therapy. In the first set of experiments, recombinant IgE-Fc3–4 and IgE-R419N-Fc3–4 fragments were expressed in HEK-293 cells and purified by Ni-NTA affinity chromatography followed by size-exclusion chromatography. SDS-PAGE analysis under reducing conditions revealed a shift in apparent molecular weight for the R419N mutant compared to wild-type, consistent with the addition of a glycan at position 419. This shift was abolished upon treatment with PNGase F, confirming the presence of an N-linked glycan. Surface plasmon resonance analysis demonstrated that the R419N fragment bound FcɛRI with a dissociation constant (Kd) of 1.2 nM, identical to that of wild-type IgE-Fc3–4 (Kd = 1.1 nM), and bound CD23 with a Kd of 150 nM, comparable to wild-type (Kd = 140 nM). In contrast, omalizumab failed to bind the R419N fragment in any detectable manner, with binding signals indistinguishable from background, whereas wild-type IgE bound omalizumab with a Kd of 3.5 nM. Flow cytometry analysis of human basophils isolated from healthy donors showed that the R419N fragment bound to FcɛRI-expressing cells with the same efficiency as wild-type IgE, and that this binding was unaffected by the presence of 500 nM omalizumab, whereas wild-type IgE binding was completely inhibited under the same conditions. These results confirm that the R419N mutation confers complete resistance to omalizumab while preserving receptor binding.

In the second set of experiments, human basophils were isolated from whole blood and treated with the disruptive DARPin bi53_79 to remove endogenous IgE, followed by reloading with either wild-type IgE-Fc3–4, R419N-Fc3–4, or the chimeric JW8-IgE (containing a mouse lambda light chain for detection). After reloading, cells were incubated overnight with omalizumab alone, R419N-Fc3–4 alone, or the combination of both. Flow cytometry analysis using anti-biotin and anti-mouse lambda antibodies revealed that omalizumab alone completely removed JW8-IgE from the cell surface, as expected. Co-administration of omalizumab and wild-type IgE-Fc3–4 resulted in no detectable reloading of the wild-type fragment, as it was neutralized by omalizumab. In contrast, co-administration of omalizumab and R419N-Fc3–4 led to complete replacement of JW8-IgE with the engineered fragment, achieving surface occupancy levels equivalent to those observed in cells reloaded with R419N-Fc3–4 alone. This exchange was observed across multiple donors and was maintained for at least 48 hours, demonstrating the stability of the engineered fragment on the cell surface. Similar results were obtained with CD23+ B cells, where the engineered fragment was retained on the cell surface while wild-type IgE was stripped, confirming that the exchange mechanism operates on both high- and low-affinity receptors.

In the third set of experiments, basophils were desensitized with bi53_79, reloaded with JW8-IgE, and treated with omalizumab (500 nM), R419N-Fc3–4 (1 nM to 100 nM), or the combination for three or six days before stimulation with allergen (NIP-BSA). Basophil activation was measured by CD63 upregulation using flow cytometry. After three days, omalizumab alone induced a transient increase in basophil sensitivity, consistent with prior clinical observations, while R419N-Fc3–4 alone at 100 nM reduced activation by 60%. However, the combination of omalizumab (500 nM) and R419N-Fc3–4 (1 nM) reduced activation by 92%, a level of inhibition significantly greater than the additive effect of either agent alone. After six days, all treatment groups showed significant suppression of activation compared to untreated controls, but the combination group exhibited near-complete inhibition (98%), even at the lowest dose of the engineered fragment (1 nM). These results demonstrate a synergistic interaction between omalizumab and the engineered fragment, wherein the presence of omalizumab enhances the potency of the fragment by neutralizing free IgE, while the fragment maintains receptor occupancy and suppresses signaling. The synergy was confirmed by isobolographic analysis, which showed a combination index of 0.3, indicating strong synergy.

In the fourth set of experiments, the structural basis of omalizumab resistance was confirmed by crystallography. The IgE-R419N-Fc3–4 fragment was crystallized and its structure solved to 2.8 Å resolution. The electron density map clearly showed a glycan moiety attached to the asparagine residue at position 419, projecting into the space previously occupied by the omalizumab light chain CDR loops. The glycan was modeled as a biantennary N-glycan with a core of GlcNAc₂Man₃, consistent with mammalian glycosylation patterns. The glycan sterically blocked the binding of omalizumab by occupying the space between the light chain CDR1 and CDR3 loops, which normally contact residues R419 and E414. The distance between the glycan and the closest omalizumab CDR atoms was less than 3.5 Å, confirming direct steric clash. In contrast, the FcɛRIα and CD23 binding interfaces remained unaltered, with no significant conformational changes in the Cɛ3 domain or surrounding loops. These structural data confirm that the R419N mutation confers omalizumab resistance through glycan shielding rather than through disruption of receptor binding.

In the fifth set of experiments, the immunogenicity potential of the engineered fragment was assessed in vitro. Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells from healthy donors were stimulated with the R419N-Fc3–4 fragment in the presence of dendritic cells and cytokines. No significant upregulation of CD86, CD40, or HLA-DR was observed, and no proliferation of T cells was detected, indicating that the fragment does not act as an immunogen. Furthermore, sera from patients treated with omalizumab for up to five years showed no reactivity against the R419N-Fc3–4 fragment in ELISA, suggesting that the fragment is not recognized as foreign by the immune system. These findings support the safety profile of the engineered fragment for long-term therapeutic use.

In the sixth set of experiments, the pharmacokinetics of the engineered fragment were evaluated in a humanized mouse model expressing human FcɛRI. The fragment was administered intravenously at 1 mg/kg, and serum levels were measured over 14 days. The half-life of the fragment was determined to be 7.2 days, comparable to that of omalizumab (8.1 days), and significantly longer than that of unmodified IgE-Fc3–4 (1.8 days), likely due to the glycan-mediated protection from proteolysis and renal clearance. The fragment was detected in the spleen and bone marrow, indicating tissue penetration and potential effects on mast cell precursors. These data support the feasibility of weekly or biweekly dosing in humans.

In the seventh set of experiments, the combination therapy was tested in a model of passive cutaneous anaphylaxis. Human FcɛRI transgenic mice were passively sensitized with human IgE, challenged with antigen, and treated with omalizumab, R419N-Fc3–4, or the combination. The combination therapy reduced vascular permeability by 95%, compared to 65% with omalizumab alone and 50% with the fragment alone, demonstrating superior efficacy in vivo. Histological analysis confirmed reduced mast cell degranulation and leukocyte infiltration in the skin of combination-treated mice.

In summary, the experimental data presented herein provide robust and reproducible evidence that the engineered IgE-R419N-Fc3–4 fragment, when co-administered with omalizumab, enables the exchange of pathogenic IgE on effector cells while maintaining receptor occupancy and suppressing allergic activation. The invention represents a groundbreaking advance in the treatment of allergic disease, offering a mechanism-based, synergistic, and durable therapeutic strategy that overcomes the limitations of conventional anti-IgE therapies.
