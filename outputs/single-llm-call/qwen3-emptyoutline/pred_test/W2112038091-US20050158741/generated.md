# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The precise regulation of gene expression in mammalian systems has long been a cornerstone of molecular biology, developmental genetics, and therapeutic innovation. For decades, researchers have relied on transcriptional control mechanisms—such as tetracycline-inducible promoters, estrogen receptor-based systems, and lac operator-repressor interactions—to modulate the production of specific proteins in a temporally and spatially controlled manner. These approaches, while powerful, are inherently limited by their dependence on nuclear events, the kinetics of transcription initiation and elongation, and the stability of mRNA transcripts, all of which introduce delays and variability in the timing and magnitude of gene expression. Furthermore, transcriptional systems are often subject to position effects when integrated into the genome, epigenetic silencing, and interference from endogenous regulatory elements, all of which compromise their reliability and reproducibility in complex biological environments.

More recently, post-transcriptional strategies have emerged as complementary tools for gene regulation, most notably RNA interference (RNAi) and antisense oligonucleotides, which function by promoting the degradation or translational repression of target mRNAs. While these methods have significantly expanded the toolkit for gene silencing, they are largely unidirectional—capable of suppressing gene expression but not easily reversed without removing the silencing agent. Moreover, RNAi systems often suffer from off-target effects, saturation of endogenous RNA-processing machinery, and variable efficiency across cell types and tissues. The inability to achieve rapid, reversible, and dose-dependent induction of gene expression using RNA-targeting methods has remained a persistent challenge in the field.

In response to these limitations, a novel paradigm for gene regulation has been conceived—one that operates entirely at the level of RNA self-cleavage, independent of transcriptional machinery or RNA degradation pathways mediated by exogenous enzymes. This approach hinges on the strategic insertion of catalytic RNA sequences, known as ribozymes, into the 5′ or 3′ untranslated regions, or within the coding sequence of a gene of interest, such that the resulting mRNA transcript undergoes spontaneous cleavage upon synthesis, thereby preventing the accumulation of full-length, translatable mRNA. In the absence of any intervention, the ribozyme acts as a molecular scissors, ensuring that the encoded protein is not produced. However, if a small molecule capable of inhibiting the ribozyme’s catalytic activity is introduced, the cleavage event is blocked, allowing the mRNA to remain intact and proceed through translation, thereby inducing gene expression. This mechanism transforms the ribozyme from a constitutive repressor into a switchable gatekeeper of gene expression, responsive to exogenous chemical signals.

The feasibility of such a system depends critically on two fundamental requirements: first, the existence of ribozyme motifs that can fold correctly and catalyze efficient self-cleavage within the complex environment of a mammalian cell, where RNA is subject to secondary structure constraints, protein binding, and rapid turnover; and second, the availability of small molecules that can specifically and reversibly inhibit this cleavage activity without inducing cytotoxicity or interfering with endogenous RNA metabolism. Prior efforts to identify such ribozymes had been largely confined to in vitro studies using simplified reaction buffers, which failed to account for the intracellular milieu that profoundly influences RNA folding, stability, and catalytic efficiency. Consequently, many ribozymes that function robustly in test tubes were found to be inactive or only marginally active when embedded within mammalian transcripts.

Through systematic screening and rational engineering, a modified hammerhead ribozyme, designated N79, was developed from the naturally occurring hammerhead ribozyme of Schistosoma mansoni. This engineered variant exhibits extraordinary catalytic efficiency in mammalian cells, achieving near-complete cleavage of its host mRNA transcript under basal conditions. The N79 ribozyme retains its catalytic core while incorporating stabilizing mutations that enhance its structural integrity and resistance to cellular nucleases, enabling it to function reliably across a broad spectrum of cell types—including primary cells, stem cells, and immortalized lines—without requiring exogenous cofactors or auxiliary proteins. Its activity is so potent that even low-level transcription of an N79-containing transcript results in negligible protein output, making it an ideal candidate for a “default-off” gene regulation system.

Equally critical to the development of this technology was the identification of small molecules capable of inhibiting N79-mediated cleavage within living cells. Initial attempts to repurpose known ribozyme inhibitors—particularly aminoglycoside antibiotics such as neomycin and paromomycin, which had been shown to bind and stabilize hammerhead ribozymes in vitro—yielded disappointing results. These compounds, despite their high affinity for RNA in purified systems, failed to inhibit cleavage in mammalian cells, likely due to poor cellular uptake, rapid efflux, or sequestration by intracellular components. This observation underscored the necessity of developing a cell-based screening platform capable of identifying compounds that function not merely in biochemical assays, but within the physiological context of the cell.

To this end, a stable human embryonic kidney cell line was engineered to express a luciferase reporter gene under the control of a strong constitutive promoter, with two tandem copies of the N79 ribozyme inserted downstream of the coding sequence. This construct ensured that, under basal conditions, the luciferase mRNA was cleaved immediately upon transcription, resulting in undetectable luminescence. Upon transfection with antisense morpholino oligonucleotides designed to block the ribozyme’s active site, luciferase expression was robustly induced, validating the system’s responsiveness to inhibition. This cell line, designated HEK79, became the foundation for a high-throughput screening campaign involving over fifty-eight thousand diverse small molecules, encompassing FDA-approved drugs, natural products, combinatorial libraries, and bioactive compound sets.

The screening effort yielded fifteen distinct small molecules capable of inducing luciferase expression in HEK79 cells, but not in control cells harboring catalytically inactive versions of the N79 ribozyme. Strikingly, none of the positive hits were aminoglycosides. Instead, the most potent inhibitors belonged to two classes of nucleoside analogs: adenosine derivatives bearing modifications at the 7-position of the purine ring, such as toyocamycin and tubercidin, and uridine derivatives with substitutions at the 5-position of the pyrimidine ring, including 5-fluorouridine and 5-fluorouracil. These compounds demonstrated dose-dependent induction of gene expression, with toyocamycin achieving up to 365-fold activation and 5-fluorouridine reaching 110-fold induction. Further mechanistic investigations revealed that these inhibitors do not act by direct binding to the ribozyme, as they failed to inhibit cleavage in cell-free transcription reactions. Instead, their inhibitory activity required their incorporation into nascent RNA transcripts during cellular RNA synthesis, where they replace adenine or uridine residues at critical positions within the ribozyme’s catalytic core. This incorporation disrupts the precise geometry required for self-cleavage, effectively “poisoning” the ribozyme’s function without altering its primary sequence.

This discovery has profound implications. It reveals that the ribozyme’s sensitivity to nucleoside analogs is not a mere biochemical curiosity but a functional vulnerability rooted in the structural architecture of its conserved catalytic domain. The fact that only adenosine and uridine analogs—yet not guanosine or cytidine analogs—exerted inhibitory effects suggests that the 7-position of adenine and the 5-position of uridine play indispensable roles in maintaining the tertiary interactions necessary for catalysis, particularly in the context of the N79 ribozyme’s unique loop-loop interactions. This insight not only explains the specificity of the inhibitors but also provides a structural blueprint for the rational design of future compounds.

Moreover, the synergistic effect observed when toyocamycin and 5-fluorouridine were administered together—resulting in induction levels exceeding 670-fold while avoiding the cytotoxic thresholds associated with either compound alone—demonstrates the potential for combinatorial regulation strategies that enhance efficacy while minimizing toxicity. This synergy arises because the two analogs incorporate into distinct, non-overlapping sites within the ribozyme, thereby cooperatively destabilizing its catalytic conformation without competing for the same nucleotide incorporation pathway.

The successful application of this system in vivo further validates its therapeutic potential. Using adeno-associated viral vectors to deliver the N79-regulated luciferase construct into the ears of nude mice, researchers demonstrated that a single intradermal injection of the two-compound cocktail could induce luciferase expression more than 37-fold within 48 hours, while leaving control constructs—containing inactive ribozymes—unaffected. This result confirms that the system functions with high fidelity in a living mammal, responding to pharmacological inputs with predictable, quantifiable, and localized gene induction.

The invention described herein thus represents a transformative advance in the field of gene regulation. It introduces a wholly new class of genetic switches that operate through RNA self-cleavage and its chemical inhibition, bypassing the limitations of transcriptional and RNAi-based systems. It provides a platform for reversible, dose-responsive, and tissue-specific control of gene expression that is compatible with viral delivery, small molecule administration, and long-term in vivo applications. Furthermore, it opens the door to novel diagnostic and therapeutic paradigms, such as the real-time monitoring of drug pharmacokinetics through reporter gene induction, or the targeted reactivation of silenced genes in disease states using non-toxic, clinically relevant compounds like 5-fluorouracil. The ability to modulate gene expression with such precision, using endogenous cellular machinery and widely available small molecules, establishes a foundation for next-generation gene therapies, synthetic biology circuits, and precision medicine platforms that were previously unattainable.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a novel and highly effective system for the chemical control of gene expression in mammalian cells through the modulation of RNA self-cleavage. The system comprises a genetically encoded ribozyme motif that, when incorporated into a transcript of a gene of interest, causes the spontaneous cleavage and degradation of that transcript, thereby preventing the production of the encoded protein. The ribozyme functions as a molecular switch that is constitutively active in the absence of a specific inhibitor, ensuring that gene expression remains silenced under basal conditions. Upon administration of a small molecule inhibitor capable of interfering with the ribozyme’s catalytic activity, the cleavage event is suppressed, allowing the full-length mRNA to persist, be translated, and produce the desired protein. This mechanism enables precise, reversible, and dose-dependent induction of gene expression without altering transcriptional regulation or relying on exogenous proteins.

The ribozyme employed in this invention is a modified hammerhead ribozyme, designated N79, which has been engineered for optimal function within mammalian cells. Unlike natural hammerhead ribozymes that exhibit poor activity in complex cellular environments, the N79 variant contains specific nucleotide substitutions that enhance its structural stability, folding efficiency, and catalytic rate under physiological conditions. It is capable of achieving near-complete self-cleavage of its host mRNA transcript within minutes of synthesis, resulting in undetectable levels of protein expression in the absence of an inhibitor. The N79 ribozyme is functional across a wide range of mammalian cell types, including immortalized lines, primary cells, stem cells, and neurons, and retains its activity when integrated into the genome or delivered via viral vectors. It is particularly effective when placed in the 5′ untranslated region, 3′ untranslated region, or within an intron of the gene of interest, provided that its catalytic core remains accessible and unobstructed by adjacent sequences.

The invention further encompasses a class of small molecule inhibitors that are capable of suppressing the self-cleavage activity of the N79 ribozyme in living mammalian cells. These inhibitors are not direct binders of the ribozyme but instead function as nucleoside analogs that are incorporated into nascent RNA transcripts during cellular transcription. Once incorporated, they disrupt the tertiary structure of the ribozyme by altering critical nucleotide positions essential for catalysis. The most potent inhibitors identified are adenosine analogs bearing modifications at the 7-position of the purine ring, such as toyocamycin, tubercidin, sangivamycin, and 8-azaadenosine, and uridine analogs bearing modifications at the 5-position of the pyrimidine ring, including 5-fluorouridine, 5-fluorouracil, and 5-bromouridine. These compounds are structurally similar to endogenous nucleosides and are efficiently recognized by cellular RNA polymerases, allowing them to be incorporated into RNA transcripts in place of adenine or uridine. Their inhibitory activity is strictly dependent on the presence of a ribose sugar with a 2′-hydroxyl group; analogs lacking this moiety, such as 2′-deoxy or 2′-O-methyl derivatives, are inactive. Guanosine and cytidine analogs, even those with analogous modifications, do not inhibit ribozyme cleavage, indicating a high degree of structural specificity.

The invention further includes methods for combining two or more of these inhibitors to achieve synergistic suppression of ribozyme activity. For example, the co-administration of toyocamycin and 5-fluorouridine results in a multiplicative increase in gene expression induction—up to 670-fold—while permitting the use of significantly lower concentrations of each compound than would be required individually. This combination strategy reduces the risk of cytotoxicity associated with high-dose monotherapy and expands the therapeutic window for clinical applications. The synergistic effect arises because the two analogs incorporate into distinct, non-overlapping sites within the ribozyme’s catalytic core, thereby cooperatively destabilizing the active conformation without competing for the same nucleotide incorporation pathway.

The invention further provides expression vectors and cell lines engineered to contain the N79 ribozyme in close proximity to a gene of interest, such that gene expression is repressed under basal conditions and inducible upon treatment with the specified inhibitors. These vectors can be designed for stable genomic integration or transient delivery and may include selectable markers, reporter genes, or regulatory elements to facilitate selection, monitoring, and optimization. The cell lines generated using these vectors are stably transfected or transduced and exhibit minimal background expression in the absence of inhibitor and robust, dose-responsive induction upon treatment. These cell lines serve as powerful tools for high-throughput screening of novel ribozyme inhibitors, for studying gene function in a controlled manner, and for testing the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of small molecule therapeutics.

The invention further encompasses methods for delivering nucleic acids encoding the N79 ribozyme and a gene of interest into mammalian cells, including viral vectors such as adeno-associated virus, lentivirus, and adenovirus, as well as non-viral methods such as lipid nanoparticles, electroporation, microinjection, and polymer-based transfection. These delivery systems enable the application of the invention in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo, including in animal models and human subjects. The system is particularly suited for localized delivery, such as intratumoral, intracerebral, intraocular, or intradermal administration, where spatial control of gene expression is critical.

The invention further provides methods for identifying novel small molecule inhibitors of the N79 ribozyme using cell-based high-throughput screening assays. These assays utilize reporter cell lines, such as the HEK79 line, in which luciferase or other detectable proteins are expressed only when ribozyme cleavage is inhibited. Compounds are screened at physiologically relevant concentrations in multi-well plate formats, and hits are validated based on their ability to induce reporter expression in ribozyme-containing cells but not in control cells lacking functional ribozyme activity. Secondary assays confirm that inhibition is dependent on RNA incorporation and not direct binding, and that the compounds do not interfere with general transcription, translation, or cell viability.

The invention further includes pharmaceutical compositions comprising one or more of the identified ribozyme inhibitors, formulated for systemic or localized administration. These compositions may be delivered as single agents or in combination, and may be formulated with pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, solubilizing agents, stabilizers, and delivery enhancers suitable for oral, intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous, intranasal, topical, or intrathecal administration. The compositions may be used to induce gene expression in therapeutic contexts, such as the reactivation of tumor suppressor genes, the expression of neuroprotective factors, the production of therapeutic proteins in gene therapy, or the controlled delivery of cytokines or antibodies.

The invention further provides methods of treatment for diseases or conditions in which regulated gene expression is beneficial, including but not limited to cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, metabolic diseases, autoimmune conditions, and genetic deficiencies. In such methods, a subject is administered a nucleic acid construct encoding the N79 ribozyme linked to a therapeutic gene, followed by administration of one or more ribozyme inhibitors to induce expression of the therapeutic protein. The timing, dosage, and duration of inhibitor administration can be tailored to achieve therapeutic efficacy while minimizing off-target effects or toxicity.

The invention further provides diagnostic methods for monitoring the uptake, distribution, and retention of ribozyme inhibitors in vivo. For instance, the administration of a ribozyme inhibitor such as 5-fluorouracil to a subject harboring an N79-regulated reporter gene allows real-time, non-invasive imaging of drug distribution through bioluminescence or fluorescence readouts. This enables the assessment of drug bioavailability in tumors, the blood-brain barrier, or other tissues, and facilitates the optimization of dosing regimens for chemotherapy or other therapeutics.

The invention thus provides a comprehensive, modular, and highly versatile platform for the chemical control of gene expression in mammalian systems. It overcomes the limitations of existing transcriptional and RNAi-based systems by introducing a novel mechanism of regulation that is rapid, reversible, dose-responsive, and compatible with small molecule therapeutics. It enables precise spatiotemporal control of gene expression in vitro and in vivo, opens new avenues for gene therapy and synthetic biology, and provides a powerful tool for drug discovery, pharmacokinetic analysis, and precision medicine.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Self-Cleaving RNA Mutant Motifs

The invention employs a modified hammerhead ribozyme, designated N79, as the central regulatory element for controlling gene expression through RNA self-cleavage. This ribozyme is derived from the naturally occurring hammerhead ribozyme of Schistosoma mansoni, a parasitic flatworm whose ribozyme has been previously characterized for its catalytic activity in vitro. However, the wild-type sequence exhibits poor folding efficiency and low cleavage rates in mammalian cells due to structural instability, interference from cellular RNA-binding proteins, and rapid degradation by endogenous nucleases. To overcome these limitations, the N79 variant was generated through a series of rational mutagenesis and selection steps designed to enhance its catalytic efficiency, structural robustness, and compatibility with the intracellular environment.

The N79 ribozyme retains the conserved catalytic core of the hammerhead ribozyme, including the three helical stems (I, II, and III) and the conserved nucleotides in the catalytic pocket, but incorporates a set of stabilizing substitutions that improve its folding kinetics and resistance to degradation. These substitutions include, but are not limited to, the replacement of G10.1 with C, the substitution of U16.1 with C, the mutation of A17 to G, and the insertion of a G-C base pair in the loop region of stem II. These modifications do not alter the primary cleavage site—located between nucleotides C17 and U1.1—but instead optimize the spatial arrangement of the catalytic core, promote the formation of key tertiary interactions, and reduce the propensity for misfolding or aggregation. The resulting ribozyme achieves greater than 95% cleavage efficiency within 10 minutes of transcription in mammalian cells, ensuring that any transcript containing the N79 sequence is rapidly degraded before it can be translated.

The N79 ribozyme is designed to be inserted into a gene expression cassette in a manner that does not interfere with transcriptional initiation, polyadenylation, or nuclear export. Preferred insertion sites include the 3′ untranslated region immediately downstream of the stop codon, the 5′ untranslated region upstream of the start codon, or within an intron that is spliced out of the mature mRNA. When placed in the 3′ untranslated region, the ribozyme is transcribed as part of the mRNA but cleaved before the polyadenylation signal, ensuring that the cleaved fragments are rapidly degraded by exonucleases. When inserted into the 5′ untranslated region, the ribozyme is cleaved before the ribosome binding site, preventing translation initiation. When placed within an intron, the ribozyme is excised along with the intron during splicing, but if splicing is incomplete or inefficient, the ribozyme remains in the transcript and induces cleavage. The choice of insertion site depends on the specific application, the gene being regulated, and the desired kinetics of induction.

The N79 ribozyme is highly versatile and functions reliably across a broad spectrum of mammalian cell types, including HEK293, HeLa, CHO, NIH/3T3, primary fibroblasts, neural stem cells, hepatocytes, and cardiomyocytes. It remains active even in cells with high levels of RNA turnover or robust RNA interference machinery, and its activity is unaffected by the presence of endogenous microRNAs or RNA-binding proteins. The ribozyme is also stable when integrated into the genome as part of a transgene, and its expression does not induce cellular stress responses or interferon activation, making it suitable for long-term applications.

The ribozyme can be encoded as a single continuous sequence or split into two or more fragments that reassemble through RNA-RNA interactions, allowing for conditional activation in response to specific RNA signals. For example, the ribozyme can be designed to be inactive until a complementary RNA trigger binds and facilitates proper folding, enabling the construction of logic gates or multi-input regulatory circuits. The ribozyme can also be engineered to respond to temperature, pH, or metal ion concentrations, although its primary mode of regulation in this invention is through chemical inhibition.

Importantly, the N79 ribozyme is not limited to the hammerhead scaffold. Other self-cleaving RNA motifs, such as the hepatitis delta virus ribozyme, the glmS ribozyme, or the hairpin ribozyme, may be similarly engineered for use in this system, provided they can be modified to achieve high cleavage efficiency in mammalian cells and are susceptible to inhibition by small molecule analogs. However, the N79 variant has been specifically optimized for this application and remains the preferred embodiment due to its unparalleled efficiency, stability, and compatibility with nucleoside analog inhibitors.

The ribozyme sequence is provided in SEQ ID NO: 1, which encodes the full-length N79 ribozyme with all stabilizing mutations. The sequence is flanked by universal cloning sites for easy insertion into expression vectors, and it may be duplicated or triplicated to enhance repression efficiency. In some embodiments, multiple ribozymes are placed in tandem, separated by short linker sequences, to ensure complete cleavage even if one ribozyme fails to fold correctly. The use of multiple ribozymes increases the stringency of repression and reduces the likelihood of leaky expression.

In addition to the ribozyme itself, the invention includes variants that contain silent mutations to alter codon usage, improve mRNA stability, or reduce immunogenicity. These variants are functionally equivalent to the original N79 sequence but may be preferred in certain applications, such as gene therapy in humans, where immune recognition of foreign RNA sequences must be minimized. The ribozyme may also be fused to RNA stability elements, such as the β-globin 3′ untranslated region or the WPRE element, to enhance its half-life in the absence of cleavage, thereby increasing the dynamic range of induction upon inhibitor treatment.

The N79 ribozyme is uniquely suited for this invention because of its high catalytic rate, its insensitivity to cellular context, and its susceptibility to inhibition through nucleoside analog incorporation. Unlike other ribozymes that require divalent metal ions or specific cofactors for activity, the N79 ribozyme functions efficiently under physiological concentrations of magnesium and does not require auxiliary proteins. Its activity is not dependent on the sequence context of the flanking regions, making it a plug-and-play module for gene regulation. The ribozyme’s mechanism of inhibition—through incorporation of modified nucleosides into the RNA transcript—provides a novel and highly specific means of control that is distinct from traditional transcriptional or post-transcriptional regulation. This mechanism ensures that inhibition is reversible, dose-dependent, and compatible with clinically approved small molecules, making the N79 ribozyme the ideal molecular switch for the invention.

### Expression Vectors and Cell Lines

The invention encompasses a suite of expression vectors specifically engineered to enable the regulated expression of a gene of interest through the use of the N79 ribozyme. These vectors are designed to be compatible with a wide range of delivery systems, including plasmid transfection, viral transduction, and transposon-mediated integration, and are optimized for high-level, stable, and reproducible gene expression in mammalian cells. Each vector contains a strong constitutive promoter, such as the cytomegalovirus immediate-early promoter (CMV), the elongation factor 1-alpha promoter (EF1α), the ubiquitin C promoter (UBC), or the simian virus 40 promoter (SV40), to ensure robust transcription of the downstream gene cassette. The promoter is followed by a multiple cloning site, into which the coding sequence of the gene of interest is inserted, and immediately downstream of the stop codon, two or more tandem copies of the N79 ribozyme are positioned to ensure complete cleavage of the transcript.

The vector may also include a polyadenylation signal, such as the bovine growth hormone poly(A) signal or the SV40 late poly(A) signal, to ensure proper mRNA processing and nuclear export. In some embodiments, the vector contains an intron within the 5′ untranslated region to enhance mRNA stability and translation efficiency, as introns have been shown to improve gene expression in mammalian cells through mechanisms involving splicing-dependent nuclear export and mRNA stabilization. The ribozyme is placed downstream of the stop codon to avoid interference with translation termination and to ensure that cleavage occurs after the entire coding sequence has been transcribed.

The vector may further include a selectable marker gene, such as puromycin N-acetyltransferase, neomycin phosphotransferase, hygromycin B phosphotransferase, or green fluorescent protein, to facilitate the isolation and maintenance of stably transfected cell lines. In preferred embodiments, the selectable marker is placed under the control of a separate promoter, such that its expression is independent of the ribozyme-regulated gene of interest. This design allows for the selection of cells that have successfully integrated the vector without requiring the expression of the regulated gene, which remains silenced under basal conditions.

The invention further includes a series of stable cell lines generated by the stable integration of these expression vectors into the genome of mammalian cells. The most preferred cell line is designated HEK79, which was derived from HEK293 cells through stable transfection with a vector containing the CMV promoter, a luciferase reporter gene, and two tandem copies of the N79 ribozyme. This cell line exhibits extremely low basal luciferase activity—less than 1% of the level observed in cells harboring an identical construct with a catalytically inactive ribozyme—while demonstrating a robust, dose-dependent induction of luciferase expression upon treatment with ribozyme inhibitors such as toyocamycin or 5-fluorouridine. The HEK79 cell line is clonally derived, genetically stable over more than 50 passages, and maintains its responsiveness to inhibitors under standard cell culture conditions, including serum starvation, hypoxia, and temperature shifts.

Additional stable cell lines have been generated using alternative promoters, reporter genes, and ribozyme configurations. For example, a HeLa-derived cell line, designated HeLa79, expresses a secreted alkaline phosphatase (SEAP) reporter under the control of the EF1α promoter and two N79 ribozymes, allowing for non-invasive monitoring of gene expression through medium-based assays. A CHO-derived cell line, CHO79, expresses a fluorescent protein, such as enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP), enabling real-time, single-cell analysis of induction kinetics using flow cytometry or fluorescence microscopy. A primary human fibroblast line, Fibro79, expresses a therapeutic protein, such as erythropoietin or factor IX, under the control of the UBC promoter, providing a physiologically relevant model for gene therapy applications.

These cell lines are invaluable tools for high-throughput screening of novel ribozyme inhibitors, as they provide a consistent, reproducible, and sensitive readout of ribozyme inhibition. The screening process involves seeding the cells in multi-well plates, exposing them to libraries of small molecules, and measuring reporter gene expression after 24 to 72 hours. Hits are validated by testing their ability to induce expression in ribozyme-containing cells but not in control cells harboring inactive ribozymes, and by confirming that induction is dose-dependent and reversible upon washout of the compound.

The cell lines may also be engineered to contain multiple independent ribozyme-regulated transgenes, each under the control of a different promoter or reporter, enabling the simultaneous regulation of multiple genes or the construction of complex genetic circuits. For example, one transgene may encode a reporter protein for monitoring, while a second encodes a therapeutic protein, allowing for real-time correlation between induction level and therapeutic output. The cell lines may also be modified to contain inducible ribozyme variants that respond to different classes of inhibitors, enabling orthogonal control of multiple genes using distinct chemical inputs.

The invention further includes vectors and cell lines in which the ribozyme is placed within an intron of the gene of interest, such that the gene is only expressed when splicing is incomplete or when the ribozyme is inhibited. This configuration allows for conditional expression in response to splicing efficiency, which can be modulated by small molecules, RNA-binding proteins, or cellular stress conditions. The ribozyme may also be placed in a bidirectional promoter system, such that its cleavage activity regulates the expression of two genes in opposite orientations, enabling coordinated control of gene pairs.

In all embodiments, the expression vectors and cell lines are designed to minimize background expression, maximize induction range, and ensure long-term stability. The use of the N79 ribozyme ensures that gene expression remains tightly repressed under basal conditions, with leakiness below the detection limit of conventional assays. The induction range, defined as the ratio of expression in the presence of inhibitor to expression in its absence, exceeds 300-fold in the most sensitive cell lines, and can reach over 600-fold when using synergistic combinations of inhibitors. These characteristics make the system ideal for applications requiring precise, quantitative, and reversible control of gene expression, including synthetic biology, drug discovery, gene therapy, and basic research.

### Methods of Delivering Nucleic Acids

The invention encompasses a variety of methods for delivering nucleic acids encoding the N79 ribozyme and a gene of interest into mammalian cells, both in vitro and in vivo. These methods are selected based on the target cell type, the desired duration of expression, the route of administration, and the clinical or experimental context. The nucleic acids may be delivered as plasmid DNA, viral vectors, synthetic RNA, or DNA-RNA hybrids, and may be formulated with delivery enhancers to improve cellular uptake, nuclear entry, and stability.

In vitro delivery methods include chemical transfection using cationic lipids, such as Lipofectamine 2000 or FuGENE HD, or polymers, such as polyethylenimine or dendrimers. These methods are highly efficient in adherent cell lines such as HEK293, HeLa, and CHO, and allow for transient or stable expression depending on whether the vector contains elements for genomic integration. Electroporation is another effective method, particularly for hard-to-transfect cells such as primary neurons, T cells, and hematopoietic stem cells. In this method, cells are suspended in a buffer containing the nucleic acid and subjected to a brief electrical pulse that transiently permeabilizes the plasma membrane, allowing the DNA or RNA to enter the cytoplasm. Microinjection is used for single-cell delivery in embryos, oocytes, or other delicate systems, and allows for precise control over the amount of nucleic acid delivered.

For long-term, stable expression, the nucleic acids may be delivered using viral vectors. Adeno-associated virus (AAV) is the preferred vector for in vivo applications due to its low immunogenicity, broad tropism, and ability to mediate long-term transgene expression in non-dividing cells. AAV serotypes such as AAV2, AAV5, AAV8, and AAV9 have been shown to efficiently transduce liver, muscle, brain, and retinal cells, making them ideal for systemic or localized gene therapy. The N79 ribozyme construct is cloned into a recombinant AAV vector under the control of a tissue-specific promoter, such as the albumin promoter for liver expression, the synapsin promoter for neuronal expression, or the insulin promoter for pancreatic β-cells. The vector is packaged into viral particles, purified, and administered via intravenous, intramuscular, intracerebral, or subcutaneous injection.

Lentiviral vectors are used when stable genomic integration is required in dividing cells, such as hematopoietic stem cells, T cells, or embryonic stem cells. Lentiviruses integrate into the host genome, ensuring that the ribozyme-regulated transgene is passed on to daughter cells. The vector is engineered to be self-inactivating, with deletions in the long terminal repeats to minimize the risk of insertional mutagenesis, and may include insulator elements to prevent silencing of the transgene. The ribozyme construct is placed downstream of a constitutive or inducible promoter, and the vector is produced in packaging cell lines such as HEK293T, purified by ultracentrifugation, and transduced into target cells at a multiplicity of infection optimized for each cell type.

Adenoviral vectors are used for high-level, transient expression in tissues such as the lung, liver, or eye. These vectors can accommodate large transgene cassettes and achieve rapid, high-titer expression, but are more immunogenic than AAV or lentiviral vectors. They are particularly useful for applications requiring acute, short-term induction, such as vaccine development or acute disease models.

Non-viral delivery methods are also employed, particularly for clinical translation. Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) composed of ionizable lipids, phospholipids, cholesterol, and polyethylene glycol are used to encapsulate plasmid DNA or mRNA encoding the ribozyme construct. These nanoparticles are administered intravenously and are taken up by hepatocytes, immune cells, or other target tissues, depending on their surface properties. The LNPs protect the nucleic acid from degradation, facilitate endosomal escape, and enable efficient cytoplasmic delivery. This method is particularly suited for mRNA-based delivery, in which the ribozyme is encoded in a synthetic mRNA molecule that is translated immediately upon delivery, bypassing the need for nuclear entry.

Other delivery methods include hydrodynamic injection, in which a large volume of nucleic acid solution is rapidly injected into the tail vein of a mouse, forcing the DNA into hepatocytes; ballistic delivery using gene guns to deliver DNA-coated particles into skin or muscle tissue; and transdermal delivery using microneedle patches or ultrasound-enhanced permeation. These methods are particularly useful for localized applications, such as wound healing, skin disease, or cancer immunotherapy.

The invention further includes methods for delivering the ribozyme construct in combination with the inhibitor molecule, either as a co-formulated mixture or as a sequential administration. For example, a single injection of an AAV vector encoding the ribozyme-regulated therapeutic gene may be followed by oral or intravenous administration of the inhibitor, allowing for temporal control of gene expression. Alternatively, the inhibitor may be incorporated into the delivery vehicle itself, such as a nanoparticle containing both the nucleic acid and the small molecule, enabling simultaneous delivery and immediate induction upon cellular uptake.

The choice of delivery method is guided by the intended application. For gene therapy in humans, AAV and LNPs are preferred due to their safety profile and clinical feasibility. For research applications, plasmid transfection and lentiviral transduction are commonly used for their ease of use and high efficiency. The invention encompasses all of these methods and provides guidance for selecting the optimal delivery strategy based on the target tissue, the required duration of expression, and the pharmacokinetic properties of the inhibitor.

### Methods of Identifying Ribozyme Regulators

The invention provides a robust and scalable method for identifying small molecules capable of inhibiting the self-cleavage activity of the N79 ribozyme in mammalian cells. This method is based on a cell-based high-throughput screening (HTS) platform that utilizes reporter cell lines engineered to express a detectable protein only when ribozyme cleavage is suppressed. The screening process is designed to identify compounds that function within the physiological context of the cell, rather than in simplified biochemical assays, thereby increasing the likelihood of discovering inhibitors with true biological activity.

The screening platform employs a stable cell line, such as HEK79, which contains a genomic integration of a vector encoding a luciferase reporter gene under the control of a constitutive promoter and two tandem copies of the N79 ribozyme. Under basal conditions, the ribozyme cleaves the mRNA transcript immediately after transcription, resulting in undetectable luciferase activity. Upon treatment with a compound that inhibits ribozyme cleavage, the mRNA remains intact, is translated, and produces luciferase, which can be quantified using a luminometer. This system provides a highly sensitive, quantitative, and reproducible readout that is directly proportional to the level of ribozyme inhibition.

The screening is performed in multi-well plate formats, typically 384-well or 1536-well plates, to maximize throughput and minimize reagent consumption. Cells are seeded at a density of 5,000 to 10,000 cells per well in culture medium containing serum and antibiotics, and allowed to adhere overnight. The following day, compounds from a diverse library are added to the wells at a final concentration ranging from 0.1 μM to 10 μM, with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) as the solvent, at a concentration not exceeding 0.5% to avoid cytotoxicity. The compounds are sourced from a variety of libraries, including FDA-approved drugs, natural product extracts, combinatorial chemical libraries, bioactive compound sets, and synthetic small molecule collections, ensuring broad chemical diversity.

The cells are incubated with the compounds for 24 to 72 hours, depending on the kinetics of the reporter gene and the expected half-life of the compounds. After incubation, the medium is removed, and a luciferase substrate is added directly to the wells. Luminescence is measured using a plate reader equipped with a sensitive photomultiplier tube, and the signal is normalized to background readings from wells containing no cells or cells transfected with an inactive ribozyme construct. Compounds that induce luciferase expression by at least threefold above the basal level are considered primary hits.

Primary hits are then subjected to secondary validation assays to confirm specificity and exclude false positives. These assays include testing the compounds on control cell lines that contain an inactive version of the N79 ribozyme, in which key catalytic nucleotides have been mutated to abolish cleavage activity. A true inhibitor will induce luciferase expression only in the ribozyme-containing cell line and not in the control line. Additional validation includes dose-response curves to determine the half-maximal effective concentration (EC50), reversibility assays to confirm that induction is abolished upon compound washout, and cytotoxicity assays to ensure that induction is not due to general cell stress or death.

To determine the mechanism of inhibition, selected hits are tested in cell-free in vitro transcription reactions. If the compound inhibits cleavage in the cell-based assay but not in the cell-free system, it is inferred that its activity requires incorporation into RNA during transcription. This is further confirmed by synthesizing RNA transcripts in the presence of radiolabeled or modified nucleoside triphosphates and analyzing the cleavage efficiency by gel electrophoresis. Compounds that induce inhibition only when their triphosphate forms are used in transcription are classified as nucleoside analogs that act through incorporation.

The screening platform is further refined by including counter-screens to exclude compounds that affect general transcription, translation, or mRNA stability. For example, cells are transfected with a plasmid encoding a luciferase gene without the ribozyme, and compounds that induce luciferase expression in this system are discarded as non-specific activators. Similarly, cells are treated with compounds and analyzed for changes in global mRNA levels using RT-qPCR or RNA sequencing to ensure that the effect is specific to the ribozyme-containing transcript.

The HTS platform is automated using robotic liquid handlers, plate washers, and data analysis software to process tens of thousands of compounds per week. The system has been validated using known inhibitors such as toyocamycin and 5-fluorouridine, which consistently produce strong induction signals, and negative controls such as 2′-deoxyadenosine and guanosine analogs, which show no activity. The platform has identified fifteen distinct inhibitors from a library of over fifty-eight thousand compounds, demonstrating its high sensitivity and low false-positive rate.

The method further includes the use of structure-activity relationship (SAR) analysis to optimize hit compounds. Analogues of the primary hits are synthesized or purchased, and their inhibitory activity is tested in the same screening system. This allows for the identification of structural features essential for activity, such as the presence of a ribose sugar with a 2′-hydroxyl group, a modified purine or pyrimidine base, or a specific stereochemistry. These insights guide the design of next-generation inhibitors with improved potency, selectivity, and pharmacokinetic properties.

The method further encompasses the use of phenotypic screening in primary cells or tissue explants, where the ribozyme-regulated transgene encodes a therapeutic protein such as insulin, erythropoietin, or a neurotrophic factor. In this context, inhibition of ribozyme cleavage leads to the secretion of the protein, which can be detected in the culture supernatant by ELISA or bioassay. This approach allows for the identification of inhibitors that function in physiologically relevant cell types, beyond immortalized lines.

The invention thus provides a comprehensive, scalable, and highly reliable method for discovering novel ribozyme regulators, enabling the rapid identification of chemical tools for gene regulation and the development of new therapeutic agents.

### Methods of Treatment and Administration

The invention provides novel methods for the treatment of diseases and disorders in which regulated expression of a therapeutic protein is beneficial. These methods involve the administration of a nucleic acid construct encoding the N79 ribozyme linked to a gene of interest, followed by the administration of one or more small molecule inhibitors capable of suppressing ribozyme cleavage and thereby inducing expression of the therapeutic protein. The system enables precise temporal, spatial, and dose-dependent control of gene expression, making it particularly suitable for conditions requiring dynamic regulation, such as cancer, metabolic disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and genetic deficiencies.

In a preferred embodiment, the method is applied to the treatment of cancer. A recombinant adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector encoding the N79 ribozyme linked to a tumor suppressor gene, such as p53, PTEN, or RB1, is injected directly into a solid tumor. Under basal conditions, the ribozyme prevents expression of the tumor suppressor, minimizing off-target effects in healthy tissues. Upon administration of a ribozyme inhibitor, such as 5-fluorouridine or a combination of toyocamycin and 5-fluorouracil, the tumor suppressor is induced specifically within the tumor, leading to cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, or senescence. The inhibitor may be administered systemically or locally, and its dosage and timing are adjusted based on tumor size, vascularization, and pharmacokinetic profiling. This approach allows for the targeted reactivation of silenced tumor suppressor genes without affecting their expression in normal tissues, thereby reducing systemic toxicity.

In another embodiment, the method is used to treat neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, or amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. AAV vectors encoding the N79 ribozyme linked to a neuroprotective gene, such as glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), or superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1), are injected into the substantia nigra, hippocampus, or spinal cord. The ribozyme ensures that the neurotrophic factor is not expressed under basal conditions, preventing potential side effects such as aberrant neuronal growth or inflammation. Upon administration of the inhibitor, the neurotrophic factor is induced locally, promoting neuronal survival, synaptic plasticity, and axonal regeneration. The inhibitor may be administered orally, intranasally, or via intrathecal infusion, allowing for repeated dosing and long-term management of the disease.

In the context of metabolic disorders, such as diabetes or phenylketonuria, the method involves delivery of a vector encoding the N79 ribozyme linked to a metabolic enzyme, such as insulin, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), or phenylalanine hydroxylase. The ribozyme prevents constitutive expression of the enzyme, avoiding hypoglycemia or toxic metabolite accumulation. The inhibitor is administered in response to physiological cues, such as elevated blood glucose or phenylalanine levels, enabling on-demand enzyme production. The inhibitor may be delivered in a controlled-release formulation, such as an implantable pump or a smart pill, to synchronize gene induction with metabolic demand.

For genetic deficiencies, such as hemophilia A or B, the method involves systemic delivery of an AAV vector encoding the N79 ribozyme linked to factor VIII or factor IX. The ribozyme ensures that the clotting factor is not expressed until the inhibitor is administered, preventing immune recognition and neutralization of the protein during development. Once the patient reaches a stable clinical state, the inhibitor is administered periodically to induce therapeutic levels of the clotting factor, mimicking natural physiological regulation. This approach avoids the need for frequent protein infusions and reduces the risk of inhibitor antibody formation.

The invention further encompasses methods for treating autoimmune diseases, such as multiple sclerosis or rheumatoid arthritis, by inducing the expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-10 or transforming growth factor-beta, in specific immune cell populations. The vector is delivered ex vivo to T cells or macrophages, which are then reinfused into the patient. The inhibitor is administered systemically to induce cytokine production only when inflammation is detected, creating a feedback loop that dampens immune activation without causing global immunosuppression.

The method may also be used for gene-based vaccination, in which a vector encoding the N79 ribozyme linked to a viral antigen, such as spike protein or matrix protein, is delivered to dendritic cells. The ribozyme prevents premature expression of the antigen, avoiding immune tolerance. Upon administration of the inhibitor, the antigen is induced, triggering a robust adaptive immune response. This approach allows for precise control over the timing and magnitude of antigen presentation, enhancing vaccine efficacy.

The route of administration for the inhibitor is selected based on the target tissue and the pharmacokinetic properties of the compound. Oral administration is preferred for systemic delivery of small, stable molecules such as 5-fluorouracil. Intravenous injection is used for rapid, high-concentration delivery of compounds such as toyocamycin. Intramuscular, subcutaneous, or intradermal injection is used for localized delivery, such as in skin diseases or tumors. Intranasal or intrathecal delivery is used for targeting the central nervous system. The inhibitor may be formulated as a solution, suspension, tablet, capsule, or sustained-release implant, depending on the clinical need.

The invention further includes methods for monitoring the efficacy of treatment using non-invasive imaging. For example, if the gene of interest encodes a reporter protein such as luciferase or fluorescent protein, bioluminescence or fluorescence imaging can be used to visualize the spatial and temporal pattern of gene induction in real time. This allows clinicians to confirm that the inhibitor is reaching the target tissue, to adjust dosing based on induction level, and to detect resistance or loss of expression.

The method is further refined by combining the ribozyme-regulated gene therapy with other therapeutic modalities, such as chemotherapy, radiation, or immunotherapy. For example, in cancer treatment, the ribozyme system may be used to induce the expression of a prodrug-converting enzyme, such as cytosine deaminase, which converts 5-fluorocytosine into the cytotoxic agent 5-fluorouracil. The inhibitor is administered to induce enzyme expression, followed by administration of the prodrug, creating a dual-control system that enhances tumor specificity.

The invention thus provides a versatile, safe, and effective method for treating a wide range of diseases through the chemical control of gene expression, enabling precision medicine at the molecular level.

The inhibitors may be administered as single agents or in combination, and may be formulated for oral, intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous, intranasal, topical, or intrathecal routes of administration.

These compounds are synthesized or purified to pharmaceutical grade, with purity exceeding 98% as determined by high-performance liquid chromatography and mass spectrometry. The compositions may contain a single inhibitor or a synergistic combination of two or more inhibitors, such as a mixture of toyocamycin and 5-fluorouridine, to enhance efficacy while reducing individual compound doses and minimizing toxicity.

In such formulations, the inhibitor is mixed with pharmaceutically acceptable carriers such as microcrystalline cellulose, lactose, starch, magnesium stearate, or hydroxypropyl methylcellulose, and compressed into tablets or encapsulated in gelatin capsules. Enteric coatings may be applied to protect the inhibitor from gastric degradation and ensure release in the intestine. Sustained-release formulations may be prepared using polymer matrices such as ethylcellulose or polylactic acid to provide prolonged exposure over hours or days.

The formulation may include penetration enhancers such as dimethyl sulfoxide, oleic acid, or ethanol to facilitate skin absorption. The compositions are sterilized by filtration or autoclaving, packaged in single-use vials, syringes, or pre-filled pens, and stored under controlled conditions, such as refrigeration or lyophilization, to maintain stability. The pharmaceutical compositions of the invention are safe, effective, and scalable, enabling the widespread application of ribozyme-based gene regulation in therapeutic and diagnostic contexts.

### Exemplification

The invention is further exemplified by the following detailed experimental embodiments, which demonstrate the practical utility, efficacy, and reproducibility of the system in both in vitro and in vivo settings.

Embodiment 1: Generation and Validation of the HEK79 Reporter Cell Line  
Human embryonic kidney 293 cells were transfected with a plasmid vector containing the cytomegalovirus immediate-early promoter, a firefly luciferase coding sequence, and two tandem copies of the N79 ribozyme inserted immediately downstream of the stop codon. Stable cell lines were selected using puromycin (1 μg/mL) over a period of 14 days. Individual clones were isolated by limiting dilution and screened for basal luciferase expression and inducibility. One clone, designated HEK79, exhibited a basal luciferase signal 20-fold lower than that of a control cell line containing an inactive ribozyme variant, and demonstrated a 20-fold induction of luciferase expression upon transfection with an antisense morpholino oligonucleotide complementary to the ribozyme’s catalytic core. This clone was selected for high-throughput screening and maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1 μg/mL puromycin. The cell line remained stable for over 100 passages, with consistent induction ratios and minimal batch-to-batch variation.

Embodiment 2: High-Throughput Screening of Compound Libraries  
A library of 58,076 compounds was screened using the HEK79 cell line in a 384-well format. Compounds were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide at 10 mM and diluted to a final concentration of 4 μg/mL in culture medium. Cells were seeded at 8,000 cells per well and incubated with compounds for 48 hours at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. Luciferase activity was measured using a Packer Fusion plate reader after addition of luciferin substrate. Hits were defined as compounds that induced luciferase expression by at least threefold over basal levels and showed no induction in a control cell line containing an inactive ribozyme. Fifteen compounds were identified as true positives, including toyocamycin, 5-fluorouridine, and tubercidin. Dose-response curves were generated for each compound, and EC50 values were calculated. The most potent compound, toyocamycin, exhibited an EC50 of 0.8 μM and a maximal induction of 365-fold.

Embodiment 3: In Vitro Transcription and Incorporation Assay  
A DNA template containing the N79 ribozyme sequence downstream of a T7 promoter was used for in vitro transcription using T7 RNA polymerase. Transcription was performed in the presence of 32P-labeled UTP or ATP to generate radiolabeled RNA. To test for incorporation-dependent inhibition, ATP was replaced with tubercidin triphosphate, and UTP was replaced with 5-fluorouridine triphosphate. RNA transcripts were purified by denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. When tubercidin triphosphate was used, full-length RNA was observed, with no cleavage products detected, whereas control transcripts synthesized with ATP were completely cleaved. Similar results were obtained with 5-fluorouridine triphosphate. These results confirmed that inhibition requires incorporation of the analog into the RNA transcript.

Embodiment 4: Synergistic Induction in HEK79 Cells  
HEK79 cells were treated with varying concentrations of toyocamycin and 5-fluorouridine, either alone or in combination. The fold induction of luciferase expression was measured after 48 hours. When toyocamycin was used alone at 0.5 μM, induction was 120-fold. When 5-fluorouridine was used alone at 10 μM, induction was 80-fold. When both compounds were used together at these concentrations, induction reached 670-fold, demonstrating a synergistic effect. The combination allowed for a 10-fold reduction in the concentration of each compound while achieving greater induction than either compound alone at its maximum effective dose.

Embodiment 5: In Vivo Induction in Nude Mice  
Nude mice were injected subcutaneously in the left ear with 1 × 10^9 genome copies of an AAV vector encoding the N79 ribozyme linked to a luciferase reporter gene. The right ear received an identical vector containing an inactive ribozyme variant as an internal control. Three weeks later, a single intradermal injection of 5 μL of a cocktail containing 20 μM toyocamycin and 12 mM 5-fluorouridine was administered to both ears. Bioluminescence imaging was performed before and 48 hours after injection. The left ear exhibited a 37-fold increase in photon flux, while the right ear showed no change. This demonstrated specific, inducible gene expression in vivo using a clinically relevant delivery method.

Embodiment 6: Therapeutic Application in a Cancer Model  
Mice bearing subcutaneous xenografts of human hepatocellular carcinoma were injected intratumorally with an AAV vector encoding the N79 ribozyme linked to the p53 tumor suppressor gene. Two weeks later, mice were administered 5-fluorouracil orally at a dose of 50 mg/kg daily for seven days. Tumor volume was measured daily. Mice treated with the vector and 5-fluorouracil showed a 70% reduction in tumor volume compared to controls, while mice treated with either the vector alone or the drug alone showed no significant effect. Histological analysis confirmed increased apoptosis and decreased proliferation in the treated tumors. This demonstrated the therapeutic potential of the system in a preclinical cancer model.

Embodiment 7: Pharmacokinetic Monitoring Using Reporter Induction  
Mice were injected with an AAV vector encoding the N79 ribozyme linked to luciferase. After 14 days, 5-fluorouracil was administered intravenously at a single dose of 100 mg/kg. Bioluminescence imaging was performed at 0, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 hours. The luciferase signal peaked at 12 hours and gradually declined over 48 hours, mirroring the known pharmacokinetic profile of 5-fluorouracil. This demonstrated that the system can be used as a real-time biosensor to monitor drug uptake and retention in vivo.

### Methods: 

The methods employed in the invention are standardized, reproducible, and suitable for both research and clinical applications. The following detailed protocols describe the key methodologies used to generate, validate, and apply the ribozyme-based gene regulation system.

Cell Culture and Transfection:  
Mammalian cell lines, including HEK293, HeLa, CHO, and primary fibroblasts, were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. For transient transfection, cells were seeded at 50–70% confluence and transfected with plasmid DNA using Lipofectamine 2000 or FuGENE HD according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For stable transfection, cells were selected with puromycin (1–5 μg/mL), neomycin (400–800 μg/mL), or hygromycin (100–200 μg/mL) for 10–14 days, and clonal lines were isolated by limiting dilution. Clones were screened for reporter expression using luciferase assays or fluorescence microscopy.

High-Throughput Screening:  
Compounds were obtained from commercial libraries and dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide at 10 mM. Stock solutions were diluted in culture medium to a final concentration of 4 μg/mL, with DMSO not exceeding 0.5%. HEK79 cells were seeded at 8,000 cells per well in 384-well white, opaque-bottom plates using a robotic dispenser. After 24 hours, compounds were added, and cells were incubated for 48 hours. Luciferase activity was measured using a Packer Fusion plate reader after addition of 4 μg/mL D-luciferin. Induction was calculated as the ratio of luminescence in ribozyme-containing cells to that in control cells with inactive ribozymes. Hits were validated in triplicate.

In Vitro Transcription and Cleavage Assay:  
DNA templates containing the N79 ribozyme under a T7 promoter were generated by PCR. Transcription was performed using the MEGAshortscript T7 kit (Ambion) in the presence of 50 μM blocking antisense oligonucleotide to reduce cleavage during transcription. For incorporation assays, ATP was replaced with tubercidin triphosphate, and UTP was replaced with 5-fluorouridine triphosphate. Reactions were incubated at 37°C for 3 hours, and RNA was purified by denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Cleavage efficiency was assessed by incubating RNA in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) with 1 mM MgCl2 at 23°C for 3 minutes, followed by quenching with urea-formamide loading buffer. Cleavage products were visualized by phosphorimaging and quantified using ImageQuant software.

In Vivo Delivery and Imaging:  
Adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9) vectors encoding the N79-regulated luciferase construct were produced by triple transfection of HEK293 cells and purified by iodixanol gradient centrifugation. Viral titers were determined by qPCR. Nude mice were injected subcutaneously in the ear with 1 × 10^9 genome copies of virus. Three weeks later, animals were anesthetized with isoflurane and imaged using a Xenogen IVIS Spectrum system after intraperitoneal injection of D-luciferin (150 mg/kg). Bioluminescence was quantified as total flux (photons/second) using Living Image software. For inhibitor administration, 5 μL of cocktail (20 μM toyocamycin, 12 mM 5-fluorouridine) was injected intradermally into the same site.

Pharmacokinetic Analysis:  
Mice were injected with AAV encoding the ribozyme-regulated luciferase construct. After 14 days, 5-fluorouracil was administered intravenously at 100 mg/kg. Bioluminescence imaging was performed at 0, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 hours. Plasma samples were collected at each time point and analyzed for 5-fluorouracil concentration by LC-MS/MS. Correlation between luciferase signal and plasma concentration was determined by linear regression.

These methods provide a complete and reproducible framework for implementing the invention in research and therapeutic applications.
