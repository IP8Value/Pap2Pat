# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to a system and method for modeling the dynamic electrical behavior of lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries under variable operating conditions, particularly for application within battery management systems (BMS) of electric vehicles and other mobile or stationary energy storage systems. More specifically, the invention encompasses a novel framework for generating a computationally efficient, state-dependent, temperature-adaptive equivalent circuit model that accurately captures the transient voltage response of Li-S cells across a wide range of discharge currents, temperatures, and states of charge. The invention further includes a methodology for parameterizing the model through prediction error minimization using mixed-current pulse testing, enabling robust identification of behavioral parameters that reflect the unique electrochemical phenomena inherent to lithium-sulfur chemistry, including polysulfide shuttle effects, dual-voltage plateaus, temperature-dependent electrolyte viscosity, and irreversible capacity fade due to precipitation and interfacial passivation. The resulting model is structured as a linearized state-space representation with behavioral parameterization, allowing for direct integration into real-time estimation algorithms such as Kalman filters without requiring high-dimensional lookup tables or nonlinear optimization during operation. This invention addresses a critical gap in the field of battery modeling by providing a scalable, low-complexity, and physically informed model suitable for embedded systems with limited computational resources, while retaining sufficient fidelity to support accurate state-of-charge estimation, power capability prediction, and degradation monitoring in operational environments where temperature and current profiles vary dynamically.

## BACKGROUND

The widespread adoption of electric vehicles has been hindered by persistent concerns regarding driving range, charging time, and long-term battery reliability—collectively referred to as range anxiety. While lithium-ion batteries have dominated the market due to their mature technology and relatively predictable performance, their theoretical energy density is approaching practical limits, prompting the search for next-generation chemistries capable of delivering significantly higher specific energy. Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) battery technology has emerged as a leading candidate, offering a theoretical specific capacity of approximately 1672 mAh/g for sulfur, nearly five times that of conventional lithium-ion cathodes. Despite this promise, Li-S batteries suffer from severe degradation mechanisms, including rapid capacity fade, high self-discharge rates, and poor coulombic efficiency, primarily attributable to the dissolution and migration of intermediate polysulfide species between the cathode and anode—a phenomenon known as the polysulfide shuttle. These chemical complexities result in an electrical behavior that is fundamentally distinct from that of lithium-ion batteries, rendering conventional modeling approaches inadequate.

In lithium-ion systems, equivalent circuit networks (ECNs), such as the Thevenin model comprising a series resistor and one or more parallel resistor-capacitor (RC) branches, have been successfully employed in battery management systems to estimate state-of-charge (SoC) and state-of-health (SoH) with low computational overhead. These models rely on the assumption that the open-circuit voltage (OCV) exhibits a stable, monotonic relationship with SoC, and that key electrical parameters—such as internal resistance and capacitance—vary slowly and predictably with temperature, current, and cycle count. However, in Li-S systems, these assumptions are invalid. The OCV does not follow a single-valued function of SoC; instead, it remains nearly constant across two distinct voltage plateaus during discharge, with the low plateau (approximately 2.1 V) exhibiting a strong tendency to return to a fixed voltage value after current interruption, regardless of prior SoC. Furthermore, the presence of soluble high-order polysulfides and insoluble low-order sulfides leads to dynamic changes in electrolyte viscosity, electrode passivation, and interfacial charge transfer resistance, all of which vary nonlinearly with SoC, current rate, and temperature. These effects manifest as non-monotonic variations in internal resistance, with a pronounced peak occurring near the transition between the two voltage plateaus, and as significant current-dependent shifts in usable capacity due to kinetic limitations in sulfur utilization.

Existing models for Li-S batteries have largely been developed for post-mortem analysis of electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) data, which provides a snapshot of cell behavior at a single, static SoC. Such models are unsuitable for real-time operation because they fail to account for the continuous evolution of internal states during discharge or charge cycles. A few operational models have been proposed using multi-RC networks, but these typically require high computational resources, are not easily parameterized under transient conditions, and do not adequately capture the temperature dependence of key parameters. Moreover, the parameter identification techniques employed in prior work often rely on steady-state assumptions or simplified pulse protocols that neglect the interaction between current magnitude, SoC trajectory, and temperature history. As a result, these models exhibit significant prediction errors under realistic driving profiles, particularly during transitions between voltage plateaus or under variable ambient temperatures.

The absence of a robust, low-complexity, temperature-adaptive model capable of predicting Li-S cell voltage with high fidelity under dynamic operating conditions has impeded the development of reliable battery management systems for electric vehicles equipped with Li-S chemistry. Without such a model, it is not possible to accurately estimate remaining range, prevent over-discharge, optimize charging protocols, or detect early signs of degradation—all of which are essential for consumer confidence and regulatory compliance. Furthermore, the lack of a standardized modeling framework has led to fragmented research efforts, with each study employing custom test protocols and parameterization methods that are neither reproducible nor scalable. There is therefore a pressing need for a unified, experimentally validated, and computationally tractable modeling approach that explicitly accounts for the unique electrochemical characteristics of Li-S batteries while remaining compatible with the processing constraints of embedded BMS hardware. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel behavioral parameterization of a linearized Thevenin model, coupled with a prediction-error minimization-based identification protocol that leverages mixed-current pulse testing to extract temperature- and current-dependent parameters, and by providing a method for interpolating these parameters across temperature ranges using polynomial functions with smooth transition boundaries, thereby enabling real-time, high-accuracy voltage prediction without requiring excessive memory or computational power.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention comprises a system and method for modeling the dynamic electrical behavior of a lithium-sulfur battery cell under variable operating conditions, including changes in state of charge, discharge current, and ambient temperature, for the purpose of enabling accurate real-time state estimation within a battery management system. The system comprises a battery model structure defined by a linearized state-space representation derived from a Thevenin equivalent circuit, wherein the model parameters are expressed not in terms of conventional electrical components but as behavioral variables that directly correspond to observable cell characteristics such as steady-state voltage drop, dynamic response bandwidth, and the fraction of voltage response occurring instantaneously versus with delay. These behavioral parameters are identified through a prediction error minimization technique applied to voltage and current measurements obtained during a mixed-current pulse discharge test, wherein multiple discharge pulses of differing magnitudes are applied sequentially to the cell, with sufficient rest periods between pulses to allow partial recovery of the open-circuit voltage, thereby isolating the effects of current magnitude on parameter values without inducing significant cell degradation. The identified parameters are then modeled as continuous functions of state of charge, with distinct polynomial representations for the high and low voltage plateaus, connected by a differentiable transition function that ensures smooth and continuous variation of model outputs across the entire discharge range. The temperature dependence of these parameter functions is captured through a set of polynomial coefficients that are interpolated linearly between discrete temperature points, yielding a fully continuous, three-dimensional parameter surface that adapts dynamically to changes in ambient temperature without requiring high-dimensional lookup tables or nonlinear optimization during operation. The resulting model is implemented as a state-space system with a state vector comprising state of charge and capacitor voltage, and is structured to be compatible with observer algorithms such as the extended Kalman filter, enabling simultaneous estimation of state of charge and terminal voltage with minimal computational overhead. The invention further includes a method for validating the model using real-world drive cycles, demonstrating that the model achieves a root mean square error in terminal voltage prediction below 40 millivolts across a wide range of temperatures and current profiles, even when operating under conditions not used during parameter identification. This level of accuracy is achieved despite the model’s simplicity, which employs only a single RC branch and neglects explicit modeling of polysulfide shuttle dynamics or precipitation kinetics, thereby proving that the behavioral parameterization and temperature interpolation framework are sufficient to capture the dominant electrochemical phenomena governing Li-S cell behavior under operational conditions. The invention thus provides a complete, end-to-end framework for generating, identifying, and deploying a high-fidelity, low-complexity battery model tailored specifically for lithium-sulfur chemistry, enabling the practical implementation of advanced battery management systems in electric vehicles and other applications where accurate, real-time state estimation is critical to performance, safety, and longevity.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE EMBODIMENTS

### Generating the Cell Model Module—Equivalent Circuit Example

The cell model module is constructed as a linearized state-space representation derived from a modified Thevenin equivalent circuit, wherein the traditional electrical components—namely, the series resistance, the charge transfer resistance, and the double-layer capacitance—are reparameterized into behavioral variables that reflect the observable macroscopic response of the lithium-sulfur cell rather than its underlying physical structure. This behavioral reparameterization is essential to the invention, as it decouples the model from the ambiguities of physical interpretation that arise when attempting to assign electrochemical meaning to circuit elements in a system governed by complex, multi-step redox reactions and heterogeneous phase transformations. The model is defined by a set of state variables, an input variable, and an output variable, with the state variables comprising the state of charge and the voltage across the dynamic RC branch, the input variable being the net current flowing into or out of the cell, and the output variable being the measured terminal voltage. The model is expressed in continuous-time state-space form, with the state equations describing the evolution of the state variables over time and the output equation relating the state variables and input to the measurable terminal voltage. The state equation for the state of charge is derived from the fundamental relationship between charge and current, integrating the net current over time and normalizing by the cell’s rated capacity to yield a dimensionless quantity ranging from zero to one. The state equation for the capacitor voltage is derived from Kirchhoff’s laws applied to the RC branch, incorporating the time constant defined by the product of the charge transfer resistance and the double-layer capacitance, as well as the influence of the state of charge on both resistance and capacitance. The output equation combines the open-circuit voltage, which is a function of state of charge and temperature, with the voltage drops across the series resistance and the dynamic RC branch, the latter of which depends on the capacitor voltage and the fraction of the total voltage response that is delayed. This fraction, termed the dynamic fraction, is a behavioral parameter that quantifies the proportion of the voltage transient attributable to the RC branch rather than the instantaneous ohmic drop, thereby enabling the model to capture the non-instantaneous response characteristic of Li-S cells during current transients. The open-circuit voltage is modeled as a piecewise polynomial function of state of charge, with separate polynomial expressions defined for the high-voltage plateau and the low-voltage plateau, each of which is determined empirically from experimental data collected during pulse discharge tests. The transition between these two polynomials is governed by a smooth, differentiable function that ensures continuity of both the open-circuit voltage and its derivative with respect to state of charge, preventing discontinuities that would otherwise introduce numerical instability during real-time estimation. The series resistance and the charge transfer resistance are similarly modeled as polynomial functions of state of charge, with the charge transfer resistance exhibiting a pronounced peak near the transition between the two plateaus, consistent with the observed increase in electrolyte viscosity due to maximum polysulfide concentration. The double-layer capacitance, in contrast, is modeled as a single polynomial function across the entire discharge range, as its variation with state of charge is found to be relatively mild and consistent across different current rates and temperatures. All polynomial coefficients are determined through nonlinear least-squares fitting of experimental data, with constraints applied to ensure physical plausibility—for example, the open-circuit voltage must remain within the thermodynamic bounds of the lithium-sulfur redox reactions, and the resistances must remain positive for all states of charge. The resulting model is inherently nonlinear due to the state-dependent nature of its parameters, but it is linearized around a nominal operating point defined by a constant current input and the corresponding steady-state state of charge and capacitor voltage, yielding a linear time-varying state-space system that is suitable for real-time implementation on embedded processors. The linearization process introduces two additional terms in the state matrix that account for the coupling between state of charge and open-circuit voltage, and between state of charge and capacitor voltage, respectively. These terms are not present in conventional equivalent circuit models for lithium-ion batteries and are critical to the accuracy of the model in capturing the transient voltage drop observed during high-current pulses in Li-S cells, where the state of charge changes significantly over the duration of the pulse. The linearized model is then transformed into a canonical form that facilitates parameter identification, with the state-space matrices expressed in terms of the behavioral parameters rather than the underlying electrical components, thereby simplifying the optimization process and improving numerical conditioning. The final model structure is compact, requiring only a small number of state variables and parameters, and is designed to be computationally efficient, with the majority of the computational load concentrated in the offline parameter identification phase, leaving only matrix-vector multiplications and simple arithmetic operations required during real-time operation. This structure enables deployment on low-power battery management systems with limited memory and processing capability, making it uniquely suited for integration into electric vehicle platforms where real-time voltage prediction is required for state estimation, thermal management, and power allocation.

### Generating the Memory Effect Model

The memory effect model is a critical component of the invention, designed to capture the hysteresis-like behavior exhibited by lithium-sulfur cells during repeated current transients, wherein the terminal voltage at a given state of charge depends not only on the current value of the state of charge but also on the history of recent current flow, particularly the magnitude and duration of preceding discharge or charge pulses. This memory effect arises from the slow diffusion and redistribution of polysulfide species within the porous cathode structure, the gradual dissolution and re-precipitation of lithium sulfide films on the anode surface, and the time-dependent changes in electrolyte composition and viscosity that occur during cycling. Unlike conventional lithium-ion batteries, where such effects are typically negligible over short time scales, the Li-S cell exhibits a pronounced dependence on prior operating conditions, with the open-circuit voltage returning to a fixed value after prolonged rest, regardless of the prior state of charge, and with the internal resistance exhibiting different values depending on whether the cell has been recently subjected to high-current discharge or low-current rest. The memory effect model is implemented as an auxiliary state variable that evolves in parallel with the primary state of charge and capacitor voltage, representing the accumulated influence of recent current history on the cell’s internal state. This auxiliary state is governed by a first-order differential equation whose time constant is determined empirically from relaxation measurements, and whose input is a filtered version of the net current, scaled by a gain factor that depends on the current magnitude and the current state of charge. The gain factor is modeled as a piecewise function that increases during high-current discharge, particularly in the low-voltage plateau, where polysulfide precipitation is most active, and decreases during rest or low-current operation, reflecting the slower kinetics of polysulfide re-dissolution. The output of the memory effect model is a voltage offset that is added to the terminal voltage predicted by the primary equivalent circuit model, thereby correcting for the deviation between the measured voltage and the model prediction that occurs when the cell has been subjected to recent transients. This offset is not a fixed correction but a dynamic, state-dependent adjustment that evolves over time, ensuring that the model adapts to the cell’s recent operating history without requiring explicit knowledge of the past current profile. The memory effect model is trained using a dataset of relaxation experiments, wherein the cell is discharged at a constant current to a specific state of charge, then allowed to rest for varying durations, with the terminal voltage recorded throughout the rest period. The recovered open-circuit voltage is then fitted to a decaying exponential function, whose time constant and asymptotic value are used to calibrate the memory effect model’s time constant and steady-state gain. The model is further refined by applying a series of mixed-current pulses with varying durations and magnitudes, and by comparing the model-predicted voltage with the measured voltage during the recovery phase, adjusting the gain and time constant to minimize the prediction error. The resulting memory effect model is incorporated into the overall state-space representation as an additional state variable, with its own state equation and output contribution, thereby extending the original model to a three-dimensional state space. The inclusion of this memory effect model significantly improves the accuracy of voltage prediction during real-world drive cycles, particularly during transitions between high-current and low-current operation, such as those encountered during urban driving or regenerative braking scenarios. Without this component, the model exhibits systematic errors of up to 80 millivolts during recovery phases, whereas with the memory effect model, these errors are reduced to less than 20 millivolts. Importantly, the memory effect model does not require additional sensor inputs or external data; it is entirely self-contained within the model structure and is updated automatically during operation based on the measured current and the estimated state of charge. This makes it fully compatible with existing battery management system architectures, which typically rely on current and voltage measurements as their sole inputs. The memory effect model is also temperature-dependent, with the time constant and gain factor varying as functions of ambient temperature, as determined through experiments conducted at multiple temperature levels. The temperature dependence is captured through linear interpolation of pre-identified parameters, similar to the method used for the primary model parameters, ensuring that the memory effect is accurately represented across the full operating temperature range of the battery. The memory effect model thus provides a novel and effective means of accounting for the long-term, history-dependent behavior of lithium-sulfur cells without increasing the computational burden beyond what is acceptable for embedded systems, thereby enabling a level of fidelity in voltage prediction that was previously unattainable with conventional equivalent circuit models.

### Parameterisation by Prediction Error Minimisation (PEM)

The parameterisation of the battery model is achieved through a rigorous application of prediction error minimisation (PEM), a system identification technique that seeks to determine the set of model parameters that minimises the difference between the model’s predicted output and the actual measured output over a given time interval. Unlike conventional parameter identification methods that rely on steady-state measurements or simplified pulse protocols, the PEM approach employed in this invention utilises a mixed-current pulse discharge test, wherein a sequence of discharge pulses of varying magnitude is applied to the cell, each pulse followed by a rest period sufficient to allow partial recovery of the open-circuit voltage. The duration of each pulse is selected to induce a measurable change in state of charge while remaining within the linear operating range of the cell, and the magnitude of the pulses is chosen to span the full range of expected operating currents, from low-rate discharge conditions typical of cruising to high-rate discharge conditions representative of acceleration or hill climbing. The voltage and current measurements obtained during these tests are recorded at a high sampling rate, typically exceeding 10 Hz, to capture the transient dynamics of the cell response. The PEM algorithm then treats the battery model as a black-box system, with the model parameters as unknown variables to be optimised, and defines a cost function as the sum of squared prediction errors over the entire dataset, where the prediction error at each time step is the difference between the measured terminal voltage and the voltage predicted by the model given the measured current and the estimated state of charge. The optimisation is performed using a numerical gradient-based method, such as the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm, with constraints applied to ensure that all parameters remain physically meaningful—for example, resistances must remain positive, capacitances must remain non-negative, and the open-circuit voltage must remain within the thermodynamic bounds of the lithium-sulfur redox reactions. The PEM algorithm is applied independently to each pulse, with the state of charge at the beginning of each pulse treated as a known initial condition, and the parameters estimated over a window of 300 seconds before and after the pulse to capture both the transient response and the recovery dynamics. This extended estimation window is critical to the success of the method, as it allows the algorithm to distinguish between the immediate ohmic response, the delayed RC response, and the slow recovery due to polysulfide redistribution, thereby enabling the accurate separation of the model’s various contributing effects. The resulting parameter estimates are then interpolated across the state of charge axis to generate continuous functions for each behavioral parameter, with the interpolation performed using polynomial functions of degree three to five, depending on the complexity of the parameter variation. The polynomial coefficients are determined through nonlinear least-squares fitting, with smoothing constraints applied to prevent overfitting to noise in the data. The PEM approach is further enhanced by incorporating a weighting scheme that assigns higher importance to data points during the transient phase of the pulse, where the model’s predictive accuracy is most critical for real-time estimation, and lower importance to data points during the rest period, where the voltage change is dominated by slow diffusion processes that are not captured by the equivalent circuit model. This weighting scheme ensures that the optimisation focuses on the regions of the data that are most relevant for battery management applications, such as state-of-charge estimation and power capability prediction, rather than on the long-term relaxation behavior, which is better suited for post-processing analysis. The use of PEM in this context is novel, as prior attempts to identify Li-S battery parameters have relied on manual fitting, graphical methods, or simplified linear regression techniques that are incapable of capturing the nonlinear, state-dependent relationships that govern the cell’s behavior. The PEM approach, by contrast, provides a statistically rigorous, automated, and reproducible method for parameter identification that is insensitive to measurement noise and capable of handling the complex, multi-dimensional parameter space inherent in Li-S chemistry. The resulting parameter set is not only accurate but also robust, as demonstrated by its ability to predict voltage responses under conditions not used during identification, including different current profiles, temperature trajectories, and rest durations. The PEM method is further validated by comparing the identified parameters across multiple cells of the same chemistry, showing consistent trends in parameter variation with state of charge and temperature, thereby confirming that the identified parameters reflect intrinsic cell behavior rather than cell-to-cell variability. The combination of mixed-current pulse testing and PEM-based parameterisation thus forms the core of the invention’s methodology, providing a complete, automated, and scientifically grounded framework for generating high-fidelity battery models that are suitable for real-time deployment in battery management systems.
