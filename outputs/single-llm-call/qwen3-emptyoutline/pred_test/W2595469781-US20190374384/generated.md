# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Glaucoma represents one of the most prevalent and insidious causes of irreversible vision loss worldwide, affecting millions of individuals across all age groups and ethnicities. The pathophysiology of glaucoma is characterized by progressive degeneration of the optic nerve, often associated with elevated intraocular pressure, although normal-tension glaucoma demonstrates that pressure is not the sole contributing factor. Despite advances in pharmacological and laser-based interventions, a significant subset of patients continues to experience disease progression despite maximal medical therapy. For these individuals, surgical intervention remains the cornerstone of long-term intraocular pressure management. The primary objective of glaucoma surgery is to establish a new, sustainable pathway for the outflow of aqueous humor from the anterior chamber of the eye, thereby reducing intraocular pressure and halting or slowing the progression of optic nerve damage.

Historically, trabeculectomy has been regarded as the gold standard surgical procedure for adult primary open-angle glaucoma. This technique involves the creation of a partial-thickness scleral flap, followed by a trephination of the underlying trabecular meshwork and ciliary body, allowing aqueous humor to bypass the conventional outflow pathway and drain into a subconjunctival bleb. While effective in many cases, the long-term success of trabeculectomy is frequently compromised by the body’s natural wound-healing response, which leads to fibrosis and scarring at the surgical site. This fibrotic encapsulation progressively obstructs the newly created drainage channel, resulting in failure of the procedure within months to years after surgery. The incidence of such failure is even higher in pediatric populations and in patients with secondary glaucomas, including neovascular, uveitic, or post-traumatic forms, where the inflammatory milieu and altered tissue architecture further exacerbate the fibrotic response.

To address the limitations of trabeculectomy, particularly in complex or refractory cases, glaucoma drainage implants were developed. These devices consist of a small, rigid or semi-rigid plate, typically made of silicone or polypropylene, affixed to the scleral surface, connected via a narrow tube to the anterior chamber. The plate functions as a reservoir, collecting aqueous humor that drains through the tube and then diffusing it into the surrounding episcleral and subconjunctival tissues. Although these implants have expanded the surgical armamentarium for difficult-to-treat glaucomas, their long-term efficacy remains suboptimal. The principal cause of failure is the formation of a dense, avascular fibrous capsule around the plate, which impedes the passive diffusion of aqueous humor into the surrounding tissue. This fibrotic reaction is not merely a passive response to foreign material but an active, dynamic process involving the recruitment of fibroblasts, myofibroblasts, macrophages, and other inflammatory cells, which deposit extracellular matrix components such as collagen, fibronectin, and proteoglycans. Over time, this capsule thickens, becomes less permeable, and ultimately isolates the implant from the host tissue, rendering it functionally inert.

Minimally invasive glaucoma surgeries (MIGS) were introduced more recently as a safer alternative to traditional procedures, particularly for patients with mild to moderate disease. These techniques, which include trabecular micro-bypass stents, suprachoroidal shunts, and ab interno canaloplasty, aim to enhance outflow through the native drainage system with minimal tissue disruption. While MIGS offer improved safety profiles and faster recovery times, they are generally not suitable for advanced glaucoma and often provide only modest intraocular pressure reductions. Moreover, even these less invasive procedures are not immune to fibrotic complications, particularly when the body’s healing response is robust or when the device is placed in an environment of chronic inflammation.

The persistent challenge across all forms of glaucoma surgery—trabeculectomy, drainage implants, and MIGS—is the universal tendency of biological tissues to encapsulate foreign bodies. This phenomenon is not unique to ophthalmology but is a well-documented response in all implantable medical devices, from cardiac pacemakers to neural electrodes. The underlying mechanisms involve mechanical mismatch between the stiff, rigid nature of conventional implants and the soft, dynamic, and compliant nature of ocular tissues. When a rigid device is implanted into the flexible, constantly moving environment of the eye, it creates persistent micro-motions and shear stresses at the tissue-implant interface. These mechanical perturbations activate mechanosensitive signaling pathways in resident fibroblasts and macrophages, triggering a cascade of pro-inflammatory cytokine release, including interleukin-1β, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, and transforming growth factor-beta. These mediators, in turn, promote fibroblast proliferation, differentiation into myofibroblasts, and excessive extracellular matrix deposition. Furthermore, the solid, impermeable surface of conventional implants physically disrupts the native microvasculature and cellular architecture of the episcleral and subconjunctival tissues, preventing the re-establishment of normal tissue homeostasis and creating a hypoxic, pro-fibrotic niche.

Attempts to mitigate this fibrotic response have included the use of anti-metabolites such as mitomycin-C and 5-fluorouracil, which inhibit fibroblast proliferation during surgery. While these agents have demonstrated some success in prolonging the patency of trabeculectomy blebs, their use is associated with significant complications, including corneal toxicity, scleral thinning, bleb leaks, and endophthalmitis. Corticosteroids, although safer, have shown limited efficacy in preventing long-term encapsulation, particularly in high-risk patients. Other strategies have focused on modifying the physical properties of the implant materials—such as switching from rigid polypropylene to softer silicone—or altering the surface topography through coatings, porous structures, or bioactive molecules. Some experimental devices have incorporated anti-inflammatory agents, amniotic membrane, or ferrofluids to modulate the tissue response. However, despite these innovations, all existing implants continue to elicit a measurable fibrotic reaction, with capsule thicknesses consistently reported in the range of 50 to 100 micrometers in preclinical models and even greater in human histological analyses.

Recent advances in neural implant technology have provided critical insights into the design of biocompatible devices that can integrate seamlessly with soft, dynamic tissues. Studies on chronic neural electrodes have demonstrated that the most successful implants are those that mimic the mechanical compliance of native tissue and are structured as porous, interconnected microfluidic networks rather than solid, monolithic bodies. These implants, often fabricated from ultra-thin, flexible polymers with microscale channels, allow for the passive exchange of fluids and signaling molecules between the implant and the surrounding tissue. Crucially, they permit the infiltration of capillaries and the migration of immune cells, thereby preserving the physiological architecture of the host tissue. This approach effectively eliminates the mechanical mismatch and prevents the formation of a dense, isolating fibrous capsule. Instead of being perceived as a foreign object, the implant becomes a functional extension of the tissue itself, recognized by the body as a continuous, non-disruptive structure.

Inspired by these findings, the present invention introduces a novel glaucoma drainage device that fundamentally reimagines the design of the traditional drainage plate. Rather than employing a solid, impermeable plate, the invention replaces it with a microfluidic meshwork composed of interconnected, cellular-dimensioned channels. This meshwork is fabricated from a highly flexible, biocompatible polymer with a thickness on the order of micrometers, enabling it to conform precisely to the curvature and motion of the scleral surface. The dimensions of the microchannels are carefully engineered to permit the passive flow of aqueous humor while simultaneously allowing the infiltration of microvasculature and immune cells. By eliminating the rigid, solid interface and replacing it with a permeable, flexible, and structurally continuous network, the device minimizes mechanical stress, preserves tissue integrity, and prevents the activation of fibrotic pathways. This innovation represents a paradigm shift in glaucoma implant design—not merely an incremental improvement, but a re-engineering of the device-tissue interface at the fundamental level of mechanical and biological interaction.

The invention is not an extension of prior art but a radical departure from the conventional paradigm of solid, encapsulation-prone implants. It draws upon principles from microfluidics, biomaterials science, and tissue engineering to create a device that does not resist the body’s healing response but instead harmonizes with it. The microfluidic meshwork does not attempt to block fibrosis through chemical suppression or physical barrier; rather, it prevents fibrosis from occurring in the first place by eliminating the conditions that trigger it. This approach offers the potential for unprecedented long-term patency, reduced need for adjunctive therapies, and improved outcomes across all categories of glaucoma—primary, secondary, pediatric, and refractory. The following sections describe in detail the structure, fabrication, and functional principles of this novel device, as well as the experimental evidence supporting its efficacy in suppressing fibrotic encapsulation.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a novel glaucoma drainage device designed to facilitate the long-term outflow of aqueous humor while minimizing the formation of fibrotic tissue around the implant. Unlike conventional glaucoma drainage implants, which consist of a rigid or semi-rigid solid plate connected to an outflow tube, the invention comprises a microfluidic meshwork that replaces the solid plate entirely. This meshwork is composed of a thin, flexible, biocompatible polymer substrate patterned with an array of interconnected microfluidic channels, each of cellular dimensions, arranged in a grid-like configuration that spans a surface area sufficient to support adequate aqueous humor drainage. The microchannels are designed to permit the passive flow of aqueous humor from the subconjunctival space into the surrounding episcleral tissues while simultaneously allowing the infiltration of capillaries, immune cells, and extracellular matrix components, thereby preserving the natural physiological architecture of the host tissue.

The key innovation of the invention lies in its structural and mechanical design, which eliminates the mechanical mismatch between the implant and the ocular tissue. Conventional implants, being rigid and solid, create persistent shear forces and micro-motions at the tissue interface, triggering a cascade of inflammatory and fibrotic responses that culminate in the formation of a dense, avascular fibrous capsule. In contrast, the microfluidic meshwork of the present invention is ultra-flexible, with a thickness on the order of a few micrometers, enabling it to conform dynamically to the natural curvature, movement, and biomechanical properties of the scleral and conjunctival tissues. This conformability ensures that the implant moves in concert with the surrounding tissue, eliminating the mechanical stress that initiates fibrosis. Furthermore, the interconnected microchannels, with inner diameters ranging from 5 to 15 micrometers and a grid period of approximately 80 to 120 micrometers, are sized to permit the passage of fluid, proteins, and cellular elements while preventing the accumulation of fibroblasts and myofibroblasts in a manner that would lead to encapsulation.

The microfluidic meshwork is fabricated using advanced photolithographic techniques, enabling precise control over channel geometry, density, and surface topology. The fabrication process involves the sequential patterning of sacrificial and structural photoresists on a silicon wafer, followed by the release of the final structure into a biocompatible buffer solution. The resulting meshwork is inherently porous, with a high surface-area-to-volume ratio, and exhibits mechanical properties that closely approximate those of native ocular tissues, including low elastic modulus, high tensile compliance, and resistance to fatigue under cyclic motion. The device is designed to be implanted onto the episcleral surface in a manner analogous to conventional glaucoma drainage implants, but without the need for sutures or fixation mechanisms, as its flexibility and low profile allow it to remain in place through natural tissue adhesion and pressure.

In a preferred embodiment, the microfluidic meshwork is integrated with a conventional outflow tube that connects to the anterior chamber of the eye, thereby forming a complete glaucoma drainage system. However, the invention also encompasses standalone configurations in which the meshwork serves as a passive drainage platform, relying on diffusion and interstitial flow rather than directed fluidic conduits. The absence of a solid plate eliminates the physical barrier that prevents vascularization and tissue integration, allowing the meshwork to become seamlessly incorporated into the subconjunctival space over time. Histological analysis has demonstrated that, in contrast to conventional implants, the microfluidic meshwork elicits minimal inflammatory response and virtually no fibrotic encapsulation, with capsule thicknesses measured in the single-digit micrometer range compared to over 80 micrometers for control implants.

The invention further includes methods for manufacturing the microfluidic meshwork using scalable, reproducible techniques that are compatible with sterile, clinical-grade production. The materials used in fabrication are biocompatible, non-toxic, and resistant to degradation under physiological conditions, ensuring long-term stability within the ocular environment. The surface of the meshwork may be optionally modified with bioactive coatings to further enhance tissue integration, such as endothelial cell-adhesive peptides, anti-inflammatory agents, or extracellular matrix-mimetic polymers, although such modifications are not required for the device to function effectively.

The methods of use for the invention encompass surgical implantation techniques that are compatible with existing glaucoma surgical protocols. The device may be implanted via a transconjunctival approach, positioned on the scleral surface in the equatorial region, and covered with a conjunctival flap, similar to standard glaucoma drainage implant procedures. The flexibility of the meshwork allows it to be gently folded or manipulated during insertion without compromising its structural integrity, and it rapidly assumes the contour of the underlying tissue upon placement. Postoperative care follows standard protocols, including topical antibiotics and corticosteroids, although the invention’s inherent biocompatibility significantly reduces the need for prolonged or high-dose anti-inflammatory therapy.

The invention provides a transformative solution to the longstanding problem of fibrotic encapsulation in glaucoma surgery. By replacing the solid plate with a microfluidic meshwork that mimics the mechanical and structural properties of native tissue, the device fundamentally alters the host-implant interaction, preventing the initiation of fibrotic pathways rather than attempting to suppress them after they have begun. This results in a device that remains patent and functional over extended periods, offering the potential for durable intraocular pressure control with minimal complications. The invention is applicable across all forms of glaucoma, including refractory, pediatric, and secondary cases, and represents a significant advancement in the field of ophthalmic implants by shifting the paradigm from mechanical obstruction to biological integration.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Definitions

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms are defined with specificity to ensure clarity and consistency in the interpretation of the claims and the description. The term “microfluidic meshwork” refers to a thin, flexible, porous structure composed of an array of interconnected microchannels, each having a cross-sectional dimension on the order of cellular scale, typically ranging from 5 to 20 micrometers in inner diameter, arranged in a periodic or semi-periodic grid pattern with a spacing between channels of approximately 80 to 120 micrometers. The meshwork is fabricated from a biocompatible polymer material that exhibits mechanical compliance similar to that of ocular tissues, with a thickness of no more than 10 micrometers, and is designed to permit the passive flow of aqueous humor while allowing the infiltration of microvasculature and immune cells. The term “cellular-dimensioned” denotes channel sizes that are comparable to the diameter of individual cells, such as fibroblasts, macrophages, or endothelial cells, such that the channels are large enough to permit cellular migration and fluid exchange but small enough to prevent the aggregation and proliferation of fibrotic cell populations in a manner that leads to encapsulation.

The term “ultra-flexible” describes the mechanical property of the meshwork such that it can conform to the natural curvature and dynamic motion of the scleral and conjunctival surfaces without generating significant interfacial shear stress or mechanical strain. This flexibility is achieved through a combination of material selection, thin-film geometry, and microstructural design, resulting in an elastic modulus of less than 1 megapascal, which is comparable to that of soft connective tissues. The term “biocompatible polymer” refers to synthetic or natural polymers that do not elicit a significant inflammatory, cytotoxic, or immunogenic response when implanted in ocular tissues, including but not limited to polydimethylsiloxane, polyurethane, polyethylene glycol-based copolymers, and fluorinated ethylene propylene, provided they are processed to remove residual monomers, catalysts, or solvents.

The term “aqueous humor outflow” refers to the passive or directed movement of aqueous fluid from the anterior chamber of the eye into the subconjunctival or episcleral space, with the purpose of reducing intraocular pressure. The term “fibrotic encapsulation” denotes the pathological process in which a dense, avascular layer of collagen-rich extracellular matrix, formed by activated fibroblasts and myofibroblasts, surrounds an implanted device, thereby impeding fluid diffusion and leading to device failure. The term “subconjunctival space” refers to the potential space between the conjunctival epithelium and the underlying episcleral tissue, which serves as the primary drainage pathway for aqueous humor in glaucoma surgeries. The term “episcleral surface” refers to the outermost layer of the sclera, immediately beneath the conjunctiva, which is the site of implantation for glaucoma drainage devices.

The term “photolithographic fabrication” refers to a microfabrication process involving the use of light-sensitive photoresists, photomasks, and UV exposure to pattern microscale features on a substrate, followed by etching or sacrificial release to produce the final structure. The term “sacrificial photoresist” denotes a photoresist material that is selectively removed after patterning to create voids or channels, such as LOR 5A or AZ1505, which are dissolved using solvents or plasma etching without damaging the structural photoresist. The term “grid period” refers to the center-to-center distance between adjacent microchannels in the meshwork pattern, typically measured in micrometers, and determines the density and porosity of the structure.

The term “passive drainage” refers to the movement of aqueous humor through the meshwork via diffusion, osmotic gradients, and interstitial flow, without the need for active pumping or pressurized conduits. The term “tissue integration” describes the biological process by which host cells, including endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and immune cells, migrate into and around the microfluidic meshwork, establishing functional continuity with the surrounding tissue and preventing the formation of a discrete fibrous capsule. The term “mechanical mismatch” refers to the disparity in elastic modulus, compliance, and dynamic behavior between an implanted device and the native tissue into which it is placed, a condition known to trigger chronic inflammation and fibrosis.

The term “monolayer configuration” refers to the state in which the microfluidic meshwork is implanted as a single, continuous layer without folding, overlapping, or stacking, thereby maintaining its intended mechanical and fluidic properties. The term “multilayer stacking” refers to the unintended or accidental folding or overlapping of the meshwork during implantation, resulting in regions where multiple layers are superimposed, which may compromise the device’s biocompatibility and lead to localized inflammatory responses. The term “inflammatory cell activation” refers to the recruitment and morphological change of immune cells, such as macrophages and lymphocytes, in response to foreign material, which may manifest as increased cell density, cytokine secretion, or granuloma formation.

The term “intraocular pressure” refers to the hydrostatic pressure within the anterior chamber of the eye, typically measured in millimeters of mercury, and is the primary therapeutic target in glaucoma management. The term “fibrous capsule thickness” refers to the distance, measured in micrometers, between the surface of the implant and the outermost boundary of the fibrotic tissue layer, as determined by histological analysis of hematoxylin and eosin-stained tissue sections. The term “biological integration” encompasses the combined effects of mechanical conformity, vascular infiltration, immune modulation, and extracellular matrix remodeling that result in the device being perceived by the host as a non-threatening, continuous extension of native tissue rather than a foreign body.

These definitions are intended to be used consistently throughout the specification and claims and are not to be interpreted in a manner inconsistent with the context of the invention as described herein.

### Glaucoma Drainage Meshwork

The glaucoma drainage meshwork of the present invention is a revolutionary device designed to overcome the fundamental limitations of conventional glaucoma drainage implants by fundamentally redefining the interface between the implant and the host tissue. Unlike traditional implants, which consist of a solid, rigid plate that acts as a physical barrier to tissue integration, the meshwork is a thin, permeable, and ultra-flexible structure composed of an intricate network of microfluidic channels that are engineered to mimic the structural and mechanical properties of native ocular tissues. The meshwork is not merely a modified version of existing devices; it represents a paradigm shift in design philosophy, moving from a model of obstruction and suppression to one of integration and harmony.

The structural architecture of the meshwork is defined by a two-dimensional grid of interconnected microchannels, each with an inner diameter ranging from 5 to 15 micrometers and an outer diameter of approximately 20 micrometers. These dimensions are not arbitrary but are the result of precise computational modeling and biomechanical optimization to ensure that the channels are large enough to permit the free flow of aqueous humor at physiologically relevant pressures and flow rates—typically 1 to 3 microliters per minute at an intraocular pressure of 10 to 15 millimeters of mercury—while remaining small enough to prevent the infiltration and proliferation of fibroblasts and myofibroblasts, which are the primary cellular mediators of fibrotic encapsulation. The spacing between adjacent channels, known as the grid period, is maintained at approximately 100 micrometers, a value selected to balance fluidic conductance with structural integrity and to allow sufficient space for the ingrowth of microvasculature and immune cells.

The meshwork is fabricated from a biocompatible polymer with an elastic modulus of less than 1 megapascal, ensuring that it is as compliant as the surrounding episcleral and conjunctival tissues. This mechanical conformity is critical, as it eliminates the persistent shear forces and micro-motion that occur when a rigid device is implanted into a soft, dynamic tissue environment. Conventional implants, whether made of silicone or polypropylene, exhibit elastic moduli on the order of tens to hundreds of megapascals, creating a stark discontinuity in mechanical behavior that triggers a cascade of inflammatory signaling. The microfluidic meshwork, by contrast, deforms and moves in concert with the eye during blinking, eye rotation, and other physiological movements, thereby minimizing mechanical stress at the tissue-implant interface and preventing the activation of mechanosensitive pathways that lead to fibrosis.

The thickness of the meshwork is maintained at approximately 4 micrometers, a dimension that is sufficiently thin to allow for complete conformation to the irregular topography of the scleral surface, yet robust enough to withstand handling during surgical implantation without tearing or permanent deformation. This thinness also ensures that the meshwork does not protrude significantly into the subconjunctival space, reducing the risk of conjunctival erosion or exposure. The surface of the meshwork is smooth and free of micro-roughness, which further reduces the potential for protein adsorption and cellular adhesion that could initiate fibrotic responses. The material composition of the meshwork is selected to be chemically inert, non-degradable under physiological conditions, and resistant to biofilm formation, ensuring long-term stability within the ocular environment.

The interconnected nature of the microchannels is perhaps the most critical feature of the invention. Rather than being isolated conduits, the channels form a continuous, three-dimensional network that allows for bidirectional fluid exchange and the free movement of cells and signaling molecules. This connectivity enables the diffusion of oxygen, nutrients, and growth factors from the surrounding tissue into the meshwork, and conversely, the efflux of aqueous humor and metabolic waste products from the meshwork into the tissue. This dynamic exchange prevents the development of hypoxic or inflammatory microenvironments that would otherwise promote fibroblast activation and collagen deposition. In contrast, solid implants create isolated compartments beneath their surfaces, where fluid stagnation, low oxygen tension, and accumulation of inflammatory mediators foster a pro-fibrotic niche.

The geometric design of the meshwork also permits the infiltration of capillaries and endothelial cells, which can migrate into the spaces between the channels and establish a vascular network that integrates the device into the host’s circulatory system. This vascularization is not merely a passive consequence of implantation but an active, beneficial process that promotes tissue homeostasis and prevents the formation of a fibrous capsule. The presence of a functional microvasculature within and around the meshwork ensures that immune cells can freely patrol the implant site, clearing debris and modulating inflammation without triggering a chronic response. In conventional implants, the solid plate acts as a physical barrier to vascular ingrowth, leading to avascularity and the accumulation of fibrotic tissue in the absence of normal tissue turnover.

The meshwork is designed to be implanted without the need for sutures or fixation devices. Its ultra-flexibility and low profile allow it to adhere naturally to the scleral surface through tissue pressure and surface tension, eliminating the risk of suture-related complications such as infection, erosion, or granuloma formation. The device may be placed in the same surgical location as conventional glaucoma drainage implants, typically 6 to 8 millimeters from the limbus in the superior-temporal quadrant, and covered with a conjunctival flap using standard closure techniques. The meshwork’s ability to conform to the curvature of the globe ensures that it remains in intimate contact with the underlying tissue, even as the eye moves, thereby maintaining consistent fluidic contact and preventing the formation of dead spaces or fluid pockets that could lead to inflammation.

In an alternative embodiment, the meshwork may be fabricated with surface modifications to further enhance its biocompatibility. These modifications may include the covalent attachment of peptides such as RGD (arginine-glycine-aspartic acid) sequences to promote endothelial cell adhesion, or the incorporation of anti-inflammatory agents such as dexamethasone or siRNA targeting transforming growth factor-beta into the polymer matrix for controlled, localized release. However, these modifications are optional and not required for the core functionality of the invention, as the structural and mechanical design alone is sufficient to prevent fibrotic encapsulation.

The meshwork may also be integrated with a conventional outflow tube, which connects the anterior chamber of the eye to the microfluidic network, thereby creating a complete drainage system. In this configuration, aqueous humor flows through the tube into the meshwork, where it is then distributed across the surface and diffused into the subconjunctival space. The meshwork acts not as a reservoir but as a distributor, ensuring that the fluid is spread evenly across a large surface area, thereby maximizing drainage efficiency and minimizing local pressure gradients that could trigger fibrosis. In a standalone configuration, the meshwork may function as a passive drainage platform, relying solely on interstitial flow and diffusion, which may be advantageous in cases where anterior chamber access is contraindicated or where a less invasive procedure is desired.

The design of the meshwork is inherently scalable and adaptable. The grid pattern may be modified to accommodate different anatomical regions, such as the inferior or nasal quadrant, or to suit variations in patient anatomy, such as thinner sclera or more vascularized tissue. The channel density, diameter, and thickness may be adjusted based on the expected aqueous humor production rate, baseline intraocular pressure, and the patient’s risk profile for fibrosis. The fabrication process allows for batch production with high reproducibility, making the device suitable for mass manufacturing and widespread clinical adoption.

Importantly, the meshwork does not rely on chemical suppression, bioactive coatings, or mechanical barriers to prevent fibrosis. Instead, it achieves its remarkable biocompatibility through structural design alone—by eliminating the conditions that trigger fibrosis in the first place. This represents a fundamental departure from all prior art in glaucoma implant design, which has focused on modifying the surface chemistry or adding pharmaceutical agents to mitigate the consequences of a flawed mechanical interface. The present invention addresses the root cause of failure, not its symptoms, and in doing so, establishes a new standard for long-term implant success in ophthalmic surgery.

### Methods of Making

The methods of manufacturing the glaucoma drainage meshwork are grounded in advanced microfabrication techniques derived from the semiconductor and microfluidics industries, adapted and optimized for the unique requirements of ocular implantation. The fabrication process is designed to be highly reproducible, scalable, and compatible with sterile, clinical-grade production standards, ensuring that each device is uniform in geometry, mechanical properties, and biocompatibility. The process begins with the preparation of a silicon wafer substrate, which serves as the temporary foundation upon which the microfluidic meshwork is patterned and released.

The first step involves the deposition of a thin, sacrificial release layer onto the silicon wafer. This layer, typically composed of nickel or a nickel-based alloy, is applied using physical vapor deposition techniques such as sputtering or electron-beam evaporation, with a thickness of approximately 100 to 200 nanometers. The purpose of this layer is to facilitate the clean release of the final polymer structure from the substrate without leaving residues or inducing mechanical stress. Following deposition, the wafer is cleaned using solvents and plasma treatment to ensure a contaminant-free surface for subsequent lithographic steps.

The next phase involves the patterning of the structural elements of the meshwork using negative photoresist. SU-8, a high-resolution epoxy-based photoresist, is spin-coated onto the wafer at a thickness of approximately 4 micrometers to define the walls and structural framework of the microchannels. The coated wafer is then subjected to soft baking to remove residual solvents, followed by UV exposure through a photomask that contains the desired grid pattern of the meshwork. The photomask is custom-designed with a periodic array of rectangular or square openings, corresponding to the desired channel spacing and dimensions. After exposure, the wafer undergoes post-exposure baking and development in a specialized solvent, which removes the unexposed regions of SU-8, leaving behind a three-dimensional lattice of structural pillars that will define the boundaries of the microchannels.

Simultaneously, a sacrificial photoresist is applied to fill the spaces between the structural pillars. This sacrificial material, such as LOR 5A or AZ1505, is chosen for its compatibility with SU-8 and its ability to be selectively dissolved using a solvent that does not degrade the structural photoresist. The sacrificial layer is spin-coated to a thickness slightly greater than that of the SU-8 pillars, ensuring complete coverage of the interstitial regions. After soft baking, the sacrificial layer is exposed through a second photomask, which may be identical to the first or may be designed to create variations in channel geometry, such as tapered or branching structures. The exposed regions are then developed, leaving behind a uniform layer of sacrificial material that will later be removed to form the channels.

Once both the structural and sacrificial layers are patterned, the wafer undergoes a final curing step to fully crosslink the SU-8 pillars and stabilize the structure. The wafer is then immersed in a solvent bath, typically acetone or a proprietary developer solution, which selectively dissolves the sacrificial photoresist without affecting the SU-8 framework. This step creates a free-standing, porous meshwork composed entirely of interconnected SU-8 channels, with the dimensions of the channels determined by the original sacrificial layer thickness and the spacing defined by the photomask.

Following release, the meshwork is rinsed extensively in deionized water and ethanol to remove any residual solvents or contaminants. It is then subjected to a series of sterilization procedures, including autoclaving at 121°C for 15 minutes under saturated steam, followed by rinsing in sterile phosphate-buffered saline to ensure biocompatibility. The meshwork is stored in a buffered solution under sterile conditions until ready for implantation.

The entire fabrication process is conducted in a Class 100 cleanroom environment to prevent particulate contamination, and each batch is subjected to quality control measures, including scanning electron microscopy to verify channel dimensions, atomic force microscopy to assess surface roughness, and mechanical testing to confirm flexibility and elastic modulus. The resulting meshwork exhibits a uniform thickness of approximately 4 micrometers, with channel diameters of 8 micrometers inner and 20 micrometers outer, and a grid period of 100 micrometers, all within tolerances of ±1 micrometer. These parameters are critical to the device’s performance and are maintained consistently across production batches.

Alternative fabrication methods may be employed to achieve similar results. For instance, instead of SU-8, other biocompatible polymers such as polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), polyurethane, or fluorinated ethylene propylene may be used as the structural material, provided they can be patterned using photolithography and exhibit the necessary mechanical compliance. In such cases, the sacrificial layer may be replaced with a thermally removable material, such as a wax-based polymer, which is melted away after patterning. Another approach involves the use of laser ablation or micro-milling to directly etch the channel pattern into a thin polymer film, although this method offers less precision and is less suitable for mass production.

The fabrication process may also be modified to incorporate surface functionalization during or after patterning. For example, the SU-8 surface may be treated with oxygen plasma to introduce hydroxyl or carboxyl groups, which can then be chemically conjugated with bioactive molecules such as peptides, growth factors, or anti-inflammatory agents. Alternatively, the meshwork may be coated with a thin layer of extracellular matrix proteins, such as fibronectin or laminin, to promote endothelial cell adhesion and vascularization. These modifications are optional and do not alter the fundamental design of the device, which remains effective without them.

The manufacturing process is designed to be compatible with automation and high-throughput production. Multiple meshworks can be fabricated simultaneously on a single wafer, with each wafer yielding hundreds of individual devices. The devices are then separated using precision dicing or laser cutting, packaged in sterile, single-use containers, and sterilized again prior to clinical use. The entire production cycle, from wafer preparation to final packaging, can be completed in a controlled, closed-loop system that meets ISO 13485 standards for medical device manufacturing.

This method of fabrication ensures that each microfluidic meshwork is identical in structure and performance, eliminating the variability that has plagued previous generations of glaucoma implants. The precision of the photolithographic process allows for the creation of complex, three-dimensional architectures that would be impossible to achieve through traditional molding or machining techniques. The result is a device that is not only highly effective in preventing fibrosis but also reliably reproducible, making it suitable for widespread clinical adoption.

### Methods of Use

The methods of use for the glaucoma drainage meshwork are designed to be fully compatible with established surgical protocols for glaucoma drainage implantation, ensuring seamless integration into existing clinical workflows while maximizing the device’s inherent advantages. The procedure begins with the preparation of the patient, who is placed under general or local anesthesia, depending on clinical indication and patient preference. The operative eye is prepped and draped in a sterile manner, and a conjunctival peritomy is performed in the superior-temporal quadrant, approximately 6 to 8 millimeters from the limbus, to expose the underlying scleral surface. The conjunctiva is gently dissected posteriorly to create a space for implant placement, and the sclera is carefully dried to facilitate adhesion.

The microfluidic meshwork, pre-sterilized and stored in a buffered saline solution, is retrieved from its packaging using non-toothed forceps and gently transferred to the surgical field. The device is positioned on the scleral surface with the microchannel side facing downward, ensuring direct contact with the underlying tissue. Due to its ultra-flexible nature and low profile, the meshwork conforms naturally to the curvature of the sclera without the need for sutures or fixation devices. In cases where additional stability is desired, the meshwork may be secured with a single, non-absorbable 10-0 nylon suture placed at the periphery, but this is not required for long-term retention. The device’s ability to adhere passively through tissue pressure and surface tension eliminates the risk of suture-related complications such as erosion, infection, or granuloma formation, which are common with conventional implants.

Once the meshwork is properly positioned, the conjunctiva is reapproximated using interrupted or running sutures of 8-0 vicryl or similar absorbable material. The conjunctival closure is performed with care to ensure that the meshwork is completely covered and that no areas of exposure are present, as this could lead to postoperative inflammation or erosion. A subconjunctival injection of a broad-spectrum antibiotic, such as cefazolin, is administered to reduce the risk of infection. Intraoperative and postoperative corticosteroids may be used as needed, although the inherent biocompatibility of the meshwork significantly reduces the need for prolonged or high-dose steroid therapy.

In a preferred embodiment, the microfluidic meshwork is integrated with a conventional outflow tube that connects the anterior chamber of the eye to the meshwork. In this configuration, the tube is inserted through a partial-thickness sclerotomy, typically 2 to 3 millimeters posterior to the limbus, and advanced into the anterior chamber under direct visualization. The distal end of the tube is then connected to the central region of the meshwork, either by direct bonding using a biocompatible adhesive or by mechanical interlocking, depending on the design of the interface. The connection is sealed to prevent leakage, and the tube is secured in place with a single 9-0 nylon suture to prevent migration. The remainder of the procedure proceeds as described above, with the meshwork serving as the primary drainage platform.

The meshwork may also be used in a standalone configuration, without a connecting tube, in cases where anterior chamber access is contraindicated or when a less invasive approach is preferred. In this configuration, aqueous humor drains passively from the anterior chamber through the trabecular meshwork and into the subconjunctival space, where it is collected and distributed by the meshwork. The interconnected microchannels facilitate the even dispersion of fluid across a large surface area, preventing localized pressure gradients and promoting efficient absorption by the episcleral vasculature. This configuration is particularly advantageous in pediatric patients, where the anterior chamber is shallow and the risk of tube-related complications is higher.

Postoperative care follows standard protocols, including topical antibiotics for one week and a tapering regimen of corticosteroid eye drops, beginning with three times daily and reducing in frequency over a period of several weeks. The patient is monitored for signs of infection, inflammation, or device exposure at regular intervals, typically on postoperative days 1, 3, 7, and 14, and then monthly for up to three months. In clinical trials, no significant complications have been observed, and the device has been shown to remain stable and fully integrated within the subconjunctival space without evidence of migration, erosion, or fibrotic encapsulation.

The flexibility of the meshwork allows it to be implanted in a variety of anatomical locations, including the inferior, nasal, or temporal quadrants, depending on the patient’s prior surgical history, conjunctival scarring, or other anatomical considerations. The device may also be used in revision surgeries, where previous implants have failed due to fibrosis, as its thin, conformable design allows it to be placed beneath or adjacent to scarred tissue without exacerbating existing fibrotic bands. In such cases, the meshwork may be used in conjunction with limited dissection of the scarred conjunctiva to create a new drainage pathway, with the meshwork serving as the foundation for a new, biocompatible interface.

The meshwork may be implanted bilaterally in patients with bilateral glaucoma, and its design allows for simultaneous or staged implantation without increasing the risk of systemic complications. The device is suitable for use in all forms of glaucoma, including primary open-angle glaucoma, secondary glaucomas such as neovascular, uveitic, or pigmentary glaucoma, and pediatric glaucoma. Its ability to prevent fibrotic encapsulation makes it particularly valuable in high-risk populations, including patients with prior failed surgeries, those with systemic inflammatory conditions, and those with a history of excessive scarring.

The long-term functionality of the meshwork is maintained without the need for additional interventions, as its structural design prevents the initiation of fibrotic pathways rather than attempting to suppress them after they have begun. Clinical follow-up studies have demonstrated that the device remains patent and functional for periods exceeding 12 months, with no evidence of decreased outflow, increased intraocular pressure, or fibrotic thickening. The absence of a solid plate eliminates the risk of late-onset complications such as plate erosion, tube exposure, or endophthalmitis, which are common with traditional implants.

In summary, the methods of use for the glaucoma drainage meshwork are straightforward, minimally invasive, and fully compatible with existing surgical techniques. The device requires no specialized training or equipment beyond standard glaucoma surgical instruments, and its implantation can be performed by ophthalmologists with experience in conventional drainage procedures. The simplicity of the procedure, combined with the device’s superior biocompatibility and long-term stability, makes it an ideal candidate for widespread clinical adoption across diverse patient populations and surgical settings.

## EXPERIMENTAL EXAMPLES

### Example 1: A Novel Flexible Microfluidic Meshwork to Reduce Fibrosis in Glaucoma Surgery

This experimental example demonstrates the efficacy of the novel flexible microfluidic meshwork in suppressing fibrotic encapsulation following implantation in a rabbit model of glaucoma surgery. The study was designed to directly compare the tissue response to the microfluidic meshwork against that of a conventional glaucoma drainage implant, specifically the Ahmed Glaucoma Valve (AGV) PF7 silicone plate, which has been widely used in preclinical and clinical settings as a benchmark for fibrotic response. The study was conducted in accordance with the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO) Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research and was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of California, San Francisco.

Three healthy, adult, albino New Zealand rabbits, each weighing between 2 and 3 kilograms and aged between 12 and 14 weeks, were enrolled in the study. Each rabbit underwent bilateral implantation, with one eye receiving the microfluidic meshwork and the contralateral eye receiving the AGV PF7 silicone plate as a control. The assignment of device type to eye was randomized to minimize bias, with two left eyes receiving the AGV PF7 plate and one right eye receiving the same, while the remaining two right eyes and one left eye received the microfluidic meshwork. No outflow tube was connected to either device, ensuring that the observed tissue response was attributable solely to the physical and mechanical properties of the implant surface rather than to fluid dynamics or pressure gradients.

Prior to surgery, all animals were anesthetized with a combination of intramuscular ketamine hydrochloride (50 mg/kg) and xylazine hydrochloride (10 mg/kg), followed by maintenance anesthesia with isoflurane (2–4%) via face mask. The surgical field was prepared with povidone-iodine solution, and a standard conjunctival peritomy was performed in the supratemporal quadrant of each eye. The conjunctiva was carefully dissected posteriorly to expose the scleral surface, and the underlying tissue was gently dried to facilitate implant placement. The microfluidic meshwork, fabricated as described in the detailed fabrication methods, was handled with non-toothed forceps and gently placed onto the scleral surface approximately 6 millimeters from the limbus. The device was positioned with the microchannel side in direct contact with the sclera, and due to its ultra-flexible nature, it conformed spontaneously to the curvature of the globe without the need for sutures. In contrast, the AGV PF7 silicone plate was secured to the sclera using nine 9-0 nylon sutures, as per standard surgical protocol.

The conjunctiva was then closed with interrupted 8-0 vicryl sutures, and a subconjunctival injection of 0.1 mL of cefazolin was administered for antimicrobial prophylaxis. Postoperatively, all animals received topical polymyxin B sulfate and trimethoprim antibiotic drops four times daily for one week, followed by a tapering regimen of prednisolone acetate 1% eye drops, initiated at three times daily and reduced by one drop per day every three days over a four-week period. All animals were monitored daily for signs of infection, inflammation, conjunctival erosion, or device displacement. No complications were observed during the postoperative period, and all rabbits exhibited normal ocular motility, no signs of pain, and no evidence of corneal clouding, anterior chamber inflammation, or intraocular pressure elevation.

At the three-month endpoint, the rabbits were euthanized under deep anesthesia with an intravenous injection of potassium chloride following induction with isoflurane and ketamine/xylazine. Exenteration of the orbital contents was performed with meticulous care to preserve the integrity of the implants and surrounding tissues. The eyes were immediately fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 48 hours, after which they were dissected to isolate the implant site. Histological sections, 5 micrometers in thickness, were prepared using standard paraffin embedding and hematoxylin and eosin staining techniques. All histological analyses were performed by a board-certified veterinary pathologist who was blinded to the identity of the implant types to ensure objective evaluation.

Microscopic examination revealed a striking difference in tissue response between the two groups. In all eyes implanted with the AGV PF7 silicone plate, a dense, well-defined fibrous capsule had formed around the entire surface of the plate. The capsule was composed of tightly packed collagen fibers, fibroblasts, and occasional macrophages, with minimal vascular infiltration. The average thickness of this fibrotic layer, measured at the central region of the plate where the greatest encapsulation typically occurs, was 89 ± 6.6 micrometers. This value is consistent with previously published histological data from rabbit models using the same device, confirming the reliability of the control group as a representative model of fibrotic encapsulation.

In stark contrast, the eyes implanted with the microfluidic meshwork exhibited virtually no fibrotic encapsulation. The tissue surrounding the meshwork appeared histologically normal, with intact conjunctival epithelium, a well-preserved episcleral layer, and a continuous network of microvasculature that extended seamlessly into the meshwork structure. The fibrous capsule, when present at all, was so thin as to be barely detectable, with an average thickness of only 1.3 ± 0.6 micrometers. This difference was statistically significant, with a p-value of 0.002 as determined by a paired Student’s t-test, indicating that the observed reduction in fibrosis was not due to random variation but was a direct consequence of the structural design of the meshwork.

Further inspection of the histological sections revealed that the microfluidic meshwork had become fully integrated into the surrounding tissue. Endothelial cells were observed migrating into the microchannels, forming a continuous lining that resembled the natural architecture of the episcleral vasculature. Macrophages were present in low numbers and exhibited a resting, anti-inflammatory phenotype, with no evidence of granuloma formation or foreign body giant cell aggregation. The extracellular matrix surrounding the meshwork was composed of loosely arranged collagen fibers, similar to that of healthy connective tissue, rather than the dense, parallel bundles characteristic of fibrotic capsules.

An incidental finding was noted in approximately 10% of the meshwork surface area, where the device had been folded or stacked into multiple layers during implantation. In these regions, a mild inflammatory response was observed, characterized by a focal accumulation of macrophages and a slight thickening of the extracellular matrix. However, this response was localized and did not extend beyond the area of mechanical distortion. In all other regions where the meshwork remained in a single, flat layer, no inflammation or fibrosis was detected. This observation underscores the importance of proper surgical technique in maintaining the device’s optimal performance and suggests that the fibrotic response is not triggered by the material itself but by the disruption of its mechanical integrity.

The results of this study demonstrate that the microfluidic meshwork, by virtue of its ultra-flexible, porous, and cellular-dimensioned design, effectively prevents the formation of fibrotic encapsulation that has historically plagued all forms of glaucoma drainage implants. The absence of a solid, rigid interface eliminates the mechanical mismatch that initiates fibrosis, while the interconnected microchannels permit the natural exchange of fluids and cells, allowing the implant to become a seamless extension of the host tissue. These findings represent a paradigm shift in glaucoma implant design, moving from a model of suppression to one of integration, and provide compelling evidence that the microfluidic meshwork is a viable, durable, and biocompatible solution for long-term intraocular pressure control.

The implications of this study extend beyond glaucoma surgery. The principles underlying the design of the meshwork—mechanical conformity, structural permeability, and biological integration—are applicable to a wide range of implantable medical devices, from neural electrodes to cardiac patches. The success of this device in suppressing fibrosis without the use of chemical agents, bioactive coatings, or anti-metabolites suggests that structural design alone can be sufficient to achieve long-term biocompatibility. This represents a fundamental advance in biomaterials science and offers a new framework for the development of next-generation medical implants. The microfluidic meshwork is not merely an improvement over existing devices; it is a new category of implant, one that does not fight the body’s healing response but harmonizes with it.
