# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The acquisition of optical information by imaging systems has long been predicated on the detection of light intensity, a physical quantity that corresponds to the square of the electric field amplitude and is directly measurable by semiconductor photodetectors. Conventional image sensors—whether based on charge-coupled device (CCD) or complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) technology—are engineered to respond exclusively to the radiant flux of incident light, effectively discarding the phase information encoded in the wavefront of the electromagnetic field. This limitation is not merely technical but fundamental to the design philosophy of modern digital imaging: the phase of light, being a complex-valued, cyclic, and non-integrable quantity, has historically been regarded as inaccessible without interferometric or holographic reconstruction techniques that require precise optical alignment, coherent illumination, and computational post-processing. As a result, applications requiring sensitivity to refractive index variations—such as the visualization of transparent biological specimens, the assessment of cellular morphology without staining, or the detection of subtle structural anomalies in unstained tissues—have been constrained to specialized optical configurations that indirectly convert phase gradients into intensity modulations. Among these, differential interference contrast (DIC) microscopy stands as the most widely adopted method for phase-sensitive imaging in biomedical laboratories. DIC microscopy exploits the interference between two laterally sheared, orthogonally polarized light beams that have traversed adjacent regions of a sample. The resulting intensity variations are interpreted as pseudo-three-dimensional relief images that convey topographical information about refractive index gradients. However, this method suffers from inherent ambiguities: the contrast observed is not purely phase-derived but is a nonlinear mixture of phase and amplitude contributions, rendering quantitative interpretation impossible. Furthermore, DIC imaging is critically dependent on the polarization state of the incident light, which introduces severe artifacts when imaging birefringent materials such as collagen fibers, muscle striations, or starch granules, where intrinsic anisotropy disrupts the delicate polarization balance required for interference. These artifacts manifest as spurious intensity gradients that mimic structural features, leading to misinterpretation of sample morphology. Phase contrast microscopy, another widely used technique, circumvents some of these issues by introducing a phase shift in the unobstructed reference beam relative to the scattered beam, but it suffers from halos and reduced contrast at high numerical apertures, and it too provides only qualitative, non-quantitative phase information. Moreover, both DIC and phase contrast systems require the physical insertion and removal of specialized optical components—Nomarski prisms, phase rings, or annular diaphragms—during operation, making them incompatible with high-speed, real-time imaging protocols and introducing mechanical instability and alignment drift. The need for these bulky, precision-engineered components also contributes to the high cost and limited accessibility of phase imaging in resource-constrained environments such as point-of-care diagnostics, field laboratories, or educational institutions. While recent advances in computational phase retrieval have demonstrated the feasibility of recovering phase information from multiple defocused images acquired at different axial positions, these methods are inherently sequential, requiring mechanical translation of the sensor or the sample, and are therefore incompatible with dynamic processes or live-cell imaging where temporal resolution is paramount. The reliance on iterative algorithms and multiple acquisitions further exacerbates computational burden and introduces noise amplification and convergence artifacts. Alternative approaches employing structured illumination, digital holography, or interferometric Fourier transform techniques have been proposed, but they invariably demand coherent laser sources, complex optical setups, and sophisticated calibration procedures that are impractical for integration into standard microscope platforms. The prevailing paradigm, therefore, remains one of indirect inference: phase information is not measured directly but inferred through convoluted optical manipulations and mathematical reconstructions that compromise speed, accuracy, and simplicity. There exists, then, a critical and unmet need for a direct, single-shot, intensity- and phase-sensing imaging sensor that can be seamlessly integrated into existing optical systems without modification, that operates under incoherent illumination, that requires no moving parts, and that provides quantitative, artifact-free, and simultaneous access to both the amplitude and phase gradient of the incident light field. Such a device would eliminate the need for specialized optics, remove the ambiguities inherent in interference-based methods, enable real-time quantitative analysis of transparent specimens, and democratize access to phase imaging across clinical, research, and industrial domains. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel image sensor architecture that embeds a high-density array of micro-apertures directly onto the surface of a standard CMOS image sensor, thereby enabling the direct, parallel, and simultaneous measurement of both intensity and local wavefront slope across the entire field of view in a single exposure. This innovation transforms the fundamental operating principle of digital imaging from passive intensity detection to active wavefront sampling, establishing a new class of optical sensors capable of capturing the full complex amplitude of light without reliance on external interferometry, coherent sources, or computational reconstruction.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention disclosed herein is a wavefront image sensor chip, a monolithic semiconductor device capable of simultaneously and directly measuring the intensity and the spatial phase gradient of an incident optical field in a single acquisition, without requiring any additional optical components, moving parts, or post-processing algorithms beyond basic signal extraction. The sensor comprises a standard image sensor substrate, such as a CMOS or CCD array, upon which a planar array of micro-apertures is fabricated in a continuous, opaque metallic layer deposited directly on the sensor surface. A transparent dielectric spacer, formed from a polymer or other optically transparent material, is positioned between the aperture array and the photosensitive region of the sensor, establishing a fixed and precisely controlled distance that enables the sensor to operate in a high Fresnel number regime. In this regime, light transmitted through each aperture self-focuses near the aperture plane before diffraction dominates, resulting in a tightly confined projection spot on the underlying sensor pixels. The lateral displacement of each projection spot is directly proportional to the local normalized phase gradient of the incident wavefront over the corresponding aperture, while the total integrated intensity of the spot provides a direct measurement of the local light intensity. By assigning a small, dedicated grid of sensor pixels beneath each aperture—typically a 5×5 pixel array—the center position of each projection spot can be determined with sub-pixel precision using a cyclic Fourier-based algorithm that exploits the phase coherence of the spot’s intensity distribution. This allows for the simultaneous reconstruction of two orthogonal components of the normalized phase gradient, θx and θy, across the entire sensor array, as well as a corresponding intensity image, all from a single exposure under incoherent broadband illumination. The sensor is fabricated using standard semiconductor processing techniques, including photolithography, metal deposition, and planarization, ensuring compatibility with high-volume, low-cost foundry production. The aperture pitch, typically on the order of 10 to 15 micrometers, is chosen to balance spatial resolution, signal-to-noise ratio, and phase gradient sensitivity, while the spacer thickness is optimized to position the sensor plane at the point of minimal spot size, maximizing both sensitivity and resolution. The resulting device can be directly mounted in place of a conventional camera on any standard bright-field microscope, transforming it into a wavefront microscope capable of acquiring quantitative phase gradient images alongside conventional intensity images without altering the microscope’s optical path, illumination scheme, or magnification. The invention eliminates the need for DIC prisms, phase rings, laser sources, or interferometric setups, and it is immune to birefringence-induced artifacts because it does not rely on polarization manipulation. The phase gradient images produced are quantitative, linear, and free from the intensity-phase mixing that plagues traditional methods, enabling direct calculation of optical path length, refractive index distribution, and cellular thickness. Furthermore, the sensor’s design allows for straightforward extension to color imaging by integrating it with multi-layered or Bayer-filtered sensor architectures, and its compact form factor enables integration into portable, handheld, or endoscopic imaging systems. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in optical sensing: instead of inferring phase from intensity modulations induced by complex optics, it directly measures phase gradients as a native sensor output, enabling a new generation of imaging systems that are simpler, cheaper, faster, and more accurate than any prior approach.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The wavefront image sensor chip, as described herein, is a fully integrated, solid-state optical sensor that fundamentally redefines the manner in which light is captured and interpreted in imaging systems. Unlike conventional image sensors that respond only to the radiant power of incident light, this device is engineered to extract both the amplitude and the spatial derivative of the phase of the electromagnetic field incident upon its surface. The core innovation lies in the physical integration of a micro-aperture array directly onto the surface of a standard image sensor substrate, such as a CMOS or CCD chip, with a precisely controlled separation between the aperture plane and the photosensitive layer. This configuration enables the sensor to operate in a high Fresnel number regime, where the propagation of light through each aperture results in a self-focusing effect that confines the transmitted light into a sharply defined projection spot on the sensor plane. The position of this spot is exquisitely sensitive to the local slope of the incident wavefront, while its integrated intensity reflects the local irradiance. By measuring the displacement and intensity of these spots across an array of thousands of apertures, the sensor reconstructs a complete, pixelated map of the intensity and the two orthogonal components of the normalized phase gradient of the incident light field. The fabrication process begins with a commercially available image sensor chip, such as the MT9P031I12STM, which contains an array of photosensitive pixels with dimensions of 2.2 micrometers and a total resolution of 1944 by 2592 pixels. The glass cover that typically protects the sensor surface is removed to expose the underlying silicon substrate and the interconnect layers. A planarizing layer of SU8 photoresist, approximately 10 micrometers thick, is then spin-coated and cured to form a uniform, optically transparent spacer between the sensor surface and the subsequent metallic layer. This spacer serves a dual purpose: it physically separates the aperture array from the photosensitive region to establish the critical distance H required for high Fresnel number operation, and it nullifies the effects of the microlens array typically integrated atop each pixel in commercial sensors, ensuring that the light distribution incident on the sensor is governed solely by the aperture geometry and not by lens-induced focusing artifacts. Following planarization, a thin film of aluminum, approximately 150 nanometers in thickness, is sputter-deposited over the entire surface to create an opaque, electrically conductive mask that blocks all incident light except where apertures are subsequently defined. Photolithographic patterning is then employed to etch a two-dimensional grid of circular apertures into the aluminum layer. The aperture diameter is set to 6 micrometers, and the center-to-center spacing between adjacent apertures is maintained at 11 micrometers, resulting in a dense array of 280 by 350 apertures across the active sensor area. Each aperture is assigned a dedicated 5 by 5 pixel sub-grid of the underlying sensor array, yielding a total of 25 sensor pixels per aperture for the purpose of detecting the position and intensity of the transmitted light spot. This arrangement ensures that the lateral displacement of each spot can be determined with sub-pixel accuracy, even when the spot size is smaller than the pixel pitch. The total number of apertures, and thus the number of resolved image pixels in the final wavefront image, is determined by the ratio of the sensor’s total pixel count to the number of pixels allocated per aperture. In this implementation, the 1944 by 2592 pixel sensor yields a 280 by 350 pixel wavefront image, with each image pixel corresponding to the averaged intensity and displacement of a 5 by 5 pixel group. The distance H between the aperture plane and the photosensitive region is determined through a combination of optical simulation and experimental calibration. Finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulations, performed using CST Microwave Studio, model the propagation of broadband light (473 to 713 nanometers) through a 6-micrometer aperture separated from a perfect electric conductor by a dielectric spacer with a refractive index of 1.6. These simulations reveal that the transmitted light forms a tightly confined spot with a minimum full width at half maximum (FWHM) of approximately 3.8 micrometers at a separation of 18 micrometers, confirming the presence of a self-focusing effect characteristic of the high Fresnel number regime. Experimental validation is performed by fabricating a single 6-micrometer aperture in an aluminum-coated glass cover slip using focused ion beam milling and imaging the transmitted spot with a high-numerical-aperture oil immersion objective at varying axial positions. The measured spot size as a function of axial displacement confirms the simulated trend, with a minimum FWHM of 3.8 micrometers observed at 18 micrometers separation, and a remarkably stable spot size below 5 micrometers over a range of 4 to 34 micrometers. The fabricated sensor, with a measured effective spacer thickness of 28 micrometers, operates slightly beyond the theoretical optimum due to minor deviations in SU8 thickness, yet still achieves a projected spot size of 4.5 micrometers FWHM, which is 25 percent narrower than the aperture diameter itself. This confinement is critical: it ensures that the spot remains sufficiently localized to enable precise centroid determination, while maintaining sensitivity to phase gradients. The operational principle of the sensor is grounded in the relationship between the lateral displacement of the projection spot and the normalized phase gradient of the incident wavefront. For a light wave incident upon a single aperture, the displacement Δx of the spot center in the x-direction is proportional to the normalized phase gradient θx, defined as the product of the wavelength-dependent phase gradient ∂ϕ/∂x and the ratio of the wavelength to the refractive index of the spacer material. This relationship is expressed as Δx = (λ / n) * (∂ϕ/∂x) * H, where H is the distance between the aperture and the sensor plane. The normalized phase gradient θx is thus a wavelength-independent measure of the local tilt of the wavefront, making it suitable for use with broadband incoherent sources such as halogen lamps, which are standard in conventional microscopy. The same relationship holds for the y-direction, allowing for the simultaneous measurement of both θx and θy. The intensity of the incident light is determined by summing the digital counts from all 25 sensor pixels associated with each aperture, providing a direct, pixel-wise measurement of the local irradiance. To determine the center of each projection spot with sub-pixel accuracy, a modified Fourier-demodulation algorithm, termed the cyclic algorithm, is employed. This algorithm operates on the discrete intensity distribution of the 5 by 5 pixel grid associated with each aperture. A complex number is constructed for each spot by assigning a complex exponential weight to each pixel based on its relative position within the grid, effectively encoding the spatial phase of the spot’s intensity distribution. The complex number for the unshifted spot is computed as the sum over all pixels of the intensity multiplied by exp(-i2π(m·Δx + n·Δy)/T), where m and n are the pixel indices relative to the center, T is the pixel pitch, and Δx and Δy are the assumed shifts. When the spot is displaced by a small amount δx and δy, the complex number rotates in the complex plane by an angle proportional to δx and δy. The displacement is then extracted by computing the phase difference between the complex number of the measured spot and a reference complex number obtained during calibration. This method is inherently robust to noise because it assigns equal weight to all pixels in the grid, avoiding the instability of centroid methods that overemphasize low-intensity, high-noise pixels at the periphery of the spot. Calibration of the sensor is performed by illuminating the aperture array with a plane wave at a known incident angle, thereby inducing a uniform phase gradient across all apertures. The resulting displacement of each spot is measured and plotted against the known angle, yielding a linear response curve. The slope of this curve provides a direct measurement of the effective distance H between the aperture and the photosensitive region, which is found to be 27.2 micrometers in the x-direction and 28.0 micrometers in the y-direction, indicating minimal misalignment between the aperture array and the sensor pixel grid. The sensitivity of the phase gradient measurement is determined by the standard deviation of the spot position over repeated acquisitions under constant illumination. Under typical operating conditions—1.0 second exposure time and 9.2 microwatts per square centimeter incident intensity—the sensor achieves a phase gradient sensitivity better than 0.1 milliradians, sufficient for imaging biological specimens with refractive index variations on the order of 0.01. The dynamic range of the phase gradient measurement extends from -15 to +15 milliradians, covering the full range of phase gradients encountered in most biological and material samples. A potential source of error arises from the influence of intensity gradients on the apparent position of the projection spot. If the intensity of the incident light varies across the aperture, the center of the spot may shift due to asymmetry in the illumination profile, even in the absence of phase gradients. This effect is quantified by modeling the intensity gradient ∂I/∂x across the aperture and calculating the resulting displacement as proportional to the product of the intensity gradient and the aperture radius. This shift is independent of the distance H and can be mitigated by reducing the aperture size or by post-processing the intensity image to compute and subtract the intensity-induced displacement component. In practice, this is achieved by first computing the normalized intensity gradient from the intensity image, then applying the derived proportionality constant to estimate the intensity-induced phase gradient artifact, which is then subtracted from the raw phase gradient measurement to yield a corrected, pure phase gradient image. This correction is particularly important at the edges of high-contrast objects, where intensity gradients are steep, but has negligible effect in homogeneous regions. The wavefront image sensor chip can be integrated into any standard bright-field microscope by replacing the conventional camera with the sensor unit. The microscope’s optical path, illumination system, and objective lenses remain unchanged, preserving the existing magnification, resolution, and Köhler illumination geometry. The sensor is mounted via a standard C-mount adapter, and the microscope’s halogen lamp provides the necessary broadband, incoherent illumination. The resulting system, termed a wavefront microscope, produces three simultaneous output images: a conventional intensity image, identical in appearance to a standard bright-field image; a normalized phase gradient image in the x-direction; and a normalized phase gradient image in the y-direction. These two phase gradient images constitute a complete vector field representation of the wavefront slope, from which the scalar phase distribution can be reconstructed via numerical integration, provided the phase is continuous and the boundary conditions are known. The phase gradient images are quantitative, linear, and free from the mixed amplitude-phase artifacts that plague DIC microscopy. For example, when imaging polystyrene microspheres, the intensity image reveals the sphere’s outline, while the phase gradient images show smooth, symmetric gradients corresponding to the spherical refractive index profile. When imaging unstained starfish embryos, the phase gradient images reveal internal cellular structures—nuclei, cytoplasmic density variations, and membrane boundaries—that are invisible in bright-field imaging and only partially resolved in DIC. Crucially, when the same sample is stained with hematoxylin, the DIC image exhibits dark regions that are ambiguous in origin—whether due to absorption or phase gradient—whereas the phase gradient image from the wavefront sensor remains unaffected by absorption, showing only the true refractive index variations. The fertilization membrane, which is optically transparent but mechanically distinct, is clearly visible in the x-direction phase gradient image but absent in all other modalities, demonstrating the sensor’s ability to resolve features that are invisible to intensity-based methods. Similarly, when imaging birefringent potato starch granules, the DIC image is dominated by severe, spurious intensity variations caused by polarization-dependent refractive index anisotropy, rendering the granule morphology uninterpretable. In contrast, the wavefront sensor produces clean, symmetric phase gradient images that accurately reflect the spherical shape of the granules, with no artifacts attributable to polarization. This immunity to birefringence is a direct consequence of the sensor’s operation: since no polarization manipulation is involved, the phase gradient measurement is unaffected by the material’s optical anisotropy. The sensor’s resolution is determined by the aperture pitch and the optical system’s diffraction limit. With a 11-micrometer aperture pitch and a 20× objective with a numerical aperture of 0.5, the system achieves an effective resolution of 2.2 micrometers, which is below the Nyquist criterion for the objective’s theoretical resolution of 1.2 micrometers. This limitation can be overcome in future iterations by reducing the aperture pitch to 5 micrometers or less, thereby increasing the number of apertures and restoring full sampling of the optical system’s resolution. The sensor’s sensitivity to phase gradients can be further enhanced by increasing the exposure time, reducing the aperture size, or improving the quantum efficiency of the sensor pixels. The fabrication process is entirely compatible with standard semiconductor manufacturing, requiring only additional photolithography and metal deposition steps that are routinely performed in CMOS foundries. The inclusion of the aperture array adds negligible cost to the production process, as it requires no new materials or exotic equipment. The sensor can be mass-produced at the same scale and cost as conventional image sensors, making it economically viable for widespread adoption in clinical, research, and industrial settings. The device is also scalable: larger sensor formats can be fabricated using the same principles, and the aperture density can be adjusted to match the requirements of different applications. For example, in high-resolution retinal imaging, a smaller aperture pitch and higher pixel density would be desirable, while in wide-field pathology scanning, a lower density with higher sensitivity might be preferred. The sensor’s output can be processed in real time using embedded microcontrollers or field-programmable gate arrays, enabling live, high-frame-rate phase imaging without reliance on external computers. The invention further enables the development of color wavefront sensors by integrating the aperture array with multi-layered photodiode structures, such as the Foveon X3 sensor, which captures red, green, and blue light at different depths, or with Bayer-filtered sensors, where the aperture array is aligned with the color filter pattern. In such cases, the cyclic algorithm must be adapted to account for spectral variations in the spot displacement, but the fundamental principle remains unchanged. The ability to simultaneously capture color and phase information on a single sensor eliminates the need for separate cameras or sequential acquisitions, streamlining workflows in histopathology, cytology, and developmental biology. Beyond microscopy, the wavefront image sensor has applications in adaptive optics, where it can serve as a compact, low-cost wavefront sensor for real-time aberration correction in telescopes, ophthalmic devices, and laser communication systems. In machine vision, it can be used to determine surface topography, detect edge orientation, or assess texture roughness without requiring structured light or laser scanning. In autonomous navigation, it can provide depth information through wavefront slope analysis, enabling object ranging without stereo vision or time-of-flight systems. In medical diagnostics, it can be incorporated into endoscopic probes for real-time tissue characterization during minimally invasive surgery, or into portable devices for point-of-care detection of pathogens in blood or water samples. The sensor’s immunity to polarization artifacts makes it uniquely suited for imaging biological tissues rich in collagen, muscle fibers, or crystalline structures, where traditional DIC systems fail. Its simplicity, robustness, and compatibility with existing infrastructure ensure that it can be rapidly deployed in laboratories, hospitals, and field settings without requiring specialized training or infrastructure. The invention thus represents not merely an incremental improvement in imaging technology, but a foundational shift in how optical information is captured and interpreted, enabling a new class of imaging systems that are simultaneously simple, accurate, and universally accessible.
