## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of natural language processing, and more specifically to systems and methods for improving the computational efficiency of zero-shot text classification models that rely on large pre-trained language models to perform classification without task-specific training data. In particular, the invention addresses the scalability bottleneck inherent in modern zero-shot classification frameworks that employ cross-encoder architectures, such as those based on Natural Language Inference (NLI) or Next Sentence Prediction (NSP) formulations, where inference time grows linearly with the number of candidate labels. The invention introduces a model-agnostic, statistically grounded pre-processing layer that employs conformal prediction to reduce the set of candidate labels presented to the primary zero-shot classifier, thereby significantly decreasing the number of forward passes required during inference while preserving the classification accuracy and maintaining a user-defined upper bound on the probability of excluding the true label. This advancement is particularly relevant in real-world applications involving large label spaces—such as customer intent classification in multi-domain virtual assistants, topic categorization in social media monitoring, or document classification in enterprise knowledge management systems—where latency, energy consumption, and infrastructure cost are critical constraints. The invention operates independently of the underlying pre-trained language model and can be seamlessly integrated into any zero-shot classification pipeline that evaluates text-label pairs, making it broadly applicable across diverse deployment environments ranging from edge devices to cloud-based inference services.

## BACKGROUND

The task of zero-shot text classification has emerged as a critical capability in modern natural language processing systems, enabling the classification of textual inputs into predefined categories without requiring labeled training examples for each class. Traditional approaches to this problem relied on embedding-based similarity measures, where both the input text and the candidate class labels were mapped into a shared vector space, and classification was performed by selecting the label whose embedding exhibited the highest cosine similarity to the text embedding. While these methods were computationally efficient due to their single-pass architecture, they suffered from limited expressiveness and an inability to capture complex semantic relationships between text and labels, particularly when labels were composed of multi-word phrases or abstract concepts. Subsequent advancements introduced more sophisticated modeling techniques, including generative approaches that conditioned text generation on label embeddings and attention-based mechanisms that dynamically weighted text tokens based on label representations. However, these methods incurred substantial computational overhead by requiring multiple passes over the input text and were often constrained by the need for architectural modifications that limited their generalizability.

More recently, the dominant paradigm for zero-shot classification has shifted toward formulations grounded in Natural Language Inference (NLI) and Next Sentence Prediction (NSP), which treat classification as a textual entailment or coherence task. In these frameworks, each candidate label is transformed into a natural language hypothesis sentence, and the input text is paired with each hypothesis to produce a classification score using a cross-encoder transformer model. The cross-encoder architecture, typically derived from large pre-trained language models such as BERT or BART, computes full self-attention over the concatenated text-hypothesis pair, enabling rich contextual interactions between the input and the label representation. While this approach has demonstrated state-of-the-art performance across a wide range of benchmarks, it suffers from a fundamental inefficiency: the number of inference operations scales linearly with the number of candidate labels. For instance, in a system with 64 possible intent classes, the model must perform 64 separate forward passes, each involving the full encoding of the input text and a distinct hypothesis, even when many of these hypotheses are semantically implausible given the input. This results in prohibitive latency and computational cost, especially in high-throughput environments such as real-time customer service chatbots or large-scale content moderation systems. Furthermore, the energy footprint of such systems has become a growing concern, as repeated inference over massive transformer models contributes significantly to the carbon emissions associated with artificial intelligence deployment.

Attempts to mitigate this inefficiency have focused on heuristic pruning strategies, such as retaining only the top-k most probable labels according to a fast but inaccurate classifier, or using clustering to group semantically similar labels and represent them with a single prototype. However, these methods lack formal guarantees regarding the preservation of classification coverage, meaning that the true label may be inadvertently excluded from the reduced set, leading to catastrophic failure in classification accuracy. Moreover, such approaches are often tailored to specific datasets or label distributions and do not generalize reliably across domains. There exists a critical gap in the literature for a method that can systematically reduce the number of candidate labels in a zero-shot classification pipeline while providing a provable statistical guarantee that the true label is retained with high probability, regardless of the underlying model architecture or label semantics. The present invention fills this gap by introducing a conformal prediction framework that operates as a pre-filtering layer, leveraging a lightweight base classifier to generate a set of candidate labels that is guaranteed to contain the true label with a user-specified confidence level, thereby enabling the downstream zero-shot model to operate on a drastically reduced label set without sacrificing reliability or performance.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Computer Environment

The system for efficient zero-shot text classification using conformal prediction is implemented within a distributed computing environment that includes one or more server-class computing devices, each equipped with one or more central processing units, high-bandwidth memory subsystems, and optional graphical processing units optimized for parallel tensor operations. The computing environment further includes persistent storage mechanisms such as solid-state drives or network-attached storage systems for housing pre-trained language models, calibration datasets, and model checkpoints. The system is accessible via a network interface that supports secure communication protocols, enabling clients to submit text classification requests over HTTP or gRPC endpoints. The classification pipeline is orchestrated by a software stack comprising a model inference server, a conformal prediction engine, and a resource manager that dynamically allocates computational resources based on workload characteristics. The inference server hosts the primary zero-shot classification model, which is typically a transformer-based cross-encoder such as BART-large-NLI or BERT-base-NSP, loaded into memory and pre-compiled for optimal inference performance using frameworks such as ONNX Runtime, TensorRT, or Hugging Face’s Transformers library with CUDA acceleration. The conformal prediction engine is implemented as a separate module that operates prior to the inference server and is responsible for generating the filtered label set using a computationally lightweight base classifier. This base classifier may be instantiated in a variety of forms, including a token overlap scorer, a cosine similarity scorer using static word embeddings, or a distilled transformer model fine-tuned on model-generated pseudo-labels. Each base classifier is pre-calibrated using a calibration dataset derived from unlabeled text samples and their corresponding pseudo-labels generated by the primary zero-shot model. The calibration process involves computing non-conformity scores for each sample-label pair and determining a quantile threshold that ensures the desired coverage probability. Once calibrated, the conformal prediction engine requires no further training and operates in a stateless manner, making it suitable for deployment in containerized environments such as Docker or Kubernetes. The system architecture is designed to support batched inference, where multiple text inputs are processed simultaneously, and the conformal prediction engine generates a distinct candidate label set for each input, which is then forwarded to the zero-shot model for final scoring. The entire pipeline is monitored for latency, throughput, and resource utilization, with logging and telemetry data collected for performance optimization and model drift detection. The system is designed to be agnostic to the underlying hardware, allowing it to operate on cloud instances, on-premises servers, or edge devices, with the choice of base classifier being dynamically selected based on the available computational budget and latency constraints.

### Work Flows

The operational workflow of the system begins with the ingestion of an unlabeled text input intended for classification into one of a predefined set of K candidate labels. Upon receipt, the input is first routed to the conformal prediction engine, which has been previously calibrated using a set of unlabeled text samples and their corresponding pseudo-labels generated by the primary zero-shot model. The conformal prediction engine selects a base classifier appropriate for the deployment context—whether a simple token overlap metric, a cosine similarity measure based on GloVe embeddings, or a fine-tuned distilled transformer model—and computes a non-conformity score for each candidate label with respect to the input text. The non-conformity score quantifies the degree to which a label deviates from being consistent with the input, as determined by the base classifier’s internal scoring mechanism. For instance, in the case of a token overlap-based classifier, the score is derived from the proportion of unique tokens in the input text that appear in the representative token set associated with each label; in the case of a cosine similarity-based classifier, the score is computed as the negative cosine similarity between the bag-of-words representation of the input and the aggregated embedding of the label description; and in the case of a fine-tuned classifier, the score corresponds to the negative logit value assigned by the model to each candidate label. These non-conformity scores are then compared against a precomputed quantile threshold, derived during calibration from the empirical distribution of scores observed on the calibration dataset under a specified error rate α. Labels whose non-conformity scores fall below this threshold are retained as candidate labels, forming a filtered set Γα that is guaranteed to contain the true label with probability at least 1−α. This filtered set is then passed to the primary zero-shot classification model, which, in a typical implementation, employs a cross-encoder architecture to compute a compatibility score between the input text and each remaining candidate label. The cross-encoder processes each text-label pair independently, generating a score that reflects the likelihood of entailment or coherence between the text and the label hypothesis. The label with the highest score is selected as the final classification output. The entire workflow is designed to minimize redundant computation: the input text is encoded only once by the conformal prediction engine, and the zero-shot model performs inference only on the reduced set of candidate labels, thereby reducing the number of forward passes by a significant margin. The system operates in a closed-loop fashion during calibration, where the pseudo-labels used to train the base classifier are generated by the same zero-shot model that will later be used for final classification, ensuring consistency between the filtering and classification stages. This design eliminates the need for human-annotated calibration data, making the system fully functional in zero-shot scenarios where labeled examples are unavailable. The workflow is fully automated and requires no manual intervention once the system is calibrated, enabling seamless integration into production systems that demand high availability and low latency. Additionally, the conformal prediction engine is designed to be model-agnostic, meaning that it can be paired with any zero-shot classification architecture, whether based on NLI, NSP, prompt-based generation, or other formulations, as long as the classification process involves evaluating text-label pairs. The system also supports dynamic re-calibration, where the calibration dataset can be periodically updated with new unlabeled text samples to adapt to evolving language patterns or domain shifts, ensuring long-term robustness and reliability.

### Example Data Experiment and Performance

The effectiveness of the conformal prediction-based label filtering mechanism was empirically validated across five benchmark datasets spanning two distinct classification domains: intent classification and topic classification. The intent classification datasets included SNIPS, which comprises seven distinct user intents such as “PlayMusic” and “AddToPlaylist”; ATIS, containing seventeen flight-related queries such as “book a flight from Boston to New York”; and HWU64, a complex dataset with sixty-four diverse intents ranging from “BookRestaurant” to “GetWeather.” The topic classification datasets included AG’s News, which categorizes news articles into four broad topics such as “World” and “Sports,” and Yahoo! Answers, which classifies user questions into ten thematic categories such as “Society & Culture” and “Science & Mathematics.” For each dataset, the zero-shot classification backbone was instantiated using two distinct transformer architectures: a moderately sized BART-large model fine-tuned for Natural Language Inference (“facebook/bart-large-nli”) and a smaller BERT-base model adapted for Next Sentence Prediction (“bert-base-uncased”). Four different base classifiers were evaluated for the conformal prediction layer: a token overlap scorer, a cosine similarity scorer using static GloVe embeddings, a fine-tuned distilled BERT model (“distilbert-base-uncased”), and a version of the distilled model specifically fine-tuned for NLI classification (“cross-encoder/nli-distilroberta-base”). The calibration process was conducted using unlabeled text samples drawn from the training or validation splits of each dataset, with pseudo-labels generated by the respective zero-shot model. The conformal prediction engine was configured with a fixed error rate α of 0.01, corresponding to a 99% coverage guarantee, ensuring that the true label was retained in the filtered set with near-certainty. The performance of the system was measured along three key dimensions: classification accuracy, average inference time relative to the full label set, and the average number of candidate labels retained after filtering. The results demonstrated that the conformal prediction framework consistently achieved valid coverage across all datasets and base classifiers, with empirical coverage rates closely matching the nominal 99% target. The token overlap classifier, despite its simplicity, achieved the greatest reduction in inference time on several datasets, particularly on ATIS and SNIPS, due to its negligible computational overhead, even though it retained a larger number of candidate labels compared to more sophisticated classifiers. The fine-tuned distilled BERT classifier (CP-CLS) achieved the smallest average prediction set size, reducing the number of candidate labels by an average of 41.09% for the BART-based NLI model and 43.38% for the BERT-based NSP model, thereby enabling the largest reduction in computational load. Notably, the CP-CLS classifier did not always yield the fastest inference time, as its own inference latency occasionally approached that of the zero-shot model, particularly when paired with the lightweight NSP-based classifier, demonstrating the importance of selecting a base classifier whose computational cost is substantially lower than that of the primary model. On the HWU64 dataset, which features the largest label space of sixty-four classes, the reduction in average inference time reached 25.6% for the NLI model and 22.2% for the NSP model, highlighting the system’s superior scalability in high-cardinality scenarios. In terms of classification accuracy, the filtered system either matched or slightly exceeded the performance of the full label set baseline, with the CP-CLS classifier achieving a marginal accuracy gain of +0.31% on average, suggesting that label filtering not only improves efficiency but may also enhance robustness by removing noisy or semantically ambiguous labels that could otherwise confound the zero-shot model. Conversely, the token overlap classifier exhibited a slight accuracy degradation of −0.46%, attributable to its coarse-grained matching mechanism, yet still maintained performance within one percentage point of the baseline. The experiments further revealed that the calibration dataset size had a minimal impact on performance when the error rate α was kept low, with as few as 1,500 unlabeled samples sufficient to achieve stable coverage and prediction set size, thereby reducing the data requirements for deployment. Transferability experiments demonstrated that a conformal predictor calibrated on one dataset could be applied to another with acceptable performance, provided the error rate α was maintained at a low value, although the resulting prediction set size increased compared to task-specific calibration. These findings collectively establish that the proposed framework delivers a consistent and quantifiable improvement in computational efficiency without compromising classification reliability, making it uniquely suited for deployment in latency-sensitive, high-volume classification systems where both speed and accuracy are paramount. The system’s performance was further validated through ablation studies that isolated the contribution of each component, confirming that the conformal prediction layer was the primary driver of efficiency gains and that its statistical guarantees were essential to maintaining coverage under label filtering. The results underscore the practical viability of the invention as a drop-in optimization for any zero-shot classification pipeline, offering substantial reductions in inference cost while preserving or even enhancing model performance.
