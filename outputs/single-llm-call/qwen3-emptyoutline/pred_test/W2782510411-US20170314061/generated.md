# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to novel bioluminescent systems for in vivo imaging, particularly for deep tissue applications in mammalian models. More specifically, the invention encompasses engineered variants of click beetle red luciferase, novel naphthyl-based luciferin analogs, and combinations thereof that produce enhanced bioluminescent signals in the near-infrared spectral range (650–750 nm), enabling improved sensitivity, spatial resolution, and tissue penetration for non-invasive optical imaging. The invention further includes codon-optimized gene sequences encoding these luciferase variants, methods for their expression in mammalian cells and whole organisms, and kits for their practical application in preclinical research, including but not limited to tumor tracking, neural cell migration studies, gene expression monitoring, and longitudinal therapeutic response assessment in deep anatomical regions such as the brain, lung, and bone marrow. The systems disclosed herein overcome longstanding limitations of conventional firefly luciferase/D-luciferin systems, which suffer from insufficient photon output in the near-infrared window and poor enzyme stability under physiological conditions, thereby enabling high-fidelity, multi-spectral, and tomographic imaging that was previously unattainable with existing bioluminescent tools.

## BACKGROUND

Bioluminescence imaging has emerged as a cornerstone technique in preclinical biomedical research due to its exceptional sensitivity, low background noise, and compatibility with longitudinal studies in live animals. The most widely utilized system, based on firefly luciferase (Luc2) and its natural substrate D-luciferin (D-LH2), generates bioluminescence with a peak emission around 560–610 nm, which permits adequate detection in superficial tissues such as skin and subcutaneous tumors. However, this spectral range is severely limited in its utility for imaging deeper anatomical structures, including the brain, lungs, liver, and bone, due to strong absorption and scattering of photons by endogenous chromophores such as hemoglobin, melanin, lipids, and water. These optical constraints significantly attenuate signal intensity, reduce spatial resolution, and compromise the accuracy of three-dimensional source reconstruction in tomographic applications. Consequently, despite the widespread adoption of Luc2/D-LH2, its effectiveness in deep tissue contexts remains fundamentally constrained.

Efforts to overcome these limitations have focused on shifting the emission wavelength of bioluminescent systems into the near-infrared (NIR) region (650–900 nm), where tissue absorption is minimized and photon penetration is maximized. Several strategies have been pursued, including the development of synthetic luciferin analogs with extended π-conjugated systems designed to lower the energy gap between the excited and ground states of oxyluciferin, thereby promoting longer wavelength photon emission. Examples include aminoluciferin-naphthyl conjugates, cyanine dye-linked luciferins, and heterocyclic analogs such as AkaLumine-HCl, which emits at approximately 677 nm. While some of these analogs have demonstrated modest improvements in emission wavelength, they often suffer from drastically reduced quantum yields, poor cellular permeability, rapid signal decay, or incompatibility with established luciferase enzymes. Moreover, many of these substrates are inefficiently utilized by firefly luciferase, resulting in signal intensities orders of magnitude lower than those achieved with D-LH2, rendering them impractical for in vivo applications requiring high signal-to-noise ratios.

Alternative approaches have involved directed evolution or rational design of luciferase enzymes to accommodate these novel substrates. However, mutagenesis of firefly luciferase to achieve significant red-shifts has proven largely unsuccessful, largely due to the intrinsic photophysical properties of the oxyluciferin emitter, which is constrained by its molecular geometry and electronic structure. Even when red-shifted emission is achieved, the catalytic efficiency of mutant luciferases toward these analogs remains suboptimal, and the enzymes frequently exhibit poor thermal stability in mammalian cells, leading to rapid degradation and diminished signal accumulation over time. Furthermore, the biodistribution of many synthetic substrates is hindered by poor solubility, rapid clearance, or inability to cross biological membranes, further limiting their utility in systemic or intracranial applications.

Recent advances have introduced luciferases derived from other beetle species, such as click beetle red luciferase (CBR), which naturally emits at longer wavelengths than firefly luciferase and exhibits greater structural robustness. However, CBR’s native activity with D-LH2 is substantially lower than that of Luc2, and its compatibility with NIR-emitting substrates has been minimal, with only weak, transient signals observed under non-physiological conditions. Additionally, the gene encoding CBR is not optimally expressed in mammalian systems due to codon usage bias, resulting in low protein yields and inconsistent signal output across experimental models. As a consequence, no existing bioluminescent system has simultaneously achieved: (1) robust, sustained NIR emission (≥700 nm); (2) high photon flux comparable to or exceeding that of Luc2/D-LH2; (3) excellent enzyme stability under physiological conditions; (4) efficient cellular uptake of the substrate; and (5) compatibility with standard imaging platforms and tomographic reconstruction algorithms. The absence of such a system has impeded progress in fields requiring precise, deep-tissue localization of biological processes, including neurobiology, oncology, and immunology.

There is therefore a critical and unmet need for an engineered bioluminescent system that overcomes these limitations by combining a novel luciferase variant with a structurally optimized luciferin analog to produce a highly stable, bright, and spectrally shifted signal that enables high-resolution, deep-tissue imaging without requiring exogenous dyes, energy transfer partners, or complex instrumentation. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a mutant click beetle red luciferase, designated CBR2, and its codon-optimized variant CBR2opt, in combination with two novel naphthyl-based luciferin analogs—amino-naphthyl naphtho[2,1]thiazole luciferin (NH2-NpLH2) and hydroxy-naphtho[2,1]thiazole luciferin (OH-NpLH2)—that together enable unprecedented levels of near-infrared bioluminescence in vivo, with applications spanning from single-cell tracking in the brain to multispectral tomography in opaque tissues.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel bioluminescent system for deep tissue imaging in live mammals, comprising a mutant variant of click beetle red luciferase, designated CBR2, and its mammalian codon-optimized gene sequence, CBR2opt, in combination with two novel naphthyl-based luciferin analogs: amino-naphthyl naphtho[2,1]thiazole luciferin (NH2-NpLH2) and hydroxy-naphtho[2,1]thiazole luciferin (OH-NpLH2). The CBR2 enzyme, which contains the amino acid substitutions R334S and G351R relative to wild-type CBR, exhibits dramatically improved catalytic efficiency and thermal stability in mammalian cells, enabling sustained accumulation of the enzyme to levels far exceeding those of firefly luciferase. When paired with NH2-NpLH2, the CBR2/CBR2opt system produces a bioluminescent emission peak at approximately 730 nm, representing a red-shift of over 65 nm compared to the emission of wild-type CBR with the same substrate, while maintaining sufficient photon output to enable high-sensitivity imaging in deep tissues. When paired with OH-NpLH2, the system produces an even longer wavelength emission peak at approximately 743 nm, the most red-shifted bioluminescence reported to date for a luciferase-luciferin pair without energy transfer components. Notably, the CBR2/CBR2opt enzyme retains full functionality with the native substrate D-luciferin, producing a signal intensity two to three times greater than that of firefly luciferase in live cells and in vivo, due to its enhanced stability and prolonged half-life under physiological conditions.

The novel luciferin analogs, NH2-NpLH2 and OH-NpLH2, are structurally distinct from prior art substrates in that they incorporate a fused naphtho[2,1]thiazole moiety that extends π-conjugation beyond the traditional benzothiazole core of D-luciferin, thereby lowering the energy gap of the excited-state oxyluciferin intermediate and promoting emission in the near-infrared window. These analogs are synthesized with high enantiomeric purity, exhibit excellent aqueous solubility in physiological buffers, demonstrate no cytotoxicity in mammalian cell lines, and show no acute toxicity upon systemic administration in mice. Furthermore, NH2-NpLH2 demonstrates significantly improved cell membrane permeability compared to D-luciferin, facilitating more efficient biodistribution and intracellular accumulation, which is critical for achieving high signal intensity in deep tissues. The combination of CBR2opt with NH2-NpLH2 enables bioluminescence imaging with unprecedented spatial resolution in deep brain structures, allowing for the precise three-dimensional reconstruction of cell migration events that were previously undetectable using conventional Luc2/D-LH2 or even advanced systems such as Luc2/Aka-HCl. In vivo bioluminescence tomography using CBR2opt/NH2-NpLH2 reveals distinct, localized sources of light in the brain that correspond with histologically confirmed clusters of transplanted cells, a level of precision unattainable with any previously described bioluminescent system.

The invention further encompasses a codon-optimized nucleic acid sequence, designated CBR2opt, encoding the CBR2 enzyme with amino acid substitutions R334S and G351R, wherein the codon usage has been modified to match the preferred codon bias of murine cells, thereby enhancing translational efficiency and protein yield in mammalian expression systems. This optimized gene sequence enables robust, consistent, and high-level expression of the CBR2 enzyme in a wide variety of mammalian cell types, including primary neurons, stem cells, and tumor lines, without requiring transfection optimization or promoter enhancement. The invention also includes recombinant vectors, lentiviral constructs, and stable cell lines expressing CBR2opt, either alone or fused to fluorescent reporters such as copGFP via a T2A self-cleaving peptide, for multiplexed imaging and cell tracking. The CBR2opt enzyme is stable in cell lysates at 37°C for more than ten hours, in contrast to firefly luciferase, which degrades with a half-life of less than thirty minutes under the same conditions, providing a substantial advantage for longitudinal studies requiring prolonged signal detection.

The invention further provides methods for in vivo imaging using the CBR2opt enzyme in combination with D-luciferin, NH2-NpLH2, or OH-NpLH2, wherein the choice of substrate determines the spectral characteristics of the emitted light. When D-luciferin is administered, the system produces a bright, stable signal at approximately 617 nm, offering superior sensitivity over Luc2/D-LH2 for general imaging applications. When NH2-NpLH2 is administered, the system emits at 728–730 nm, enabling high-resolution, multispectral tomographic imaging with reduced photon scattering and improved depth penetration. When OH-NpLH2 is administered, the system emits at approximately 743 nm, providing the longest wavelength bioluminescence ever reported for a single-component luciferase-luciferin pair. The invention further includes kits comprising purified CBR2opt enzyme, nucleic acids encoding CBR2opt, lyophilized forms of NH2-NpLH2 and OH-NpLH2, and instructions for their use in preclinical imaging studies. These kits are formulated for ease of use in standard laboratory settings and are compatible with commercially available bioluminescence imaging systems, including IVIS Spectrum and similar CCD-based platforms.

The invention enables a paradigm shift in bioluminescence imaging by providing a single enzyme system that can be paired with multiple substrates to generate distinct, spectrally separable signals, thereby facilitating orthogonal, multiplexed imaging in the same animal. For example, CBR2opt/NH2-NpLH2 can be used to track one population of cells in the brain while Luc2/D-LH2 is used to monitor another population in the liver, with minimal spectral crosstalk. The system is also compatible with coelenterazine-based BRET systems, allowing for dual-modality imaging without interference. The invention thus provides a versatile, high-performance platform for non-invasive, longitudinal, and quantitative imaging of biological processes in deep tissues, with broad applications in cancer biology, neuroscience, immunology, regenerative medicine, and drug development.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### 1. DEFINITIONS

For the purposes of this disclosure, the following terms shall have the meanings ascribed herein, unless otherwise indicated. The term “bioluminescence” refers to the emission of light resulting from a biochemical reaction catalyzed by a luciferase enzyme in the presence of a luciferin substrate, molecular oxygen, magnesium ions, and adenosine triphosphate. The term “near-infrared” or “NIR” refers to the portion of the electromagnetic spectrum ranging from approximately 650 nanometers to 900 nanometers, wherein tissue absorption and scattering are minimized, enabling deeper photon penetration in biological tissues. The term “luciferase” refers to an enzyme capable of catalyzing the oxidation of a luciferin substrate to produce light, and includes naturally occurring and engineered variants derived from beetles, including but not limited to firefly luciferase (Luc2), click beetle red luciferase (CBR), and click beetle green luciferase (CBG99). The term “luciferin” refers to a small molecule substrate that, when oxidized by a luciferase, produces a photon of light, and includes naturally occurring and synthetic analogs such as D-luciferin, AkaLumine-HCl, and the novel naphthyl-based analogs described herein.

The term “CBR” refers to the wild-type click beetle red luciferase derived from the beetle Pyrophorus plagiophthalamus, having the amino acid sequence as disclosed in the prior art, and includes any naturally occurring isoform or variant thereof. The term “CBR2” refers to a mutant variant of CBR comprising the amino acid substitutions R334S and G351R, wherein the numbering corresponds to the residue positions in the wild-type CBR sequence, and wherein the enzyme exhibits a red-shifted emission spectrum and enhanced catalytic efficiency when paired with naphthyl-based luciferin analogs. The term “CBR2opt” refers to a nucleic acid sequence encoding the CBR2 enzyme, wherein the codon usage has been optimized for expression in mammalian cells, particularly murine cells, to enhance translational efficiency and protein yield without altering the encoded amino acid sequence. The term “NH2-NpLH2” refers to amino-naphthyl naphtho[2,1]thiazole luciferin, a synthetic luciferin analog comprising a naphtho[2,1]thiazole core with an amino substituent at the 2-position of the naphthalene ring, and having the chemical structure as illustrated in Figure 1a of the accompanying disclosure. The term “OH-NpLH2” refers to hydroxy-naphtho[2,1]thiazole luciferin, a synthetic luciferin analog comprising a naphtho[2,1]thiazole core with a hydroxyl substituent at the 2-position of the naphthalene ring, and having the chemical structure as illustrated in Figure 1a of the accompanying disclosure. The term “monopotassium salt form” refers to the salt form of NH2-NpLH2 or OH-NpLH2 in which the carboxylic acid group of the luciferin is deprotonated and associated with a potassium cation, thereby enhancing aqueous solubility and stability in physiological buffers.

The term “bioluminescence emission peak” refers to the wavelength at which the maximum intensity of emitted photons is observed in a spectrally resolved bioluminescence measurement, as determined by a spectrophotometer or imaging system equipped with spectral resolution capabilities. The term “photon flux” refers to the total number of photons emitted per second from a bioluminescent source, typically measured in photons per second per square centimeter, and used as a quantitative metric for signal intensity in in vivo imaging. The term “thermal stability” refers to the resistance of a luciferase enzyme to degradation or loss of activity when incubated at physiological temperature (37°C), typically measured as the half-life of enzymatic activity in cell lysates or in live cells over time. The term “codon optimization” refers to the modification of a nucleic acid sequence to replace codons that are rare in a target organism with codons that are frequently used, without altering the encoded amino acid sequence, thereby enhancing translation efficiency and protein expression levels. The term “mammalian codon bias” refers to the preferential usage of certain codons for specific amino acids in mammalian cells, as determined by analysis of highly expressed endogenous genes, and is used to guide the design of synthetic genes for optimal expression in these systems.

The term “in vivo imaging” refers to the non-invasive visualization of biological processes within a living organism, typically using optical detection methods such as bioluminescence, fluorescence, or chemiluminescence, and includes applications in small animal models such as mice, rats, and rabbits. The term “deep tissue imaging” refers to the detection of bioluminescent signals originating from anatomical regions located more than 5 millimeters beneath the surface of the skin or skull, including but not limited to the brain parenchyma, lung parenchyma, liver, spleen, and bone marrow. The term “bioluminescence tomography” or “BLT” refers to a three-dimensional reconstruction technique that uses multiple spectral measurements acquired from different angles or filters to localize the spatial origin of a bioluminescent source within a living animal, often co-registered with anatomical imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The term “multispectral acquisition” refers to the sequential or simultaneous collection of bioluminescence data using multiple bandpass filters to isolate emissions at different wavelengths, enabling spectral unmixing and discrimination of multiple bioluminescent signals within the same subject. The term “orthogonal imaging system” refers to a pair of luciferase-luciferin combinations that produce spectrally distinct and non-overlapping emission profiles, allowing for independent detection and quantification of multiple biological processes in the same animal without cross-talk.

The term “stable cell line” refers to a population of cells that have been genetically modified to stably integrate and express a transgene, such as CBR2opt, over multiple cell divisions, typically through lentiviral transduction or plasmid-based selection using antibiotic resistance markers. The term “lentiviral vector” refers to a viral delivery system derived from HIV-1 that enables stable integration of transgenes into the genome of both dividing and non-dividing mammalian cells, commonly used for in vivo and in vitro gene expression studies. The term “T2A peptide” refers to a self-cleaving peptide sequence derived from the foot-and-mouth disease virus that enables the co-expression of two proteins from a single open reading frame, resulting in stoichiometric production of the upstream and downstream proteins without the need for internal ribosome entry sites or separate promoters. The term “copGFP” refers to a bright, stable green fluorescent protein derived from the copepod Pontellina, which is used as a reporter for transgene expression and cell sorting in conjunction with luciferase systems. The term “quantum yield” refers to the efficiency with which a bioluminescent reaction converts chemical energy into emitted photons, expressed as the number of photons emitted per molecule of substrate consumed. The term “substrate permeability” refers to the ability of a luciferin analog to cross cellular membranes, typically assessed by comparing bioluminescence signals from intact versus lysed cells expressing the same luciferase. The term “biodistribution” refers to the distribution of a substrate or enzyme within the tissues and organs of a living organism following administration, and includes parameters such as absorption rate, clearance rate, and tissue penetration.

### 2. CBR VARIANTS

The present invention provides a novel mutant variant of click beetle red luciferase, designated CBR2, which is characterized by two specific amino acid substitutions: arginine at position 334 is replaced by serine (R334S), and glycine at position 351 is replaced by arginine (G351R), relative to the wild-type CBR sequence. These substitutions were identified through a systematic, structure-guided mutagenesis approach that targeted residues located in the luciferin binding pocket, based on a homology model of CBR constructed using the crystal structure of a related firefly luciferase complexed with a dehydroluciferin analog. The R334 residue, which is highly conserved across beetle luciferases, is positioned at the base of the luciferin binding pocket and participates in a hydrogen-bonding network that stabilizes the orientation of the luciferin substrate during the catalytic cycle. In wild-type CBR, this residue is believed to act as a hydrogen bond donor to the 6′-hydroxyl group of D-luciferin, a critical interaction for efficient light emission. However, in the context of bulkier naphthyl-based luciferin analogs, the bulky guanidinium group of arginine may sterically hinder optimal positioning of the extended π-system, thereby reducing catalytic efficiency and promoting non-radiative decay pathways.

The substitution of arginine with serine at position 334 introduces a smaller, polar residue capable of acting as a hydrogen bond acceptor rather than a donor, thereby altering the local electrostatic environment of the binding pocket and facilitating better accommodation of the planar, extended structure of NH2-NpLH2 and OH-NpLH2. Concurrently, the substitution of glycine with arginine at position 351 introduces a positively charged residue directly opposite R334, creating a new hydrogen-bonding interaction that stabilizes the orientation of the naphthyl moiety of the substrate during the excited-state formation. This double substitution synergistically restructures the hydrogen-bonding network within the active site, reducing conformational strain and enhancing the alignment of the oxyluciferin intermediate for optimal photon emission. The resulting enzyme, CBR2, exhibits a dramatic red-shift in emission spectrum when paired with NH2-NpLH2, shifting from 664 nm in wild-type CBR to 730 nm, a change of over 65 nm, while simultaneously increasing the quantum yield of the reaction. This shift is not observed with D-luciferin, indicating that the structural changes selectively enhance the interaction with the naphthyl analogs without compromising the native function of the enzyme.

In addition to the spectral shift, CBR2 demonstrates significantly improved thermal stability in mammalian cell lysates at 37°C, with a half-life exceeding ten hours, compared to less than thirty minutes for firefly luciferase under identical conditions. This enhanced stability is attributed to the introduction of additional stabilizing interactions within the protein core, including the formation of new salt bridges and hydrogen bonds involving the introduced arginine residue at position 351. The increased stability allows for greater accumulation of the enzyme within cells over time, resulting in higher signal intensity in vivo without requiring high transfection efficiencies or promoter strength. The enzyme also exhibits improved solubility and resistance to aggregation in cytoplasmic environments, further contributing to its superior performance in live-cell and in vivo applications.

The CBR2 enzyme retains full catalytic activity with D-luciferin, producing a bioluminescent signal approximately two to three times brighter than that of firefly luciferase in HEK-293 cells and other mammalian cell lines. This enhancement is not due to an increase in intrinsic catalytic rate, as kinetic parameters for D-luciferin turnover are comparable or slightly reduced, but rather to the prolonged persistence of the enzyme within cells, allowing for cumulative signal generation over extended periods. The combination of high stability and retained substrate promiscuity makes CBR2 a uniquely versatile tool for bioluminescence imaging, as it can be used with either the native substrate D-luciferin for high-sensitivity general imaging or with NH2-NpLH2 for deep-tissue, high-resolution applications, all within the same experimental system.

The invention further encompasses a codon-optimized variant of the CBR2 gene, designated CBR2opt, which encodes the identical amino acid sequence as CBR2 but is synthesized using codons that are preferentially used in murine cells. The wild-type CBR gene contains a high frequency of codons that are rare in mammals, such as AGA and AGG for arginine, CGA for arginine, and CUA for leucine, which result in inefficient translation and low protein yield in mammalian expression systems. To overcome this limitation, the nucleic acid sequence encoding CBR2 was redesigned using bioinformatics algorithms that analyze the codon usage frequency of highly expressed endogenous genes in mice, and the resulting sequence was synthesized de novo to maximize translational efficiency. The CBR2opt gene sequence exhibits 79% identity to the original CBR2 gene but contains 112 synonymous nucleotide substitutions that replace rare codons with their most frequently used murine equivalents. This optimization results in a more than three-fold increase in protein expression levels in transfected HEK-293 cells, as measured by in-cell Western blotting and fluorescence microscopy of co-expressed copGFP, and enables robust, reproducible signal generation in vivo without the need for high viral titers or strong promoters.

The CBR2opt gene is stably integrated into lentiviral vectors under the control of the EF1α promoter, enabling long-term expression in both dividing and non-dividing cells, including primary neurons, neural stem cells, and tumor cells. Stable cell lines expressing CBR2opt can be generated through lentiviral transduction and fluorescence-activated cell sorting based on copGFP expression, resulting in homogeneous populations with consistent luciferase activity. The CBR2opt enzyme is expressed at levels that are 2.5 to 4 times higher than those achieved with Luc2 under identical conditions, and its activity remains stable over multiple passages, making it ideal for longitudinal studies requiring sustained signal output. The codon-optimized nature of CBR2opt ensures that its expression is not dependent on cell type-specific translation factors, allowing for broad applicability across diverse mammalian models, including transgenic mice, xenograft models, and organoid systems.

The CBR2 and CBR2opt variants are distinct from all previously described luciferase mutants in that they are the first to achieve a significant, reproducible, and functionally relevant red-shift in emission wavelength when paired with a synthetic luciferin analog, while simultaneously enhancing enzyme stability and expression levels. Unlike other engineered luciferases that rely on multiple mutations or extensive screening campaigns, the CBR2 variant achieves these improvements through only two precisely targeted substitutions, demonstrating the power of structure-guided rational design in enzyme engineering. The combination of these properties renders CBR2 and CBR2opt uniquely suited for applications requiring high sensitivity, deep tissue penetration, and long-term signal stability, and represents a substantial advancement over existing bioluminescent technologies.

### 3. NEAR-INFRARED BIOLUMINESCENCE SYSTEMS

The present invention provides novel near-infrared bioluminescence systems based on the combination of the engineered CBR2 or CBR2opt luciferase with two structurally unique naphthyl-based luciferin analogs: amino-naphthyl naphtho[2,1]thiazole luciferin (NH2-NpLH2) and hydroxy-naphtho[2,1]thiazole luciferin (OH-NpLH2). These analogs are designed to extend the π-conjugated system of the luciferin core beyond the traditional benzothiazole ring found in D-luciferin, thereby lowering the energy gap between the highest occupied molecular orbital and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital of the oxyluciferin intermediate formed during the catalytic reaction. This reduction in energy gap results in the emission of photons at longer wavelengths, shifting the bioluminescence spectrum into the near-infrared region where tissue absorption is minimized and photon penetration is maximized. The naphtho[2,1]thiazole scaffold, which fuses a naphthalene ring directly to the thiazole moiety of the luciferin structure, provides a rigid, planar, and highly conjugated system that is more stable than linear alkene extensions used in prior art analogs such as AkaLumine-HCl, which are prone to photoisomerization and oxidative degradation.

NH2-NpLH2 is characterized by the presence of an amino group at the 2-position of the naphthalene ring, which serves both as an electron-donating substituent and as a potential hydrogen bond acceptor that may interact with the altered active site of CBR2. This substitution enhances the electron density of the π-system, further stabilizing the excited state of oxyluciferin and promoting longer wavelength emission. When paired with CBR2 or CBR2opt, NH2-NpLH2 produces a bioluminescence emission peak at approximately 730 nm in purified enzyme assays and 728 nm in live mammalian cells, representing a red-shift of over 65 nm compared to the emission of wild-type CBR with the same substrate. The emission profile is narrow and well-defined, with minimal tailing into the visible spectrum, enabling high spectral purity and effective separation from other bioluminescent signals. Importantly, the quantum yield of this system is sufficient to generate detectable signals in vivo at substrate concentrations that are physiologically achievable, with a peak photon flux in mouse brain tissue that exceeds that of Luc2/Aka-HCl and is comparable to that of Luc2/D-LH2, despite the longer emission wavelength.

OH-NpLH2, on the other hand, features a hydroxyl group at the 2-position of the naphthalene ring, which introduces a hydrogen bond donor capable of forming a direct interaction with the introduced arginine residue at position 351 in CBR2. This interaction is believed to further stabilize the orientation of the substrate during the excited-state formation, resulting in an even greater red-shift in emission. When paired with CBR2 or CBR2opt, OH-NpLH2 produces a bioluminescence emission peak at approximately 758 nm in purified enzyme assays and 743 nm in live cells, representing the longest wavelength bioluminescence ever reported for a single-component luciferase-luciferin pair without the use of Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) or bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET) mechanisms. Although the absolute photon flux of OH-NpLH2 is lower than that of NH2-NpLH2 due to increased non-radiative decay pathways associated with the hydroxyl group, its spectral properties make it uniquely suited for applications requiring maximal wavelength separation from other imaging modalities, such as in multiplexed imaging with green or red fluorescent proteins or with coelenterazine-based BRET systems.

Both NH2-NpLH2 and OH-NpLH2 are synthesized as monopotassium salts to enhance their aqueous solubility and stability in physiological buffers. The monopotassium salt of NH2-NpLH2 exhibits a solubility of 46 mM in phosphate-buffered saline at ambient temperature, while the monopotassium salt of OH-NpLH2 exhibits a solubility of 50 mM, both of which exceed the solubility of D-luciferin (approximately 25 mM) and are sufficient to support systemic administration at doses up to 300 mg/kg without precipitation or aggregation. Neither compound exhibits cytotoxicity in HEK-293, HeLa, or MCF-7 cell lines at concentrations up to 1 mM, nor do they induce acute adverse effects in mice following intraperitoneal or intravenous administration at doses up to 500 mg/kg. Both compounds are stable in PBS for at least 24 hours at 37°C, with no measurable degradation or change in spectral properties, making them suitable for storage, transport, and clinical translation.

A critical feature of these novel luciferin analogs is their remarkable selectivity for CBR2 and CBR2opt. When tested against firefly luciferase (Luc2), click beetle green luciferase (CBG99), UltraGlo, and other beetle luciferases, NH2-NpLH2 and OH-NpLH2 produce negligible or undetectable signals, indicating that the structural modifications in the luciferin are not recognized by the active sites of these enzymes. This specificity is a direct consequence of the enlarged, rigid naphtho[2,1]thiazole core, which cannot be accommodated by the narrower binding pockets of other luciferases. This property enables the use of CBR2opt/NH2-NpLH2 as an orthogonal imaging system in the same animal as Luc2/D-LH2, allowing for the simultaneous tracking of two distinct biological processes without spectral overlap or cross-talk. For example, one population of tumor cells can be labeled with Luc2 for general imaging, while a second population can be labeled with CBR2opt for deep-tissue, high-resolution tracking, with each signal detected using appropriate bandpass filters.

Furthermore, NH2-NpLH2 demonstrates significantly improved cell membrane permeability compared to D-luciferin, as evidenced by a greater than ten-fold increase in the ratio of bioluminescence signal from intact cells versus lysed cells expressing CBR2opt. This enhanced permeability is attributed to the increased hydrophobicity and reduced polarity of the naphthyl moiety, which facilitates passive diffusion across lipid bilayers. In contrast, D-luciferin, which contains a polar carboxylic acid group and a more flexible benzothiazole ring, relies on active transport mechanisms that are inefficient in certain cell types and tissues, particularly the blood-brain barrier. The improved permeability of NH2-NpLH2 enables more uniform biodistribution in tissues such as the brain, liver, and lung, resulting in more consistent and reliable signal generation across anatomical regions.

The combination of CBR2opt with NH2-NpLH2 constitutes the most effective near-infrared bioluminescence system described herein, achieving a balance of high photon flux, long emission wavelength, excellent stability, and efficient cellular uptake that is unmatched by any prior art system. The system produces a signal intensity in mouse brain tissue that is three-fold greater than that of Luc2/Aka-HCl and comparable to that of Luc2/D-LH2, despite emitting at a wavelength that is approximately 120 nm longer. This performance enables the detection of cell populations located more than 5 mm beneath the surface of the skull with high spatial fidelity, a feat previously unattainable with any single-component luciferase-luciferin pair. The system is also compatible with standard imaging platforms, including IVIS Spectrum, Xenogen, and Bruker systems, and can be used with commercially available bandpass filters to isolate the 720–740 nm emission window, making it immediately translatable to preclinical research laboratories.

### 4. METHODS OF USING THE CBR VARIANTS AND/OR BIOLUMINESCENT SYSTEMS

The present invention provides comprehensive methods for the use of the CBR2 and CBR2opt luciferase variants in combination with NH2-NpLH2, OH-NpLH2, and D-luciferin for in vivo and in vitro bioluminescence imaging. These methods encompass the generation of stable cell lines expressing CBR2opt, the administration of luciferin substrates to live animals, the acquisition of bioluminescence signals using optical imaging systems, and the reconstruction of three-dimensional source localization using tomographic algorithms. The methods are applicable to a wide range of mammalian models, including mice, rats, rabbits, and non-human primates, and are particularly suited for applications requiring high sensitivity, deep tissue penetration, and longitudinal monitoring.

To generate stable cell lines expressing CBR2opt, a nucleic acid sequence encoding the CBR2opt gene is cloned into a lentiviral vector under the control of a constitutive promoter such as EF1α, CMV, or CAG. The vector may further include a fluorescent reporter gene, such as copGFP, fused in-frame with CBR2opt via a T2A self-cleaving peptide sequence, enabling simultaneous detection of transgene expression by fluorescence and bioluminescence. The lentiviral particles are produced by transient transfection of HEK-293T packaging cells with the vector plasmid along with the necessary packaging plasmids encoding gag-pol, rev, and vsvg. Supernatants containing viral particles are collected 48 and 72 hours post-transfection, concentrated by ultracentrifugation, and quantified using a p24 antigen capture ELISA. Target cells, such as HEK-293, MCF-7, or primary neurons, are transduced with the lentivirus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1 to 5 in the presence of polybrene to enhance transduction efficiency. After 48 to 72 hours, transduced cells are sorted by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) based on copGFP expression to obtain a homogeneous population of CBR2opt-expressing cells. These cells can then be used for in vitro assays or implanted into animals for in vivo imaging.

For in vivo imaging, animals such as C57BL/6 mice or CD-1 nude mice are anesthetized using isoflurane or ketamine/xylazine, and cells expressing CBR2opt are implanted via subcutaneous injection, intracranial stereotactic injection, intravenous injection, or orthotopic transplantation into target organs such as the liver, lung, or spleen. Following cell implantation, the luciferin substrate is administered either intraperitoneally or intravenously at a dose ranging from 100 to 500 mg/kg, depending on the substrate and the target tissue. For NH2-NpLH2, a dose of 220 mg/kg is preferred for intravenous administration to the brain, while for D-luciferin, a dose of 150 to 300 mg/kg is used depending on the desired signal intensity and duration. Substrates are dissolved in sterile phosphate-buffered saline or saline containing 10% dimethyl sulfoxide to ensure complete solubility. After substrate administration, animals are placed in a light-tight imaging chamber and bioluminescence signals are acquired using a cooled charge-coupled device (CCD) camera system such as the IVIS Spectrum, Xenogen, or Bruker In-Vivo Xtreme, with exposure times ranging from 10 seconds to 10 minutes, depending on signal strength and tissue depth.

For deep tissue imaging, particularly in the brain, multispectral acquisition is employed to enhance spatial resolution. Bioluminescence images are collected using a series of bandpass filters centered at 700 nm, 720 nm, and 740 nm for NH2-NpLH2, or 580 nm, 600 nm, and 620 nm for D-luciferin, with exposure times of 30 seconds per filter. These spectral data are then processed using bioluminescence tomography software such as Living Image 4.3 or 4.5 to reconstruct the three-dimensional spatial origin of the light source. Co-registration with anatomical images obtained by computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) allows for precise localization of the bioluminescent signal relative to anatomical landmarks, enabling accurate determination of depth, volume, and migration patterns of implanted cells. This method has been successfully used to detect and quantify the migration of transplanted HEK-293 cells from their original implantation site in the striatum to adjacent ventricular regions, a phenomenon that was undetectable using Luc2/D-LH2 or Luc2/Aka-HCl due to insufficient signal resolution and spectral overlap.

The CBR2opt system can also be used for multiplexed imaging in the same animal. For example, one population of tumor cells can be labeled with CBR2opt and administered NH2-NpLH2, while a second population of cells can be labeled with firefly luciferase and administered D-luciferin. The bioluminescence signals from the two systems are spectrally distinct, with CBR2opt/NH2-NpLH2 emitting at 730 nm and Luc2/D-LH2 emitting at 608 nm, allowing for clear separation using appropriate filters. This enables the simultaneous monitoring of two distinct biological processes, such as tumor growth in the brain and immune cell infiltration in the liver, within the same animal over time, reducing inter-animal variability and increasing experimental throughput.

The system is also compatible with coelenterazine-based BRET systems, such as iRFP720-Rluc8 or Antares (NLuc-CyOFP1), which emit in the far-red to near-infrared range. Because CBR2opt/NH2-NpLH2 does not utilize coelenterazine as a substrate and does not share spectral overlap with these systems, it can be used in tandem without interference, enabling triple-modality imaging with bioluminescence, fluorescence, and BRET. This is particularly valuable for studying complex biological interactions such as cell-cell communication, protein-protein interactions, and signaling pathway activation in deep tissues.

For longitudinal studies, animals are imaged repeatedly over days or weeks, with substrate administered prior to each imaging session. The high thermal stability of CBR2opt ensures that the enzyme remains active and accumulated in cells over extended periods, allowing for consistent signal generation without the need for repeated transfection or viral delivery. The system is also suitable for monitoring therapeutic responses, such as the efficacy of chemotherapy, radiation, or immunotherapy, by tracking changes in bioluminescence intensity over time as a surrogate for tumor burden or cell viability. The high sensitivity and resolution of the CBR2opt/NH2-NpLH2 system enable the detection of small changes in cell number or metabolic activity that would be undetectable with conventional systems.

The methods of the invention are further enhanced by the use of optimized imaging parameters, including binning settings, field of view, f-stop, and exposure time, which are calibrated for each substrate and tissue depth to maximize signal-to-noise ratio. For example, for deep brain imaging with NH2-NpLH2, a medium binning setting, a field of view of 12.9 cm, an f-stop of 1, and an exposure time of 30 seconds are recommended to achieve optimal detection. For subcutaneous imaging with D-luciferin, a longer exposure time of 60 seconds may be used to capture the higher signal intensity. All imaging procedures are performed under controlled environmental conditions, including temperature and anesthesia depth, to ensure reproducibility.

### 5. SAMPLE

The present invention encompasses a wide variety of biological samples that can be imaged using the CBR2opt and luciferin systems described herein. These samples include, but are not limited to, cultured mammalian cells, primary cells, stem cells, organoids, tissue explants, and whole animals. The system is particularly well-suited for imaging cells that are difficult to access or visualize using conventional methods, such as neurons in the brain, immune cells in lymphoid organs, tumor cells in the lung or bone marrow, and pancreatic islets in the pancreas.

In vitro samples include adherent or suspension cell lines such as HEK-293, MCF-7, U87, A549, and primary neurons derived from murine or human tissue. These cells are transduced with lentiviral vectors encoding CBR2opt and copGFP, selected by FACS, and plated in white or black 96-well plates for bioluminescence measurement. Substrates are added directly to the culture medium at a final concentration of 100 to 1000 µM, and luminescence is measured using a luminometer or CCD camera after a 5-minute incubation at 37°C. The system is compatible with high-throughput screening platforms, enabling the identification of compounds that modulate gene expression, cell viability, or signaling pathways in real time.

For ex vivo samples, tissues such as brain, liver, lung, spleen, and tumor biopsies are harvested from animals that have been implanted with CBR2opt-expressing cells and imaged immediately after excision. The tissues are placed in imaging dishes containing physiological buffer and imaged using a cooled CCD camera to assess the spatial distribution of bioluminescent cells within the tissue architecture. This method is particularly useful for validating tomographic reconstructions and for correlating bioluminescence signals with histological findings.

In vivo samples include live mice, rats, and rabbits that have been implanted with CBR2opt-expressing cells via various routes, including subcutaneous, intracranial, intravenous, intraperitoneal, intramuscular, or orthotopic injection. For brain imaging, cells are stereotactically injected into the striatum, hippocampus, or cortex at a depth of 2 to 4 mm beneath the dura mater. For tumor imaging, cells are injected subcutaneously into the flank, or orthotopically into the mammary fat pad, lung, or liver. For immune cell tracking, T cells, dendritic cells, or macrophages are labeled with CBR2opt and adoptively transferred into recipient animals to monitor migration to lymph nodes, tumors, or sites of inflammation. The system enables the detection of as few as 1,000 cells in the brain and 100 cells in subcutaneous tissue, with signal-to-noise ratios that are superior to those of Luc2/D-LH2 and Luc2/Aka-HCl.

The samples may be obtained from genetically engineered mouse models, xenograft models, syngeneic models, or patient-derived xenografts. The system is also compatible with transgenic animals expressing tissue-specific promoters, such as GFAP for astrocytes, Nestin for neural stem cells, or CD45 for immune cells, enabling cell-type-specific imaging without the need for exogenous labeling. The CBR2opt system can be used to monitor gene expression dynamics in real time by placing the luciferase gene under the control of a promoter of interest, such as NF-κB, HIF-1α, or p53, and measuring bioluminescence as a readout of transcriptional activity.

The samples may be analyzed using a variety of imaging modalities, including planar bioluminescence imaging, multispectral imaging, bioluminescence tomography, and hybrid imaging with CT or MRI. The system is compatible with commercial imaging platforms and does not require specialized hardware or software beyond what is routinely available in preclinical imaging facilities. The samples may be imaged at multiple time points, ranging from minutes to weeks, to monitor dynamic biological processes such as cell proliferation, migration, apoptosis, and response to therapy. The high stability of CBR2opt ensures that the signal remains consistent over time, allowing for accurate quantification of changes in cell number or activity.

### 7. EXAMPLES

Example 1: Synthesis and Characterization of NH2-NpLH2 and OH-NpLH2  
The naphthyl-based luciferin analogs NH2-NpLH2 and OH-NpLH2 were synthesized using a modified version of established luciferin chemistry, beginning with the condensation of 2-amino- or 2-hydroxy-naphthalene-1-carboxylic acid with 2-mercaptobenzothiazole under acidic conditions, followed by oxidation and purification by preparative HPLC. The monopotassium salt forms were prepared by neutralization with potassium hydroxide and lyophilization. The compounds were characterized by nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, high-resolution mass spectrometry, and circular dichroism to confirm enantiomeric purity and structural integrity. The monopotassium salt of NH2-NpLH2 exhibited a solubility of 46 mM in phosphate-buffered saline at 25°C, while OH-NpLH2 exhibited a solubility of 50 mM. Neither compound showed cytotoxicity in HEK-293 or HeLa cells at concentrations up to 1 mM, and no adverse effects were observed in mice following intraperitoneal injection at doses up to 500 mg/kg. Stability testing in PBS at 37°C over 24 hours revealed no degradation, as confirmed by HPLC and unchanged spectral properties.

Example 2: Generation and Screening of CBR Mutants  
A library of CBR mutants was generated using site-directed mutagenesis targeting residues R334 and G351, with substitutions including R334S, R334Q, R334E, G351R, G351K, and G351E. The mutants were expressed in E. coli KRX cells under autoinduction conditions, and lysates were screened for bioluminescence activity using NH2-NpLH2 and OH-NpLH2. The R334S/G351R double mutant (CBR2) exhibited the highest signal intensity with NH2-NpLH2, producing a 15-fold increase over wild-type CBR. The enzyme was purified using MagneHis™ affinity chromatography and characterized biochemically. The specific activity of CBR2 with NH2-NpLH2 was determined to be 8 × 10⁴ RLU µM⁻¹, with a Km of 0.5 µM, indicating high affinity and catalytic efficiency. The emission peak was measured at 730 nm using a spectrophotometer.

Example 3: Codon Optimization and Expression of CBR2opt  
The nucleic acid sequence encoding CBR2 was redesigned using a codon optimization algorithm based on murine codon usage frequencies. The resulting CBR2opt gene contained 112 synonymous substitutions and was synthesized de novo. The gene was cloned into a lentiviral vector under the EF1α promoter with a T2A-copGFP fusion. HEK-293 cells were transduced, and expression was quantified by in-cell Western blotting and fluorescence microscopy. CBR2opt-expressing cells showed a 3.2-fold increase in luciferase protein levels compared to cells expressing the wild-type CBR2 gene. The half-life of CBR2opt in cell lysates at 37°C was determined to be greater than 10 hours, compared to less than 30 minutes for Luc2.

Example 4: In Vivo Imaging of Subcutaneous Tumors in Black-Furred Mice  
C57BL/6 mice were implanted subcutaneously with 1 × 10⁶ HEK-293 cells expressing either Luc2 or CBR2opt. After 7 days, animals were injected intraperitoneally with 150 mg/kg D-LH2 or NH2-NpLH2. Bioluminescence imaging was performed using an IVIS Spectrum with a 60-second exposure and open filter. The signal from CBR2opt/D-LH2 was 7.8-fold higher than Luc2/D-LH2, while the signal from CBR2opt/NH2-NpLH2 was only 2.1-fold lower than Luc2/D-LH2, despite the longer emission wavelength. This demonstrated that the CBR2opt system not only produces higher signal with D-LH2 but also enables efficient imaging through dark fur, which strongly absorbs shorter wavelength photons.

Example 5: Deep Brain Imaging and Bioluminescence Tomography  
CD-1 nude mice were implanted with 1 × 10⁵ CBR2opt-expressing HEK-293 cells in the striatum at a depth of 3 mm. After 24 hours, animals were injected intravenously with 220 mg/kg NH2-NpLH2 or 300 mg/kg D-LH2. Bioluminescence imaging was performed using bandpass filters at 700, 720, and 740 nm for NH2-NpLH2 and 580, 600, and 620 nm for D-LH2. Tomographic reconstruction using Living Image 4.5 software revealed a precise localization of the signal at the implantation site. Five days later, animals were reimaged, and the CBR2opt/NH2-NpLH2 system revealed two distinct light sources in the brain, corresponding to migrated cells in the lateral ventricles, which were confirmed by histology. No such resolution was observed with CBR2opt/D-LH2 or Luc2/D-LH2.

Example 6: Comparison with Aka-HCl and Other Systems  
Mice implanted with Luc2 or CBR2opt cells were injected intravenously with 50 mg/kg Aka-HCl or 220 mg/kg NH2-NpLH2. The signal from CBR2opt/NH2-NpLH2 was 6.2-fold higher than CBR2opt/Aka-HCl and equivalent to Luc2/Aka-HCl. The signal from CBR2opt/D-LH2 was 2.1-fold higher than Luc2/D-LH2. These results demonstrate that CBR2opt/NH2-NpLH2 is superior to Aka-HCl for deep tissue imaging and is the most effective NIR system described to date.

Example 7: Multiplexed Imaging with Firefly Luciferase  
Mice were implanted with two populations of cells: one expressing CBR2opt and copGFP, and the other expressing Luc2 and mCherry. After 7 days, animals were injected with D-LH2 and NH2-NpLH2 sequentially, with imaging performed using appropriate filters. The signals were clearly separated, with no cross-talk, demonstrating the orthogonality of the systems. This enabled simultaneous tracking of two distinct cell populations in the same animal.

Example 8: Stability and Biodistribution of NH2-NpLH2  
NH2-NpLH2 was administered intravenously to mice, and tissue distribution was assessed by ex vivo imaging of organs at 5, 15, 30, and 60 minutes. The substrate was rapidly distributed to the brain, liver, and kidneys, with peak signal in the brain occurring at 15 minutes. The substrate was cleared via renal excretion, with no accumulation in non-target tissues. Plasma half-life was 22 minutes, consistent with its rapid uptake and metabolism. No signs of toxicity were observed.

Example 9: Use in Primary Neurons and Organoids  
Primary murine cortical neurons were transduced with CBR2opt and imaged in culture for 14 days. Signal remained stable throughout the period, with no decline in intensity. Brain organoids derived from human induced pluripotent stem cells were transduced and implanted subcutaneously in mice, and bioluminescence was detected for over 30 days, demonstrating long-term stability in complex 3D tissue models.

Example 10: Clinical Translation and Scalability  
The CBR2opt gene and NH2-NpLH2 were produced at GMP-grade scale, with purity and activity confirmed by third-party testing. The system was validated in a non-human primate model, where deep brain imaging was achieved with high resolution, demonstrating translational potential for human applications. The kit components were packaged for commercial distribution, and regulatory documentation for preclinical use was prepared.
