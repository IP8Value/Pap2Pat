## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of high-dimensional flow cytometry and mass cytometry data analysis, specifically to automated methods for the alignment, matching, and comparative analysis of cell populations across multiple biological samples. The invention provides a computationally efficient, statistically robust, and biologically meaningful framework for identifying and matching phenotypically distinct cell subsets in complex, multidimensional cytometric datasets, even when such subsets vary significantly in their spatial distribution, relative abundance, or marker expression levels between samples. The invention is particularly useful in clinical and translational research settings where comparisons between healthy and diseased states, pre- and post-treatment responses, or inter-individual variability must be quantified with high precision and reproducibility. The method is applicable to both fluorescence-based flow cytometry and mass cytometry platforms, and it is designed to function independently of the clustering algorithm used to initially define cell populations, thereby offering broad compatibility with existing analytical workflows. The invention addresses a critical bottleneck in modern immunophenotyping by enabling accurate, objective, and scalable comparison of cell populations across hundreds or thousands of samples without manual intervention, thereby facilitating large-scale cohort studies, biomarker discovery, and diagnostic algorithm development.

## BACKGROUND

The analysis of high-dimensional cytometry data has become a cornerstone of modern immunology, oncology, and infectious disease research, enabling researchers to dissect the phenotypic complexity of immune cell populations at unprecedented resolution. However, the ability to reliably compare cell populations across multiple samples remains a persistent challenge due to biological variability, technical noise, instrument drift, and differences in sample preparation. Traditional approaches to this problem have relied heavily on manual gating, a process that is subjective, time-consuming, and prone to inter-operator variability, rendering it unsuitable for high-throughput applications or large-scale studies involving hundreds of samples. Although automated clustering algorithms have been developed to reduce human bias, these methods typically operate independently on each sample, producing clusters that are not inherently aligned across samples. Subsequent attempts to match these clusters post hoc have been fraught with limitations, particularly when cell populations exhibit substantial variation in location, size, or marker expression between samples.

Existing computational methods for cluster matching can be broadly categorized into two classes. The first class involves clustering each sample individually and then attempting to match clusters across samples using distance metrics such as Euclidean distance, Mahalanobis distance, or Kullback-Leibler divergence. These methods, exemplified by tools such as FLAME and flowMatch, are computationally efficient but fail when populations are displaced, split, or absent across samples. For instance, if a rare cell population is detected in one sample but not in another due to biological variation or sampling error, these methods often misassign clusters or produce false matches based solely on proximity in low-dimensional projections, ignoring the multidimensional nature of the data. The second class of methods employs joint clustering approaches, such as Joint Clustering and Matching, FlowSOM, ASPIRE, and PhenoGraph, which pool data from multiple samples and simultaneously cluster them under a unified model. While these methods are theoretically more robust, they suffer from severe computational complexity, especially as the number of dimensions increases beyond four or five. This curse of dimensionality leads to exponential growth in parameter space, requiring heuristic tuning of numerous input parameters, which often results in inconsistent or biologically implausible cluster assignments. Moreover, these methods frequently rely on parametric assumptions—such as Gaussian mixture models or graph-based modularity optimization—that are ill-suited to the complex, non-Gaussian, and often multimodal distributions observed in real-world cytometry data.

A further limitation of existing methods is their inability to distinguish biologically meaningful differences from technical artifacts. Many commonly used dissimilarity measures, such as the chi-square statistic, are insensitive to spatial displacement of cell populations and respond only to changes in frequency, thereby failing to detect shifts in phenotype that are critical for biological interpretation. Other metrics, such as the Earth Mover’s Distance, are sensitive to both location and frequency but are prohibitively slow for high-dimensional datasets, making them impractical for routine use in clinical or large-scale research settings. Additionally, most existing tools are not designed to handle the presence of missing populations, such as those observed in genetically modified animal models or immunocompromised patients, and often misinterpret such absences as mismatches or clustering failures. There is a clear and unmet need for a method that combines the sensitivity of the Earth Mover’s Distance with the computational efficiency of simpler metrics, while being robust to variations in population location, frequency, and dimensionality, and capable of explicitly identifying missing or split populations without requiring prior knowledge of expected cell types. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel multivariate extension of the quadratic form distance, which enables accurate, rapid, and biologically interpretable matching of cell populations across complex, high-dimensional cytometry datasets.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method and system for the automated alignment and matching of cell populations across multiple high-dimensional flow cytometry and mass cytometry samples. The method utilizes a multivariate extension of the quadratic form distance to quantify dissimilarity between cell subsets identified in separate samples, enabling robust matching even when populations vary significantly in location, frequency, or dimensionality. The invention operates on pre-clustered data generated by any clustering algorithm, whether manual or automated, and does not require the pooling of samples or the fitting of parametric models, thereby avoiding the computational burden and biological assumptions inherent in existing joint clustering approaches. The method employs adaptive binning on the combined dataset to construct a shared binning framework that preserves the structural relationships between data points across samples, ensuring that spatial relationships are accurately captured regardless of dimensionality. For each pair of clusters—one from each sample—the quadratic form distance is computed using a similarity matrix derived from the Euclidean distances between bin centers, ensuring that the dissimilarity score increases not only with differences in population frequency but also with the spatial separation between populations. This property allows the method to distinguish biologically relevant shifts in phenotype from minor technical variations caused by instrument drift or staining variability.

The invention further includes a hierarchical matching algorithm that identifies matched cluster pairs based on minimal dissimilarity scores, automatically detects cluster splits by evaluating whether merging adjacent clusters within the same sample reduces the overall dissimilarity, and identifies missing populations when no viable match can be found and merging does not improve the score. The algorithm outputs not only the matched cluster pairs but also quantitative metrics describing the relative frequency difference and the multidimensional displacement between matched populations, expressed in units of standard deviation along each marker axis. This enables researchers to not only determine which populations are conserved across samples but also to quantify the extent and direction of phenotypic variation. The method is computationally efficient, scaling linearly with the number of dimensions and requiring only seconds to process datasets containing hundreds of thousands of events on standard desktop hardware. The invention is implemented as a software module compatible with existing cytometry analysis platforms and is freely available for non-commercial use. The method has been validated across multiple biological contexts, including the detection of absent lymphocyte populations in immunodeficient mice, the alignment of rare cell subsets with highly variable marker expression in human patient samples, and the comparative analysis of tissue-specific immune cell distributions between the peritoneal cavity and spleen. The invention enables objective, reproducible, and scalable comparative analysis of cytometry data, transforming the way researchers identify, quantify, and interpret phenotypic differences across complex biological systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Definitions

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms shall have the meanings ascribed below. A “cell population” refers to a group of cells within a biological sample that share a common phenotypic profile as defined by the expression levels of a set of molecular markers detected by flow cytometry or mass cytometry. A “cluster” is a computationally identified subset of cells that exhibit statistically significant similarity in their multidimensional marker expression profile, typically derived through automated clustering algorithms such as density-based merging, t-SNE, PhenoGraph, or FlowSOM, or through manual gating by an operator. A “sample” refers to a single biological specimen, such as peripheral blood, tissue homogenate, or cell line suspension, that has been stained with a panel of fluorescent or metal-tagged antibodies and analyzed by a cytometer to generate a multidimensional dataset of cellular events. A “high-dimensional dataset” denotes a cytometry dataset comprising measurements from five or more distinct markers, each representing a different cellular characteristic, such as surface protein expression, intracellular signaling molecule concentration, or cell granularity. “Adaptive binning” is a data partitioning technique in which the multidimensional measurement space is recursively divided into hyper-rectangular bins such that each bin contains an equal number of cellular events, with the axis of division selected based on the dimension exhibiting the highest variance at each step. A “dissimilarity score” is a numerical value quantifying the degree of difference between two cell populations, calculated using a mathematical function that accounts for both the frequency distribution and spatial separation of the populations in multidimensional space. The “quadratic form distance” is a specific dissimilarity metric defined as the quadratic form of the vector difference between two normalized frequency histograms, weighted by a symmetric positive semi-definite matrix whose elements reflect the spatial distance between corresponding bins. A “matched pair” refers to two clusters, one from each of two samples, that have been identified by the algorithm as corresponding to the same biological cell population based on minimal dissimilarity score. A “merging candidate” is a cluster within a single sample that is considered for combination with another cluster in the same sample to evaluate whether the original cluster may have been split during the clustering process. A “missing population” is a cell population present in one sample but absent in another, as determined by the algorithm when no cluster in the second sample can be matched to it and no merging of clusters in the second sample reduces the dissimilarity score. “Geometric mean” refers to the central tendency of a cell population in multidimensional space, calculated as the exponential of the mean of the log-transformed marker intensities for each dimension, providing a robust estimate of the population center that is less sensitive to outliers than the arithmetic mean. “Standard deviation units” (σ) denote the distance between the geometric means of two matched populations, normalized by the standard deviation of the reference population along each marker axis, enabling cross-dimensional comparison of phenotypic displacement. “Biologically meaningful difference” refers to a variation in cell population characteristics that reflects underlying biological phenomena, such as disease state, treatment response, or developmental stage, as opposed to technical artifacts arising from instrument calibration, staining variability, or sample handling. “Computational efficiency” in this context refers to the ability of the method to process large datasets (in excess of 100,000 events per sample) in under one minute on standard computing hardware, without requiring specialized hardware or parallelized computing infrastructure. “Non-parametric” describes a statistical method that does not assume a specific mathematical form for the underlying distribution of the data, such as Gaussian or multinomial, and instead relies on empirical distributions derived directly from the observed data points. “High-throughput analysis” refers to the ability of the method to be applied to hundreds or thousands of samples in an automated, unattended manner without manual intervention or parameter tuning for each sample.

### Example 1—Workflow for Automated Clustering and Alignment of Cell Populations in Flow Cytometry Data

The workflow for automated clustering and alignment of cell populations in flow cytometry data begins with the acquisition of high-dimensional cytometry data from two or more biological samples, each of which has been stained with a consistent panel of fluorescent or metal-tagged antibodies targeting a defined set of cellular markers. The raw data, comprising thousands to millions of cellular events, are first preprocessed using standard cytometry analysis tools to correct for spectral overlap through compensation, apply a nonlinear transformation such as the Logicle transformation to stabilize variance across dynamic ranges, and remove debris and doublet events using forward and side scatter parameters. Following preprocessing, each sample is independently clustered using a density-based clustering algorithm such as two-dimensional density-based merging, which identifies cell populations based on local density maxima without requiring prior specification of the number of clusters. The output of this step is a set of discrete clusters for each sample, each defined by a centroid in multidimensional marker space and a set of associated events. These clusters serve as the input to the alignment algorithm of the present invention. The algorithm first merges the datasets from all samples involved in the comparison to form a single combined dataset, which is then subjected to adaptive binning. This process recursively partitions the multidimensional space into hyper-rectangular bins such that each bin contains an equal number of events, with the axis of division at each step chosen as the dimension exhibiting the greatest variance within the current subset of data. This ensures that bins are larger in regions of low cell density and smaller in regions of high density, preserving the structural integrity of the data while avoiding the curse of dimensionality. Once the binning scheme is established, it is applied uniformly to each individual sample, resulting in a set of frequency histograms for each cluster, where each histogram represents the relative distribution of events across the shared binning grid. For every possible pair of clusters—one from the first sample and one from the second sample—a dissimilarity score is computed using the quadratic form distance. This involves constructing a symmetric similarity matrix whose elements are derived from the Euclidean distances between the centroids of the bins, normalized such that the maximum distance between any two bins is scaled to unity. The dissimilarity score is then calculated as the quadratic form of the difference vector between the two histograms, weighted by this similarity matrix. The resulting score reflects not only the magnitude of frequency differences between the clusters but also the spatial separation of their distributions in multidimensional space. Cluster pairs with the lowest dissimilarity scores are identified as potential matches. The algorithm then evaluates whether any clusters within the same sample may represent a split version of a single population by iteratively merging adjacent clusters and recalculating the dissimilarity score against their matched counterpart in the other sample. A significant reduction in the dissimilarity score following merging indicates that the original cluster was likely split during the clustering process, and the merged cluster is then designated as the true matched population. Conversely, if no cluster in the second sample yields a low dissimilarity score with a given cluster in the first sample, and no merging of clusters in the second sample improves the score, the cluster is flagged as a missing population. The algorithm outputs a comprehensive report detailing all matched pairs, including the relative frequency difference, the multidimensional displacement expressed in standard deviation units, and the identification of any split or missing populations. This entire process can be executed in under ten seconds for datasets containing 200,000 events per sample on standard desktop computing hardware, enabling rapid, high-throughput analysis of large cohorts. The method does not require any manual intervention, parameter tuning, or prior knowledge of expected cell populations, making it suitable for unbiased discovery of phenotypic differences across complex biological systems.

### Example 2—Matching of Basophil Populations Between Patient Samples, Even when Marker Expression Levels Vary Between Patients

The present invention enables the accurate alignment of basophil populations across patient samples despite substantial inter-individual variation in marker expression levels and population frequency. In a dataset derived from human peripheral blood samples, basophils were identified using a standard gating strategy involving sequential exclusion of debris and doublets, followed by selection of CD41a-negative live cells and subsequent identification of CD123-positive, dump-negative cells. However, the mean fluorescence intensity of CD123 varied by more than six-fold between patients, ranging from approximately 1,000 to over 6,500, and the relative frequency of basophils varied by nearly an order of magnitude, from 0.1% to 0.8% of total leukocytes. Traditional clustering methods, including PhenoGraph and FlowSOM, failed to consistently identify a single basophil population across all samples, instead generating multiple, fragmented clusters that did not correspond to the known biological identity of basophils. Manual gating, while accurate, was inconsistent between operators and could not be scaled to analyze hundreds of samples. The method of the present invention, however, successfully aligned basophil populations across all samples by first clustering each sample using a density-based merging algorithm, then applying adaptive binning to the combined dataset to establish a shared reference frame. The quadratic form distance was computed between every pair of clusters from different samples, and the algorithm identified a single cluster in each sample as the basophil population based on the lowest dissimilarity score relative to a reference sample. The spatial displacement between matched basophil populations was quantified in standard deviation units along each marker axis, revealing that while the CD123 expression levels differed substantially, the overall phenotypic profile—including expression of CD203c, CD193, and FcεRI—remained consistent across patients. The algorithm further confirmed that the variation in CD123 expression was not indicative of a different cell type, as no other cluster in any sample exhibited a lower dissimilarity score with the reference basophil cluster than the one identified as the matched population. In cases where the basophil population was extremely rare, the algorithm still successfully identified it by leveraging the adaptive binning scheme, which concentrated bins in regions of high cell density, thereby preserving resolution even for low-abundance populations. The method also correctly identified samples in which basophils were absent, flagging them as missing populations without requiring prior knowledge of the patient’s clinical status. This capability is of critical importance in clinical studies of allergic diseases, where the presence or absence of basophils may correlate with disease severity or response to therapy. The invention thus provides a reliable, automated means of comparing basophil phenotypes across diverse patient populations, overcoming the limitations of traditional methods that rely on fixed thresholds or manual gating, and enabling the discovery of subtle phenotypic shifts that may be missed by conventional approaches.

### Example 3—Detection of Missing Lymphocyte Populations in the Peritoneal Cavity of RAG Knockout (RAG−/−) Mice

The present invention enables the unambiguous detection of completely absent cell populations in biological samples, a capability that is essential for the analysis of genetically modified animal models and immunodeficient patients. In a study comparing peritoneal cavity cells from wild-type BALB/c mice and RAG−/− knockout mice, the goal was to confirm the absence of T and B lymphocytes in the knockout strain, as predicted by the genetic disruption of the recombination-activating genes. Conventional clustering methods, including PhenoGraph and FlowSOM, failed to reliably distinguish between the absence of lymphocytes and the presence of non-specific background events or debris, often assigning residual CD5- and CD19-negative cells to spurious clusters that mimicked lymphocyte populations. Manual gating, while accurate, was subjective and dependent on operator experience. The method of the present invention was applied to the datasets from both strains, with each sample independently clustered using a density-based merging algorithm based on CD5 and CD19 expression. The adaptive binning procedure was performed on the combined dataset, establishing a shared binning framework that captured the full range of expression patterns observed in the wild-type sample. The quadratic form distance was computed between every cluster in the RAG−/− sample and every cluster in the BALB/c sample. The algorithm identified several matched pairs corresponding to non-lymphoid populations such as macrophages, dendritic cells, and granulocytes, which were present in both strains. However, for the two clusters in the wild-type sample corresponding to CD5hiCD19− T cells and CD19hiCD5lo B cells, no cluster in the RAG−/− sample produced a dissimilarity score below the threshold for matching. Furthermore, attempts to merge clusters within the RAG−/− sample to reconstruct a putative lymphocyte population resulted in no reduction in dissimilarity score, confirming that no equivalent population existed in the knockout sample. The algorithm therefore flagged these two populations as definitively missing, with a confidence metric derived from the magnitude of the residual dissimilarity score and the absence of any merging improvement. This result was consistent with the known biology of RAG−/− mice, which lack functional T and B cells due to the failure of V(D)J recombination. The invention thus provides a rigorous, automated, and objective method for confirming the absence of expected cell populations, eliminating the ambiguity inherent in manual gating or parametric modeling approaches. This capability is of profound importance in immunology research, where the validation of knockout models, the assessment of immunotherapies that deplete specific cell types, or the diagnosis of primary immunodeficiencies requires unambiguous evidence of population absence. The method does not rely on predefined thresholds, population size assumptions, or statistical significance testing, but instead uses the intrinsic structure of the data to determine absence, making it a powerful tool for discovery and validation in both basic and clinical research.

### Example 4—Cluster Matching of the Murine Lymphoid, Myeloid and Granuloid Lineages Between PerC and Spleen

The present invention enables the precise comparative analysis of immune cell distributions across anatomically distinct tissues, revealing both conserved and tissue-specific populations with high fidelity. In a study comparing the peritoneal cavity and spleen of wild-type mice, the goal was to identify which immune cell subsets were shared between these two compartments and which were uniquely enriched or absent in one or the other. Both tissues were processed using identical staining protocols and analyzed on the same cytometer, with data collected for twelve parameters including CD11b, SSC-A, CD5, CD19, NK1.1, Gr-1, F4/80, CD11c, CD4, CD8, CD45, and CD3. The datasets were independently clustered using a density-based merging algorithm, resulting in over thirty distinct clusters per sample. The adaptive binning procedure was applied to the combined dataset, creating a shared binning framework that captured the full phenotypic diversity of both tissues. The quadratic form distance was computed between every cluster in the peritoneal cavity and every cluster in the spleen, yielding a dissimilarity matrix of over nine hundred pairwise comparisons. The algorithm identified a core set of matched populations shared between the two tissues, including dendritic cells, natural killer cells, eosinophils, and monocytes, each with dissimilarity scores below a predefined threshold. For each matched pair, the algorithm quantified the multidimensional displacement in standard deviation units, revealing that while the overall phenotype was conserved, splenic eosinophils exhibited significantly higher expression of Gr-1 than their peritoneal counterparts, consistent with known tissue-specific activation states. The algorithm also identified populations that were uniquely present in one tissue but absent in the other: large and small peritoneal macrophages were detected only in the peritoneal cavity, while neutrophils and certain subsets of monocytes were enriched in the spleen and absent from the peritoneum. Importantly, the method correctly identified these as missing populations, not as mismatches or clustering artifacts, because no combination of clusters in the opposing tissue could reproduce the dissimilarity profile of the unique populations. The invention thus provides a comprehensive, automated map of immune cell distribution between tissues, enabling researchers to move beyond qualitative descriptions of “more” or “less” of a cell type and instead quantify the precise phenotypic conservation and divergence of cell populations across anatomical sites. This capability is of critical importance in understanding tissue-specific immune responses, the trafficking of immune cells during inflammation, and the development of targeted therapies that act in specific anatomical niches. The method does not require prior knowledge of expected cell types, making it suitable for unbiased discovery of novel tissue-specific populations, and its computational efficiency allows for the analysis of multiple tissue pairs in a single workflow, facilitating large-scale comparative immunology studies.

### Example 5—Guided Gating of SSc Eosinophil Data

The present invention enables the automated identification and comparative analysis of rare cell populations in complex datasets, even when those populations are obscured by high background noise or overlapping marker expression. In a study of systemic sclerosis (SSc) patients, the goal was to characterize eosinophil populations in peripheral blood, a cell type known to be associated with disease severity but difficult to isolate due to low frequency and phenotypic overlap with other granulocytes. Eosinophils were initially identified using a standard gating strategy based on CD45, SSC-A, and CD123, but the population was small, heterogeneous, and partially obscured by basophils and neutrophils. Manual gating was inconsistent across operators, and automated clustering algorithms such as PhenoGraph produced multiple fragmented clusters, none of which could be confidently assigned as eosinophils. The method of the present invention was applied to the dataset by first clustering all events using a density-based merging algorithm, then performing adaptive binning on the combined dataset to establish a shared reference frame. The quadratic form distance was computed between every cluster and a reference eosinophil population defined in a healthy control sample. The algorithm identified a single cluster in each SSc sample with the lowest dissimilarity score to the reference, confirming the presence of a phenotypically similar eosinophil population. To further validate this match, the algorithm employed a guided gating procedure in which the most informative marker dimensions for distinguishing eosinophils from other granulocytes were identified by sequentially projecting the matched populations onto all possible pairs of markers and computing the dissimilarity score for each projection. The method revealed that Gr-1, CD123, and CD11b were the most discriminatory dimensions, with Gr-1 expression being the most significant differentiator between eosinophils and basophils. Using this information, the algorithm generated a custom gating template that could be applied to future samples, effectively transforming the method into a guided gating tool that combines the objectivity of automated matching with the interpretability of manual gating. The resulting template was validated on an independent cohort of SSc patients and healthy controls, demonstrating high reproducibility and accuracy in identifying eosinophils even in samples with extreme heterogeneity. The invention thus provides a novel bridge between automated analysis and manual gating, enabling researchers to leverage the power of high-dimensional data to define robust, data-driven gating strategies that can be applied consistently across laboratories and instruments. This capability is of immense value in clinical diagnostics, where standardized, reproducible identification of rare cell populations is essential for biomarker development and patient stratification. The method eliminates the subjectivity and variability inherent in manual gating while preserving the biological interpretability that is often lost in purely algorithmic approaches, thereby establishing a new paradigm for the analysis of rare cell populations in complex disease contexts.
