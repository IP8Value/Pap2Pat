## BACKGROUND

The structural and functional integrity of chromatin is essential for the regulation of gene expression, DNA replication, repair, and recombination. The fundamental repeating unit of chromatin, the nucleosome, consists of approximately 147 base pairs of DNA wrapped around an octamer of histone proteins composed of two copies each of histones H2A, H2B, H3, and H4. This highly conserved architecture enables the compaction of the genome while permitting dynamic access to genetic information through post-translational modifications, histone variant incorporation, and ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling. Historically, histones were regarded as inert structural scaffolds, but recent advances in cancer genomics have revealed that somatic mutations in core histones can directly contribute to oncogenesis by altering chromatin structure and function. These mutations, termed oncohistones, are found in a variety of aggressive and treatment-resistant cancers, particularly in pediatric gliomas, chondroblastomas, and certain lymphoid malignancies. Despite their low frequency within the total histone pool—often comprising less than five percent of the total histone content—oncohistones exert a profound influence on the epigenetic landscape by disrupting nucleosome stability, impairing higher-order chromatin folding, and altering interactions with chromatin-associated factors.

Among the most extensively studied oncohistones are mutations within the histone fold domain (HFD) of histone H2A, a region critical for both intra-nucleosomal contacts and inter-nucleosomal interactions. Specific substitutions, such as H2A-R29Q and H2A-E121Q, have been repeatedly identified in large-scale cancer sequencing projects, including The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). These residues are evolutionarily conserved across vertebrates and participate in direct hydrogen bonding with DNA phosphate groups or in salt bridges with adjacent histones. Their mutation leads to a measurable reduction in nucleosome stability in biochemical assays and correlates with increased chromatin accessibility in vivo. Importantly, oncohistones rarely initiate tumorigenesis independently; instead, they function as potent collaborators with known oncogenic drivers such as PI3KCA, MYC, or RAS, lowering the threshold for malignant transformation by creating a permissive chromatin environment that facilitates aberrant transcriptional programs. This paradigm has shifted the understanding of cancer from a purely genetic disease to one in which epigenetic perturbations serve as critical co-factors in oncogenesis.

In parallel with the discovery of oncohistones, the biological roles of histone variants have come into increasing focus. Histone variants are non-allelic isoforms of canonical histones that are incorporated into chromatin in a replication-independent manner and often confer specialized functional properties to nucleosomes. Among these, the short histone H2A (sH2A) variants represent a unique class expressed predominantly in the testis during spermatogenesis. These variants, including H2A.B, H2A.P, and H2A.Q, are encoded by a cluster of genes on the X chromosome and exhibit rapid evolutionary divergence compared to canonical H2A. Unlike canonical H2A, sH2A variants possess a truncated C-terminal tail, a divergent histone fold domain, and a disrupted acidic patch—a surface critical for nucleosome-nucleosome interactions and recruitment of chromatin regulators. These structural features result in nucleosomes that wrap only approximately 120 base pairs of DNA, exhibit increased DNA flexibility, and display reduced stability under physiological salt conditions. These properties are thought to facilitate the dramatic chromatin remodeling required during spermatogenesis, including histone-to-protamine exchange and the activation of transcriptionally silent regions in meiotic cells.

Despite their restricted expression in normal physiology, the structural similarities between sH2A variants and canonical H2A oncohistones have remained unexplored. The amino acid substitutions found in cancer-associated H2A mutants—including R29Q, E121Q, and other residues involved in DNA or histone-histone contacts—are not mutations at all in sH2A variants; rather, they are the wild-type residues in these testis-specific proteins. This observation suggests that the evolutionary trajectory of sH2A variants has naturally selected for a chromatin architecture that mimics the destabilizing effects of oncohistone mutations. Furthermore, the conservation of these features across primates, despite the rapid evolution of the sH2A locus, implies that these structural characteristics are not neutral but confer a selective advantage in the context of germ cell development. The question arises whether this naturally evolved chromatin destabilization, beneficial in the testis, becomes pathogenic when reactivated in somatic tissues. Prior studies have detected low levels of H2A.B transcripts in Hodgkin’s lymphoma, but the broader prevalence, mechanistic implications, and functional consequences of sH2A expression in cancer have not been systematically investigated.

The prevailing paradigm in cancer biology has largely overlooked the possibility that the reactivation of developmentally restricted histone variants could serve as a driver of oncogenesis. While epigenetic reprogramming is recognized as a hallmark of cancer, most research has focused on the dysregulation of DNA methylation, histone modification enzymes, or chromatin remodelers. The notion that the expression of an entire histone variant—whose very sequence encodes nucleosome destabilization—could be sufficient to alter the epigenetic state of a somatic cell and promote malignancy has not been considered. Moreover, the potential for sH2A expression to influence RNA processing, particularly alternative splicing, remains unexplored. Given that H2A.B has been shown to interact directly with splicing factors and modulate transcript isoform usage in germ cells, its re-expression in cancer could provide a direct mechanistic link between chromatin architecture and transcriptomic dysregulation, two pillars of cancer biology that have thus far been studied in isolation.

The absence of a comprehensive analysis of sH2A expression across cancer types, the lack of mechanistic insight into how sH2A expression alters transcriptional and splicing programs, and the failure to recognize the inherent oncohistone nature of these variants collectively represent a significant gap in the understanding of epigenetic drivers of cancer. This invention addresses these gaps by demonstrating that sH2A variants, particularly H2A.B, are not merely bystanders but active contributors to oncogenesis through their intrinsic ability to destabilize nucleosomes and rewire gene expression. The invention further reveals that the expression of these variants is not confined to rare malignancies but occurs across a broad spectrum of common cancers, including diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, endometrial carcinoma, bladder cancer, and acute lymphoblastic leukemia. The discovery that sH2A expression correlates with distinct splicing signatures and the upregulation of cancer-testis antigens provides a novel framework for understanding how chromatin instability translates into functional oncogenic phenotypes. This invention therefore represents a paradigm shift: rather than viewing oncohistones as rare somatic mutations, this work reveals that the human genome inherently encodes a set of histone variants that, when aberrantly expressed, recapitulate the molecular consequences of oncohistone mutations without requiring any genetic alteration. This insight opens new avenues for cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and therapy by identifying sH2A expression as a biomarker and therapeutic target in hundreds of thousands of cancer patients worldwide.

## SUMMARY

The invention provides a novel and non-obvious discovery that certain histone variants, specifically the short histone H2A (sH2A) family, inherently possess structural and functional properties that mimic those of somatic oncohistone mutations found in human cancers. These variants, which are normally restricted to expression in the testis during spermatogenesis, are aberrantly reactivated in a wide array of malignant tumors, where they contribute to oncogenesis by destabilizing nucleosomes, altering chromatin architecture, and inducing widespread changes in gene expression and RNA splicing. The invention encompasses the identification of sH2A variants—including H2A.B.1.1 (H2AFB2), H2A.B.1.2 (H2AFB3), and H2A.B.2 (H2AFB1)—as endogenous oncohistone mimics whose wild-type sequences contain amino acid substitutions that correspond precisely to recurrent cancer-associated mutations in canonical H2A, such as R29Q and E121Q. These substitutions, which are evolutionarily conserved across primates, result in nucleosomes with reduced stability, decreased DNA wrapping, and altered interactions with chromatin regulators, thereby creating a permissive chromatin environment conducive to oncogenic transcriptional programs.

The invention further provides evidence that sH2A expression is not an isolated phenomenon but occurs with high frequency across multiple cancer types, including diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma, urothelial bladder carcinoma, cervical squamous cell carcinoma, and B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia. In these cancers, sH2A expression is not associated with broad chromosomal amplifications, X-chromosome reactivation, or global epigenetic derepression, but instead arises through localized, paralog-specific transcriptional activation, suggesting the existence of cancer-specific regulatory mechanisms that reactivate these normally silenced genes. The expression of sH2A variants is correlated with distinct gene expression signatures, including the upregulation of cancer-testis antigens, which are typically restricted to germ cells and are known to elicit immune responses. This association suggests that sH2A expression may contribute to immune evasion or immune recognition depending on the tumor microenvironment, thereby offering a dual role in tumor biology.

Importantly, the invention demonstrates that sH2A expression is strongly associated with aberrant RNA splicing patterns, including reduced usage of alternative cassette exons, decreased proximal polyadenylation site selection, and diminished intron retention. These splicing alterations are consistent with the known ability of H2A.B to interact with splicing factors and accelerate RNA polymerase II elongation, leading to inefficient recognition of weak splice signals. The splicing changes are observed across multiple cancer types and are not dependent on the specific sH2A paralog expressed, indicating a conserved functional consequence of nucleosome destabilization. The invention further reveals that the splicing dysregulation induced by sH2A expression is distinct from that caused by mutations in canonical splicing factors, suggesting a novel mechanism of transcriptomic instability in cancer that is driven by chromatin architecture rather than direct RNA-binding protein dysfunction.

The invention provides a method for diagnosing cancer by detecting the presence of sH2A transcripts or proteins in a biological sample obtained from a patient, wherein the detection of sH2A expression above a defined threshold is indicative of malignancy. The invention further provides a method for prognosticating cancer outcomes based on the level of sH2A expression, with higher expression correlating with specific molecular subtypes and clinical behaviors. The invention also provides a method for identifying therapeutic targets by selecting cancer patients whose tumors express sH2A variants for treatment with agents that inhibit sH2A incorporation into chromatin, disrupt sH2A interactions with splicing factors, or target sH2A-derived peptides presented on human leukocyte antigen molecules. The invention further encompasses the use of sH2A expression as a biomarker for patient stratification in clinical trials of epigenetic therapies, immunotherapies, or splicing-modulating agents.

The invention further provides a composition of matter comprising a nucleic acid molecule encoding an sH2A variant, a polypeptide comprising an sH2A variant, or a fragment thereof, for use in the manufacture of a medicament for the treatment of cancer. The invention further provides a method of treating cancer by administering to a patient a therapeutically effective amount of an agent that selectively inhibits the expression, stability, or chromatin incorporation of an sH2A variant. The invention further provides a diagnostic kit comprising reagents for detecting sH2A transcripts or proteins in a biological sample, wherein the kit includes primers, probes, antibodies, or hybridization buffers optimized for the specific detection of H2AFB1, H2AFB2, or H2AFB3 mRNA or protein. The invention further provides a method of screening for compounds that modulate sH2A expression or function by assaying the effect of test compounds on nucleosome stability, splicing patterns, or gene expression profiles in cells expressing sH2A variants.

The invention thus represents a fundamental advance in the understanding of cancer biology by identifying a new class of endogenous oncohistone mimics whose reactivation drives oncogenesis through chromatin destabilization and transcriptomic rewiring. The invention provides novel diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic strategies based on the detection and targeting of sH2A expression, thereby opening new avenues for precision oncology in a broad spectrum of malignancies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention is directed to the discovery that short histone H2A (sH2A) variants, which are normally expressed exclusively in the testis during spermatogenesis, possess intrinsic structural features that recapitulate the molecular consequences of somatic oncohistone mutations found in human cancers. These variants, including H2A.B.1.1 (H2AFB2), H2A.B.1.2 (H2AFB3), and H2A.B.2 (H2AFB1), are encoded by a cluster of genes located on the X chromosome and are characterized by a highly divergent histone fold domain, a truncated C-terminal tail, and a disrupted acidic patch—all of which are critical for nucleosome stability and higher-order chromatin organization. Unlike canonical histone H2A, which is uniformly expressed in somatic cells and maintains a stable nucleosomal structure, sH2A variants have evolved to form unstable nucleosomes that wrap only approximately 120 base pairs of DNA, exhibit increased DNA flexibility, and demonstrate reduced resistance to salt-induced disassembly. These properties, which are essential for the chromatin remodeling events that occur during spermatogenesis, are identical to those induced by somatic mutations in canonical H2A that are recurrently observed in a variety of human cancers. The invention demonstrates that these sH2A variants are not merely homologous to oncohistones but are, in fact, natural oncohistone mimics whose expression in somatic tissues is sufficient to drive oncogenic phenotypes.

The invention provides a comprehensive analysis of sH2A expression across multiple cancer types using transcriptomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE), and independent cohorts of B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL). The data reveal that sH2A transcripts are detectable in a substantial proportion of tumors across diverse histological subtypes, including diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC), urothelial bladder carcinoma (BLCA), cervical squamous cell carcinoma, and B-ALL. In each of these cancers, sH2A expression is absent or below detectable levels in adjacent normal tissues and is rarely observed in non-testicular somatic tissues from healthy individuals, as confirmed by analysis of the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) database. The expression levels in tumors vary widely, with some specimens exhibiting transcript levels exceeding 100 transcripts per million (TPM), whereas others show more modest but consistently elevated expression above a threshold of 1.5 TPM, which was established as the cutoff for defining sH2A positivity. Importantly, the expression of sH2A paralogs is not uniform across tumors; while H2AFB1 is frequently expressed alone, H2AFB2 and H2AFB3 are often co-expressed, likely due to their genomic proximity and shared regulatory elements. This paralog-specific expression pattern suggests that distinct transcriptional mechanisms govern the reactivation of each sH2A gene in different cancer contexts, and that these mechanisms are not driven by global chromosomal amplifications or X-chromosome reactivation, as no significant differences in X-chromosome-to-autosome transcript ratios were observed between sH2A-positive and sH2A-negative tumors.

The invention further demonstrates that the amino acid sequences of sH2A variants contain residues that correspond precisely to the most common cancer-associated mutations in canonical H2A. For example, the arginine residue at position 29 in canonical H2A, which is frequently mutated to glutamine (R29Q) in cancers, is naturally replaced by glutamine in all wild-type sH2A variants. Similarly, the glutamic acid residue at position 121, which is commonly mutated to glutamine (E121Q) in cancer, is absent in sH2A variants due to a C-terminal truncation that removes this residue entirely. These substitutions are not random mutations but are evolutionarily conserved across primates, including humans, chimpanzees, gorillas, orangutans, and macaques, indicating strong selective pressure to maintain these destabilizing features. Phylogenetic analysis confirms that these oncohistone-like features emerged early in primate evolution and have been preserved for tens of millions of years, despite the rapid divergence of the sH2A locus. This conservation strongly implies that these structural features are functionally critical for the role of sH2A in germ cell biology, yet when expressed in somatic tissues, they confer oncogenic properties by destabilizing nucleosomes and altering chromatin accessibility.

The invention further provides evidence that sH2A expression is associated with a distinct gene expression signature in cancer. Transcriptomic profiling of sH2A-positive tumors reveals a consistent set of differentially expressed genes across multiple cancer types, including the upregulation of 146 genes and the downregulation of 90 genes that are commonly altered in sH2A-expressing tumors. Among the upregulated genes, twelve are classified as cancer-testis antigens (CTAs), a class of proteins normally restricted to germ cells but aberrantly expressed in many cancers and known to be targets of endogenous immune responses. The expression of CTAs is quantified using a composite CTA score derived from the sum of Z-normalized log-transformed expression values of the top 40 most variably expressed CTAs within each cancer type. This score is significantly elevated in sH2A-positive tumors of certain types, including head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, lung squamous cell carcinoma, and endometrial carcinoma, but not in others, such as DLBCL or sarcoma, suggesting that the relationship between sH2A expression and CTA upregulation is context-dependent. The lack of consistent enrichment of immune cell markers in sH2A-positive tumors, despite the presence of CTAs, indicates that sH2A-driven CTA expression may occur in an immunologically silent or suppressed microenvironment, potentially contributing to immune evasion.

The invention further establishes a direct link between sH2A expression and widespread alterations in RNA splicing. Analysis of alternative splicing events in sH2A-positive versus sH2A-negative tumors reveals a consistent pattern of reduced usage of alternative cassette exons, decreased selection of proximal polyadenylation sites, and diminished intron retention. These changes are observed across multiple cancer types and are not attributable to mutations in canonical splicing factors, as many of the tumors analyzed, particularly B-ALL, lack such mutations. The splicing alterations are consistent with the known ability of H2A.B to interact with RNA and splicing factors, and to accelerate RNA polymerase II elongation, which reduces the efficiency of splice site recognition, particularly for weakly defined alternative exons and polyadenylation signals. The magnitude of these changes is modest at individual loci but highly pervasive, affecting thousands of splicing events across the transcriptome. This suggests that sH2A expression induces a global, low-amplitude but high-impact rewiring of RNA processing that may collectively enhance the expression of oncogenes by preventing the inclusion of alternative exons that trigger nonsense-mediated decay or by promoting the use of proximal polyadenylation sites that generate more stable mRNA isoforms.

The invention further demonstrates that sH2A expression is not associated with the upregulation of other histone variants, such as H2A.Z, H3.3, or H2A.X, nor with increased expression of the histone chaperone NAP1L1, which is known to facilitate the incorporation of H2A.B into chromatin. While NAP1L1 is expressed at high levels in some sH2A-positive tumors, such as DLBCL, its expression does not consistently correlate with sH2A levels across all cancer types, suggesting that alternative chaperones or mechanisms may be responsible for sH2A incorporation in different contexts. The absence of co-upregulation of other testis-specific histones, such as TH2A or TH2B, further supports the specificity of sH2A reactivation and argues against a general reversion to a germ cell-like transcriptional program. Instead, the data indicate that sH2A expression is a targeted, paralog-specific event that selectively alters chromatin and transcriptome dynamics without broadly reprogramming the cell’s epigenetic state.

The invention further provides evidence that sH2A-derived peptides are capable of binding to human leukocyte antigen (HLA) molecules, as predicted by computational algorithms that assess peptide-HLA binding affinity. A total of 12 distinct peptides derived from the amino acid sequences of H2A.B.1.1, H2A.B.1.2, and H2A.B.2 were predicted to bind with high affinity to common HLA class I alleles, including HLA-A*0201, HLA-B*0702, and HLA-B*4402. These peptides are derived from regions of the sH2A proteins that are unique to the variants and are not present in canonical H2A, making them potential neoantigens that could be recognized by cytotoxic T cells. The presence of these peptides suggests that sH2A-expressing tumors may be susceptible to immunotherapy targeting these epitopes, either through cancer vaccines, adoptive T-cell transfer, or immune checkpoint blockade in combination with sH2A-targeted therapies.

The detection of sH2A transcripts above a threshold of 1.5 transcripts per million (TPM) or the presence of sH2A protein at levels above the background detected in normal somatic tissues is indicative of malignancy.
