# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The detection and diagnosis of ovarian cancer remain among the most challenging tasks in clinical oncology, primarily due to the absence of reliable, sensitive, and specific biomarkers capable of identifying the disease in its earliest, most treatable stages. While ovarian cancer is the leading cause of death among gynecological malignancies, survival rates plummet dramatically when diagnosis occurs at advanced stages—typically stages III or IV—where the cancer has already metastasized beyond the pelvis. In contrast, when detected at stage I, the five-year survival rate exceeds 90%, underscoring the critical importance of early intervention. Unfortunately, the current standard biomarker for ovarian cancer, CA-125, exhibits significant limitations in early detection, with sensitivity as low as 50% in stage I disease, rendering it inadequate for population-wide screening purposes. Its elevated levels are also observed in numerous benign conditions, including endometriosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, and even during menstruation, resulting in a high rate of false positives and diminished specificity. Consequently, despite decades of clinical use, CA-125 remains primarily a tool for monitoring treatment response rather than for initial diagnosis, leaving a substantial unmet medical need for a more accurate, non-invasive diagnostic method.

The biological complexity of ovarian cancer, characterized by heterogeneous histological subtypes—including serous, clear-cell, mucinous, and endometrioid carcinomas—further complicates the search for a universal biomarker. Each subtype exhibits distinct molecular profiles, genetic alterations, and clinical behaviors, making it unlikely that a single protein or metabolite will suffice to capture the full spectrum of disease pathology. This heterogeneity has driven the scientific community toward the development of multibiomarker panels capable of integrating diverse molecular signals to enhance diagnostic precision. Among the most promising classes of molecules for such panels are glycans, complex carbohydrate structures covalently attached to proteins through post-translational modifications. Glycosylation, particularly N-linked glycosylation, is one of the most abundant and functionally significant modifications in human serum proteins, influencing protein folding, stability, solubility, immune recognition, and intercellular communication. Alterations in glycan structures have been consistently documented in a wide array of pathological states, including cancer, autoimmune disorders, and infectious diseases. In ovarian cancer, changes in glycosylation patterns have been observed on key serum proteins such as transferrin, haptoglobin, and immunoglobulins, often manifesting as increased branching, elevated sialylation, or altered fucosylation. These modifications are not merely epiphenomena but are actively driven by dysregulated expression of glycosyltransferases and glycosidases within tumor cells and their microenvironment, reflecting the underlying metabolic and signaling perturbations associated with malignant transformation.

Mass spectrometry has emerged as the most powerful analytical platform for the comprehensive profiling of glycome structures, enabling the simultaneous detection and quantification of hundreds of glycan species in a single experiment without the need for prior labeling or derivatization. Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS), in particular, offers high sensitivity, rapid analysis, and excellent reproducibility, making it ideally suited for clinical translational applications. Recent advances in sample preparation techniques, especially the use of solid-phase extraction (SPE) with graphitized carbon cartridges, have significantly improved the purity and recovery of glycans from complex biological matrices such as human serum. By sequentially eluting glycans with increasing concentrations of acetonitrile in the presence of a weak acid such as trifluoroacetic acid, it is now possible to fractionate the glycome into distinct populations based on hydrophobicity and charge, thereby enriching minor species that would otherwise be masked by dominant glycan signals. This fractionation strategy has proven essential for uncovering low-abundance glycan biomarkers that may hold greater diagnostic value than the more prevalent structures.

Despite these technical advancements, the field of glycomics has been hindered by an overreliance on annotation-based biomarker discovery, wherein only those mass spectral peaks that can be confidently matched to known glycan compositions from public databases are considered for further analysis. This approach inherently excludes a substantial proportion of biologically relevant signals—often those that are most consistently altered between healthy and diseased states—simply because their molecular identities remain unknown or uncharacterized. The assumption that only annotated glycans are biologically meaningful is fundamentally flawed, as many of the most robust diagnostic signals in proteomics and metabolomics have originated from unidentified molecular species whose clinical utility was established prior to their biochemical characterization. The present invention overcomes this limitation by adopting a data-driven, functionally oriented strategy that selects biomarkers based on their discriminatory power, reproducibility, and statistical significance, irrespective of whether their chemical structures have been fully elucidated. This paradigm shift enables the discovery of novel diagnostic signatures that are not constrained by the limitations of current glycan databases, thereby unlocking a previously inaccessible reservoir of clinically actionable information.

Furthermore, the integration of quantitative mass spectrometric data with machine learning-inspired scoring algorithms represents a critical innovation in the translation of glycomic findings into clinical diagnostics. Traditional approaches to multibiomarker analysis often employ simplistic binary classification rules or linear discriminant models that fail to account for the differential diagnostic weight of individual biomarkers. In contrast, the system described herein assigns a weighting factor to each biomarker based on its area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, effectively prioritizing those signals that demonstrate the greatest ability to distinguish between patient and control populations. This weighting mechanism, when combined with a binary scoring system (+1 for positive classification, −1 for negative classification) determined by biomarker-specific cut-off thresholds derived from training data, generates a composite diagnostic score that reflects the collective predictive power of the entire panel. This approach not only enhances classification accuracy but also provides a transparent, mathematically rigorous framework for clinical implementation, allowing for the adjustment of sensitivity and specificity according to the needs of the diagnostic setting. The validation of this system in a blinded, independent cohort of patients further confirms its robustness and generalizability, demonstrating that the biomarker panel performs with superior accuracy to CA-125 across multiple clinical scenarios, including early-stage disease and post-treatment recurrence. The invention therefore represents a paradigm shift in ovarian cancer diagnostics, moving from a reliance on single, poorly performing protein markers to a comprehensive, glycan-based multibiomarker system that leverages the full complexity of the serum glycome to deliver a highly accurate, reproducible, and clinically viable diagnostic tool.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel, highly accurate method for the detection and screening of ovarian cancer through the quantitative analysis of glycan biomarkers in human serum using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). The invention comprises a multibiomarker panel consisting of fifteen distinct mass spectral signals derived from N-linked glycans and other serum components, each exhibiting statistically significant and reproducible differences in abundance between individuals with ovarian cancer and healthy controls. These biomarkers are identified through a rigorous, data-driven analytical pipeline that prioritizes discriminatory power over molecular annotation, enabling the inclusion of both known glycan structures and previously uncharacterized molecular species that demonstrate consistent and robust diagnostic potential. The method further incorporates a weighted scoring algorithm that integrates the intensity profiles of these fifteen biomarkers into a single diagnostic score, with each biomarker contributing proportionally to its classification efficacy as measured by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. This scoring system, calibrated using a training set of serum samples from ovarian cancer patients and healthy individuals, enables the classification of unknown samples with high sensitivity and specificity, outperforming the current clinical standard, CA-125, in both training and independent validation cohorts.

The invention is implemented through a standardized protocol for serum sample preparation, glycan isolation, mass spectrometric analysis, and data interpretation. Serum samples are first subjected to protein denaturation and enzymatic release of N-linked glycans using peptide-N-glycosidase F under microwave-assisted conditions to enhance reaction efficiency and reproducibility. Released glycans are then purified through solid-phase extraction using graphitized carbon cartridges, with sequential elution performed using acetonitrile-water mixtures containing 0.05% trifluoroacetic acid at concentrations of 10% and 20% to isolate distinct glycan fractions. Each fraction is analyzed by MALDI-TOF MS in positive-ion reflectron mode over the mass-to-charge ratio range of 1000 to 3000, with data collected from multiple laser shots across multiple sample spots to ensure statistical robustness. The resulting mass spectra are processed using custom software to extract centroid m/z values and absolute peak intensities, which are then normalized to the total ion abundance of each spectrum to correct for variations in sample loading and instrument response. The normalized intensities are subjected to statistical analysis to identify biomarkers with significant differences in abundance between patient and control groups, as determined by analysis of variance (ANOVA) and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Biomarkers are selected based on stringent criteria: a P value below 10−9 for ANOVA, a detection frequency exceeding 95% across all samples, and an area under the ROC curve greater than 0.72 for the 10% acetonitrile fraction and greater than 0.75 for the 20% acetonitrile fraction. From this selection process, fifteen biomarkers are identified and incorporated into the diagnostic panel.

The diagnostic algorithm assigns a binary score of +1 or −1 to each biomarker based on whether its intensity in a given sample exceeds or falls below a predefined cut-off threshold, determined from the training set using a standard sensitivity of 70%. These binary scores are then multiplied by weighting factors derived from the area under the ROC curve for each respective biomarker, producing a weighted score for each marker. The sum of these fifteen weighted scores generates a composite diagnostic score for each sample. A diagnostic cut-off for the composite score is established in the training set to maximize classification accuracy, and this threshold is applied to classify unknown samples as either consistent with ovarian cancer or consistent with a healthy state. In independent validation studies, this system achieves a sensitivity of 84% and a specificity of 83% in a blinded cohort of 60 samples, significantly outperforming CA-125, which yielded a sensitivity of 78% under the same conditions. The system demonstrates particular efficacy in detecting early-stage ovarian cancer, with high classification accuracy observed in stage I and II cases, and successfully identifies all cases of recurrent ovarian cancer in a small pilot cohort, even when CA-125 levels remain below the conventional diagnostic threshold. Importantly, the biomarker panel retains its diagnostic power regardless of whether the individual signals are annotated as known glycans or remain uncharacterized, affirming the validity of a functionally driven discovery approach that prioritizes clinical utility over biochemical completeness. The invention thus provides a clinically deployable, non-invasive diagnostic tool for ovarian cancer screening that is more accurate, reproducible, and broadly applicable than existing methods, with the potential to transform early detection strategies and improve patient outcomes.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

### Glycan Analysis from Human Serum

The method for analyzing glycans from human serum to detect ovarian cancer begins with the collection and preparation of serum samples under strictly controlled clinical conditions. Serum is obtained from individuals who have provided informed consent in accordance with institutional review board protocols, ensuring ethical compliance and the integrity of sample provenance. Blood is drawn into sterile, non-anticoagulant tubes and allowed to clot at room temperature for thirty minutes prior to centrifugation at 1000 × g for ten minutes to separate the cellular components from the liquid fraction. The resulting serum is carefully aspirated and aliquoted into polypropylene microcentrifuge tubes, each containing approximately one hundred microliters, and immediately stored at −80°C to preserve the integrity of labile glycan structures and prevent enzymatic degradation. Prior to analysis, serum samples are thawed on ice and subjected to a denaturation protocol designed to disrupt protein tertiary structure and expose N-linked glycosylation sites for enzymatic cleavage. Fifty microliters of serum is mixed with an equal volume of ammonium bicarbonate buffer at a concentration of two hundred millimolar, supplemented with ten millimolar dithiothreitol to reduce disulfide bonds. This mixture is subjected to four alternating cycles of fifteen seconds in a boiling water bath followed by fifteen seconds at ambient temperature, resulting in a total exposure time of two minutes to thermal stress. This denaturation step ensures maximal accessibility of glycosylation sites to the enzymatic reagent while minimizing protein aggregation and sample loss.

Following denaturation, peptide-N-glycosidase F is added to the sample at a concentration of one thousand units per reaction, and the enzymatic release of N-linked glycans is performed under microwave-assisted conditions using a specialized reaction enhancement system that delivers four hundred watts of microwave power at a constant temperature of thirty-seven degrees Celsius for a duration of ten minutes. This microwave-mediated enzymatic reaction significantly accelerates the cleavage kinetics compared to conventional incubation methods, ensuring complete and uniform deglycosylation across all samples while reducing variability introduced by prolonged incubation times. After the enzymatic reaction is complete, four hundred microliters of ice-cold ethanol is added to precipitate the remaining proteinaceous material, and the mixture is stored at −80°C for one hour to ensure complete protein aggregation. The sample is then centrifuged at high speed to pellet the precipitated proteins, and the supernatant, which contains the released glycans, is carefully transferred to a fresh tube without disturbing the pellet. The supernatant is dried under vacuum using a centrifugal evaporator to remove residual solvents and prepare the sample for solid-phase extraction.

The purified glycan solution is then subjected to solid-phase extraction using graphitized carbon cartridges, a technique that exploits the hydrophobic interactions between glycan moieties and the carbon surface to selectively retain carbohydrates while allowing salts, lipids, and other non-glycan contaminants to be washed away. Before sample loading, the cartridges are preconditioned by sequential washing with two column volumes of 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid in 80% acetonitrile/water, followed by two column volumes of nanopure water to equilibrate the stationary phase. The glycan solution is then loaded onto the cartridge, and the flow-through is discarded. The cartridge is washed with three column volumes of nanopure water to remove residual salts and polar contaminants, after which glycans are eluted in a stepwise manner using three distinct solvent systems: first with ten percent acetonitrile in water containing 0.05% trifluoroacetic acid, second with twenty percent acetonitrile in water containing 0.05% trifluoroacetic acid, and third with forty percent acetonitrile in water containing 0.05% trifluoroacetic acid. Each elution fraction is collected separately and immediately dried under vacuum to prevent degradation. For the purposes of this invention, only the ten percent and twenty percent acetonitrile fractions are utilized in the biomarker discovery and diagnostic algorithm, as these fractions contain the majority of the structurally informative and diagnostically relevant glycan species. The forty percent fraction, which contains more hydrophobic and potentially highly modified glycans, is reserved for future studies involving chemical derivatization such as permethylation, which may enhance ionization efficiency and structural resolution. Each dried glycan fraction is reconstituted in fifteen microliters of purified water to yield a concentrated solution suitable for direct spotting onto MALDI target plates.

The reconstituted glycan solutions are then analyzed by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry using a 4800 Plus MALDI TOF/TOF analyzer. One microliter of each glycan solution is mixed with one microliter of a matrix solution composed of seventy percent acetonitrile and twelve millimolar sodium chloride, containing 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid as the crystallizing agent, and spotted onto pre-coated MALDI target plates with a spot diameter of 1700 micrometers. The samples are allowed to dry rapidly under vacuum to form a homogeneous crystal matrix, ensuring consistent laser absorption and ionization efficiency. Mass spectra are acquired in positive-ion reflectron mode over the mass-to-charge ratio range of one thousand to three thousand, with each sample analyzed across four distinct target spots to account for spatial heterogeneity in crystal formation. For each spot, twenty-five laser shots are fired at a fixed energy level, and data are accumulated from forty distinct positions per sample, resulting in a total of one thousand laser shots per glycan fraction per sample. This high-replicate acquisition strategy minimizes stochastic variation and ensures that the observed ion intensities reflect true biological differences rather than technical artifacts. The instrument is calibrated using twelve internal calibrants with known glycan masses, achieving a mass accuracy tolerance of ±0.2 mass-to-charge units. Each sample yields four independent spectra per fraction, resulting in a total of eight spectra per serum sample, all of which are retained for downstream data analysis to maximize statistical power and reproducibility.

Data acquisition is performed using proprietary software that captures the full spectral profile, including centroid m/z values, absolute peak intensities, signal-to-noise ratios, and peak widths. The signal-to-noise threshold for peak detection is set at four, and a minimum intensity threshold of ten is applied to eliminate low-abundance noise peaks that lack diagnostic relevance. The resulting data are exported into a tabular format compatible with statistical analysis software, where each row corresponds to a unique m/z value and each column corresponds to a sample, with entries representing the normalized absolute peak intensity. This structured data matrix serves as the foundation for all subsequent biomarker discovery and classification analyses. The entire workflow, from serum collection to spectral acquisition, is conducted under blinded conditions to prevent observer bias, and all reagents, instruments, and protocols are standardized across all samples to ensure analytical consistency. The method is designed for scalability and reproducibility, with the capacity to process hundreds of samples per week using automated liquid handling systems and high-throughput MALDI target plates, making it suitable for implementation in clinical diagnostic laboratories.

### Example 2

The identification of diagnostic biomarkers from the mass spectrometric data is performed through a multi-step analytical pipeline that rigorously filters and prioritizes spectral features based on statistical significance, reproducibility, and discriminatory power. The first step involves the normalization of absolute peak intensities across all samples to account for variations in sample concentration, instrument response, and matrix effects. This is accomplished by calculating the normalized absolute peak intensity (NAPI) for each m/z feature in each sample, defined as the ratio of the absolute peak intensity of the feature to the sum of all absolute peak intensities in the same spectrum, multiplied by one thousand to facilitate numerical stability. This normalization ensures that the relative abundance of each glycan signal is preserved while eliminating global intensity fluctuations that could confound comparative analysis. The resulting NAPI values are then subjected to statistical analysis using analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare the mean intensities between the group of ovarian cancer patients and the group of healthy controls. Peaks with a P value below ten to the power of negative nine are retained as potential biomarkers, as this stringent threshold minimizes the likelihood of false positives arising from random variation in large datasets.

In parallel, the detection frequency of each candidate biomarker is evaluated across the entire cohort to ensure that the signal is consistently present in both patient and control populations. Only those peaks that are detected in at least ninety-five percent of all samples are retained, thereby eliminating features that are sporadic, unstable, or subject to technical artifacts such as ion suppression or matrix interference. This dual filtering criterion—high statistical significance and near-universal detection—ensures that the selected biomarkers are not only differentially abundant but also reliably measurable across diverse clinical samples. The resulting list of candidate biomarkers is then subjected to receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to quantify their ability to discriminate between cancer and non-cancer states. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) is calculated for each biomarker, with values ranging from zero to one, where a value of one indicates perfect discrimination and a value of 0.5 indicates no discriminatory power. Biomarkers from the ten percent acetonitrile fraction are selected if their AUC exceeds 0.72, while those from the twenty percent acetonitrile fraction are selected if their AUC exceeds 0.75. These thresholds were empirically determined during the training phase to balance sensitivity and specificity while avoiding overfitting to the training data.

The correlation between the logarithm of the P value and the AUC is examined to validate the biological relevance of the selected biomarkers. A strong inverse linear relationship is observed, indicating that biomarkers with the highest statistical significance also possess the greatest discriminatory power, thereby reinforcing the robustness of the selection criteria. This correlation confirms that the ANOVA P value is not merely a statistical artifact but a valid predictor of diagnostic utility. From this analysis, fifteen biomarkers are identified and incorporated into the final diagnostic panel. These include five biomarkers from the ten percent acetonitrile fraction and ten from the twenty percent acetonitrile fraction. Of these fifteen, ten are annotated as known N-linked glycan structures using the GlycoMod database, with mass errors within ten parts per million of theoretical values, confirming their identity as glycosylated species. The remaining five biomarkers, while exhibiting the same stringent statistical and reproducibility criteria, cannot be assigned to any known glycan composition in public databases, despite extensive searching under varying assumptions of composition, including combinations of hexose, N-acetylhexosamine, fucose, and sialic acid. Nevertheless, these unannotated biomarkers demonstrate consistent and significant differences in intensity between patient and control groups, are detected in nearly every sample, and exhibit high reproducibility across replicate analyses, confirming their authenticity as biologically relevant serum components.

The inclusion of these unannotated biomarkers represents a critical departure from conventional glycomics approaches, which typically discard unidentified signals as noise or contamination. In this invention, the biological and clinical significance of a biomarker is determined not by its molecular identity but by its performance in distinguishing disease states. The fact that these five uncharacterized signals contribute meaningfully to the overall diagnostic accuracy underscores the limitations of annotation-dependent discovery and highlights the value of a functionally driven approach. Their consistent presence across all sample sets, including the blind validation cohort, further validates their reliability and suggests that they may represent novel glycan structures, glycan-protein conjugates, or other serum-derived molecules whose biological roles in ovarian cancer remain to be elucidated. Their inclusion in the panel enhances the sensitivity and robustness of the diagnostic system, as their absence would result in a measurable decline in classification performance. The decision to retain these signals is supported by the observation that the composite diagnostic score derived from all fifteen biomarkers achieves superior accuracy compared to any subset excluding the unannotated features. This finding confirms that the diagnostic power of the system arises not from the sum of known glycan alterations alone, but from the collective behavior of all signals that exhibit consistent differential abundance, regardless of their biochemical identity.

### Example 3

The diagnostic algorithm developed in this invention integrates the fifteen selected biomarkers into a single, interpretable classification score through a weighted scoring system that reflects the relative diagnostic contribution of each biomarker. This system is designed to transform raw mass spectrometric intensities into a binary decision that can be easily implemented in a clinical laboratory setting. For each biomarker, a cut-off threshold is determined from the training set by analyzing its receiver operating characteristic curve and selecting the intensity value that corresponds to a standard sensitivity of seventy percent. This threshold is chosen to ensure that the biomarker maintains a high rate of true positive detection while minimizing false negatives, a critical consideration in cancer screening where missing a diagnosis has severe clinical consequences. Once determined, this cut-off is applied uniformly to all subsequent samples, whether from the training set or from independent validation cohorts, ensuring consistency and reproducibility across analyses.

For each sample, the intensity of each biomarker is compared to its respective cut-off threshold. If the intensity exceeds the threshold, the biomarker is assigned a score of +1, indicating a positive signal consistent with ovarian cancer. If the intensity falls below the threshold, the biomarker is assigned a score of −1, indicating a negative signal consistent with a healthy state. This binary assignment simplifies the interpretation of complex spectral data into a discrete, actionable outcome. However, not all biomarkers contribute equally to the diagnostic outcome; some exhibit greater discriminatory power than others. To account for this variation, each binary score is multiplied by a weighting factor proportional to the area under the ROC curve for that biomarker. This weighting ensures that biomarkers with higher diagnostic accuracy—those that more reliably distinguish cancer from non-cancer states—have a greater influence on the final composite score. For example, a biomarker with an AUC of 0.90 contributes nearly twice as much to the total score as a biomarker with an AUC of 0.55, reflecting its superior ability to predict disease status. The weighted scores for all fifteen biomarkers are then summed to produce a single composite diagnostic score for each sample.

The composite score is then used to classify the sample as either consistent with ovarian cancer or consistent with a healthy state by applying a diagnostic cut-off threshold determined during the training phase. This threshold is established by analyzing the distribution of composite scores across all samples in the training set and selecting the value that maximizes the sum of sensitivity and specificity, or alternatively, that achieves a clinically acceptable balance between the two based on the intended use of the test. In this invention, the optimal threshold is determined to be 4.2, such that samples with a composite score greater than or equal to 4.2 are classified as positive for ovarian cancer, while those below this value are classified as negative. This threshold is fixed and applied identically to all future samples, ensuring that the diagnostic algorithm remains stable and unbiased across different laboratories and testing environments.

The performance of this scoring system is rigorously evaluated in the training set, where it achieves a sensitivity of 88% and a specificity of 81% at the established cut-off. These values are further validated in an independent, blinded test set composed of sixty samples, including twenty-three healthy controls and thirty-seven ovarian cancer patients. In this validation cohort, the system maintains a sensitivity of 84% and a specificity of 83%, demonstrating that the diagnostic algorithm generalizes effectively to new, unseen data and is not overfitted to the training set. The consistency of specificity across varying score thresholds confirms that the system is robust and not overly sensitive to minor fluctuations in biomarker intensities. In contrast, the conventional biomarker CA-125, measured using the same samples and a cut-off of 35 U/mL, achieves a sensitivity of only 78% in the same validation cohort, with a specificity of 79%. The diagnostic superiority of the glycan-based panel is statistically significant, with a p-value less than 0.01 when comparing the areas under the respective ROC curves. Furthermore, the system demonstrates particular efficacy in detecting early-stage ovarian cancer, correctly classifying 89% of stage I cases and 83% of stage II cases, whereas CA-125 fails to detect more than half of these early-stage patients. The ability to identify early-stage disease is a critical advantage, as it enables intervention at a time when curative surgery is still feasible and survival outcomes are dramatically improved.

The scoring system also proves effective in identifying patients with recurrent ovarian cancer, a population for whom CA-125 is often unreliable due to fluctuating levels and the presence of post-surgical inflammation. In a pilot cohort of eight patients with confirmed recurrence following initial treatment, all eight were correctly classified as positive by the glycan-based panel, even though three of them had CA-125 levels below the diagnostic threshold. This suggests that the glycan signature reflects underlying molecular changes associated with tumor recurrence that are independent of CA-125 expression, potentially offering a more sensitive tool for monitoring disease progression and treatment response. The algorithm’s ability to detect recurrence without prior knowledge of the patient’s clinical history further underscores its potential as a standalone diagnostic tool. The system is not dependent on the histological subtype of the tumor, although certain subtypes, such as clear-cell carcinoma, exhibit even higher sensitivity, suggesting that the panel may be adaptable to subtype-specific refinements in future iterations. The method is fully automated, requiring no manual interpretation of spectra, and can be integrated into existing clinical laboratory workflows with minimal modification, making it a practical and scalable solution for population-based ovarian cancer screening.

### Example 4

The reproducibility and robustness of the diagnostic method are demonstrated through extensive technical validation across multiple experimental parameters, including intra-assay and inter-assay variability, sample stability, and instrument performance. The coefficient of variation (CV) for the absolute peak intensities of the fifteen biomarkers is calculated across all replicate analyses within a single sample, as well as across multiple days and operators, to assess the precision of the method. The average CV for all biomarkers across all samples is less than 15%, with the majority of signals exhibiting CV values below 10%, indicating high technical reproducibility. This level of precision is achieved through the use of standardized sample preparation protocols, automated liquid handling systems, and consistent MALDI target plate preparation techniques. The use of four independent spots per sample, each analyzed with one thousand laser shots, further enhances the reliability of the intensity measurements by averaging out spatial heterogeneity and stochastic ionization effects. The consistency of the results across replicates confirms that the observed differences in biomarker intensities are biological in origin and not artifacts of sample handling or instrumental drift.

To evaluate the stability of the glycan biomarkers under various storage and handling conditions, serum samples are subjected to multiple freeze-thaw cycles, prolonged storage at −20°C instead of −80°C, and delayed processing for up to seventy-two hours after blood collection. In all cases, the relative intensities of the fifteen biomarkers remain unchanged, with no significant deviation in the composite diagnostic score. This resilience to pre-analytical variables is critical for clinical implementation, as it ensures that the diagnostic system remains reliable even when samples are collected in remote locations or processed under suboptimal conditions. The stability of the glycan structures is further supported by the fact that the biomarkers are derived from N-linked glycans, which are covalently bound to proteins and therefore less susceptible to enzymatic degradation than free sugars or other labile metabolites. The use of microwave-assisted enzymatic release also ensures complete and uniform deglycosylation, minimizing batch-to-batch variability that might arise from incomplete reactions in conventional protocols.

The method is also validated for its performance across diverse patient populations, including variations in age, body mass index, and coexisting medical conditions such as diabetes, chronic inflammation, or benign ovarian cysts. Statistical analysis confirms that the diagnostic score is not significantly influenced by these confounding factors, as the distribution of scores in healthy controls remains tightly clustered regardless of demographic or clinical variables. This specificity is further reinforced by the fact that the biomarker panel performs equally well in both premenopausal and postmenopausal women, a critical consideration given that ovarian cancer incidence increases with age and that many existing biomarkers are affected by hormonal fluctuations. The system’s independence from age-related changes is particularly advantageous, as it eliminates the need for age-adjusted reference ranges, simplifying clinical interpretation and broadening its applicability across different screening populations.

The diagnostic algorithm is also tested for its ability to distinguish ovarian cancer from other malignancies, including breast, endometrial, and colorectal cancers, to assess its specificity for ovarian pathology. In a preliminary analysis of serum samples from patients with these other cancers, the composite score remains below the diagnostic threshold in the majority of cases, with only a small subset exhibiting borderline elevations. This suggests that the glycan signature identified in this invention is not a general marker of malignancy but is specifically associated with the pathophysiology of ovarian cancer. This specificity is further supported by the observation that the unannotated biomarkers, which constitute one-third of the panel, are not elevated in other cancer types, reinforcing the notion that their biological origin is tied to ovarian tumor biology rather than systemic inflammation or tumor burden. The method’s ability to discriminate ovarian cancer from other gynecological and non-gynecological malignancies enhances its potential as a targeted screening tool, reducing the likelihood of false positives that could lead to unnecessary invasive procedures.

The system is also evaluated for its compatibility with existing clinical workflows. The entire process, from serum collection to diagnostic score generation, can be completed within a single working day, with the mass spectrometric analysis requiring less than two minutes per sample once the glycan fractions are prepared. The use of standardized reagents, commercially available MALDI-TOF instruments, and open-source data analysis software ensures that the method can be implemented in hospitals and diagnostic laboratories without requiring specialized instrumentation or proprietary software. The output of the system—a single numerical score with a binary classification—is easily interpretable by clinicians and can be integrated into electronic health records without modification. The method requires no specialized training beyond standard clinical laboratory protocols, and quality control measures are built into the workflow through the use of internal reference samples and replicate analyses. The cost per test is comparable to that of CA-125 immunoassays, with the primary expense arising from the MALDI target plates and reagents, which are inexpensive and widely available. The scalability of the method allows for high-throughput processing, with the capacity to analyze over five hundred samples per week using automated platforms, making it suitable for population-based screening programs.

### Example 5

The clinical utility of this invention extends beyond the initial diagnosis of ovarian cancer to encompass a broad range of applications in oncology, including early detection, risk stratification, monitoring of treatment response, and detection of disease recurrence. The high sensitivity of the glycan-based panel in detecting early-stage disease positions it as a potential screening tool for asymptomatic women at average or elevated risk for ovarian cancer, such as those with a family history of breast or ovarian cancer, BRCA1/2 mutations, or a history of endometriosis. Unlike CA-125, which is unsuitable for population screening due to its low sensitivity and high false-positive rate, this method demonstrates sufficient accuracy to justify its use in routine clinical screening, potentially enabling the detection of ovarian cancer at a stage when curative intervention is still possible. The ability to classify patients with a sensitivity of 84% and a specificity of 83% in an independent validation cohort meets or exceeds the performance thresholds established by regulatory agencies for diagnostic tests intended for widespread use.

In the context of monitoring disease progression, the system offers a significant advantage over CA-125, which often fails to reflect tumor burden accurately in the post-treatment setting due to non-specific elevation from inflammation or residual benign tissue. The glycan signature, however, remains stable and specific even in the presence of post-surgical changes, as demonstrated by its ability to correctly identify all eight patients with recurrent ovarian cancer, regardless of their CA-125 levels. This suggests that the biomarkers reflect intrinsic molecular alterations associated with tumor cell biology rather than secondary inflammatory responses, making them more reliable indicators of active disease. The method may therefore be used to monitor patients after primary treatment, providing an early warning of recurrence before clinical symptoms or radiographic evidence appear, thereby enabling earlier initiation of salvage therapy and improved survival outcomes.

The system also has potential applications in risk stratification and personalized medicine. By analyzing the individual contributions of the fifteen biomarkers, it may be possible to identify subgroups of patients with distinct glycan signatures that correlate with specific histological subtypes, genetic mutations, or prognostic outcomes. For example, the clear-cell subtype of ovarian cancer, which is often resistant to conventional chemotherapy, demonstrates exceptionally high sensitivity to the glycan panel, suggesting that this subtype may be particularly amenable to early detection using this method. Future studies may explore whether specific combinations of biomarkers can predict response to platinum-based chemotherapy or identify patients who are likely to benefit from targeted therapies such as PARP inhibitors. The non-invasive nature of the test, requiring only a small volume of serum, makes it ideal for longitudinal monitoring, allowing for repeated sampling over time to track dynamic changes in the glycome as the disease evolves.

The invention also opens new avenues for biomarker discovery and biological insight. The five unannotated biomarkers, while not yet chemically characterized, represent a novel class of serum molecules whose differential abundance in ovarian cancer suggests a direct or indirect role in tumor biology. Future research may focus on isolating and identifying these molecules using advanced techniques such as tandem mass spectrometry, high-resolution chromatography, or glycan sequencing. Their identification could lead to the discovery of previously unknown glycoproteins, glycan-binding proteins, or novel glycosylation pathways involved in ovarian carcinogenesis. Even without full structural characterization, their inclusion in the diagnostic panel provides immediate clinical value, demonstrating that functional biomarkers can be developed and deployed without complete biochemical knowledge—a paradigm shift with broad implications for the field of molecular diagnostics.

The method is also amenable to integration with other diagnostic modalities. For instance, combining the glycan-based panel with CA-125 measurement, imaging modalities such as transvaginal ultrasound, or genetic risk profiling could yield a multi-modal screening algorithm with even greater accuracy. Preliminary analysis suggests that such a combination could increase sensitivity to over 90% while maintaining specificity above 80%, potentially creating a gold-standard screening protocol for high-risk populations. The system’s modular design allows for the addition of new biomarkers as they are discovered, enabling continuous improvement and adaptation to emerging clinical needs. The use of standardized data formats and open-source analysis tools ensures that the method can be shared, validated, and adopted by laboratories worldwide, promoting global equity in access to advanced ovarian cancer diagnostics.

In summary, this invention provides a transformative diagnostic tool for ovarian cancer that overcomes the limitations of current biomarkers through a novel, data-driven approach to glycan analysis. By leveraging the full complexity of the serum glycome and incorporating both annotated and unannotated biomarkers into a weighted scoring algorithm, the method delivers superior diagnostic accuracy, reproducibility, and clinical utility. It represents a paradigm shift in cancer diagnostics, moving from single-molecule markers to multivariate, functionally validated signatures that reflect the underlying biology of disease. The system is ready for clinical validation in large-scale prospective trials and has the potential to become a standard of care in ovarian cancer screening, significantly improving early detection rates and ultimately saving lives.
