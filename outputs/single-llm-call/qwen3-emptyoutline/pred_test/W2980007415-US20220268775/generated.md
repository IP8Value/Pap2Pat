# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of cancer immunotherapy has undergone a profound transformation over the past three decades, driven by a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms that govern immune tolerance and immune activation within the tumor microenvironment. Among the most pivotal discoveries in this domain is the identification of the programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), a transmembrane receptor expressed on activated T cells, B cells, natural killer cells, and monocytes, which functions as a critical immune checkpoint regulator. First characterized in 1992 by Tasuku Honjo and colleagues at Kyoto University, PD-1 was initially recognized as a novel gene induced during apoptosis in a murine T cell line. Subsequent investigations revealed its essential role in maintaining peripheral immune tolerance by attenuating T cell receptor signaling upon engagement with its ligands, PD-L1 and PD-L2, which are frequently upregulated by tumor cells and antigen-presenting cells within the tumor microenvironment. This interaction effectively dampens cytotoxic T lymphocyte activity, thereby enabling tumors to evade immune surveillance—a phenomenon now widely acknowledged as one of the hallmarks of cancer immune evasion.

The clinical relevance of this pathway was dramatically underscored by the development of monoclonal antibodies that block the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, leading to unprecedented durable responses in a subset of patients with advanced melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, renal cell carcinoma, and other malignancies. These immune checkpoint inhibitors, including nivolumab and pembrolizumab, have since become standard-of-care therapies for numerous solid tumors, fundamentally altering the therapeutic landscape and earning Honjo the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2018. However, despite their transformative impact, these agents are not universally effective; response rates vary significantly across tumor types and individual patients, and many patients either do not respond initially or eventually develop resistance. This heterogeneity in clinical outcomes has spurred intense research into predictive biomarkers that could identify patients most likely to benefit from PD-1 pathway blockade, as well as those who may be at risk of hyperprogression or immune-related adverse events.

In parallel with the development of checkpoint inhibitors, therapeutic cancer vaccines have emerged as a complementary strategy aimed at actively priming or boosting tumor-specific T cell responses. Unlike checkpoint blockade, which removes inhibitory signals, vaccines seek to enhance the initiation and magnitude of anti-tumor immunity by presenting tumor-associated antigens in an immunostimulatory context. Autologous dendritic cell vaccines, in particular, represent a highly personalized approach in which patient-derived dendritic cells are loaded ex vivo with tumor antigens derived from autologous tumor tissue, then reinfused to stimulate endogenous T cell responses. Clinical trials evaluating such vaccines have demonstrated variable efficacy, with some studies reporting prolonged survival in subsets of patients, while others have shown minimal benefit. The reasons for this inconsistency remain poorly understood, but emerging evidence suggests that the pre-existing immune state of the patient—particularly the degree of pre-activation or exhaustion of tumor-reactive T cells—may critically influence vaccine responsiveness.

One potential window into this dynamic immune state lies in the measurement of soluble programmed cell death protein 1 (sPD-1), a circulating isoform of the membrane-bound PD-1 receptor generated through alternative splicing or proteolytic cleavage. sPD-1 retains the extracellular ligand-binding domain of PD-1 and is capable of binding PD-L1 and PD-L2, thereby potentially acting as a decoy receptor that competes with membrane-bound PD-1 for ligand interaction. This has led to the hypothesis that sPD-1 may serve as a biomarker reflecting the intensity and regulation of ongoing anti-tumor immune responses. In theory, low levels of sPD-1 might indicate a quiescent or absent T cell response, whereas elevated levels could signify active T cell activation followed by compensatory upregulation of the PD-1 pathway to restrain excessive inflammation. However, the biological significance and clinical utility of sPD-1 as a biomarker have remained controversial, with conflicting reports across different cancer types and treatment modalities. Some studies have associated high sPD-1 levels with improved survival in non-small cell lung cancer patients treated with EGFR inhibitors, while others have linked elevated sPD-1 with poor prognosis in hepatocellular carcinoma or advanced pancreatic cancer. These inconsistencies suggest that the interpretation of sPD-1 levels is context-dependent, influenced by tumor type, treatment modality, timing of sampling, and the underlying immune architecture of the host.

Prior to this invention, no method had been established to integrate baseline levels of sPD-1 with dynamic changes in sPD-1 following therapeutic intervention in order to predict long-term clinical outcomes in patients receiving cancer vaccines. While prior studies had examined sPD-1 as a static biomarker—either at diagnosis or during treatment—none had systematically evaluated the combination of baseline concentration and the direction and magnitude of change following immunotherapy as a composite predictive signature. Moreover, no prior work had demonstrated differential predictive value of this composite signature across distinct vaccine platforms, such as autologous dendritic cell vaccines versus autologous tumor cell vaccines, despite their mechanistic differences in antigen presentation and co-stimulatory signaling. The absence of such a method has left clinicians without a reliable tool to stratify patients for vaccine therapy, resulting in the administration of costly, labor-intensive, and often ineffective treatments to patients unlikely to benefit, while potentially depriving others who might respond profoundly.

The need for a robust, biologically grounded, and clinically actionable biomarker strategy to guide the use of cancer vaccines remains unmet. Current approaches rely heavily on tumor mutational burden, PD-L1 expression by immunohistochemistry, or circulating tumor DNA—all of which are imperfect, invasive, or not universally applicable. There is a critical gap in the art for a non-invasive, easily measurable, and dynamically interpretable biomarker that can be assessed in routine blood samples and used to predict not only whether a patient is likely to respond to a vaccine, but also how the immune system is likely to evolve in response to vaccination. This invention addresses that gap by introducing a novel classification system that combines baseline sPD-1 concentration with the direction of change in sPD-1 levels following the initial series of vaccine administrations to define two distinct patient cohorts: one with a favorable immune trajectory and another with a poor immune trajectory. This system, validated in a randomized clinical trial of metastatic melanoma patients treated with autologous dendritic cell or tumor cell vaccines, demonstrates a statistically significant association with long-term survival specifically in the dendritic cell vaccine cohort, and provides a framework for prospective patient selection, treatment personalization, and surrogate endpoint development in future vaccine trials.

The invention further introduces a method for interpreting sPD-1 dynamics not as an isolated signal, but as a temporal indicator of immune system engagement and regulation. By defining thresholds for baseline levels and change trajectories, the invention transforms sPD-1 from a passive biomarker into an active decision-making tool. It enables clinicians to identify patients with very low baseline sPD-1 who may benefit from vaccine monotherapy, patients with very high baseline sPD-1 who may require concurrent checkpoint blockade, and patients with intermediate baseline levels who may derive maximal benefit from a vaccine-induced decrease in sPD-1, suggesting successful immune activation without subsequent suppression. This nuanced, multi-dimensional interpretation represents a paradigm shift from static biomarker measurement to dynamic immune monitoring, and provides the first clinically validated algorithm for using sPD-1 kinetics to guide cancer vaccine therapy.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a method for predicting long-term survival in patients with metastatic melanoma who are undergoing treatment with autologous dendritic cell vaccines, based on the measurement of soluble programmed cell death protein 1 (sPD-1) levels in serum at two distinct time points: prior to the initiation of vaccination and following the administration of the first three vaccine doses. The invention provides a classification system that stratifies patients into two mutually exclusive cohorts based on a combination of baseline sPD-1 concentration and the direction of change in sPD-1 levels between these two time points. The first cohort, designated as sPD-1 low/decreased, includes patients who either have a baseline sPD-1 level below 100 picograms per milliliter or who have a baseline sPD-1 level between 100 and 200,000 picograms per milliliter and exhibit a decrease in sPD-1 concentration following the first three vaccine doses. The second cohort, designated as sPD-1 high/increased, includes patients who either have a baseline sPD-1 level exceeding 200,000 picograms per milliliter or who exhibit an increase in sPD-1 concentration following the first three vaccine doses, regardless of baseline level. This classification system is specifically predictive of survival outcomes in patients treated with autologous dendritic cell vaccines, but not in those treated with autologous tumor cell vaccines, thereby distinguishing it from previously known biomarkers that lack treatment-specific predictive power.

The invention further provides a method for determining whether a patient is likely to achieve long-term survival following autologous dendritic cell vaccination by measuring the concentration of sPD-1 in a serum sample obtained one week prior to the first vaccine dose and comparing it to the concentration of sPD-1 in a second serum sample obtained one week after the third vaccine dose. If the patient is classified as sPD-1 low/decreased according to the defined criteria, the method predicts a high probability of survival exceeding three years. If the patient is classified as sPD-1 high/increased, the method predicts a low probability of survival exceeding three years. This predictive capability is demonstrated with a classification accuracy of 80% for patients who survive beyond three years and 86% for those who do not, in a cohort of 17 patients treated with autologous dendritic cell vaccines. The invention further includes a method for excluding patients from receiving autologous dendritic cell vaccines if their baseline sPD-1 level exceeds 200,000 picograms per milliliter, as such patients are highly unlikely to benefit from this modality and may be better suited for alternative therapies such as immune checkpoint blockade.

The invention also encompasses a method for enriching clinical trial populations for patients with metastatic melanoma who are candidates for autologous dendritic cell vaccination by pre-selecting individuals whose baseline sPD-1 levels fall within the range of 100 to 200,000 picograms per milliliter and who demonstrate a subsequent decrease in sPD-1 levels following the initial vaccine series. This approach allows for the prospective identification of patients most likely to respond to dendritic cell vaccination, thereby increasing the statistical power of clinical trials and reducing the number of patients exposed to ineffective therapies. The invention further provides a method for monitoring the immunological response to dendritic cell vaccination by serially measuring sPD-1 levels and interpreting a decline in concentration as a surrogate marker of successful immune activation without subsequent suppression by the PD-1/PD-L1 axis.

The invention is distinct from prior art in that it does not rely on the absolute value of sPD-1 at a single time point, nor does it treat sPD-1 as a universal biomarker applicable across all cancer types or treatment modalities. Instead, it introduces a dynamic, context-dependent, and treatment-specific algorithm that integrates temporal change with baseline concentration to generate a predictive signature. This signature is validated in a randomized clinical trial setting with long-term follow-up and demonstrates a statistically significant association with overall survival exclusively in the dendritic cell vaccine arm, with no comparable predictive value observed in the tumor cell vaccine arm. The invention further provides a method for differentiating between two distinct biological states: one in which the immune system is primed but not suppressed, and another in which the immune system is either inactive or actively restrained. This distinction enables the rational selection of combination therapies—for instance, pairing dendritic cell vaccination with PD-1 blockade in patients classified as sPD-1 high/increased, or administering vaccine monotherapy to those classified as sPD-1 low/decreased.

The invention further includes a method for generating a therapeutic decision tree based on sPD-1 kinetics, wherein the baseline sPD-1 level and its change following the first three vaccine doses determine the subsequent therapeutic pathway. For patients classified as sPD-1 low/decreased, the recommended course of action is continuation of dendritic cell vaccination without addition of checkpoint inhibitors. For patients classified as sPD-1 high/increased, the recommended course of action is discontinuation of dendritic cell vaccination and initiation of anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1 therapy. For patients with baseline sPD-1 levels exceeding 200,000 picograms per milliliter, the invention recommends immediate initiation of checkpoint blockade without vaccination, as these patients are unlikely to benefit from antigen priming alone. This decision-making framework is reproducible, scalable, and compatible with standard clinical laboratory assays, enabling its implementation in routine oncology practice.

The invention further provides a method for identifying patients who are candidates for de-escalation of therapy, wherein those classified as sPD-1 low/decreased and who achieve sustained clinical remission may be candidates for reduced frequency of vaccination or cessation of treatment, based on the favorable immune trajectory indicated by sPD-1 kinetics. Conversely, patients classified as sPD-1 high/increased may be candidates for escalation of therapy, including combination with checkpoint inhibitors, cytokine therapy, or adoptive cell transfer. The invention thus transforms sPD-1 from a passive biomarker into an active guide for therapeutic decision-making, enabling precision immunotherapy tailored to the individual’s immune dynamics.

The invention further encompasses a kit for performing the method, comprising a container holding a serum sample from a patient with metastatic melanoma, a reagent for quantifying sPD-1 levels by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, instructions for measuring sPD-1 at baseline and at week four, and a reference chart correlating baseline sPD-1 levels and change in sPD-1 levels with predicted survival outcomes. The kit further includes a digital interface or algorithm capable of receiving sPD-1 measurements and automatically classifying the patient into one of the two cohorts, with a recommendation for therapeutic intervention. The invention further provides a computer-readable medium storing a set of instructions that, when executed by a processor, cause a computer system to receive sPD-1 values from two time points, compare them to the predefined thresholds, classify the patient according to the sPD-1 low/decreased or sPD-1 high/increased criteria, and output a recommendation for treatment selection or clinical trial enrollment.

The invention also provides a method for validating the predictive power of the sPD-1 classification system in prospective clinical trials by enrolling patients with metastatic melanoma, measuring sPD-1 at baseline and week four, classifying them according to the invention’s criteria, and correlating classification with survival outcomes over a minimum of three years. The invention further provides a method for using the sPD-1 classification system as a surrogate endpoint in phase II clinical trials of dendritic cell vaccines, thereby enabling accelerated approval based on early immune biomarker response rather than prolonged survival follow-up. The invention further provides a method for adapting the classification thresholds to other cancer types or vaccine platforms by conducting similar longitudinal analyses in other patient populations, with the understanding that the specific numerical thresholds may vary, but the underlying principle of combining baseline concentration with change trajectory remains universally applicable.

The invention is not intended to be limited to metastatic melanoma or dendritic cell vaccines; rather, it establishes a foundational framework for using dynamic sPD-1 kinetics as a predictive biomarker in any setting where immune activation is induced by therapeutic intervention. The invention further provides a method for integrating sPD-1 kinetics with other biomarkers, such as tumor mutational burden, circulating tumor DNA, or cytokine profiles, to generate a multi-modal predictive algorithm with enhanced accuracy. The invention further provides a method for real-time monitoring of sPD-1 during vaccination cycles, wherein repeated measurements are used to adjust the therapeutic strategy dynamically, allowing for adaptive immunotherapy that evolves in response to the patient’s immune status. The invention thus represents a significant advancement in the field of cancer immunotherapy by introducing a clinically validated, biologically grounded, and actionable method for predicting survival and guiding treatment decisions based on the temporal dynamics of a single, easily measurable serum biomarker.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention describes a novel method for predicting long-term survival outcomes in patients with metastatic melanoma who are undergoing treatment with autologous dendritic cell vaccines, based on the quantitative measurement of soluble programmed cell death protein 1 (sPD-1) in serum at two defined time points: immediately prior to the initiation of vaccination and one week following the administration of the third vaccine dose. This method is not a general biomarker assay, but a precisely defined classification algorithm that integrates baseline concentration with the direction and magnitude of change in sPD-1 levels to generate a predictive signature that is both statistically significant and clinically actionable. The invention is grounded in empirical observations derived from a randomized clinical trial of 39 patients with metastatic melanoma, in which serum samples were collected longitudinally and correlated with survival outcomes extending up to five years. The analysis revealed that while baseline sPD-1 levels alone were not predictive of survival across the entire cohort, a combination of baseline concentration and change trajectory following vaccination yielded a powerful and treatment-specific predictive model, particularly in patients treated with dendritic cell vaccines. This model was not observed in patients treated with autologous tumor cell vaccines, underscoring the specificity of the invention to the biological mechanism of dendritic cell-mediated antigen presentation.

The invention defines two distinct patient cohorts based on a binary classification system derived from two parameters: the baseline sPD-1 concentration measured in serum one week prior to the first vaccine injection, and the change in sPD-1 concentration measured one week after the third vaccine injection. The first cohort, designated as sPD-1 low/decreased, includes patients who meet either of two criteria: (1) a baseline sPD-1 concentration below 100 picograms per milliliter, regardless of subsequent change, or (2) a baseline sPD-1 concentration between 100 and 200,000 picograms per milliliter, coupled with a measurable decrease in sPD-1 concentration between the baseline and week-four time points. The second cohort, designated as sPD-1 high/increased, includes patients who meet either of two criteria: (1) a baseline sPD-1 concentration exceeding 200,000 picograms per milliliter, regardless of subsequent change, or (2) an increase in sPD-1 concentration between baseline and week four, regardless of baseline level. These thresholds were empirically determined through statistical analysis of survival outcomes and were selected to maximize the separation between patients with favorable and unfavorable prognoses, while maintaining biological plausibility and clinical feasibility.

The invention is predicated on the observation that patients classified as sPD-1 low/decreased exhibit a markedly higher probability of surviving beyond three years compared to those classified as sPD-1 high/increased. In the cohort of 17 patients treated with autologous dendritic cell vaccines, 9 out of 17 patients met the sPD-1 low/decreased criteria, and 8 of these 9 patients survived for more than three years. In contrast, only 2 out of 8 patients in the sPD-1 high/increased cohort survived beyond three years. This resulted in a statistically significant difference in survival probability (p = 0.0152), with a median survival exceeding four years in the sPD-1 low/decreased group and less than two years in the sPD-1 high/increased group. The classification system demonstrated a sensitivity of 80% for correctly identifying patients who survived beyond three years and a specificity of 86% for correctly identifying those who did not. These results were not observed in the 22 patients treated with autologous tumor cell vaccines, where no significant association was found between sPD-1 classification and survival, indicating that the predictive power of this method is specific to the dendritic cell vaccine platform and not a general property of cancer immunotherapy.

The biological rationale underlying this classification system is rooted in the functional role of sPD-1 as a soluble decoy receptor that competes with membrane-bound PD-1 for binding to PD-L1 and PD-L2. In patients with very low baseline sPD-1 levels, the immune system may be in a state of relative quiescence, with minimal T cell activation and consequently low expression of PD-1 on the surface of lymphocytes. In such patients, the administration of a dendritic cell vaccine may effectively prime tumor-specific T cells without triggering immediate compensatory upregulation of the PD-1 pathway, resulting in a sustained and effective anti-tumor response. The absence of a significant increase in sPD-1 following vaccination in these patients suggests that the immune system is not being actively suppressed, allowing the newly activated T cells to persist and mediate durable tumor control. In contrast, patients with very high baseline sPD-1 levels may represent a state of chronic immune activation that has been suppressed by the PD-1/PD-L1 axis. In these individuals, the tumor microenvironment is likely saturated with PD-L1-expressing cells, leading to persistent T cell exhaustion and the shedding of sPD-1 as a byproduct of ongoing, but futile, immune engagement. In such patients, further antigenic stimulation via dendritic cell vaccination may be insufficient to overcome this suppression, resulting in poor clinical outcomes.

The most compelling aspect of the invention lies in the intermediate group of patients—those with baseline sPD-1 levels between 100 and 200,000 picograms per milliliter. In this group, the direction of change in sPD-1 following vaccination becomes the critical determinant of outcome. A decrease in sPD-1 concentration in this cohort is associated with a high likelihood of long-term survival, whereas an increase is associated with poor survival. This finding suggests that in patients with moderate baseline immune activation, the vaccine’s ability to induce a productive immune response without triggering subsequent suppression is the key to success. A decline in sPD-1 may reflect the resolution of immune exhaustion, the expansion of non-exhausted T cell clones, or the downregulation of PD-L1 expression on tumor cells in response to effective immune pressure. Conversely, an increase in sPD-1 may indicate that the vaccine has successfully activated T cells, but that these cells are rapidly being suppressed by the PD-1 pathway, leading to functional anergy and tumor escape. This dynamic interpretation of sPD-1 kinetics provides a unique window into the immune system’s response to vaccination, distinguishing between productive immune activation and abortive immune stimulation.

The invention further provides a method for determining whether a patient is eligible for autologous dendritic cell vaccination based on their sPD-1 profile. Patients with baseline sPD-1 levels exceeding 200,000 picograms per milliliter are not recommended for dendritic cell vaccination under this method, as their likelihood of benefit is negligible. Instead, these patients are identified as candidates for immediate initiation of anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1 therapy, which may reverse the existing immune suppression and restore T cell function. Patients with baseline sPD-1 levels below 100 picograms per milliliter are considered ideal candidates for dendritic cell vaccine monotherapy, as their immune systems are primed for activation without pre-existing suppression. For patients with baseline sPD-1 levels between 100 and 200,000 picograms per milliliter, the decision to proceed with vaccination is contingent upon the observed change in sPD-1 after the first three doses. A decrease in sPD-1 supports continuation of the vaccine regimen, while an increase suggests that the vaccine is inducing immune activation that is being suppressed, and that alternative or combination strategies should be considered.

The invention further encompasses a method for monitoring the immunological response to dendritic cell vaccination over time. Serial measurements of sPD-1 may be obtained at additional time points, such as after the sixth or eighth vaccine dose, to assess whether the initial trajectory is maintained or altered. A sustained decrease in sPD-1 following multiple vaccine doses may indicate a durable and expanding anti-tumor immune response, whereas a rebound increase may signal the emergence of resistance mechanisms. This allows for adaptive immunotherapy, wherein treatment decisions are not fixed at initiation but are dynamically adjusted based on real-time biomarker feedback. The invention further provides a method for integrating sPD-1 kinetics with other clinical parameters, such as lactate dehydrogenase levels, tumor burden, or performance status, to refine predictive accuracy and account for confounding factors.

The invention further provides a method for selecting patients for inclusion in clinical trials of dendritic cell vaccines. By pre-screening potential participants for sPD-1 levels and change trajectory, investigators can enrich trial populations with patients who are more likely to respond, thereby increasing the probability of demonstrating statistical significance in phase II trials. This approach reduces the number of patients required to achieve a given level of power, lowers the cost and duration of trials, and minimizes the exposure of non-responders to ineffective therapies. The invention further provides a method for using the sPD-1 classification system as a surrogate endpoint in clinical trials, allowing for accelerated regulatory approval based on early biomarker response rather than prolonged survival follow-up. This is particularly valuable in the context of dendritic cell vaccines, which often require extended observation periods to demonstrate survival benefit due to their mechanism of action.

The invention further provides a method for differentiating between the immunological effects of dendritic cell vaccines and tumor cell vaccines. While both modalities utilize autologous tumor antigens, dendritic cell vaccines are characterized by ex vivo loading of antigens onto professional antigen-presenting cells, which provide co-stimulatory signals necessary for robust T cell priming. Tumor cell vaccines, in contrast, rely on endogenous dendritic cells to capture and present antigens from irradiated tumor cells, a process that is less efficient and more susceptible to immune suppression. The invention demonstrates that sPD-1 kinetics are predictive only in the dendritic cell vaccine cohort, suggesting that the ability of dendritic cells to overcome immune suppression and induce a productive T cell response is reflected in the sPD-1 trajectory. This distinction provides a mechanistic explanation for the superior clinical outcomes observed with dendritic cell vaccines in prior studies and underscores the importance of selecting the appropriate vaccine platform based on the patient’s immune status.

The invention further provides a method for identifying false positives and false negatives in the classification system and for refining the thresholds based on additional data. For example, two patients classified as sPD-1 high/increased nevertheless survived beyond five years, suggesting that some individuals may overcome immune suppression through alternative mechanisms, such as subsequent treatment with checkpoint inhibitors or adoptive cell therapy. These cases do not invalidate the classification system but highlight the importance of considering prior and subsequent therapies in the interpretation of sPD-1 kinetics. The invention further provides a method for adjusting the classification thresholds in different populations based on variations in assay methodology, patient demographics, or disease stage. While the thresholds of 100 and 200,000 picograms per milliliter were determined in a cohort of metastatic melanoma patients, similar principles may be applied to other cancers with appropriate validation.

The invention further provides a method for implementing the classification system in clinical practice using standardized laboratory assays. The measurement of sPD-1 is performed using a quantitative, multiplex enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay conducted under good laboratory practice standards, with results reported in picograms per milliliter. The assay is validated for precision, accuracy, and reproducibility across multiple laboratories and is compatible with standard serum samples collected during routine clinical visits. The invention further provides a method for automating the classification process using a digital algorithm that receives sPD-1 values from two time points, compares them to the predefined thresholds, and outputs a classification and therapeutic recommendation. This algorithm may be integrated into electronic health record systems, enabling real-time decision support for oncologists.

The invention further provides a method for developing companion diagnostics based on the sPD-1 classification system. A diagnostic kit may be manufactured comprising reagents for sPD-1 quantification, instructions for sample collection and timing, a reference chart for classification, and a digital interface for result interpretation. This kit may be approved by regulatory authorities as a companion diagnostic for dendritic cell vaccines, enabling its use in both clinical trials and routine practice.
