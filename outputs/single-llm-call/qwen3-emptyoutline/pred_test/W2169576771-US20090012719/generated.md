# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The inference of functional gene-gene interactions that are specifically associated with a disease phenotype, particularly in the context of complex pathologies such as cancer, has long presented a fundamental challenge in systems biology and molecular diagnostics. Traditional approaches to gene interaction network reconstruction have primarily relied on the analysis of gene expression data from a single biological state—either healthy or diseased—using methodologies such as Bayesian networks, graphical Gaussian models, or pairwise mutual information metrics. These techniques are effective at identifying co-expression patterns, regulatory dependencies, or correlation structures among genes under a given condition, but they lack the capacity to distinguish between interactions that are universally present across biological states and those that are uniquely activated, suppressed, or reconfigured in the presence of a specific phenotype. As a result, the resulting networks often reflect general cellular processes—such as ribosome biogenesis, metabolic homeostasis, or cell cycle regulation—that are active regardless of disease status, thereby obscuring the subtle, context-dependent interactions that may be mechanistically responsible for disease initiation or progression.

A critical limitation of these conventional methods lies in their inability to isolate cooperative, non-additive relationships between gene pairs that are only functionally relevant in the context of a particular pathological state. For instance, two genes may each exhibit strong individual correlations with cancer due to their roles in oncogenic pathways, yet their joint behavior may not deviate from what would be expected by their independent contributions. Conversely, two genes may show weak or negligible individual associations with cancer, yet their combined expression pattern may be highly predictive of disease status due to a synergistic interaction—such as the co-dependence of a transcription factor and its co-activator, or the requirement of a dimeric protein complex for downstream signaling. In such cases, traditional network inference methods fail to detect these interactions because they do not explicitly model the concept of synergy: the phenomenon wherein the combined informational value of two variables about a phenotype exceeds the sum of their individual contributions. This distinction is not merely semantic; it represents a fundamental shift in the analytical paradigm—from identifying genes that are merely associated with disease to identifying gene pairs whose cooperative behavior is causally or functionally implicated in disease mechanisms.

Previous attempts to address this gap have included the introduction of auxiliary nodes into gene interaction networks, such as a “cancer node” connected to genes whose expression correlates with disease status. While such models can identify genes associated with cancer, they do not reveal the pairwise relationships among those genes that are uniquely active in the disease context. The resulting network remains a collection of gene-cancer associations rather than a map of disease-specific gene-gene interactions. Moreover, the use of binary or thresholded gene expression data—where genes are classified as either “on” or “off”—has been a common simplification in earlier information-theoretic analyses of synergy. This binarization process, while computationally convenient, leads to substantial loss of information, as it collapses the continuous, nuanced variation in gene expression levels into discrete states that may not reflect biological reality. The choice of binarization threshold is often arbitrary, sensitive to data normalization methods, and inconsistent across genes, leading to unreliable and non-reproducible results. Furthermore, the discrete representation precludes the detection of subtle, non-linear relationships that manifest only within intermediate expression ranges, thereby limiting the sensitivity and biological relevance of the inferred interactions.

To overcome these limitations, a novel computational framework has been developed that enables the direct quantification of gene-gene synergy from continuous gene expression data without any prior binarization, thresholding, or reliance on biological annotations. This framework is grounded in information theory and leverages the concept of synergy, defined as the difference between the mutual information shared by a gene pair and a phenotype, and the sum of the mutual informations shared by each gene individually with that phenotype. This metric, I(G1, G2; C) − [I(G1; C) + I(G2; C)], captures the extent to which the joint expression of two genes provides information about disease status that cannot be explained by their independent contributions. A positive synergy value indicates that the two genes interact cooperatively in relation to the phenotype—suggesting a functional partnership, such as co-regulation, physical interaction, or participation in a shared pathway that is specifically activated or disrupted in disease. A negative synergy value, in contrast, indicates redundancy, where the information provided by one gene is largely overlapping with that of the other. This distinction is critical: while mutual information identifies gene pairs that are collectively predictive of disease (useful for classification), synergy identifies gene pairs whose interaction is mechanistically relevant to disease (useful for pathway discovery).

The central innovation of this methodology lies in its ability to compute this synergy metric directly from continuous expression values using a dendrogram-based clustering approach that partitions samples into homogeneous groups based on joint gene expression patterns. Rather than imposing artificial thresholds, the method constructs hierarchical clusterings of samples using the UPGMA algorithm and the Chebyshev distance metric, which preserves the interpretability of biological thresholds across dimensions. For each possible partition of the sample space defined by a horizontal cut through the dendrogram, the conditional entropy of disease status given the gene expression pattern is computed as a weighted average of the entropies of individual clusters. This entropy reflects the uncertainty in predicting disease status when the sample is assigned to a particular expression cluster. The mutual information and, subsequently, the synergy between gene pairs are then derived from the difference between the entropy of disease status in the absence of gene information and the entropy in its presence. By integrating this entropy calculation across all possible clusterings up to a biologically meaningful cutoff distance, the method effectively averages over the discrete ambiguities inherent in hierarchical clustering, yielding a continuous, robust, and statistically sound estimate of synergy that is fully compatible with the underlying continuous nature of gene expression data.

This approach is not only theoretically grounded but also computationally scalable. By employing an exhaustive search over all possible gene pairs in a transcriptome-wide dataset, the method identifies the most statistically significant synergistic interactions without bias toward known biological pathways or prior annotations. The statistical significance of each identified interaction is rigorously evaluated through permutation testing, where the class labels (healthy vs. diseased) are randomly shuffled under two distinct null models: one that preserves gene-gene correlations within samples but breaks the association with disease status, and another that preserves individual gene-disease associations but destroys sample-level co-expression structure. The resulting empirical distributions of maximum synergy values are fitted to a Gumbel extreme value distribution, enabling the calculation of highly conservative P-values for observed synergies. Furthermore, false discovery rate correction is applied to account for the multiple comparisons inherent in testing tens of thousands of gene pairs, ensuring that only interactions with overwhelming statistical support are retained in the final synergy network.

The biological relevance of this methodology has been demonstrated through its application to a well-characterized prostate cancer gene expression dataset comprising 102 clinical samples, 50 of which are histologically confirmed as healthy and 52 as malignant. The resulting synergy network reveals a striking pattern: a single gene, RBP1 (cellular retinol-binding protein 1), emerges as a central hub, exhibiting significant positive synergy with a diverse set of partner genes, many of which are involved in ribosomal biogenesis, oxidative phosphorylation, and apoptosis regulation. Notably, these partner genes are not themselves among the top individually correlated genes with prostate cancer, underscoring the unique capacity of synergy to uncover cooperative interactions that are invisible to conventional differential expression or correlation analyses. The molecular logic underlying these synergistic interactions is remarkably consistent: prostate cancer occurs when RBP1 is underexpressed in conjunction with the overexpression of its partner gene. This pattern is preserved across independent validation datasets, suggesting that it reflects a robust biological phenomenon rather than a technical artifact. The enrichment of ribosomal genes among the top synergistic partners further supports a mechanistic hypothesis linking RBP1 downregulation with the activation of mTOR-mediated translational control, a known oncogenic pathway. The concurrent identification of genes involved in oxidative stress response—such as FTL, UQCRH, and SLC25A6—suggests that the synergy network captures a coherent biological program in which cellular damage from oxidative stress is coupled with the suppression of apoptosis, creating a permissive environment for tumorigenesis.

Importantly, this methodology does not require prior biological knowledge, making it applicable to any disease context for which paired healthy and diseased expression datasets are available. It is equally suited to other phenotypes, such as neurodegenerative disorders, autoimmune conditions, or developmental anomalies, provided that sufficient sample sizes are available to ensure statistical power. The framework is also extensible to higher-order synergies, enabling the detection of gene triplets or quadruplets whose combined expression patterns are more informative than any subset of their components—a capability that could reveal multi-gene regulatory modules or protein complexes uniquely active in disease. Furthermore, the method is agnostic to the type of molecular data used; it can be applied to RNA-seq expression levels, protein abundance measurements, post-translational modification states, or even alternatively spliced isoforms, thereby facilitating multi-omic integration. The computational efficiency of the algorithm, which enables exhaustive search over 80 million gene pairs in under an hour on a modest computing cluster, ensures its practicality for large-scale genomic studies.

In summary, the background of this invention is rooted in the recognition that disease-specific gene-gene interactions cannot be reliably identified through traditional network inference methods that treat gene expression as a static, independent, or binary variable. The invention addresses this gap by introducing a novel, information-theoretic, dendrogram-based methodology for computing synergy directly from continuous gene expression data, thereby enabling the discovery of cooperative gene interactions that are specifically associated with a disease phenotype. This approach transforms the analysis of gene expression data from a tool for gene ranking into a mechanism for uncovering functional partnerships that drive disease, offering a powerful new lens through which to understand the molecular logic of complex pathologies.

## SUMMARY

The invention disclosed herein is a method for identifying gene-gene interactions that are specifically associated with a disease phenotype by quantifying the synergistic information shared between pairs of genes and the phenotype, using continuous gene expression data without reliance on prior biological knowledge or arbitrary binarization thresholds. The method operates by constructing hierarchical clusterings of biological samples based on the joint expression profiles of gene pairs, computing the conditional entropy of disease status given these expression patterns, and deriving the synergy metric as the difference between the mutual information of the gene pair with the phenotype and the sum of the individual mutual informations of each gene with the phenotype. This synergy metric captures the extent to which the combined expression of two genes provides predictive information about disease status that exceeds what would be expected from their independent contributions, thereby identifying cooperative interactions that are functionally relevant to the phenotype. The clustering is performed using the UPGMA algorithm with the Chebyshev distance measure, which ensures dimensional consistency and biological interpretability across gene sets of varying size. The conditional entropy is computed as a weighted average of cluster entropies, integrated over all possible partitioning thresholds up to a biologically motivated cutoff, thereby smoothing discontinuities inherent in hierarchical clustering and providing a robust, continuous estimate of information content. The method is applied exhaustively to all possible gene pairs in a transcriptome-wide dataset, enabling the construction of a synergy network in which nodes represent genes and edges represent statistically significant synergistic interactions. Statistical significance is assessed through permutation testing under two distinct null models—one that randomizes disease labels while preserving within-sample gene correlations, and another that randomizes gene expression values within disease classes while preserving individual gene-disease associations—followed by fitting the maximum synergy values to a Gumbel extreme value distribution to compute P-values. False discovery rate correction is applied to account for multiple comparisons, ensuring that only interactions with overwhelming statistical support are retained. The resulting synergy network reveals gene pairs whose cooperative behavior is uniquely associated with the disease phenotype, irrespective of their individual expression patterns, and provides mechanistic insights into disease pathways that are inaccessible to conventional correlation-based or differential expression analyses. The method is computationally efficient, scalable to large datasets, and applicable to any disease context for which paired healthy and diseased expression profiles are available, making it a transformative tool for systems biology, biomarker discovery, and therapeutic target identification. The invention further encompasses the use of this synergy network to prioritize combinatorial therapeutic interventions, as synergistic gene pairs represent functional units whose simultaneous modulation may be required for therapeutic efficacy, and to guide the design of multi-gene diagnostic signatures that capture cooperative biological logic rather than additive signals.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention provides a comprehensive computational methodology for the identification of gene-gene interactions that are specifically associated with a disease phenotype through the quantification of synergistic information between pairs of genes and the phenotype, using continuous gene expression data without the need for prior biological knowledge, arbitrary binarization, or heuristic assumptions regarding gene regulation. The method is grounded in information theory and is designed to distinguish cooperative interactions—where the joint behavior of two genes yields predictive power about disease status that exceeds the sum of their individual contributions—from redundant or independent associations that are commonly detected by conventional gene network inference techniques. The core innovation lies in the direct computation of synergy from continuous expression values using a dendrogram-based clustering framework that preserves the biological continuity of gene expression while enabling statistically rigorous inference of functional relationships.

The method begins with the acquisition of a gene expression dataset comprising measurements from a set of biological samples, each of which is classified as either belonging to a healthy state or to a disease state. The dataset consists of expression values for a plurality of genes, measured across a plurality of samples, where each expression value is a continuous numerical representation of the abundance of a transcript or protein in a given sample. The expression data are normalized using a standardized method such as Robust Multi-array Average (RMA) or other appropriate normalization techniques to ensure comparability across samples and to minimize technical variability. The dataset is then partitioned into two subsets: one containing samples from the healthy state and the other containing samples from the disease state. The number of samples in each subset is sufficient to ensure statistical power, with a preference for balanced representation to avoid bias in entropy estimation. The total number of genes in the dataset may range from several thousand to tens of thousands, depending on the platform used, and the method is designed to handle such high-dimensional data efficiently.

The first step in the method involves the computation of the mutual information between each individual gene and the disease phenotype. Mutual information, denoted as I(Gi; C), quantifies the amount of information that the expression level of gene Gi provides about the disease status C, where C is a binary variable indicating whether a sample is healthy or diseased. This is calculated by first estimating the entropy of the disease status, H(C), which reflects the uncertainty in predicting disease status in the absence of any gene expression information. The entropy H(C) is computed as H(C) = −p log₂ p − (1−p) log₂ (1−p), where p is the proportion of diseased samples in the dataset. The conditional entropy of disease status given the expression of gene Gi, denoted H(C|Gi), is then computed using a dendrogram-based clustering approach. Specifically, the expression levels of gene Gi across all samples are used as a one-dimensional feature space, and a hierarchical clustering is performed using the UPGMA algorithm with the Chebyshev distance metric. The Chebyshev distance, defined as the maximum absolute difference between expression values along any single dimension, is selected to ensure that the distance metric remains bounded and interpretable across dimensions, avoiding the inflation of distances that occurs with Euclidean measures when additional genes are included in the analysis. This choice is critical, as it ensures that the biological interpretation of clustering thresholds remains consistent whether analyzing single genes or gene pairs.

Following clustering, a series of horizontal cuts are made through the resulting dendrogram at increasing distances from the leaves, each cut defining a partition of the samples into a set of disjoint clusters. For each such partition, the entropy of each cluster is computed as h(Q) = −Q log₂ Q − (1−Q) log₂ (1−Q), where Q is the proportion of diseased samples within that cluster. The conditional entropy H(C|Gi) is then obtained as the weighted average of these cluster entropies, where the weight for each cluster is proportional to the number of samples it contains. To account for the fact that no single clustering threshold may capture the full biological relevance of the gene-disease relationship, the method integrates the conditional entropy over all possible clustering thresholds up to a maximum distance D*, which is defined as a threshold of biological significance. The integrated conditional entropy is computed as the average of H(C|Gi) over the interval [0, D*], effectively smoothing the discontinuities that arise when clusters are merged at specific dendrogram nodes. This integration step ensures that the method is not sensitive to arbitrary choices of clustering depth and yields a continuous, robust estimate of information content that is consistent with the continuous nature of gene expression data. The mutual information I(Gi; C) is then calculated as the difference between H(C) and the integrated H(C|Gi), and normalized by dividing by H(C) to yield a dimensionless value between 0 and 1, where 1 indicates perfect predictability of disease status from gene expression.

The method then proceeds to compute the synergy between every possible pair of genes in the dataset. For each gene pair (Gi, Gj), the joint mutual information I(Gi, Gj; C) is computed using the same dendrogram-based clustering procedure, but now in a two-dimensional expression space defined by the joint expression levels of Gi and Gj. The UPGMA clustering is performed on the pair of expression values, again using the Chebyshev distance metric to preserve dimensional consistency. The conditional entropy H(C|Gi, Gj) is computed as the integrated weighted average of cluster entropies over all clustering thresholds up to D*, as described above. The synergy between the gene pair and the disease phenotype is then defined as the difference between the joint mutual information and the sum of the individual mutual informations: Syn(Gi, Gj; C) = I(Gi, Gj; C) − [I(Gi; C) + I(Gj; C)]. This quantity represents the amount of information about disease status that is uniquely attributable to the cooperative interaction between Gi and Gj, beyond what can be explained by their independent contributions. A positive synergy value indicates that the joint expression of the two genes provides more predictive power than the sum of their individual effects, suggesting a functional partnership such as co-regulation, physical interaction, or participation in a shared biochemical pathway that is specifically activated or disrupted in the disease state. A negative synergy value indicates redundancy, where the information provided by one gene is largely overlapping with that of the other, and a value near zero indicates independence.

The synergy metric is computed for every possible pair of genes in the dataset, resulting in a set of synergy scores for approximately N(N−1)/2 gene pairs, where N is the total number of genes. For a typical dataset with 12,000 genes, this results in over 70 million pairwise synergy calculations. To ensure computational efficiency, the algorithm is implemented in parallel across a distributed computing environment, with each gene pair assigned to a separate processing node. The UPGMA clustering and entropy integration are performed in a single pass for each gene pair, with intermediate results cached to avoid redundant computations. The entire process, including the computation of individual gene mutual informations and pairwise synergies, is completed in under one hour on a 200-node computing cluster, demonstrating the scalability of the method to genome-wide datasets.

The next critical step in the method is the statistical evaluation of the significance of each observed synergy score. To determine whether a given synergy value could have arisen by chance, two distinct permutation strategies are employed. In the first permutation strategy, referred to as Permutation A, the class labels (healthy vs. diseased) of the samples are randomly shuffled across the entire dataset, thereby breaking the association between gene expression and disease status while preserving the internal correlation structure of gene expression within each sample. In the second permutation strategy, referred to as Permutation B, the expression values of each gene are independently shuffled within the healthy samples and within the diseased samples, thereby preserving the marginal distribution of each gene’s expression in each class but destroying any sample-level co-expression relationships between genes. Both permutations are performed 100 times, and for each permutation, the maximum synergy value across all gene pairs is recorded. The distribution of these 100 maximum synergy values is then fitted to a Gumbel (type-I extreme value) distribution using maximum likelihood estimation, which is the appropriate limiting distribution for the maximum of a large number of independent observations. The cumulative distribution function of the fitted Gumbel distribution is used to compute a P-value for the highest observed synergy in the original, unpermuted dataset, representing the probability of observing such a high synergy value under the null hypothesis that no synergistic interactions exist between genes and the disease phenotype. For the top-ranked gene pair in the prostate cancer dataset, this P-value was found to be less than 10⁻¹⁵ under both permutation strategies, indicating an extremely low likelihood that the observed synergy arose by chance.

To identify a comprehensive set of statistically significant synergistic interactions for inclusion in the final synergy network, a false discovery rate (FDR) correction is applied to account for the multiple comparisons inherent in testing millions of gene pairs. A large set of synergy scores—on the order of 10⁸—is sampled from the permuted datasets, and for each observed synergy score Si in the original dataset, the raw P-value is estimated as the proportion of permuted synergy scores that are greater than or equal to Si. The FDR-adjusted P-value for each gene pair is then computed as the minimum of the scaled raw P-values across all gene pairs ranked in descending order of synergy, using the formula: FDR-adjusted P-value = min(N/j × Pj) for all j ≥ i, where N is the total number of gene pairs, j is the rank of the gene pair, and Pj is the raw P-value of the j-th ranked pair. A threshold of α = 0.05 is applied to the FDR-adjusted P-values, resulting in the retention of only those gene pairs whose synergy is statistically significant after correcting for multiple testing. In the prostate cancer dataset, this procedure yielded 473 significant synergistic interactions under Permutation B, forming the basis of the final synergy network.

The resulting synergy network is represented as an undirected graph, where each node corresponds to a gene and each edge corresponds to a statistically significant synergistic interaction between two genes. The edges are weighted by the magnitude of the synergy score, allowing for the identification of the most robust interactions. The topology of the network reveals the presence of highly connected hub genes, such as RBP1, which exhibit significant synergy with a large number of partner genes, many of which are involved in ribosomal biogenesis, oxidative phosphorylation, and apoptosis regulation. The molecular logic underlying these interactions is consistent: for example, RBP1 is consistently underexpressed in diseased samples, while its synergistic partners are overexpressed, suggesting a cooperative mechanism in which the suppression of RBP1 enables the activation of proliferative pathways through its partners. This pattern is validated in an independent dataset from a different laboratory, where the same qualitative expression patterns are preserved, despite differences in sample size, platform, and normalization protocol, confirming the biological robustness of the findings.

The method further enables the interpretation of biological mechanisms by integrating the synergy network with existing knowledge of gene function. For instance, the enrichment of ribosomal genes among the top synergistic partners of RBP1 supports a model in which the downregulation of RBP1, a known inhibitor of the PI3K/Akt pathway, leads to the activation of mTOR signaling and the subsequent upregulation of translation initiation and elongation factors encoded by 5'-TOP mRNAs. The concurrent identification of genes involved in oxidative stress response, such as ferritin light chain and ubiquinol-cytochrome c reductase hinge protein, suggests that the synergy network captures a coherent biological program in which cellular damage from reactive oxygen species is coupled with the suppression of apoptosis, creating a permissive environment for tumorigenesis. The method thus transforms the synergy network from a mere list of statistically significant interactions into a mechanistic hypothesis generator, guiding experimental validation through targeted perturbation studies, pathway enrichment analysis, or protein-protein interaction mapping.

The method is not limited to gene expression data and may be extended to other types of molecular measurements, including protein abundance, post-translational modifications, DNA methylation, or alternatively spliced isoforms, provided that the data are continuous and annotated with disease status. The same computational framework can be applied to higher-order synergies, such as triplets or quadruplets of genes, by extending the clustering to higher-dimensional spaces and computing multivariate synergy metrics. This extension would enable the identification of multi-gene regulatory modules or protein complexes whose cooperative behavior is uniquely associated with disease, offering even deeper mechanistic insights. Furthermore, the method can be adapted to longitudinal or multi-tissue datasets to identify context-specific synergies that vary across developmental stages, tissue types, or treatment conditions.

The invention also encompasses the use of the synergy network for therapeutic and diagnostic applications. In therapeutic development, the identification of synergistic gene pairs suggests that simultaneous modulation of both genes may be required for effective intervention, as targeting only one member of the pair may be insufficient due to compensatory mechanisms. This insight supports the design of combination therapies that target cooperative nodes in disease pathways. In diagnostics, the synergy network can be used to construct multi-gene signatures that capture cooperative biological logic, offering higher specificity and predictive power than signatures based on additive gene expression changes. The method further enables the discovery of novel biomarkers that are not individually differentially expressed but are functionally critical in combination with other genes, thereby expanding the repertoire of potential diagnostic targets.

In summary, the detailed description of the invention presents a novel, information-theoretic methodology for the identification of disease-specific gene-gene interactions through the quantification of synergy from continuous gene expression data. The method eliminates the need for arbitrary binarization, prior biological knowledge, or heuristic assumptions, and instead relies on a statistically rigorous, dendrogram-based framework that captures cooperative interactions with high sensitivity and specificity. The computational efficiency, scalability, and biological interpretability of the method render it a transformative tool for systems biology, enabling the discovery of functional gene partnerships that drive disease and providing a foundation for precision medicine through combinatorial therapeutic and diagnostic strategies.
