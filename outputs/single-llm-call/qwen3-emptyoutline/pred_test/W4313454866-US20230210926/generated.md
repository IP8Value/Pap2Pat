## BACKGROUND

### Field

The present invention relates to the field of molecular imaging and synthetic biology, specifically to the development and use of genetically encoded acoustic reporter genes that enable non-destructive, high-resolution, deep-tissue ultrasound imaging of living cells in vivo. More particularly, the invention provides novel bacterial and mammalian acoustic reporter gene constructs derived from gas vesicle gene clusters that produce robust, non-linear acoustic contrast when expressed heterologously in bacterial and mammalian cells. These constructs permit the real-time, longitudinal visualization of cellular location, proliferation, gene expression dynamics, and spatial distribution within intact organisms without the need for exogenous contrast agents, destructive imaging protocols, or ionizing radiation. The invention further encompasses methods for utilizing these acoustic reporter genes in therapeutic monitoring, tumor biology, microbiome tracking, and image-guided interventions such as biopsy and targeted ablation. The technology addresses a critical unmet need in biomedical research and clinical translation by enabling the non-invasive, high-fidelity detection of genetically defined cell populations at depths exceeding one centimeter with sub-millimeter resolution, surpassing the capabilities of optical reporters and avoiding the cost, complexity, and radiation exposure associated with nuclear and magnetic resonance imaging modalities.

### Description of the Related Art

Prior art in the field of non-invasive cellular imaging has been dominated by fluorescent proteins, bioluminescent enzymes, and radiolabeled tracers, each of which suffers from significant limitations in depth penetration, spatial resolution, or physiological perturbation. Fluorescent proteins, while widely adopted, are severely limited by light scattering and absorption in biological tissues, restricting their utility to superficial structures or ex vivo applications. Bioluminescent reporters, though sensitive, lack spatial resolution and require substrate administration, limiting their temporal control and quantitative accuracy. Radiolabeled agents, while capable of deep-tissue imaging, involve ionizing radiation, short half-lives, and poor anatomical resolution, necessitating co-registration with computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging for meaningful anatomical context. Magnetic resonance imaging, although capable of high-resolution anatomical imaging, lacks the sensitivity to detect small numbers of genetically labeled cells without the use of iron oxide nanoparticles or other exogenous contrast agents, which often trigger immune responses and do not permit genetic encoding.

Ultrasound imaging has long been recognized as an attractive modality for in vivo imaging due to its real-time capability, portability, low cost, and absence of ionizing radiation. However, conventional ultrasound relies on acoustic impedance mismatches between tissues and lacks molecular specificity. The development of ultrasound contrast agents based on microbubbles has enabled vascular imaging and perfusion assessment, but these agents are transient, non-genetically encoded, and cannot be targeted to specific cell types without complex surface functionalization. Moreover, microbubbles are destroyed during imaging, precluding longitudinal tracking of the same cell population over time.

Attempts to engineer genetically encoded ultrasound reporters have been limited to the expression of gas vesicles—hollow, gas-filled protein nanostructures naturally produced by certain aquatic microorganisms. Early efforts to adapt gas vesicle gene clusters from halophilic archaea and cyanobacteria into Escherichia coli yielded weak, inconsistent, and non-linear acoustic signals that were only detectable using destructive imaging protocols involving high-pressure pulses that collapsed the vesicles. These first-generation bacterial acoustic reporter genes required high cell densities, prolonged induction times, and extensive clonal selection to achieve detectable signal, rendering them unsuitable for dynamic, in vivo applications. Furthermore, the metabolic burden imposed by the expression of large, multi-gene operons often led to plasmid instability, reduced cell fitness, and loss of reporter function over time.

Mammalian adaptations of gas vesicle technology faced even greater challenges. The native gas vesicle gene clusters from prokaryotes are not naturally compatible with eukaryotic transcriptional and translational machinery. Attempts to express these clusters in mammalian cells resulted in minimal or no gas vesicle formation, and where vesicles were detected, they produced only linear acoustic scattering, indistinguishable from background tissue. To overcome this, prior art required the use of epigenetic modifiers such as histone deacetylase inhibitors to enhance expression, which introduced non-specific cellular effects and compromised the fidelity of the reporter. Additionally, the large size of the gene clusters—often exceeding 20 kilobases—precluded efficient viral delivery, limiting their application to transient transfection or labor-intensive stable cell line generation.

The field has thus lacked a robust, genetically encoded ultrasound reporter system capable of producing strong, non-linear acoustic contrast in both bacterial and mammalian cells under physiological conditions, without requiring destructive imaging, exogenous contrast agents, or pharmacological manipulation. No prior art has demonstrated the ability to non-destructively image the microscale distribution of bacteria colonizing deep tumors, to longitudinally monitor gene expression dynamics in developing tumors over multiple days, or to guide real-time biopsy procedures based on genetically defined cell populations using ultrasound. The inability to achieve these capabilities has hindered the translation of cell-based therapies, limited the study of tumor heterogeneity, and restricted the utility of ultrasound as a molecular imaging platform. There remains a critical need for a new class of acoustic reporter genes that combine high sensitivity, genetic stability, low metabolic burden, and compatibility with both prokaryotic and eukaryotic hosts, enabling a new paradigm in non-invasive, high-resolution, longitudinal cellular imaging.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides novel genetically encoded acoustic reporter genes that enable non-destructive, high-sensitivity, deep-tissue ultrasound imaging of genetically modified cells in vivo. The invention comprises two distinct classes of acoustic reporter constructs: a bacterial acoustic reporter gene designated bARG Ser, derived from a genomic mining and optimization effort targeting the gas vesicle gene cluster of Serratia sp. 39006, and a mammalian acoustic reporter gene designated mARG Ana, derived from the gas vesicle gene cluster of Anabaena flos-aquae. Both constructs produce strong, non-linear acoustic contrast when expressed in their respective host cells, enabling detection at depths exceeding one centimeter with sub-millimeter resolution using standard clinical ultrasound systems equipped with non-linear imaging pulse sequences.

The bacterial acoustic reporter gene, bARG Ser, comprises a modified gas vesicle gene cluster from Serratia sp. 39006 in which the hypothetical open reading frame Ser39006_001280 has been deleted, resulting in enhanced gas vesicle production and non-linear acoustic signal intensity. This construct is operably linked to an L-arabinose-inducible promoter and includes a toxin-antitoxin stabilization cassette (Axe-Txe) to ensure plasmid retention in the absence of antibiotic selection. When expressed in the probiotic bacterium Escherichia coli Nissle 1917, bARG Ser enables the non-destructive, longitudinal visualization of bacterial colonization within solid tumors at depths greater than one centimeter, with signal intensity proportional to cell density and detectable as early as seven hours post-induction. The construct exhibits exceptional genetic stability, with less than 0.3% of colonies exhibiting loss of function after 35 generations of growth without selection, and produces a 9-fold increase in non-linear ultrasound signal compared to previously reported bacterial acoustic reporter genes.

The mammalian acoustic reporter gene, mARG Ana, comprises a polycistronic cassette encoding the gas vesicle assembly factors gvpN, gvpJ, gvpK, gvpF, gvpG, gvpW, and gvpV, linked via P2A self-cleaving peptides and driven by a doxycycline-inducible promoter, and a separate plasmid encoding the structural protein gvpA under a cytomegalovirus promoter. Notably, the gvpC gene, which encodes a reinforcing protein that dampens non-linear acoustic scattering, is omitted from the construct, thereby enhancing the deformability of the gas vesicles under acoustic pressure and increasing non-linear signal intensity by more than 38-fold compared to prior mammalian constructs. When stably integrated into human breast cancer cells, mouse fibroblasts, and human hepatocytes, mARG Ana enables the non-destructive, longitudinal imaging of gene expression patterns within orthotopic tumors, with signal detectable as early as one day post-induction and increasing over six days. The signal is spatially restricted to viable tumor regions and absent in necrotic cores, enabling precise mapping of viable tumor cell populations. The construct permits detection of as few as 30,000 cells per milliliter using non-linear ultrasound and enables real-time, ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration biopsies targeted specifically to genetically labeled cells.

The invention further encompasses methods for imaging and therapeutic monitoring using these acoustic reporter genes. These methods include the intravenous administration of bacteria expressing bARG Ser to colonize tumors, followed by non-destructive ultrasound imaging to visualize bacterial distribution and quantify colonization dynamics over time. The invention further includes the implantation of mammalian cells expressing mARG Ana into animal models, followed by doxycycline-induced expression and longitudinal ultrasound imaging to monitor tumor growth, response to therapy, and spatial heterogeneity. The invention further enables image-guided biopsy procedures by using non-linear ultrasound to identify regions of high mARG Ana expression and directing needle placement to those regions, thereby enabling the isolation of genetically defined cell subpopulations for downstream molecular analysis.

The invention represents a paradigm shift in molecular imaging by providing the first genetically encoded acoustic reporter systems capable of non-destructive, longitudinal, high-resolution imaging in both bacterial and mammalian contexts without requiring exogenous contrast agents, destructive imaging protocols, or pharmacological enhancers. The invention enables the direct visualization of cellular behavior in vivo with unprecedented fidelity, opening new avenues for monitoring cell-based therapies, studying tumor biology, tracking the microbiome, and guiding precision interventions.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Definitions

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms shall have the meanings ascribed herein. The term “acoustic reporter gene” refers to a nucleic acid sequence encoding one or more proteins that, when expressed in a host cell, result in the formation of gas vesicles capable of producing non-linear acoustic contrast under ultrasound imaging, thereby enabling the detection and localization of the host cell in vivo without the use of exogenous contrast agents. The term “gas vesicle” refers to a hollow, proteinaceous, gas-filled nanostructure produced by certain prokaryotic organisms, which imparts buoyancy and, when heterologously expressed, generates non-linear acoustic scattering due to its compressible gas core and rigid protein shell. The term “non-linear acoustic contrast” refers to the generation of harmonic or subharmonic frequencies in an ultrasound signal that arise from the non-proportional response of a material to acoustic pressure, as opposed to linear scattering, which is proportional to incident pressure and characteristic of most biological tissues. Non-linear contrast is detectable using specialized pulse sequences such as cross-propagating amplitude modulation (xAM) and is indicative of the presence of gas vesicles. The term “non-destructive imaging” refers to ultrasound imaging techniques that do not cause irreversible collapse or destruction of gas vesicles during signal acquisition, thereby permitting repeated imaging of the same cell population over time. The term “bacterial acoustic reporter gene” refers to an acoustic reporter gene designed for expression in prokaryotic hosts such as Escherichia coli, Salmonella enterica, or other genetically engineered bacteria, and comprising a gas vesicle gene cluster derived from a prokaryotic source. The term “mammalian acoustic reporter gene” refers to an acoustic reporter gene designed for expression in eukaryotic mammalian cells, including but not limited to human, mouse, rat, or rabbit cell lines, and comprising a modified gas vesicle gene cluster optimized for eukaryotic transcription, translation, and post-translational processing. The term “operably linked” refers to the functional connection between a promoter and a coding sequence such that the promoter directs the transcription of the coding sequence in the host cell. The term “plasmid” refers to a circular, extrachromosomal DNA molecule capable of autonomous replication in a host cell, and may be maintained episomally or integrated into the host genome. The term “stable cell line” refers to a population of mammalian cells in which the acoustic reporter gene has been integrated into the genome and is expressed constitutively or inducibly across multiple generations without requiring selection pressure. The term “inducible promoter” refers to a DNA sequence that drives gene expression only in the presence of a specific chemical inducer, such as L-arabinose or doxycycline, enabling temporal control of reporter expression. The term “toxin-antitoxin stabilization cassette” refers to a genetic module comprising a stable toxin and a labile antitoxin, such that plasmid loss results in degradation of the antitoxin and subsequent activation of the toxin, thereby selecting against plasmid-free cells. The term “P2A self-cleaving peptide” refers to a 19–22 amino acid sequence derived from porcine teschovirus-1 that mediates co-translational cleavage between adjacent proteins, enabling the expression of multiple proteins from a single open reading frame. The term “IRES” refers to an internal ribosome entry site, a structured RNA element that permits cap-independent translation initiation. The term “WPRE-hGH polyA” refers to a chimeric 3’ untranslated region comprising the woodchuck hepatitis virus posttranscriptional regulatory element and the human growth hormone polyadenylation signal, which enhances mRNA stability and translational efficiency in mammalian cells. The term “xAM” refers to cross-propagating amplitude modulation, a non-linear ultrasound imaging pulse sequence that enhances signals from non-linear contrast agents while suppressing linear background scattering. The term “BURST” refers to a destructive ultrasound imaging technique that employs high-pressure pulses to collapse gas vesicles and detect the resulting transient acoustic signature. The term “transmitted acoustic pressure” refers to the peak negative pressure, measured in megapascals, applied to tissue by an ultrasound transducer during imaging. The term “signal-to-background ratio” refers to the ratio of the mean acoustic intensity detected from a region of interest containing gas vesicle-expressing cells to the mean acoustic intensity detected from adjacent tissue lacking such expression. The term “orthotopic tumor” refers to a tumor implanted in the anatomical location corresponding to its tissue of origin, such as breast cancer cells implanted into the mammary fat pad. The term “colonization” refers to the stable, persistent presence of bacteria within a host tissue, such as a tumor, over multiple days, without causing acute host toxicity. The term “microscale distribution” refers to the spatial arrangement of cells at the level of individual cells or small cell clusters, resolvable by ultrasound imaging with sub-millimeter resolution. The term “genetically defined cell population” refers to a subset of cells within a heterogeneous tissue that express a specific transgene, such as an acoustic reporter gene, enabling their selective identification and isolation.

### Acoustic Reporter Genes for Nondestructive In Vivo Imaging

The present invention provides novel acoustic reporter genes that enable the non-destructive, longitudinal, and high-resolution ultrasound imaging of genetically modified cells in vivo. These reporter genes are derived from gas vesicle gene clusters originally identified in diverse prokaryotic species and have been systematically optimized through genomic mining, genetic deletion, promoter engineering, and stoichiometric tuning to achieve unprecedented levels of non-linear acoustic contrast in both bacterial and mammalian hosts. The invention is grounded in the discovery that the gas vesicle gene cluster from Serratia sp. 39006, when modified by the deletion of a hypothetical open reading frame designated Ser39006_001280, produces a 9-fold increase in non-linear ultrasound signal intensity compared to previously reported bacterial acoustic reporter genes. This modified construct, designated bARG Ser, enables the robust and stable expression of gas vesicles in Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 under physiological conditions, facilitating the non-destructive visualization of bacterial colonization within tumors at depths exceeding one centimeter. The invention further provides a mammalian acoustic reporter gene, designated mARG Ana, derived from the gas vesicle gene cluster of Anabaena flos-aquae, which produces a 38-fold increase in non-linear ultrasound signal compared to prior mammalian constructs when the gvpC gene is omitted. This construct enables the detection of as few as 30,000 mammalian cells per milliliter using non-linear ultrasound and permits the longitudinal monitoring of gene expression patterns in orthotopic tumors over multiple days without the need for exogenous contrast agents or destructive imaging protocols.

The bacterial acoustic reporter gene, bARG Ser, comprises a modified gas vesicle gene cluster originating from Serratia sp. 39006, wherein the open reading frame Ser39006_001280 has been deleted. This deletion was identified through a systematic screening of deletion variants of the native Serratia gene cluster, wherein the removal of Ser39006_001280 resulted in a significant enhancement of both gas vesicle formation and non-linear acoustic signal intensity, as confirmed by phase-contrast microscopy and transmission electron microscopy. The resulting construct includes the genes gvpA, gvpB, gvpD, gvpE, gvpF, gvpG, gvpH, gvpI, gvpJ, gvpK, gvpL, gvpM, gvpN, gvpV, gvpW, gvpX, gvpY, and gvpZ, arranged in their native operonic structure, with the exception of the deletion of Ser39006_001280. This gene cluster is operably linked to the L-arabinose-inducible pBAD promoter, which provides tight transcriptional control and minimal metabolic burden at 37°C, the physiological temperature of mammalian hosts. To ensure plasmid retention in the absence of antibiotic selection, the construct further includes the Axe-Txe toxin-antitoxin stabilization cassette, which functions by expressing a stable toxin protein and a labile antitoxin protein; plasmid loss results in the degradation of the antitoxin and subsequent activation of the toxin, thereby eliminating plasmid-free cells. The entire construct is maintained on a plasmid backbone with a ColE1 origin of replication and a chloramphenicol resistance gene for selection during construction and initial transformation.

The mammalian acoustic reporter gene, mARG Ana, comprises two separate plasmids designed for co-expression in mammalian cells. The first plasmid encodes the gas vesicle structural protein gvpA, under the control of a cytomegalovirus immediate early promoter, and includes a WPRE-hGH polyadenylation signal to enhance mRNA stability and translational efficiency. The second plasmid encodes a polycistronic cassette comprising the gas vesicle assembly factor genes gvpN, gvpJ, gvpK, gvpF, gvpG, gvpW, and gvpV, linked in-frame via P2A self-cleaving peptides to enable co-translational processing and equimolar expression of each protein. This cassette is driven by a doxycycline-inducible TRE3G promoter, which allows for precise temporal control of expression in response to low concentrations of doxycycline. The polycistronic cassette further includes an Emerald GFP coding sequence fused in-frame downstream of gvpV via a P2A linker, enabling fluorescent co-detection and fluorescence-activated cell sorting of successfully transfected cells. The gvpC gene, which encodes a minor structural protein known to reinforce the gas vesicle shell and dampen non-linear acoustic scattering, is intentionally omitted from the construct to enhance the deformability of the gas vesicles under acoustic pressure, thereby maximizing non-linear signal generation. The entire polycistronic cassette is flanked by WPRE-hGH polyadenylation signals to enhance transcript stability. The gvpA plasmid further includes an internal ribosome entry site (IRES) upstream of the EBFP2 fluorescent protein, enabling independent translation of EBFP2 and facilitating dual-color fluorescence-based selection of cells co-expressing both gvpA and the assembly factors.

The invention further encompasses the use of these constructs in a variety of host cells. The bacterial acoustic reporter gene, bARG Ser, is particularly effective when expressed in Escherichia coli Nissle 1917, a probiotic strain known for its ability to colonize the gastrointestinal tract and infiltrate solid tumors. However, the construct is also functional in other bacterial species, including attenuated strains of Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium, demonstrating broad applicability across genetically engineered bacterial platforms. The mammalian acoustic reporter gene, mARG Ana, is functional in a variety of human and murine cell lines, including MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells, 3T3 fibroblasts, and HuH7 hepatocytes, indicating that the construct is not restricted to a single cell type and can be adapted for use in diverse cancer models, regenerative therapies, and cell tracking applications. The invention further includes the use of PiggyBac transposon systems for genomic integration of the mARG Ana constructs, enabling the generation of stable, non-clonal cell lines without the need for prolonged antibiotic selection or clonal expansion. The use of PiggyBac transposase allows for efficient, single-copy integration of the reporter gene into the host genome, resulting in consistent, long-term expression across multiple cell divisions.

The non-linear acoustic contrast generated by both bARG Ser and mARG Ana is detectable using standard clinical ultrasound systems equipped with non-linear imaging pulse sequences such as cross-propagating amplitude modulation (xAM). Unlike previous acoustic reporter genes that required destructive imaging techniques such as BURST, which rely on the irreversible collapse of gas vesicles to generate signal, the constructs of the present invention produce strong, persistent non-linear signals that can be imaged repeatedly over time without loss of reporter function. This enables longitudinal tracking of bacterial colonization dynamics, tumor growth kinetics, and gene expression patterns in living animals over periods of days to weeks. The signal intensity is directly proportional to cell density, with detection limits as low as 10^7 bacterial cells per milliliter and 30,000 mammalian cells per milliliter, surpassing the sensitivity of previous acoustic reporter systems by orders of magnitude. The spatial resolution of the imaging is sub-millimeter, enabling the visualization of individual bacterial microcolonies within tumors and the delineation of viable tumor regions from necrotic cores. The invention further enables the re-expression of gas vesicles following their collapse, permitting multiple rounds of imaging and intervention on the same subject, a capability previously unattainable with any acoustic reporter system.

The invention further provides a method for the identification and optimization of novel gas vesicle gene clusters through genomic mining of diverse prokaryotic species. The discovery of bARG Ser and mARG Ana was enabled by the systematic cloning and expression of 15 distinct gas vesicle gene clusters from 11 phylogenetically diverse species, including halophiles, thermophiles, and mesophiles, followed by high-throughput screening using non-linear ultrasound imaging. This approach revealed that only a subset of gene clusters, including those from Serratia sp. 39006 and Anabaena flos-aquae, produced detectable non-linear contrast when expressed heterologously, and that subtle modifications such as gene deletion and stoichiometric tuning could dramatically enhance signal intensity. The invention thus provides a blueprint for the discovery of next-generation acoustic reporter genes by applying similar mining and engineering strategies to other gas vesicle-producing organisms, including those not previously known to produce gas vesicles, thereby expanding the potential toolkit for molecular imaging.

### Methods of Imaging and Treatment

The present invention provides novel methods for the non-destructive, longitudinal, and high-resolution imaging of genetically modified cells in vivo using the acoustic reporter genes bARG Ser and mARG Ana. These methods enable the visualization of cellular location, proliferation, and gene expression dynamics in living organisms without the need for exogenous contrast agents, ionizing radiation, or destructive imaging protocols. The methods are applicable to both bacterial and mammalian systems and are particularly suited for applications in cancer biology, cell-based therapy monitoring, microbiome tracking, and image-guided interventions.

In the context of bacterial imaging, the method comprises the intravenous administration of a suspension of bacteria expressing the bARG Ser acoustic reporter gene into a subject harboring a solid tumor. The bacteria are administered at a dose sufficient to achieve colonization of the tumor, typically ranging from 10^7 to 10^9 colony-forming units per animal, depending on tumor size and model. Following administration, the subject is allowed to recover for a period of at least 48 hours to permit bacterial homing and colonization of the tumor microenvironment, particularly the necrotic core, which provides an immune-privileged niche favorable for bacterial persistence. At this point, the expression of bARG Ser is induced by the systemic administration of L-arabinose, typically via intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 100–200 mg per kilogram of body weight. Within 7 to 9 hours following induction, non-linear ultrasound imaging is performed using a clinical or research-grade ultrasound system equipped with a high-frequency transducer operating at frequencies between 10 and 20 MHz and a cross-propagating amplitude modulation (xAM) pulse sequence. The imaging is performed at an acoustic pressure of 1.29 MPa, which is sufficient to generate maximal non-linear signal while minimizing gas vesicle collapse. The resulting ultrasound images reveal bright, spatially localized acoustic contrast corresponding to the distribution of bacterial microcolonies within the tumor, with signal intensity proportional to bacterial density. The imaging can be repeated at intervals of 24 to 72 hours to monitor changes in bacterial colonization over time, and the same subject may be imaged multiple times over a period of up to 14 days without loss of signal fidelity.

In the context of mammalian imaging, the method comprises the implantation of mammalian cells expressing the mARG Ana acoustic reporter gene into a subject, typically via subcutaneous or orthotopic injection. The cells are administered at a density sufficient to form a detectable tumor, typically ranging from 10^5 to 10^6 cells per injection site. Following implantation, the subject is maintained under standard conditions until tumor growth is established, typically over a period of 7 to 14 days. At this point, gene expression is induced by the systemic administration of doxycycline, typically via intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 100–200 µg per kilogram of body weight, administered daily for a period of 1 to 6 days. Non-linear ultrasound imaging is performed using a clinical or research-grade ultrasound system equipped with a high-frequency transducer operating at frequencies between 10 and 20 MHz and a cross-propagating amplitude modulation (xAM) pulse sequence. The imaging is performed at an acoustic pressure of 0.42 MPa, which is sufficient to generate maximal non-linear signal while maintaining gas vesicle stability over time. The resulting ultrasound images reveal bright, spatially localized acoustic contrast corresponding to the distribution of viable tumor cells, with signal intensity proportional to the number of mARG Ana-expressing cells. The imaging can be repeated at intervals of 24 to 72 hours to monitor tumor growth, response to therapy, and spatial heterogeneity, and the same subject may be imaged multiple times over a period of up to 21 days without loss of signal fidelity.

The invention further provides methods for image-guided interventions, including biopsy and targeted ablation. In the context of biopsy, the method comprises the generation of a chimeric tumor in a subject by the co-implantation of mARG Ana-expressing cells and non-expressing control cells at adjacent sites. Following tumor establishment and induction of mARG Ana expression, the subject is imaged using non-linear ultrasound to generate a three-dimensional map of acoustic signal distribution. Using this map, a fine needle aspiration biopsy is performed under real-time ultrasound guidance, with the needle tip directed to regions of high acoustic signal intensity corresponding to mARG Ana-expressing cells or to regions of low signal intensity corresponding to non-expressing cells. The aspirated material is then processed for downstream analysis, including flow cytometry, genomic sequencing, or transcriptomic profiling, to confirm the genetic identity of the collected cells. The method enables the isolation of genetically defined cell subpopulations with a success rate exceeding 90%, allowing for precise sampling of tumor heterogeneity without the need for invasive surgical resection.

In the context of targeted ablation, the method comprises the administration of a subject with a tumor containing mARG Ana-expressing cells, followed by induction of reporter expression and non-linear ultrasound imaging to identify regions of high reporter activity. A focused ultrasound transducer is then used to deliver localized, high-intensity acoustic energy to these regions, resulting in thermal or mechanical ablation of the mARG Ana-expressing cells. The non-destructive nature of the acoustic reporter enables pre-treatment mapping of target regions and post-treatment verification of ablation efficacy, allowing for precise, image-guided tumor destruction. The invention further enables the use of the acoustic reporter gene as a therapeutic reporter, wherein the expression of the reporter gene is linked to the expression of a therapeutic protein, such as a cytokine, toxin, or immune modulator, enabling simultaneous monitoring of therapeutic delivery and biological effect.

The invention further provides methods for the quantification of bacterial colonization and mammalian cell burden using acoustic signal intensity. The signal-to-background ratio measured by non-linear ultrasound imaging is linearly correlated with cell density over a wide dynamic range, from 10^7 to 2×10^9 bacterial cells per milliliter and from 3×10^4 to 1×10^6 mammalian cells per milliliter. This relationship enables the use of ultrasound imaging as a quantitative tool for monitoring therapeutic efficacy, tumor burden, or bacterial load without the need for euthanasia, tissue harvesting, or ex vivo assays. The invention further provides methods for the validation of reporter expression using complementary imaging modalities, including fluorescence microscopy, transmission electron microscopy, and histological staining, to confirm the presence of gas vesicles and the absence of off-target effects. The invention further provides methods for the multiplexing of multiple acoustic reporter genes by engineering variants of the gas vesicle shell proteins that respond differently to acoustic pressure, thereby enabling the simultaneous imaging of multiple genetically distinct cell populations within the same subject.

The invention further provides methods for the delivery of the acoustic reporter genes using viral vectors, including adeno-associated virus, lentivirus, and adenovirus, for the transduction of endogenous cells in vivo. The modified mARG Ana construct, with its reduced size and optimized regulatory elements, is amenable to packaging into viral vectors with limited cargo capacity, enabling the direct in vivo delivery of the reporter gene to target tissues such as the brain, liver, or pancreas. This approach permits the non-invasive imaging of endogenous cell populations, such as neurons, hepatocytes, or immune cells, without the need for ex vivo manipulation or transplantation. The invention further provides methods for the regulation of reporter expression using synthetic promoters responsive to endogenous biological signals, such as hypoxia, inflammation, or metabolic stress, thereby enabling the imaging of cellular states rather than simply cell location. The invention thus represents a transformative platform for non-invasive, longitudinal, and molecularly specific imaging of biological processes in vivo, with broad applicability across biomedical research and clinical medicine.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The following example demonstrates the successful generation, characterization, and in vivo application of the bacterial acoustic reporter gene bARG Ser and the mammalian acoustic reporter gene mARG Ana as described herein. The example is presented in a stepwise manner to illustrate the full scope of the invention, including the design, construction, validation, and application of the acoustic reporter genes in both bacterial and mammalian systems.

The bacterial acoustic reporter gene, bARG Ser, was constructed by first identifying the native gas vesicle gene cluster from Serratia sp. 39006 through genomic mining of publicly available sequence databases. The cluster, comprising 18 genes including gvpA through gvpZ and an unannotated hypothetical protein designated Ser39006_001280, was amplified from genomic DNA using high-fidelity PCR and assembled into a pET28a(+) vector using Gibson assembly. A series of deletion variants were generated by removing individual genes or combinations of genes, and each variant was transformed into Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) for expression testing. The patches of cells expressing each variant were imaged using a home-built robotic ultrasound imaging apparatus equipped with a cross-propagating amplitude modulation (xAM) pulse sequence. The variant lacking Ser39006_001280 exhibited a 9-fold increase in xAM signal intensity compared to the wild-type cluster, as well as increased optical opacity under phase-contrast microscopy, indicating enhanced gas vesicle production. This variant was designated bARG Ser and was transferred into Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 using electroporation. To ensure plasmid retention without antibiotic selection, the Axe-Txe toxin-antitoxin stabilization cassette was inserted downstream of the promoter, resulting in the final construct pBAD-bARG Ser-AxeTxe.

The construct was validated for stability by serial passaging of the bacteria in liquid culture for seven days in the absence of chloramphenicol. Colony-forming unit counts on selective and non-selective media demonstrated that greater than 99.7% of cells retained the plasmid after 35 generations of growth. The expression of bARG Ser was induced using L-arabinose at concentrations ranging from 0.001% to 1%, and non-linear ultrasound imaging revealed detectable signal at concentrations as low as 0.001%, with maximal signal achieved at 0.1%. The signal was detectable as early as seven hours post-induction and plateaued by 24 hours. The detection limit of the system was determined to be 10^7 cells per milliliter, and the signal was linearly proportional to cell density over a range of four orders of magnitude. Transmission electron microscopy confirmed the presence of gas vesicles within the bacterial cells, with an average diameter of approximately 150 nanometers and a uniform distribution throughout the cytoplasm.

The mammalian acoustic reporter gene, mARG Ana, was constructed by synthesizing the gas vesicle assembly factor genes gvpN, gvpJ, gvpK, gvpF, gvpG, gvpW, and gvpV from Anabaena flos-aquae, codon-optimized for human expression, and assembling them into a single polycistronic cassette linked by P2A self-cleaving peptides. The cassette was cloned into a PiggyBac transposon vector under the control of a doxycycline-inducible TRE3G promoter and fused to an Emerald GFP reporter via a P2A linker. The structural gene gvpA was cloned separately into a second PiggyBac vector under the control of a cytomegalovirus promoter and fused to an IRES-EBFP2 reporter. The two plasmids were co-transfected into HEK293T cells, and the resulting non-linear ultrasound signal was measured using xAM imaging. Omission of gvpC from the construct resulted in a 38.8-fold increase in xAM signal compared to constructs containing gvpC. The signal was detectable at acoustic pressures as low as 0.31 MPa and increased with doxycycline concentration up to 1 µg/mL. The construct was then stably integrated into MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells using PiggyBac transposase and sorted for triple-positive expression of EBFP2, Emerald GFP, and Antares. The resulting polyclonal cell line exhibited strong, inducible non-linear ultrasound signal that increased over six days of doxycycline induction and was detectable in vivo in orthotopic tumors at depths exceeding one centimeter.

The bARG Ser-expressing Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 strain was intravenously injected into mice bearing subcutaneous MC26 tumors. Three days after injection, L-arabinose was administered intraperitoneally, and ultrasound imaging performed 24 hours later revealed strong, localized xAM contrast within the tumor core, corresponding to bacterial colonization. The same tumors were imaged again after 24 hours of collapse and re-induction, demonstrating re-expression of gas vesicles and confirming the non-destructive nature of the imaging. Histological analysis confirmed the presence of bacterial microcolonies at the interface between viable and necrotic tissue, and colony-forming unit counts demonstrated stable colonization of over 10^9 CFU per gram of tumor tissue. The mARG Ana-expressing MDA-MB-231 cells were implanted bilaterally into the mammary fat pads of immunocompromised mice. Doxycycline was administered daily, and ultrasound imaging performed over nine days revealed progressive increases in xAM signal intensity, with signal restricted to viable tumor regions and absent in necrotic cores. Three-dimensional reconstruction of the signal enabled visualization of tumor architecture and heterogeneity. Biopsy procedures were performed under real-time ultrasound guidance, targeting either high-signal or low-signal regions of the tumor, and flow cytometry confirmed that over 95% of aspirated cells matched the expected reporter expression profile.

These results demonstrate the successful development and application of the bARG Ser and mARG Ana acoustic reporter genes as described herein, providing a robust, non-destructive, and highly sensitive platform for in vivo imaging of genetically modified cells in both bacterial and mammalian contexts.

## Materials and Methods

### Genomic Mining of ARG Clusters

A comprehensive literature search was conducted to identify all prokaryotic species reported to produce gas vesicles, using search terms including “gas vesicle,” “gas vacuole,” “aerotope,” and “aerotype.” From this search, a list of 288 species was compiled, each with at least one published report of gas vesicle production. For each species, the 16S rRNA gene sequence was retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database using a custom Python script to ensure phylogenetic accuracy and to avoid reliance on potentially misannotated genome assemblies. A multiple sequence alignment of these 16S rRNA sequences was constructed using Clustal Omega, and a phylogenetic tree was generated using ClustalW2 and visualized using Evolview to guide the selection of representative species spanning diverse taxonomic groups, including halophiles, thermophiles, and mesophiles. From this phylogenetic framework, 15 distinct gas vesicle gene clusters were selected for cloning, including those from Serratia sp. 39006, Anabaena flos-aquae, Bacillus megaterium, Desulfobacterium vacuolatum, and other species with previously uncharacterized clusters. The gene clusters were identified in genomic sequences using a combination of homology searches against known gvp gene sequences and manual annotation of open reading frames based on conserved domain architecture. Clusters were selected for testing only if they contained a minimum of 10 gvp genes and were contiguous within the genome, with fewer than five non-gvp genes present, to minimize metabolic burden. Clusters from species with multiple gas vesicle operons, such as Halobacterium salinarum, were cloned and tested separately. Where genome sequences contained discrepancies, such as duplicated gvpA genes, the sequence was verified by PCR amplification from genomic DNA of the original strain, and the correct copy was used for cloning. All gene clusters were amplified using high-fidelity Q5 polymerase and assembled into a pET28a(+) vector using Gibson assembly. The resulting constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing to ensure fidelity of the cloned sequences.

### Bacterial Plasmid Construction and Molecular Biology

All gas vesicle gene clusters were cloned into the pET28a(+) vector (Novagen) using Gibson assembly, with the exception of constructs intended for expression in Escherichia coli Nissle 1917, which were transferred into a pBAD-based plasmid with a ColE1 origin of replication and a chloramphenicol resistance marker. The pBAD promoter was selected for its tight regulation and low basal expression at 37°C. The Axe-Txe toxin-antitoxin cassette was amplified from a previously characterized plasmid and inserted downstream of the promoter using Gibson assembly. All plasmids were transformed into NEB Stable E. coli for propagation and verified by restriction digest and Sanger sequencing. For expression in Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium, the bARG Ser construct was transferred into a pACYC184-based vector with a kanamycin resistance marker. All plasmid transformations were performed using electrocompetent cells, with 1.5 µL of purified plasmid DNA used per transformation. Electroporation was carried out using a Gene Pulser Xcell system (Bio-Rad) with a 0.1 cm cuvette and a 1.8 kV pulse. Transformants were selected on LB agar plates containing the appropriate antibiotic and incubated at 37°C for 16–24 hours. Plasmid DNA was extracted using a commercial miniprep kit and quantified by spectrophotometry. All constructs were verified by sequencing of the entire insert before use in any downstream experiment.

### In Vitro Bacterial Expression of ARGs

For initial screening, all constructs were expressed in Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) by transforming electrocompetent cells with purified plasmid DNA and plating on dual-layer LB agar plates. The bottom layer contained 100 µg/mL kanamycin and varying concentrations of IPTG (0–1%) to induce expression, while the top layer contained 1% glucose to repress the PlacUV5 promoter and prevent leaky expression. Plates were dried at 37°C for 24 hours to remove condensation and then incubated at 37°C for 24 hours or 30°C for 48 hours to allow patch formation. For expression in Escherichia coli Nissle 1917, glycerol stocks were used to inoculate overnight cultures in LB medium supplemented with 25 µg/mL chloramphenicol and 1% glucose. The next day, cultures were diluted to an OD600 of 0.1–0.3 and induced with L-arabinose at concentrations ranging from 0.001% to 1%. For solid media expression, 1 µL of overnight culture was spotted onto dual-layer LB agar plates containing 50 µg/mL chloramphenicol and 0.2% L-arabinose. For liquid culture expression, cultures were grown in baffled flasks at 37°C and 250 rpm, and samples were taken at 0, 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, and 24 hours post-induction for optical density and ultrasound measurements. All cultures were prescreened for gas vesicle formation using phase-contrast microscopy before ultrasound imaging. For plasmid stability assays, cultures were passaged daily for seven days in the presence or absence of chloramphenicol and 0.1% L-arabinose, and colony-forming unit counts were performed on selective and non-selective media to determine plasmid retention rates.

### In Vitro Ultrasound Imaging of Bacteria Expressing ARGs on Solid Media

Bacterial patches grown on dual-layer LB agar plates were imaged using a home-built robotic ultrasound imaging apparatus equipped with a 15 MHz transducer and a cross-propagating amplitude modulation (xAM) pulse sequence. The apparatus was calibrated using a hydrophone to ensure consistent acoustic pressure delivery, and the transducer was mounted on a motorized stage capable of precise x-y-z movement. Each patch was imaged at an acoustic pressure of 1.74 MPa, and the resulting signal was processed to calculate the signal-to-background ratio (SBR) by comparing the mean intensity of the patch to the mean intensity of adjacent agar. The SBR values were averaged across six biological replicates, and statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t-test. Images were acquired in real-time and stored as raw radiofrequency data for offline analysis. Phase-contrast microscopy was performed on scraped patches suspended in PBS at an OD600 of 1–2 using a Zeiss Axiocam microscope with a ×40 Ph2 objective to confirm gas vesicle presence.

### In Vitro Ultrasound Imaging of Bacteria Expressing ARGs Suspended in Agarose Phantoms

To standardize cell density for quantitative comparisons, bacterial cultures were centrifuged, washed four times in phosphate-buffered saline, and resuspended in 1% low-melt agarose at a concentration of 5×10^9 cells per milliliter. The suspension was pipetted into wells of a 96-well plate pre-filled with 1% agarose in PBS and allowed to solidify at 4°C. The phantoms were imaged using the same robotic ultrasound apparatus and xAM pulse sequence as described above, with acoustic pressures ranging from 0.72 MPa to 1.74 MPa. Signal-to-background ratios were calculated for each phantom, and the data were normalized to the mean signal of a negative control phantom containing uninduced bacteria. The detection limit was determined as the lowest cell concentration at which the SBR exceeded three standard deviations above background. All measurements were performed in triplicate, with each biological replicate consisting of two technical replicates.

### Microscopy of Bacteria

For transmission electron microscopy, bacterial cells expressing gas vesicles were diluted to an OD600 of approximately 1 in 10 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.5) and applied to glow-discharged formvar/carbon-coated copper grids for one minute. Grids were washed three times in HEPES buffer, blotted dry, and air-dried. Images were acquired using a Tecnai T12 transmission electron microscope operated at 120 kV and equipped with a Gatan Ultrascan CCD camera. For phase-contrast microscopy, bacterial cells were scraped from agar plates, resuspended in PBS at an OD600 of 1–2, and placed on glass slides. Images were acquired using a Zeiss Axiocam microscope with a ×40 Ph2 objective. All images were processed using Fiji (ImageJ) software for brightness and contrast adjustment, and scale bars were added using the built-in measurement tool.

### In Vivo Bacterial ARG Expression and Ultrasound Imaging

All animal experiments were conducted under protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the California Institute of Technology. Female Balb/cJ mice aged 6–8 weeks were injected subcutaneously with 5×10^6 MC26 tumor cells in 100 µL of PBS. Tumors were allowed to grow for 14 days until reaching a volume of 200–300 mm³. At this point, 10^8 colony-forming units of Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 expressing bARG Ser were injected intravenously via the lateral tail vein. Three days after bacterial injection, mice were injected intraperitoneally with 120 mg of L-arabinose to induce gas vesicle expression. Ultrasound imaging was performed 24 hours later using a 15 MHz transducer and the xAM pulse sequence at 1.29 MPa. After imaging, the entire tumor was exposed to 3.0 MPa acoustic pressure for 30 seconds to collapse gas vesicles. Mice were then re-injected with 120 mg of L-arabinose, and imaging was repeated 24 hours later to assess re-expression. Tumors were harvested on day 20, homogenized, serially diluted, and plated on selective media to quantify bacterial colonization. All imaging was performed under isoflurane anesthesia, and body temperature was maintained at 37°C using a heating pad.

### Histology of Tumors Colonized by Bacteria

Tumors colonized by bARG Ser-expressing bacteria were harvested 24 hours after L-arabinose induction and imaging. Tumors were fixed in 10% buffered formalin for 48 hours, washed in 70% ethanol, and embedded in paraffin. Sections were cut at 5 µm thickness and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for general histology. Immunohistochemistry was performed using a polyclonal rabbit anti-E. coli antibody (ViroStat, 1001) and an Opal 520 polymer anti-rabbit HRP antibody (Akoya Biosciences), with DAPI counterstaining. Slides were imaged using a Leica Aperio VERSA high-throughput scanner with 40-µm resolution. Fluorescence images were processed using ImageJ software to quantify bacterial signal intensity and spatial distribution relative to tumor morphology.

### Mammalian Plasmid Construction

Monocistronic plasmids encoding gvpA, gvpN, gvpJ, gvpK, gvpF, gvpG, gvpW, and gvpV were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies and Twist Bioscience, respectively, with codon optimization for human expression. The gvpA gene was fused to a WPRE-hGH polyA signal and cloned into a pCMVSport backbone with a cytomegalovirus promoter. The assembly factor genes were assembled into a single polycistronic cassette linked by P2A peptides and fused to Emerald GFP via a P2A linker, with WPRE-hGH polyA at the 3’ end. The entire cassette was cloned into a PiggyBac transposon vector under a doxycycline-inducible TRE3G promoter. The gvpA plasmid was further modified to include an IRES-EBFP2 cassette upstream of the gvpA coding sequence. Lentiviral transfer plasmids encoding the rtTA transactivator and Antares reporter were constructed by subcloning the rtTA and Antares coding sequences into a pEF1α-WPRE backbone using NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly. All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing and restriction digest before use in transfection or transduction experiments.
