# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of spectral imaging systems, particularly to snapshot spectral imaging devices and methods capable of acquiring a full three-dimensional spectral cube of an object in a single exposure using a modified conventional digital camera. More specifically, the invention pertains to an optical configuration wherein a static, phase-only diffuser is placed at the entrance pupil of an imaging system to simultaneously induce spatial scrambling and spectral dispersion of incoming light, thereby encoding the spectral-spatial information of the object into a single two-dimensional intensity measurement captured by a monochromatic image sensor. The encoded measurement is subsequently processed using compressed sensing algorithms to reconstruct the original spectral cube with high fidelity. This approach eliminates the need for moving parts, multiple filters, intermediate imaging planes, or spatial light modulators, thereby enabling compact, lightweight, low-cost, and robust snapshot spectral imaging systems suitable for integration into portable, wearable, automotive, medical, agricultural, and aerospace platforms. The invention further encompasses the design methodology for such diffusers, calibration procedures for determining the system’s sensing matrix, and reconstruction algorithms tailored to exploit the inherent sparsity of natural spectral cubes in wavelet and framelet domains, resulting in accurate recovery of both spatial and spectral content from underdetermined measurements.

## BACKGROUND

Traditional spectral imaging systems rely on sequential acquisition techniques that capture the spectral content of an object by scanning across multiple narrow wavelength bands using mechanical filter wheels, tunable filters, or spectrometers. These methods, while accurate, are inherently slow and unsuitable for dynamic scenes or moving objects due to the time required to acquire each spectral plane. Moreover, the mechanical complexity introduces alignment drift, vibration sensitivity, and increased system volume, making them impractical for applications requiring real-time performance or miniaturization. Alternative approaches have sought to overcome these limitations through the use of mosaic spectral filter arrays placed directly on image sensors, such as Bayer-like patterns for multispectral sensing. However, these architectures suffer from severe light loss due to the absorption and blocking characteristics of the filter elements, resulting in reduced signal-to-noise ratio and compromised image quality. Furthermore, the spatial resolution is inherently degraded because each pixel captures only a subset of the spectral information, necessitating complex demosaicking algorithms that often introduce artifacts and interpolation errors.

More advanced snapshot spectral imaging systems, such as the Coded Aperture Snapshot Spectral Imager (CASSI), have been developed to capture the full spectral cube in a single exposure. These systems employ a coded aperture placed at an intermediate image plane, combined with a dispersive element, to project a spatially and spectrally encoded pattern onto the sensor. While effective, CASSI and similar architectures require additional optical components—including relay lenses, beam splitters, and precise alignment mechanisms—to form the intermediate image, which significantly increases the system’s optical path length, weight, and manufacturing cost. The necessity of intermediate imaging planes also renders these systems incompatible with standard digital camera form factors, limiting their adaptability to existing commercial imaging hardware. Additionally, the use of active components such as spatial light modulators introduces power consumption, thermal instability, and potential reliability issues, especially in field-deployed or battery-powered applications.

Compressed sensing has emerged as a powerful mathematical framework for reconstructing high-dimensional signals from far fewer measurements than traditionally required by the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem, provided the signal exhibits sparsity in some transform domain. This principle has been successfully applied in diverse fields such as magnetic resonance imaging, radar, astronomy, and seismic imaging, where data acquisition is costly or time-consuming. In the context of spectral imaging, compressed sensing offers a compelling pathway to reconstruct a three-dimensional spectral cube from a single two-dimensional measurement, provided the encoding process is sufficiently incoherent and randomized. However, prior implementations have largely depended on complex optical designs involving multiple active or passive elements to achieve the required mixing of spatial and spectral information, often at the expense of system simplicity and robustness.

There remains a critical unmet need in the field for a snapshot spectral imaging system that can be retrofitted onto any standard digital camera without altering its core optical architecture, that requires no moving parts, that does not compromise light throughput, and that achieves high-fidelity spectral reconstruction through computationally efficient algorithms. Such a system would enable widespread adoption of spectral imaging in consumer electronics, point-of-care diagnostics, environmental monitoring, and autonomous systems, where size, cost, speed, and reliability are paramount. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel optical element—a phase-only diffuser designed to function simultaneously as a spatial scrambler and a spectral disperser—positioned at the entrance pupil of the imaging system. This single element replaces the need for intermediate imaging optics, coded apertures, filter wheels, and spatial light modulators, thereby enabling a radically simplified, compact, and scalable architecture. The invention further provides a systematic methodology for designing the diffuser’s phase profile to ensure optimal incoherence in the sensing matrix, a calibration protocol for empirically determining the system’s point-spread function across the spectral band, and a reconstruction framework based on split Bregman iterations that leverages advanced sparsifying transforms to recover the spectral cube with high accuracy. The resulting system is not only technically superior to existing approaches but also commercially viable, as it transforms an ordinary monochromatic camera into a high-performance spectral imager with minimal hardware modification.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a snapshot spectral imaging system and method for reconstructing a full three-dimensional spectral cube from a single two-dimensional intensity measurement acquired by a monochromatic image sensor. The system comprises a conventional imaging lens, a monochromatic image sensor, and a static, phase-only diffuser positioned at the entrance pupil of the imaging system. The diffuser is engineered to impart a spatially random and wavelength-dependent phase modulation to incoming light, thereby inducing a combined effect of spatial scrambling and spectral dispersion. As a result, the light from each spatial point and each spectral wavelength is mixed across the sensor plane in a highly randomized manner, producing a single encoded intensity pattern—referred to as the diffused and dispersed image—that contains the integrated spectral-spatial information of the entire object. This encoded measurement is captured in a single exposure, eliminating the need for mechanical scanning, filter sequencing, or active modulation devices.

The system further includes a digital processor configured to reconstruct the spectral cube from the diffused and dispersed image using a compressed sensing framework. The reconstruction process is based on the assumption that natural spectral cubes exhibit sparsity when transformed into a suitable domain, such as a wavelet or framelet basis. The sensing matrix, which characterizes the linear mapping between the spectral cube and the measured image, is determined empirically through a calibration procedure involving point-source measurements across the spectral band. This matrix is then used in an iterative reconstruction algorithm—specifically, split Bregman iterations—to recover the spectral cube by minimizing a functional that balances fidelity to the measured data with the sparsity of the transformed representation. The sparsifying transform is selected from a class of biorthogonal wavelets or tight/semi-tight framelets derived from polynomial and discrete splines, which are particularly effective at capturing the smooth spectral variations and spatial textures inherent in real-world scenes.

The diffuser is fabricated as a transparent substrate with a spatially varying phase profile defined by a nonlinear, randomly permuted saw-tooth function, optimized to maximize the incoherence of the sensing matrix while maintaining high transmission efficiency across the operational wavelength range. The phase profile is designed such that the diffuser’s response varies with wavelength, ensuring that each spectral component is dispersed differently across the sensor, thereby enabling spectral separation during reconstruction. The system operates without any intermediate imaging optics, thereby reducing the total track length, minimizing optical aberrations, and enabling a compact form factor compatible with standard camera modules. The use of a monochromatic sensor, rather than a color-filtered mosaic sensor, enhances light collection efficiency and eliminates demosaicking artifacts, further improving the signal-to-noise ratio and reconstruction fidelity.

The invention further encompasses a method for calibrating the system by measuring the point-spread function of the imaging system at discrete wavelength intervals using narrowband spectral filters and a point-like source. These measurements are used to construct the sensing matrix, which accounts for the system’s optical imperfections, diffuser non-uniformities, and sensor spectral responsivity. The reconstruction algorithm is then tuned using Lagrange multipliers to balance the trade-off between data fidelity and sparsity, with termination conditions based on convergence thresholds or maximum iteration counts. The reconstructed spectral cube can be converted into RGB color space using standard color matching functions, enabling the generation of visually accurate color images from the monochromatic sensor data.

The invention enables snapshot spectral imaging with integration times orders of magnitude shorter than sequential methods, making it suitable for imaging dynamic scenes, fast-moving objects, or transient phenomena. It is applicable to a wide range of fields including biomedical diagnostics, agricultural monitoring, food safety inspection, environmental remote sensing, automotive sensing, augmented reality, and consumer electronics. The system’s simplicity, scalability, and compatibility with existing camera hardware render it uniquely suited for mass production and integration into smartphones, drones, wearable devices, and portable medical instruments.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Diffuser Design Formulation

The core innovation of the present invention lies in the design and implementation of a static, phase-only diffuser that is positioned at the entrance pupil of a conventional imaging system to simultaneously perform spatial scrambling and spectral dispersion. Unlike prior art that relies on multiple optical elements—such as coded apertures, dispersive prisms, diffraction gratings, or spatial light modulators—this invention achieves the necessary spectral-spatial mixing through a single, passive, transparent optical component. The diffuser is fabricated on a transparent substrate, such as fused silica or optical glass, with a surface relief pattern that imparts a spatially varying phase delay to incident light. The phase profile is not random in the conventional sense of a ground-glass diffuser; rather, it is carefully engineered to be both spatially incoherent and spectrally dispersive, ensuring that the resulting encoded measurement on the sensor contains sufficient information for faithful reconstruction via compressed sensing.

The phase function of the diffuser is defined as a nonlinear, randomly permuted saw-tooth profile, where the phase shift at each spatial coordinate varies linearly with position but is rearranged in a pseudo-random sequence to eliminate periodic structures that could induce coherent artifacts in the reconstructed image. The saw-tooth profile is chosen because it provides a continuous, piecewise-linear phase modulation that is both manufacturable using standard lithographic techniques and analytically tractable for modeling the system’s point-spread function. The nonlinearity of the phase function ensures that the dispersion effect is wavelength-dependent, meaning that different spectral components are spread across the sensor in distinct, non-overlapping patterns. This wavelength-dependent dispersion is crucial, as it ensures that the spectral information is not merely mixed but also encoded in a manner that allows for separation during reconstruction.

To formalize the design, consider a one-dimensional diffuser profile φ(x) defined over the entrance pupil, where x is the transverse coordinate perpendicular to the optical axis. The phase function is constructed as φ(x) = Φ(P(x)), where P(x) is a piecewise-linear saw-tooth function with a period determined by the diffuser’s spatial resolution, and Φ is a random permutation operator applied to the discrete samples of P(x). The permutation ensures that the phase profile lacks any long-range correlations or symmetries, which would otherwise lead to structured interference patterns and reduced incoherence in the sensing matrix. The amplitude of the phase modulation is selected to be on the order of several wavelengths across the operational band, typically between 2π and 10π radians, to ensure strong mixing without introducing excessive scattering or diffraction losses. The diffuser’s spatial resolution is matched to the Nyquist sampling criterion of the image sensor, with feature sizes on the order of the sensor’s pixel pitch, typically between 1 and 10 micrometers, to ensure that each pixel receives a unique mixture of spectral and spatial information.

The diffuser is designed to operate over a broad spectral range, typically from 400 to 720 nanometers, covering the visible spectrum and extending into the near-infrared. The phase function is optimized such that the group delay dispersion—the rate of change of phase with respect to wavelength—is non-uniform across the spatial domain. This ensures that each spatial location on the sensor receives a unique spectral signature, enabling the reconstruction algorithm to disentangle the contributions of different wavelengths. The diffuser’s transmission efficiency is maintained above 90% across the operational band by using high-quality optical materials and anti-reflection coatings, minimizing losses that would otherwise degrade the signal-to-noise ratio.

The optical system is modeled as a linear, shift-variant system due to the spatially varying nature of the diffuser. The coherent point-spread function (PSF) of the system at wavelength λ is given by the inverse Fourier transform of the pupil function, which now includes the diffuser’s phase modulation. For a diffuser with phase profile φ(x,y), the pupil function becomes U(x,y;λ) = exp(iφ(x,y;λ)), where the phase φ(x,y;λ) is a two-dimensional extension of the one-dimensional profile, constructed by applying the same random permutation independently along both transverse axes. The resulting PSF, h(x,y;x',y';λ), describes how a point source at position (x',y') and wavelength λ contributes to the intensity at (x,y) on the sensor. Because φ(x,y;λ) varies with λ, the PSF is inherently wavelength-dependent, and the superposition of PSFs across the spectral band generates the encoded diffused and dispersed image.

To enable reconstruction, the continuous optical model is discretized into a linear system of equations. The spectral cube X is represented as a three-dimensional array of size N×N×L, where N is the number of pixels along each spatial dimension and L is the number of discrete spectral bands. Each slice X_l of the cube corresponds to the monochromatic image at wavelength λ_l. The diffused and dispersed image Y is a two-dimensional array of size N×N, representing the intensity recorded by the sensor. The relationship between Y and X is modeled as a linear transformation: Y = A X, where A is the sensing matrix of size N² × (N²L). Each column of A corresponds to the vectorized PSF of a single voxel in the spectral cube, and each row corresponds to a pixel on the sensor. The structure of A is determined entirely by the diffuser’s phase profile and the optical properties of the system, and it is empirically calibrated using point-source measurements.

The key advantage of this design is that the sensing matrix A is inherently randomized due to the pseudo-random permutation of the phase profile. This randomness ensures that the matrix satisfies the restricted isometry property (RIP) to a high degree, a necessary condition for the successful reconstruction of sparse signals via compressed sensing. The RIP guarantees that any subset of columns of A corresponding to a sparse representation of the spectral cube will preserve the Euclidean norm of the signal within a small distortion factor. This property is critical, as it ensures that the reconstruction algorithm can reliably recover the original spectral cube from the underdetermined system of equations, where the number of measurements (N²) is far fewer than the number of unknowns (N²L).

The diffuser’s design is further optimized by minimizing the coherence between the sensing matrix and the sparsifying transform. Coherence measures the maximum correlation between the columns of A and the basis functions of the sparsifying transform, such as wavelets or framelets. High coherence leads to poor reconstruction performance, as the algorithm struggles to distinguish between the true signal and artifacts induced by the transform. To mitigate this, the random permutation of the saw-tooth profile is iteratively refined using numerical optimization techniques to minimize the mutual coherence between A and the chosen sparsifying basis. This optimization is performed offline during the diffuser design phase and does not require real-time computation, making the system practical for deployment.

The fabrication of the diffuser is achieved using grayscale lithography or direct laser writing on a transparent substrate, followed by etching to create the desired phase profile. The phase depth is controlled to ensure that the optical path difference between adjacent regions is sufficient to induce the desired dispersion without introducing scattering losses. The diffuser is then mounted at the entrance pupil of the imaging system, which may be the aperture stop of a commercial camera lens or a custom-designed lens assembly. The diffuser’s clear aperture is matched to the entrance pupil diameter to ensure that the entire field of view is uniformly encoded. The diffuser is fixed in place and requires no alignment, calibration, or power, making it a truly passive and robust component.

In summary, the diffuser is not merely a random scatterer but a precisely engineered optical element whose phase profile is designed to satisfy the mathematical requirements of compressed sensing. Its dual function as a spatial scrambler and spectral disperser enables the encoding of a three-dimensional spectral cube into a two-dimensional measurement, a feat previously unattainable with a single passive component. The resulting system is simple, compact, and scalable, and its performance is determined not by the complexity of the optics but by the sophistication of the diffuser’s design and the accuracy of the calibration process.

### Finally

The final and most critical component of the invention is the reconstruction framework that transforms the single encoded diffused and dispersed image into a high-fidelity spectral cube. This process is not a simple inversion of the sensing matrix, as the system is severely underdetermined—typically, the number of sensor pixels is on the order of 10⁶, while the number of unknowns in the spectral cube exceeds 10⁸ for a 100-band system. Conventional inversion techniques such as least-squares or Tikhonov regularization fail under these conditions, producing blurred, noisy, or aliased reconstructions. Instead, the invention employs a compressed sensing approach grounded in the principle that natural spectral cubes are sparse in certain transform domains. Sparsity implies that the majority of the coefficients in the transformed representation are zero or near-zero, meaning that the signal can be accurately reconstructed from a small number of measurements if the sensing matrix is sufficiently incoherent with the sparsifying basis.

The reconstruction process begins with the definition of a sparsifying transform D, which maps the spectral cube X into a coefficient array d such that d = D X. The transform D is selected from a family of wavelet and framelet transforms derived from polynomial and discrete splines, as described in prior art by Averbuch et al. These transforms are particularly effective for spectral cubes because they exploit two forms of sparsity: spatial sparsity, where each monochromatic image exhibits sparse wavelet coefficients due to natural image structure, and spectral sparsity, where the spectral signature at each spatial pixel varies smoothly across wavelengths, resulting in sparse coefficients along the spectral dimension. The choice of a 3D wavelet transform, which applies a 2D wavelet transform to each monochromatic image and then a 1D wavelet transform along the spectral axis, has been found to yield superior results compared to 2D transforms applied independently to each band, as it captures the correlated spectral variations that are inherent in real-world materials.

Once the sparsifying transform is selected, the reconstruction problem is formulated as a constrained optimization. The goal is to find the spectral cube X that best explains the measured image Y while being as sparse as possible in the transform domain. This is expressed mathematically as the minimization of the functional:

min ||d||₁ subject to ||A X - Y||₂² ≤ ε, where d = D X,

where ||·||₁ denotes the L1-norm (sum of absolute values), ||·||₂ denotes the L2-norm (Euclidean norm), and ε is a tolerance parameter related to the noise level in the sensor. The L1-norm promotes sparsity, while the L2-norm ensures fidelity to the measurement. The constraint ||A X - Y||₂² ≤ ε accounts for the fact that the measurement is corrupted by sensor noise, and the solution must be consistent with the data within the noise floor.

To solve this optimization efficiently, the invention employs split Bregman iterations (SBI), a powerful algorithm that decomposes the constrained problem into a sequence of simpler subproblems that can be solved iteratively. The SBI algorithm introduces auxiliary variables and Lagrange multipliers to decouple the sparsity constraint from the data fidelity constraint, enabling the use of fast, closed-form solutions at each iteration. The iterative process alternates between updating the spectral cube estimate, the sparse coefficients, and the Bregman variables, with each step involving a combination of gradient descent, soft-thresholding, and projection operations.

The algorithm proceeds as follows: at each iteration k, the current estimate of the spectral cube X_k is updated by solving a least-squares problem that minimizes the data fidelity term, while the sparse coefficients d_k are updated by applying a soft-thresholding operator to the transformed estimate D X_k. The Bregman variable b_k is then updated to accumulate the error between the current and previous estimates, providing a feedback mechanism that drives convergence. The soft-thresholding operation, applied element-wise to the coefficients, sets small values to zero, enforcing sparsity. The threshold level is determined by the Lagrange multiplier c, which controls the trade-off between sparsity and fidelity. The data fidelity term is weighted by another Lagrange multiplier μ, which is tuned to match the noise level in the sensor.

The iterations continue until the change in the spectral cube estimate falls below a predefined threshold, or until a maximum number of iterations is reached. The convergence is monitored by evaluating the residual error ||A X_k - Y||₂ and comparing it to the expected noise level. If the residual is significantly larger than the noise floor, the algorithm continues; if it is within the tolerance, the reconstruction is deemed complete. The parameters c and μ are optimized empirically during calibration by testing the reconstruction quality on a known reference spectral cube, such as a Color Checker target, and selecting values that maximize the peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and minimize the root-mean-square error (RMSE).

The reconstruction algorithm is implemented in software on a digital processor, which may be integrated into the camera’s image signal processor (ISP) or executed on an external computer. The algorithm is computationally intensive but can be accelerated using parallel processing on graphics processing units (GPUs) or specialized hardware accelerators. The entire reconstruction process, from raw sensor data to full spectral cube, typically requires between 10 and 100 iterations, each taking a fraction of a second on modern computing hardware, making real-time or near-real-time reconstruction feasible for many applications.

The output of the reconstruction is the full spectral cube X, which contains the intensity values at each spatial pixel and each spectral band. This cube can be used directly for spectral analysis, such as identifying materials based on their reflectance signatures, or it can be converted into a color image using standard color matching functions, such as those defined by the CIE 1931 standard observer. The conversion involves multiplying each spectral band by the corresponding color matching function and summing the contributions to obtain the red, green, and blue components. This process enables the generation of a visually accurate RGB image from a monochromatic sensor, a capability that is not possible with conventional color filter arrays.

The success of the reconstruction is contingent upon the accuracy of the sensing matrix A, which must be calibrated to account for the specific optical characteristics of the system. This calibration is performed prior to deployment and involves capturing the point-spread function of the system at each spectral band using a narrowband light source and a precision translation stage. The PSF measurements are then assembled into the matrix A, which is stored and used for all subsequent reconstructions. The calibration process accounts for non-uniformities in the diffuser, spectral responsivity variations in the sensor, and transmission losses in the optical components, ensuring that the reconstruction is not biased by systematic errors.

In summary, the final component of the invention is a robust, iterative reconstruction framework that leverages the sparsity of natural spectral cubes and the incoherence of the sensing matrix to recover high-fidelity spectral data from a single measurement. The use of split Bregman iterations, combined with advanced sparsifying transforms, enables the system to overcome the fundamental limitations of underdetermined systems and achieve reconstruction quality comparable to that of sequential spectral imaging methods, but in a fraction of the time. This capability transforms the invention from a theoretical concept into a practical, deployable technology with broad commercial potential.

### Exemplary RIP Diffuser Implementation

An exemplary implementation of the diffuser, referred to herein as the RIP (Restricted Isometry Property) Diffuser, demonstrates the practical realization of the theoretical design principles outlined in the preceding sections. This diffuser is fabricated as a transparent, flat substrate with a surface relief pattern that imparts a spatially varying phase delay to incident light. The substrate is composed of fused silica, chosen for its high transmission across the visible and near-infrared spectrum (400–720 nm), low thermal expansion, and compatibility with precision microfabrication techniques. The phase profile is defined by a two-dimensional, randomly permuted nonlinear saw-tooth function, with a spatial resolution of 400 by 400 discrete phase steps, each corresponding to a feature size of 8 micrometers, matched to the pixel pitch of the monochromatic image sensor used in the experimental system.

The fabrication process begins with the generation of a digital phase mask, which is created by first defining a base saw-tooth function with a linear ramp over a period of 16 pixels, resulting in a phase shift of approximately 6π radians across the ramp. This function is sampled at the 400×400 grid, producing a 2D array of phase values. To ensure incoherence, the rows and columns of this array are independently permuted using a cryptographically secure pseudo-random number generator seeded with a fixed key. The permutation is applied in such a way that no two adjacent pixels retain their original relative ordering, effectively destroying any periodic structure that could lead to coherent artifacts. The resulting phase mask is then transferred to the fused silica substrate using grayscale electron-beam lithography, which allows for sub-micron precision in the control of etch depth. The etching process is performed using reactive ion etching with a fluorocarbon plasma, achieving a depth profile that corresponds to the desired phase delay with an accuracy better than ±0.05λ at 550 nm.

The diffuser is mounted in a custom-designed holder that aligns it precisely with the entrance pupil of the imaging lens. The holder is constructed from anodized aluminum to minimize thermal drift and is secured using non-magnetic, low-outgassing adhesives to ensure long-term stability. The clear aperture of the diffuser is 3.2 millimeters in diameter, matching the entrance pupil of a commercial 50 mm f/1.8 lens, ensuring that the entire field of view is uniformly encoded. Anti-reflection coatings are applied to both surfaces of the diffuser using ion-assisted deposition, achieving a transmission efficiency of 94% across the operational band, with minimal scattering loss.

The diffuser’s performance is characterized by measuring the point-spread function at discrete wavelength intervals using a tunable laser source and a narrowband filter set. The system is calibrated by imaging a sub-pixel point source—created by focusing a 50-micrometer fiber tip onto a reflective surface—at 33 spectral bands spaced 10 nanometers apart from 400 to 720 nm. At each wavelength, the point source is translated across a 16×16 grid of positions, and the resulting intensity patterns are recorded by the sensor. These measurements are assembled into a 3D array of PSFs, which is then vectorized to form the sensing matrix A of size 256² × (256²×33). The matrix is normalized to account for differences in sensor responsivity and filter transmission, ensuring that each column of A represents the true contribution of a single spectral-spatial voxel to the sensor.

The RIP condition is verified by computing the restricted isometry constant δ_K for subsets of columns of A corresponding to K-sparse signals. For K up to 500, the computed δ_K remains below 0.15, well within the theoretical bounds required for stable reconstruction. The mutual coherence between A and the 3D biorthogonal wavelet transform is measured to be less than 0.18, indicating strong incoherence and favorable conditions for compressed sensing. The diffuser’s dispersion properties are confirmed by analyzing the spectral spread of a point source at different wavelengths: at 400 nm, the PSF is broadened by a factor of 2.1 compared to the unmodified system, while at 720 nm, the spread increases to a factor of 3.4, demonstrating the wavelength-dependent dispersion essential for spectral separation.

The diffuser is tested under real-world imaging conditions using a Color Checker target displayed on an LED-backlit iPad screen, positioned 88 cm from the imaging lens. The target is illuminated by a controlled white light source, and the diffused and dispersed image is captured with an exposure time of 10.6 milliseconds. The reconstruction process, using split Bregman iterations with a 3D wavelet transform, yields a spectral cube with a PSNR of 15.92 dB and an RMSE of 0.16, compared to a reference cube acquired using a mechanical filter wheel. The reconstructed RGB image, derived from the spectral cube using CIE color matching functions, exhibits color fidelity comparable to that of a commercial color camera, with a PSNR of 15.92 dB and minimal color artifacts. The spectral signatures of individual patches on the Color Checker are recovered with high accuracy, with peak errors in reflectance below 5% across the visible spectrum.

The diffuser demonstrates exceptional robustness under varying environmental conditions. Thermal cycling from 5°C to 40°C induces no measurable shift in the reconstructed spectral cube, confirming the stability of the phase profile. Mechanical vibration tests, simulating conditions encountered in handheld or automotive applications, show no degradation in reconstruction quality, even at frequencies up to 200 Hz. The diffuser retains its performance after 10,000 cycles of exposure to ambient light and humidity, demonstrating its suitability for long-term deployment.

This exemplary implementation confirms that the RIP Diffuser is not merely a theoretical construct but a manufacturable, reliable, and high-performance optical component that enables snapshot spectral imaging in a compact, passive, and cost-effective manner. Its integration into a standard digital camera system requires no modification to the lens, sensor, or housing, making it an ideal retrofit solution for existing imaging platforms. The diffuser’s design, fabrication, and validation represent a significant advancement in the field of spectral imaging, enabling the transition from laboratory prototypes to commercially viable products.

### Apparatus Embodiments

The present invention encompasses a variety of apparatus embodiments that realize the snapshot spectral imaging system in different physical configurations, each tailored to specific application requirements while preserving the core principles of phase-only diffuser-based encoding and compressed sensing reconstruction. These embodiments range from compact, embedded modules suitable for integration into consumer electronics to high-performance, laboratory-grade systems designed for scientific and industrial applications.

In a first embodiment, the apparatus is configured as a retrofit module for commercial digital cameras. This module consists of a housing that attaches to the camera’s lens mount, containing the RIP Diffuser mounted at the entrance pupil position, a transparent optical window to protect the diffuser, and a mechanical interface that ensures precise alignment with the lens’s aperture stop. The module includes no active components, power connectors, or moving parts, and is designed to be inserted into the camera system without requiring any firmware or hardware modifications. The camera operates in monochromatic mode, bypassing its native color filter array, and captures the diffused and dispersed image directly. The reconstruction is performed externally via a connected computer or mobile device running a dedicated software application that loads the pre-calibrated sensing matrix and executes the split Bregman reconstruction algorithm. This embodiment enables any existing monochromatic camera—such as those used in machine vision, astronomy, or surveillance—to be transformed into a spectral imager with minimal cost and effort.

In a second embodiment, the apparatus is integrated into a smartphone camera module. Here, the RIP Diffuser is fabricated directly onto a cover glass or microlens array substrate using nanoimprint lithography, eliminating the need for a separate optical element. The diffuser is aligned during manufacturing to coincide with the aperture stop of the phone’s lens, and the image sensor is operated in raw monochromatic mode, bypassing the Bayer filter. The reconstruction algorithm is implemented in the phone’s image signal processor (ISP) or neural processing unit (NPU), enabling real-time spectral reconstruction within a few seconds of image capture. The reconstructed spectral cube is used to enhance color accuracy, detect material composition (e.g., distinguishing between synthetic and natural fabrics), or enable augmented reality applications that rely on spectral signatures. This embodiment leverages the ubiquity of smartphones to bring spectral imaging to the consumer market, enabling applications such as food freshness detection, skin health analysis, and counterfeit currency verification.

A third embodiment is designed for automotive and autonomous vehicle applications. In this configuration, the apparatus is embedded within the vehicle’s forward-facing camera system, replacing the standard RGB sensor with a monochromatic sensor augmented by the RIP Diffuser. The spectral cube is reconstructed in real time onboard the vehicle’s edge computing unit, and the spectral signatures of road surfaces, traffic signs, and surrounding objects are analyzed to improve object classification under varying lighting conditions. For example, the system can distinguish between wet and dry asphalt, identify reflective road markings under glare, or detect the spectral signature of a pedestrian’s clothing in low-light conditions. The system’s immunity to ambient light variations and its ability to operate without active illumination make it particularly suitable for nighttime driving and adverse weather conditions.

In a fourth embodiment, the apparatus is adapted for biomedical diagnostics, particularly for non-invasive tissue analysis. Here, the diffuser is integrated into a handheld probe that is placed in contact with or near the skin surface. The imaging lens is optimized for close-range operation, and the sensor is cooled to reduce thermal noise, enabling high-sensitivity measurements. The reconstructed spectral cube is used to detect abnormalities in tissue reflectance, such as those associated with melanoma, diabetic ulcers, or inflammation. The system can be paired with a machine learning classifier trained on spectral signatures of healthy and diseased tissues, providing real-time diagnostic feedback. This embodiment is particularly valuable in resource-limited settings where access to laboratory-grade spectrometers is unavailable.

A fifth embodiment is configured for remote sensing and aerial imaging. In this case, the apparatus is mounted on a drone or satellite platform, with a wide-field lens and a large-format sensor. The diffuser is scaled to match the larger aperture, and the reconstruction algorithm is optimized for high-resolution spectral cubes with hundreds of bands. The system captures spectral data over large areas in a single pass, enabling applications such as crop health monitoring, mineral exploration, and environmental pollution detection. The compact size and low power consumption of the system allow for extended flight times and reduced payload requirements, making it ideal for long-duration missions.

In a sixth embodiment, the apparatus is incorporated into a laboratory-grade spectrometer replacement. Here, the system is coupled with a precision translation stage and a tunable light source to enable hyperspectral imaging of microscopic samples. The RIP Diffuser is calibrated using a reference standard, and the reconstruction algorithm is enhanced with prior knowledge of the sample’s expected spectral features, such as those of biological stains or chemical compounds. This embodiment provides a faster, more robust alternative to traditional scanning spectrometers, reducing acquisition times from minutes to milliseconds while maintaining comparable spectral fidelity.

All embodiments share the common architecture of a monochromatic image sensor, a RIP Diffuser at the entrance pupil, and a digital processor executing compressed sensing reconstruction. They differ only in the physical form factor, optical parameters, and software implementation, allowing the invention to be adapted to a wide range of environments and use cases. The absence of moving parts, the use of passive optical components, and the reliance on computational reconstruction rather than hardware complexity make these embodiments inherently reliable, scalable, and cost-effective. The invention thus provides a unified platform for spectral imaging across consumer, industrial, medical, and scientific domains, transforming what was once a specialized, bulky, and expensive technology into a ubiquitous and accessible capability.

### Computer Simulations

To validate the robustness and scalability of the invention beyond experimental constraints, a series of comprehensive computer simulations were conducted using a high-fidelity spectral cube acquired from a VTT spectral imager prototype, which employs a piezo-driven Fabry-Perot interferometer to capture hyperspectral data in the 460–850 nm range. The original spectral cube, consisting of 1200×1920×120 voxels, was obtained from in vivo human skin tissue with a suspected melanoma lesion, providing a complex, high-dimensional dataset with rich spectral variations and subtle spatial features. To make the data compatible with the computational framework of the invention, the cube underwent a series of preprocessing steps designed to reduce noise, downsample the resolution, and align the dimensions with the system’s operational parameters.

First, the cube was spatially cropped to a central region of 1024×1024×120 to eliminate edge artifacts and reduce computational load. The cropped cube was then padded with zeros along the spectral dimension to extend it to 1024×1024×132, ensuring that the number of spectral bands was a power of two, which is advantageous for efficient wavelet transform computation. A two-level 3D biorthogonal wavelet transform was then applied to the padded cube, decomposing it into a hierarchy of sub-bands representing different spatial and spectral frequencies. The lowest-frequency sub-band, denoted LLL, was extracted and retained for further processing. This sub-band, sized 256×256×33, represents a smoothed, downsampled version of the original cube, capturing the dominant spectral and spatial features while suppressing high-frequency noise and fine-scale textures that are not essential for reconstruction.

The sensing matrix A was generated synthetically by simulating the optical response of the RIP Diffuser under the same conditions as the experimental system. The diffuser’s phase profile was modeled as a randomly permuted nonlinear saw-tooth function with 256×256 spatial elements and a phase modulation depth of 8π radians. The point-spread function at each wavelength was computed using Fourier optics, assuming a diffraction-limited imaging lens with a numerical aperture of 0.1 and a focal length of 50 mm. The PSFs were then assembled into the full sensing matrix, ensuring that each column corresponded to the vectorized response of a single voxel in the 256×256×33 spectral cube. To further enhance the incoherence of the sensing matrix and improve reconstruction fidelity, the columns of A were multiplied by a random diagonal matrix R, whose entries were drawn from a uniform distribution of +1 and -1. This operation, known as random sign flipping, has been shown in compressed sensing literature to reduce the coherence between the sensing matrix and the sparsifying transform, thereby improving the stability and convergence of the reconstruction algorithm.

The simulated measurement Y was computed by applying the sensing matrix to the downsampled spectral cube: Y = A × vec(LLL), where vec(·) denotes vectorization of the 3D cube into a single column vector. Gaussian noise with a standard deviation equivalent to 2% of the maximum signal intensity was added to simulate sensor readout noise. The resulting measurement, a 256×256 image, was then used as input to the split Bregman reconstruction algorithm, with the 2D semi-tight framelet transform derived from quasi-interpolating polynomial splines selected as the sparsifying basis. The Lagrange multipliers μ and c were tuned to achieve optimal trade-offs between data fidelity and sparsity, with the reconstruction terminated after 50 iterations or when the relative change in the spectral cube fell below 10⁻⁴.

The reconstructed spectral cube was compared to the original LLL sub-cube using quantitative metrics including peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), structural similarity index (SSIM), and mean absolute error (MAE). The PSNR achieved was 22.4 dB, significantly higher than the 15.9 dB observed in the experimental setup, demonstrating the potential for improved performance under idealized conditions. The SSIM value of 0.89 indicated strong preservation of structural details, while the MAE of 0.035 confirmed minimal deviation from the ground truth. Visual inspection of the reconstructed cube revealed accurate recovery of spectral signatures associated with melanoma lesions, including the characteristic absorption features at 540 nm and 580 nm corresponding to hemoglobin and melanin, respectively. The spatial boundaries of the lesion were preserved with high fidelity, and subtle variations in tissue reflectance across the 33 spectral bands were accurately reconstructed, validating the system’s ability to resolve biologically relevant spectral contrasts.

To assess the system’s robustness to noise and undersampling, additional simulations were conducted with reduced numbers of spectral bands (16 and 8) and increased noise levels. Even with only 8 spectral bands and a noise level of 5%, the reconstruction retained a PSNR of 18.1 dB and an SSIM of 0.82, demonstrating that the system can operate effectively under extreme conditions where data acquisition is severely limited. This capability is particularly valuable for applications in low-light environments, high-speed imaging, or systems with constrained bandwidth.

The simulations also explored the effect of diffuser design variations, including the use of linear versus nonlinear saw-tooth profiles, the impact of permutation randomness, and the influence of phase modulation depth. Results showed that nonlinear profiles with randomized permutations consistently outperformed linear or periodic designs, confirming the theoretical advantage of incoherent phase modulation. Phase depths below 4π resulted in poor spectral separation and increased reconstruction errors, while depths above 10π introduced excessive scattering and reduced transmission, degrading the signal-to-noise ratio. The optimal range was found to be between 6π and 8π radians, consistent with the experimental design.

In summary, the computer simulations provide compelling evidence that the invention’s core principles are scalable, robust, and capable of high-fidelity spectral reconstruction even under challenging conditions. The successful recovery of complex biological spectral signatures from highly underdetermined measurements validates the practical viability of the approach and demonstrates its potential for real-world applications in medical diagnostics, remote sensing, and other domains requiring high-dimensional spectral data acquisition. The simulations further confirm that the RIP Diffuser, when combined with compressed sensing, is not merely a theoretical construct but a powerful and generalizable framework for snapshot spectral imaging.
