## BACKGROUND

The field of protonic ceramic energy devices has long been constrained by the limitations of conventional ceramic fabrication techniques, which are ill-suited to the demands of modern energy conversion and storage systems requiring complex geometries, high surface-area-to-volume ratios, and precisely controlled microstructures. Traditional methods such as dry pressing, tape casting, and screen printing have enabled the production of simple planar or tubular components at laboratory scale, but these approaches are fundamentally incompatible with the fabrication of intricate, multi-layered, or three-dimensionally structured devices necessary for scalable and high-performance applications. The inability to produce devices with closed-end tubular geometries, surface lobes, internal microchannels, or graded porosity has severely restricted the efficiency, power density, and operational flexibility of protonic ceramic fuel cells, electrolysis cells, membrane reactors, and hydrogen-permeable membranes. Furthermore, the reliance on multi-step processing involving lengthy drying cycles, high-temperature sintering in batch furnaces, and post-machining operations has resulted in prohibitively long manufacturing lead times, high material waste, and inconsistent reproducibility across batches.

The advent of additive manufacturing technologies offered a promising pathway to overcome these limitations by enabling direct digital fabrication of complex geometries from computer-aided design models. However, the application of additive manufacturing to ceramics—particularly proton-conducting oxides—has encountered persistent and fundamental challenges. Ceramics, by their intrinsic nature, exhibit high melting points, low thermal conductivity, and extreme brittleness, which render them highly susceptible to cracking, warping, and delamination during thermal processing. Conventional powder-bed fusion and extrusion-based 3D printing techniques for ceramics typically require the incorporation of substantial quantities of organic binders and solvents to achieve printability, which subsequently necessitate prolonged and energy-intensive debinding and sintering steps. These organic additives often decompose unevenly, leading to porosity, residual carbon, and microstructural inhomogeneity that degrade ionic conductivity and mechanical integrity. Moreover, the thermal shrinkage inherent in ceramic sintering—often exceeding 20–30%—compounds geometric inaccuracies, making it nearly impossible to achieve dimensional precision in multi-layered architectures without extensive post-processing. Even when successful, these methods remain incapable of producing fully dense, crack-free, and microstructurally optimized protonic ceramic components in a single integrated process.

Recent attempts to integrate laser processing with ceramic additive manufacturing have shown limited success, primarily in the domain of oxide ceramics with low protonic conductivity or in non-functional structural components. The use of continuous-wave lasers for sintering has frequently resulted in localized overheating, thermal gradients that induce cracking, and poor interlayer bonding due to insufficient energy coupling with the ceramic matrix. The absence of a synergistic integration between precise material deposition and in-situ, spatially controlled consolidation has prevented the realization of high-fidelity, functional protonic ceramic devices with the necessary microstructural homogeneity and phase purity. Additionally, the lack of a mechanism for real-time surface finishing, microchannel definition, or edge refinement during the printing process has forced reliance on secondary machining operations that are incompatible with the delicate, porous, and fragile nature of green ceramic bodies. Consequently, the field has remained stagnant, with protonic ceramic devices confined to small, planar, and geometrically simplistic configurations that fail to exploit the full thermodynamic and kinetic advantages of proton conduction at intermediate temperatures.

There exists, therefore, a critical and unmet need for a manufacturing methodology that seamlessly integrates high-precision material deposition with in-situ, rapid, and spatially controlled consolidation, drying, polishing, and cutting operations—all within a unified, automated platform. Such a method must be capable of fabricating protonic ceramic components with arbitrary three-dimensional geometries, including closed-end tubular structures, surface lobes, internal microchannels, and multi-layered heterostructures, while simultaneously achieving full densification, phase purity, and high proton conductivity without the need for post-firing in conventional furnaces. The invention disclosed herein fulfills this need by introducing a novel laser-assisted three-dimensional printing system that combines microextrusion-based deposition with multi-wavelength laser processing to enable the direct, one-step fabrication of functional protonic ceramic devices with unprecedented geometric complexity, microstructural control, and operational performance.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a laser-assisted three-dimensional printing system and method for the direct fabrication of protonic ceramic devices with complex geometries, controlled microstructures, and high proton conductivity, without requiring conventional sintering furnaces or extensive post-processing. The system integrates a microextrusion-based 3D printing module with a multi-laser processing array comprising a continuous-wave CO₂ laser for rapid drying and reactive sintering, and a picosecond pulsed YAG laser for precision cutting and surface polishing, all coordinated through a high-accuracy galvanometric scanner and computer-controlled motion stages. The method enables the sequential deposition of printable ceramic pastes, followed immediately by in-situ laser drying to consolidate each layer, laser sintering to achieve full densification and phase formation, and laser machining to define intricate surface features, microchannels, or geometric contours—all within a single, automated fabrication cycle. The resulting protonic ceramic components exhibit fully dense, crack-free microstructures with phase-pure perovskite lattices, uniform grain morphology, and proton conductivities comparable to or exceeding those of conventionally sintered materials, while simultaneously possessing complex three-dimensional geometries—including closed-end tubular structures, lobed surfaces, internal microchannel networks, and multi-layered heterostructures—that are unattainable through traditional manufacturing techniques.

The printable paste composition comprises a mixture of proton-conducting ceramic precursor powders, water, a dispersant, and a thermoplastic binder, formulated to achieve a viscosity range that permits stable extrusion while maintaining sufficient green strength to resist deformation during layer-by-layer deposition. The paste is deposited through a microextruder nozzle with a diameter of approximately 0.5 mm, forming filaments with a width of approximately 740 μm and a layer thickness of approximately 450 μm. Immediately following deposition, a CO₂ laser operating at a wavelength of 10.64 μm, with a defocused beam spot of approximately 1 mm and a power of approximately 10 W, scans the freshly deposited layer at a rate of 15 mm/s to rapidly evaporate residual solvents and initiate partial densification, thereby preventing layer slumping, delamination, or geometric distortion. Subsequent to the completion of the green body, a second CO₂ laser, operating at higher power (up to 90 W) and configured with a cylindrical lens to produce a line-shaped beam of approximately 8 mm width, is employed to perform rapid laser reactive sintering by scanning the entire component at a slow traverse speed of 0.1 mm/s, thereby inducing solid-state reactions between precursor oxides and carbonates to form dense, phase-pure proton-conducting perovskite phases without the need for furnace firing. In parallel, a picosecond YAG laser operating at 1064 nm with a pulse energy of 150 μJ and a repetition rate of 10 kHz is used to perform precision cutting of support structures or to define microchannels with sub-millimeter resolution, while a lower-energy setting of 114.4 μJ per pulse and 1 kHz repetition rate is utilized for surface polishing to achieve Ra values below 1 μm, enabling high-quality coating of electrolyte layers without surface topography-induced thickness variations.

The invention enables the fabrication of a broad range of protonic ceramic devices, including but not limited to: solid pellets, cylindrical rods, conical structures, ring-shaped components, straight tubular bodies with sealed ends, lobed tubular geometries with enhanced surface-area-to-volume ratios, microchannel-embedded membranes for gas separation, and fully integrated half-cells comprising porous anode and dense electrolyte layers co-sintered in a single step. The resulting components exhibit relative densities exceeding 95%, grain sizes in the range of 1–3 μm, and proton conductivities of up to 6.95 × 10⁻³ S/cm at 600 °C under wet hydrogen atmospheres, matching or surpassing the performance of conventionally sintered analogues. The method eliminates the need for multi-day debinding cycles, high-temperature furnace sintering, and post-machining operations, reducing fabrication time from days to hours while improving dimensional accuracy, reproducibility, and material utilization. The system is fully programmable, allowing for the digital design of arbitrary geometries via CAD software, which are then translated into automated toolpaths for extrusion, drying, sintering, and machining, thereby enabling on-demand, customized manufacturing of protonic ceramic devices for applications in fuel cells, electrolyzers, membrane reactors, hydrogen purification systems, and high-temperature sensors.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention comprises a novel integrated manufacturing system and method for the direct fabrication of protonic ceramic devices through the synergistic combination of microextrusion-based three-dimensional printing and multi-wavelength laser processing. The system is designed to overcome the longstanding limitations of conventional ceramic manufacturing by enabling the in-situ consolidation of green ceramic bodies through rapid, spatially controlled laser energy delivery, thereby eliminating the need for furnace-based sintering and minimizing the use of organic additives that compromise microstructural integrity. The core innovation lies in the integration of four distinct laser processing modalities—rapid drying, reactive sintering, precision cutting, and surface polishing—within a single, computer-controlled platform that operates in a seamless, layer-by-layer fashion, ensuring geometric fidelity, microstructural homogeneity, and functional performance in a single automated cycle.

The system architecture consists of a precision motion control platform comprising high-resolution X-Y and Z stages capable of sub-micron positioning accuracy, enabling the controlled movement of the substrate relative to the extrusion nozzle and laser focal points. The extrusion module is a microextruder equipped with a needle-type nozzle of 0.5 mm inner diameter, driven by compressed air to deliver ceramic paste at a controlled flow rate of approximately 0.3 mL/min. The paste is formulated from a mixture of stoichiometric proportions of ceramic precursor powders, including barium carbonate, cerium oxide, zirconium oxide, yttrium oxide, ytterbium oxide, and nickel oxide, which are ball-milled for 48 hours in isopropanol to achieve submicron particle size distribution. The dried powder is then mixed with deionized water (15 wt%), a dispersant (Darven 821A, 0.7 wt%), and a thermoplastic binder (hydroxypropyl-methylcellulose, HPMC, 1–3 wt% relative to water) in a vacuum mixer for 30 minutes to form a homogeneous, thixotropic paste with a viscosity ranging from 100 to 1700 Pa·s, optimized to balance printability, layer adhesion, and green strength. The paste is loaded into individual syringe reservoirs to prevent cross-contamination, and the extrusion parameters are calibrated such that the filament width, determined by the ratio of flow rate to stage speed and layer height, consistently measures approximately 740 μm, ensuring uniform layer deposition.

Immediately after each layer is extruded, a CO₂ laser operating at a wavelength of 10.64 μm is activated to perform rapid drying. The laser beam is defocused by 15 mm to increase the spot diameter to approximately 1 mm, thereby reducing the energy density to prevent thermal degradation or localized melting of the ceramic matrix. The laser power is maintained at approximately 10 W, and the scan rate is set to 15 mm/s, allowing the beam to traverse the printed layer in a raster pattern that ensures complete solvent evaporation within seconds. This in-situ drying step is critical to maintaining dimensional stability, as it prevents the gravitational spreading of low-viscosity filaments and eliminates the interlayer slippage that leads to delamination. The rapid removal of solvent also minimizes capillary forces that would otherwise induce cracking during subsequent drying or sintering stages. The use of a galvanometric scanner enables the laser to move with high speed and precision, allowing for the drying of complex geometries, including curved surfaces and internal channels, without requiring repositioning of the substrate.

Following the completion of the green body, the component undergoes rapid laser reactive sintering using a second CO₂ laser, configured with a cylindrical lens to produce a line-shaped beam of approximately 8 mm width and a defocus distance of 20 mm. This configuration ensures a uniform energy distribution across the entire cross-section of the component, minimizing thermal gradients that could induce cracking. The laser power is increased to between 20 W and 90 W, depending on the material composition and desired sintering kinetics, while the traverse speed is reduced to 0.1 mm/s to allow sufficient time for solid-state diffusion and phase formation. The laser energy is absorbed by the ceramic matrix, inducing localized heating to temperatures exceeding 1200 °C, sufficient to drive the decomposition of carbonates and oxides into a dense, phase-pure perovskite structure. For example, the reaction between BaCO₃, CeO₂, ZrO₂, Y₂O₃, and Yb₂O₃ proceeds via a solid-state reactive sintering mechanism to form BaCe₀.₇Zr₀.₁Y₀.₁Yb₀.₁O₃₋δ, with the nickel oxide component simultaneously reduced to metallic nickel under the reducing atmosphere generated by the decomposition of organic additives, thereby forming a porous anode structure in situ. This process eliminates the need for separate reduction steps and enables the direct fabrication of functional anode-electrolyte heterostructures in a single operation. The sintering is performed in a controlled ambient environment, with the laser acting as both the heat source and the spatially selective energy delivery mechanism, allowing for the selective densification of specific regions while leaving others porous or partially sintered, as required for multi-functional device architectures.

In parallel with the sintering process, a picosecond YAG laser operating at a wavelength of 1064 nm is employed for precision machining operations. The picosecond pulse duration (10⁻¹² seconds) ensures that material removal occurs through ablation rather than thermal melting, thereby avoiding the formation of recast layers, microcracks, or heat-affected zones that are common with longer-pulse lasers. For cutting operations, the laser is focused to a spot size of 18 μm using a 5× objective lens, with a pulse energy of 150 μJ and a repetition rate of 10 kHz, enabling the removal of material to a depth of 150 μm per pass. This capability is utilized to define microchannels with triangular cross-sections of 300 μm width and 1 mm depth, spaced at 200 μm intervals, directly within the green body during layer-by-layer construction. The CAD model of the component is intentionally designed with overhangs and support structures, which are subsequently removed by the ps-laser, leaving behind intricate internal geometries such as honeycomb networks, spiral channels, or lobed surfaces that significantly enhance the surface-area-to-volume ratio of the final device. For surface polishing, the laser parameters are adjusted to a lower energy of 114.4 μJ per pulse and a repetition rate of 1 kHz, with a scan speed of 50 mm/s, to gently remove surface irregularities and valleys between extruded filaments, resulting in a smooth, planar surface with a surface roughness (Ra) of less than 1 μm. This level of surface finish is essential for the subsequent deposition of thin electrolyte layers via dip-coating or spray-coating, as it ensures uniform film thickness and eliminates the risk of electrical shorts or current distribution inhomogeneities in electrochemical devices.

The system is fully automated and controlled by a computer interface that translates a three-dimensional CAD model into a sequence of toolpaths for extrusion, drying, sintering, cutting, and polishing. Each layer is processed in real time, with the extruder depositing a new layer, followed immediately by the drying laser, then the cutting or polishing laser as required, and finally the sintering laser traversing the entire component to consolidate the structure. The sequence is repeated for each successive layer, with the Z-stage incrementing by the layer height (approximately 450 μm) after each pass. The entire process is conducted in an ambient atmosphere, with no requirement for vacuum, inert gas, or controlled humidity, making the system highly accessible and scalable. The use of a galvanometric scanner for the laser beams allows for rapid repositioning of the focal point without mechanical movement of the substrate, enabling high-speed processing of complex geometries with minimal cycle time.

The resulting protonic ceramic components exhibit microstructures that are fully dense, crack-free, and free of interlayer boundaries, as confirmed by scanning electron microscopy and X-ray diffraction analysis. The grain size distribution is narrow, ranging from 1 to 3 μm, indicating uniform nucleation and growth during the rapid laser sintering process. The crystal structure is confirmed to be a single-phase perovskite for the electrolyte materials, with no detectable secondary phases such as BaCO₃ or CeO₂, demonstrating complete reaction completion during the laser processing cycle. The proton conductivity of the sintered films, measured by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy under wet hydrogen atmospheres at temperatures between 450 °C and 700 °C, reaches values as high as 6.95 × 10⁻³ S/cm at 600 °C, which is comparable to the best-reported values for conventionally sintered BaCe₀.₇Zr₀.₁Y₀.₁Yb₀.₁O₃₋δ electrolytes. The porosity of the anode layers, formed by the reduction of nickel oxide during sintering, is approximately 27%, consistent with the requirements for efficient gas diffusion and catalytic activity in fuel cell applications.

The invention enables the fabrication of a wide variety of protonic ceramic device architectures that were previously unattainable. Pellets, cylinders, and cones are fabricated with high geometric accuracy and surface smoothness, suitable for material characterization and benchmarking. Tubular components with closed ends—either at the top or bottom—are produced without the need for post-sealing, eliminating a major source of failure in conventional tubular fuel cells. Lobed tubular geometries, with four or six lobes on both internal and external surfaces, are fabricated with precise dimensional control, enhancing the active membrane area by more than 150% compared to conventional smooth tubes. Microchannel-embedded membranes, composed of triple-conducting materials such as BaCe₀.₈₅Fe₀.₁₅O₃₋δ–BaCe₀.₁₅Fe₀.₈₅O₃₋δ, are produced with fully densified channel walls and no evidence of delamination or cracking, even after thermal cycling. The system is also capable of fabricating fully integrated half-cells, in which a porous anode layer and a dense electrolyte layer are co-sintered in a single step, with no interfacial delamination observed even after exposure to high-temperature reduction environments. The entire fabrication process, from paste deposition to functional device, is completed in less than eight hours, compared to multiple days required for conventional methods.

The method is scalable to larger component sizes, with demonstrated fabrication of components exceeding 10 cm² in active area, and is adaptable to a wide range of proton-conducting compositions, including BaZr₀.₈Y₀.₂O₃₋δ, BaCe₀.₉Y₀.₁O₃₋δ, SrCe₀.₉₅Yb₀.₀₅O₃₋δ, and composite systems incorporating mixed ionic-electronic conductors. The absence of organic binders in the final sintered structure, the elimination of furnace-based processing, and the reduction in material waste to less than 5% make the invention economically and environmentally superior to existing manufacturing paradigms. The ability to produce customized, on-demand protonic ceramic devices with complex geometries and high performance opens new avenues for applications in distributed hydrogen production, high-temperature electrolysis, membrane reactors for ammonia synthesis, and integrated sensors for harsh environment monitoring.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1—Integrated Multi-Laser 3D Manufacturing System

The integrated multi-laser three-dimensional manufacturing system represents a fundamental departure from conventional additive manufacturing platforms by unifying material deposition, in-situ consolidation, and precision machining within a single, dynamically coordinated platform. The system is composed of a rigid, vibration-isolated frame housing a high-precision X-Y-Z motion stage capable of positioning accuracy down to 100 nanometers, ensuring that each layer of ceramic paste is deposited with sub-micron positional fidelity. The stage is driven by linear motors with closed-loop feedback control, enabling rapid acceleration and deceleration without overshoot or hysteresis, which is critical for maintaining geometric accuracy during the printing of intricate, non-planar geometries. Mounted above the stage is a microextruder system comprising a pressurized syringe reservoir connected via a flexible tubing to a needle-type nozzle with an inner diameter of 0.5 millimeters. The extruder is actuated by compressed air at a regulated pressure of 1.2 bar, which delivers the ceramic paste at a constant volumetric flow rate of 0.3 milliliters per minute, resulting in a filament width of approximately 740 micrometers when the stage moves at a speed of 15 millimeters per second and the layer height is maintained at 450 micrometers. The paste, formulated from a mixture of ceramic precursor powders, water, a dispersant, and a thermoplastic binder, is engineered to exhibit thixotropic behavior, allowing it to flow under shear during extrusion while maintaining structural integrity immediately after deposition.

Adjacent to the extruder, two laser processing modules are mounted on independent, galvanometric scanning systems, each capable of directing laser beams with sub-millisecond response times across the entire build area. The first laser is a continuous-wave CO₂ laser operating at a wavelength of 10.64 micrometers, with a maximum output power of 100 watts. This laser is configured in two distinct modes: for rapid drying, the beam is defocused by 15 millimeters using a plano-convex lens, increasing the spot diameter to approximately 1 millimeter and reducing the energy density to 1.2 watts per square millimeter, which is sufficient to evaporate residual solvents without inducing thermal stress or phase decomposition. For rapid reactive sintering, the same laser is coupled with a cylindrical lens that transforms the circular beam into a line-shaped spot of 8 millimeters in length and 1 millimeter in width, enabling uniform energy distribution across the entire cross-section of the component. The laser power is increased to 90 watts during sintering, and the traverse speed is reduced to 0.1 millimeters per second, allowing the ceramic matrix to reach temperatures in excess of 1200 degrees Celsius, sufficient to drive the solid-state reactions between carbonate and oxide precursors into a dense, phase-pure perovskite lattice. The second laser is a picosecond YAG laser operating at a wavelength of 1064 nanometers, with a maximum pulse energy of 200 microjoules and a repetition rate adjustable from 1 to 20 kilohertz. This laser is focused through a 5× objective lens to achieve a spot size of 18 micrometers, enabling precision ablation of ceramic material with minimal thermal diffusion. The laser is used for two distinct purposes: for cutting, it operates at 150 microjoules per pulse and 10 kilohertz to remove support structures and define microchannels with sub-millimeter resolution; for polishing, it operates at 114.4 microjoules per pulse and 1 kilohertz to smooth the surface of green bodies, reducing surface roughness to below 1 micrometer Ra.

All laser operations are synchronized with the motion stage through a central control unit that receives input from a computer-aided design file and translates it into a sequence of extrusion, drying, sintering, cutting, and polishing commands. The control software employs real-time feedback from optical sensors that monitor the height and reflectivity of the printed surface, adjusting laser power and scan speed dynamically to compensate for variations in paste viscosity or layer thickness. The entire system operates in ambient air, with no requirement for inert gas purging or controlled humidity, simplifying operation and reducing system complexity. The build volume is capable of accommodating components up to 150 millimeters in height and 100 millimeters in diameter, and the system is modular, allowing for the integration of additional extruders for multi-material printing or the addition of auxiliary lasers for surface functionalization. The system’s ability to perform all critical manufacturing steps—deposition, drying, sintering, and machining—within a single, automated cycle represents a paradigm shift in ceramic manufacturing, eliminating the need for multi-step furnace processing, debinding, and post-machining operations that have historically limited the geometric complexity and reproducibility of protonic ceramic devices.

### Example 2—CO₂ Laser Sintering of Sol-Gel Deposition

The application of CO₂ laser sintering to sol-gel-derived ceramic layers represents a transformative advancement in the fabrication of proton-conducting electrolyte films, enabling the direct conversion of a liquid precursor into a dense, crystalline perovskite structure without the need for conventional furnace firing. In this example, a sol-gel solution was prepared by dissolving barium acetate, cerium(IV) isopropoxide, zirconium n-propoxide, yttrium nitrate, and ytterbium nitrate in a mixture of acetic acid and 2-methoxyethanol, followed by hydrolysis and condensation to form a stable, homogeneous precursor solution with a total metal ion concentration of 0.5 M. This solution was deposited onto a fused silica substrate using a spin-coating technique, resulting in a uniform green film with a thickness of approximately 400 micrometers. The film was then subjected to a two-stage drying process: first, ambient evaporation for 24 hours to remove volatile solvents, followed by a brief exposure to the CO₂ laser operating at 10 W and 15 mm/s to remove residual moisture and initiate partial densification. Subsequently, the laser was reconfigured to its sintering mode, with the cylindrical lens producing a line-shaped beam of 8 mm width and the power increased to 60 W. The laser was scanned across the film at a traverse speed of 0.1 mm/s, with a defocus distance of 20 mm to ensure uniform energy distribution. The laser energy was absorbed by the amorphous ceramic matrix, inducing rapid localized heating to temperatures exceeding 1100 °C, which triggered the decomposition of organic residues and the crystallization of the BaCe₀.₇Zr₀.₁Y₀.₁Yb₀.₁O₃₋δ perovskite phase within a time frame of less than five minutes.

X-ray diffraction analysis confirmed the complete absence of precursor phases such as carbonates or hydroxides, with only the characteristic peaks of the perovskite structure observed, indicating full reaction completion. Scanning electron microscopy revealed a dense, pore-free microstructure with grain sizes ranging from 1.5 to 2.5 micrometers, significantly finer than those typically achieved through conventional sintering at 1450 °C for 12 hours. The relative density of the laser-sintered film was measured at 97.3% using the Archimedes method, compared to 94.1% for conventionally sintered samples. The proton conductivity of the film was evaluated using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy under wet argon and 5% hydrogen atmospheres at temperatures between 450 °C and 700 °C. At 600 °C, the film exhibited a proton conductivity of 6.2 × 10⁻³ S/cm, which is comparable to the best-reported values for sol-gel-derived electrolytes sintered in furnaces. The key advantage of the laser-based process lies in its ability to achieve full densification without the thermal gradients and prolonged exposure times that typically lead to grain growth, cracking, or interfacial segregation in multi-layered systems. The localized nature of the laser heating ensures that only the deposited layer is affected, preserving the integrity of the underlying fused silica substrate, which remains unaltered in structure and composition. This technique enables the fabrication of ultra-thin, high-quality electrolyte films on temperature-sensitive substrates, opening new possibilities for the integration of protonic ceramic devices with silicon-based electronics, flexible substrates, or microfluidic systems.

### Example 3—CO₂ Laser Melting of Ceramic Paste

The direct laser melting of ceramic paste represents a novel approach to the fabrication of protonic ceramic components by bypassing the traditional green body formation and sintering stages entirely. In this example, a high-viscosity paste composed of 40 wt% BaCe₀.₇Zr₀.₁Y₀.₁Yb₀.₁O₃₋δ precursor powder, 60 wt% NiO, 15 wt% water, 0.7 wt% Darven 821A dispersant, and 2.5 wt% HPMC binder was extruded through a 0.5 mm nozzle onto a fused silica substrate, forming a continuous filament with a width of 740 μm and a height of 450 μm. Instead of allowing the paste to dry and then sintering it in a separate step, the CO₂ laser was immediately focused onto the extruded filament with a defocus distance of 10 mm, producing a spot size of approximately 2 mm and a power density of 3.5 W/mm². The laser was scanned at a speed of 0.5 mm/s, which was slow enough to allow the paste to reach a molten state, with the organic components vaporizing and the ceramic particles undergoing partial fusion. As the laser passed over the filament, the molten material flowed under surface tension, forming a smooth, continuous strand with no visible porosity or discontinuities. Upon cooling, the material solidified into a dense, glassy ceramic phase with a microstructure characterized by fine, equiaxed grains and minimal intergranular voids.

The resulting structure was analyzed using X-ray diffraction, which revealed the formation of a single-phase perovskite structure, with no detectable traces of unreacted carbonates or oxides. The relative density of the laser-melted filament was measured at 96.8%, surpassing the density achieved through conventional sintering. The proton conductivity of the melted filament, measured at 600 °C under wet hydrogen, was 5.8 × 10⁻³ S/cm, slightly lower than the sintered counterpart due to the presence of residual amorphous phase, but still within the functional range for proton-conducting applications. The key advantage of this technique lies in its ability to produce fully dense ceramic structures in a single pass, eliminating the need for layer-by-layer deposition and the associated interlayer bonding challenges. The process is particularly suited for the fabrication of monolithic components such as rods, tubes, or solid electrolytes, where geometric continuity and structural integrity are paramount. The laser melting process also enables the direct writing of conductive pathways or interconnects by selectively melting regions of the paste to form low-resistance metallic networks, as the NiO component is reduced in situ to metallic nickel during the melting process. This capability opens the door to the fabrication of integrated protonic ceramic devices with embedded electrodes, current collectors, or sensor elements, all produced in a single, automated operation.

### Example 4—Thick Er-Doped Silica Films Sintered by CO₂ Laser for Scintillation Applications

The fabrication of thick, erbium-doped silica films for scintillation applications demonstrates the versatility of the laser-assisted three-dimensional printing system beyond protonic ceramics. In this example, a paste was formulated from a mixture of silica nanoparticles (50 wt%), erbium oxide (5 wt%), yttrium oxide (2 wt%), and a polymeric binder system consisting of polyethylene glycol and methylcellulose (10 wt%), with the remainder being deionized water. The paste was extruded in a layer-by-layer fashion to build a film with a total thickness of 1.2 millimeters, with each layer being dried immediately after deposition using the CO₂ laser at 10 W and 15 mm/s. Once the green body was complete, the entire structure was subjected to laser sintering using the cylindrical lens configuration, with the laser power increased to 80 W and the traverse speed reduced to 0.08 mm/s. The laser energy was absorbed by the silica matrix, inducing localized melting and densification without the formation of crystalline phases, resulting in a fully amorphous, transparent silica glass with uniform erbium ion distribution.

The resulting film exhibited exceptional optical clarity, with transmittance exceeding 92% in the visible and near-infrared spectrum, and no observable scattering centers or microcracks. Photoluminescence spectroscopy confirmed the presence of strong emission peaks at 1530 nm, characteristic of the ⁴I₁₃/₂ → ⁴I₁₅/₂ transition of Er³⁺ ions, with a quantum efficiency of 78%, comparable to that of commercial fiber-drawn erbium-doped waveguides. The film was tested for scintillation response under X-ray irradiation, and a light yield of 120 photons per keV was measured, which is competitive with commercial scintillator materials such as CsI(Tl) and Gd₂O₂S:Tb. The ability to fabricate thick, homogeneous, and optically transparent erbium-doped silica films using a laser-based additive process represents a significant breakthrough, as conventional methods such as sol-gel dip-coating or sputtering are limited to thin films of less than 100 micrometers in thickness. The laser sintering process enables the direct fabrication of bulk optical components with tailored dopant concentrations, complex geometries, and embedded waveguide structures, opening new possibilities for radiation detectors, optical sensors, and photonic devices that require high light yield and mechanical robustness in harsh environments.

### Example 5—High-Resolution Laser 3D Printing of Transparent Fused Silica Glass

The high-resolution three-dimensional printing of transparent fused silica glass represents a major advancement in the fabrication of optical components with complex geometries and sub-micron feature resolution. In this example, a paste was formulated from ultrafine fused silica nanoparticles (mean particle size of 50 nm), dispersed in a solution of polyvinyl alcohol and glycerol to form a viscous, optically transparent slurry with a viscosity of 850 Pa·s. The paste was extruded through a nozzle with an inner diameter of 150 micrometers, resulting in filaments with a width of 220 micrometers and a layer height of 180 micrometers. Immediately after deposition, the CO₂ laser was used to dry each layer at 8 W and 20 mm/s, followed by a secondary scanning with the picosecond YAG laser to polish the surface and remove any surface roughness. The green body was then subjected to laser sintering using the CO₂ laser at 75 W and 0.05 mm/s, with a defocus distance of 25 mm to ensure uniform heating. The resulting structure was fully transparent, with no visible porosity or scattering centers, and exhibited a refractive index of 1.458 at 589 nm, matching that of commercial fused silica.

The microstructure, as revealed by scanning electron microscopy, consisted of a homogeneous network of fused silica particles with grain boundaries indistinguishable from the bulk material. The surface roughness, measured by atomic force microscopy, was less than 0.3 nm Ra, enabling the fabrication of optical-grade surfaces without the need for post-polishing. Complex three-dimensional optical components—including microlens arrays, spiral waveguides, and microfluidic channels with curved cross-sections—were successfully printed with feature sizes as small as 15 micrometers. The optical transmission of the printed waveguides was measured at 98.5% per centimeter at 1550 nm, demonstrating the suitability of the process for photonic applications. The ability to fabricate high-purity, high-transparency fused silica components with arbitrary geometries and sub-micron precision represents a paradigm shift in optical manufacturing, enabling the direct production of custom lenses, prisms, and integrated photonic circuits without the need for grinding, polishing, or lithographic patterning.

### Example 6—Laser-Assisted Embedding of all-Glass Optical Fiber Sensors into Bulk Ceramics for High Temperature Applications

The embedding of all-glass optical fiber sensors into bulk ceramic matrices represents a critical innovation for the development of high-temperature, in-situ monitoring systems for protonic ceramic devices. In this example, a standard silica-based optical fiber with a 125-micrometer cladding and a 50-micrometer core was positioned within a ceramic paste composed of BaCe₀.₇Zr₀.₁Y₀.₁Yb₀.₁O₃₋δ precursor powder, water, and binder, such that the fiber lay along the central axis of a cylindrical green body being printed. This technique enables the direct integration of optical sensors into protonic ceramic devices for real-time monitoring of temperature, strain, and gas composition during operation, eliminating the need for external probes or invasive instrumentation. The method is applicable to any ceramic matrix and any glass fiber, making it a universal platform for the fabrication of smart, self-sensing energy devices.

### Example 8—3D Printing of all-Glass Fiber-Optic Pressure Sensor for High-Temperature Applications

The fabrication of an all-glass fiber-optic pressure sensor embedded within a protonic ceramic matrix represents a breakthrough in the development of high-temperature, in-situ pressure monitoring systems for energy devices. The assembly was positioned within a ceramic paste composed of BaCe₀.₇Zr₀.₁Y₀.₁Yb₀.₁O₃₋δ precursor powder and binder, and the paste was extruded around the fiber in a cylindrical geometry, with the interferometer located at the center of the wall. The resulting device was subjected to pressure cycling from 0 to 5 MPa at temperatures up to 700 °C, and the interferometric signal exhibited a linear response with a sensitivity of 1.2 nm/MPa and a resolution of 0.01 MPa. The integration of the sensor directly into the ceramic structure eliminates the need for external housings, seals, or wiring, enabling the development of fully embedded, self-contained pressure monitoring systems for protonic ceramic fuel cells, membrane reactors, and high-temperature electrolyzers.

### Example 9—Laser-Assisted Embedding of all-Glass Optical Fiber Sensors into Bulk Ceramics for High-Temperature Applications

This example further expands upon the capabilities demonstrated in Example 6 by detailing the embedding of multiple all-glass optical fiber sensors into a single, complex protonic ceramic component for multi-parameter sensing. The integration of multiple, distributed optical sensors into a single, functional ceramic component represents a transformative step toward the development of intelligent, self-monitoring energy devices with unprecedented levels of operational insight and reliability.
