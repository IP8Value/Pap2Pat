# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of spoken language understanding systems, particularly to neural network-based methods for extracting semantic slot values from natural language utterances in conversational dialogue systems when only sentence-level annotations are available and when the set of possible slot values includes out-of-vocabulary terms that are not predefined or enumerable. The invention specifically addresses the challenge of slot value prediction in environments where word-level alignment between utterance tokens and semantic annotations is absent, and where traditional sequence labeling approaches such as conditional random fields or recurrent neural networks with BIO tagging are infeasible due to the lack of fine-grained annotations. The invention further pertains to the integration of pointer networks within sequence-to-sequence architectures to enable dynamic selection of slot values directly from the input utterance, thereby circumventing the limitations of fixed vocabularies and eliminating the need for handcrafted rules, lexical mappings, or domain-specific dictionaries. This technology is applicable to voice assistants, customer service chatbots, intelligent virtual agents, and any automated dialogue system requiring robust semantic interpretation of user inputs under conditions of sparse, noisy, or incomplete training data.

## BACKGROUND

In modern spoken language understanding systems, the accurate extraction of semantic information from user utterances is a critical component for enabling natural and effective human-machine interaction. This process, commonly referred to as slot filling, involves identifying and classifying key pieces of information—such as locations, times, names, quantities, or preferences—within a spoken or text-based utterance and mapping them to predefined semantic categories or slots. Historically, slot filling has been treated as a sequence labeling problem, where each word in an utterance is assigned a label according to a tagging scheme such as BIO (Begin, Inside, Outside), indicating whether the word is part of a slot value and its position within that value. Methods based on conditional random fields (CRFs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs) have been widely adopted for this purpose, achieving notable success in domains where high-quality, word-level annotated datasets are available, such as the ATIS corpus. However, the requirement for such fine-grained annotations imposes substantial burdens on data collection and annotation efforts, often necessitating manual labor by linguistic experts to align each word in an utterance with its corresponding semantic role. This process is not only time-consuming and expensive but also highly domain-dependent, making it impractical for rapid deployment across new domains or languages.

In many real-world applications, particularly in emerging or low-resource domains, only sentence-level annotations are available. For example, an utterance such as “I want a moderately priced restaurant” may be annotated with the semantic pair “pricerange=moderate,” without any indication of which words in the utterance correspond to the value “moderate.” In such cases, prior systems have attempted to bridge the gap between sentence-level annotations and word-level predictions by employing handcrafted rules or manually constructed word aliases to infer alignments. These rules typically rely on lexical matching, synonym dictionaries, or heuristic patterns to map phrases in the utterance to known slot values. However, such approaches are brittle and fragile; they fail to generalize across variations in phrasing, speech recognition errors, or domain-specific terminology. Studies have shown that even with carefully curated rules, alignment accuracy rarely exceeds 73%, and models trained on such noisy, imperfectly aligned data exhibit significantly degraded performance. Moreover, adapting these rules to new domains requires extensive manual effort, rendering them unsuitable for scalable or dynamic applications.

Alternative approaches have emerged that treat slot filling not as a sequence labeling task but as a classification problem, where the entire utterance is mapped to one or more predefined slot-value pairs using classifiers such as support vector machines (SVMs) or convolutional neural networks (CNNs). While these methods avoid the need for word-level annotations, they introduce new challenges. First, many slots—such as restaurant names, street addresses, or product titles—have an open-ended, potentially infinite set of possible values that cannot be predefined or enumerated in advance. This leads to severe data sparsity issues during training, as the classifier must learn to recognize and predict values that may appear only once or not at all in the training set. Second, these systems are inherently vulnerable to the out-of-vocabulary (OOV) problem, where previously unseen slot values—such as a newly opened restaurant or an unfamiliar street name—are completely unrecognizable to the model. Since the classifier operates over a fixed set of possible outputs, any value not present in the training vocabulary is assigned zero probability, resulting in complete failure to extract the correct information. This limitation is particularly acute in real-world conversational systems, where user inputs frequently contain novel, context-specific, or dynamically generated entities that defy predefinition.

Another class of methods attempts to circumvent these issues by leveraging generative models, such as sequence-to-sequence (Seq2Seq) architectures with attention mechanisms, which learn to map input utterances directly to output sequences of slot values without requiring explicit word-level alignment. These models operate by encoding the input utterance into a dense representation and then decoding a sequence of output tokens one at a time, using an attention mechanism to focus on relevant parts of the input. While such models offer greater flexibility than classification-based approaches, they still rely on a fixed target vocabulary, which must include all possible slot values seen during training. Consequently, they suffer from the same OOV limitations as classification models, often generating hallucinated or incorrect values that do not appear in the input utterance, especially when the true slot value is rare or unseen. For instance, given the utterance “I’d like to dine at the Blue Dragon,” a standard Seq2Seq model might generate “Chinese restaurant” instead of “Blue Dragon,” because the latter does not exist in its predefined vocabulary, even though the exact phrase appears verbatim in the input. This inability to reproduce input tokens directly undermines the reliability and fidelity of the predicted slot values.

Recent advances in neural sequence modeling have introduced pointer networks (Ptr-Net), a variant of the Seq2Seq architecture that enables the model to select output tokens by pointing to positions in the input sequence rather than generating them from a fixed vocabulary. Originally developed for combinatorial optimization tasks such as the traveling salesman problem, Ptr-Nets have since been successfully applied to tasks like abstractive summarization and question answering, where the output must include exact spans of text from the input. The key innovation of Ptr-Nets lies in their ability to dynamically reference elements of the input, thereby naturally handling out-of-vocabulary items without requiring them to be pre-defined or learned during training. This property makes Ptr-Nets uniquely suited to the problem of slot value prediction in unaligned data, where the correct value is often a direct substring of the utterance itself. However, no prior work has integrated this mechanism into a unified framework for slot filling in spoken language understanding systems under sentence-level supervision, nor has any system combined the generative capability of Seq2Seq models with the pointing mechanism of Ptr-Nets to simultaneously handle both enumerable and non-enumerable slot values in a single, end-to-end trainable architecture.

Furthermore, existing approaches fail to account for the practical reality that slot values in real-world dialogues are heterogeneous: some slots, such as “time” or “cuisine type,” have a finite and enumerable set of possible values, while others, such as “restaurant name” or “address,” are open-ended and context-dependent. Most systems either treat all slots uniformly as classification targets or apply separate models for enumerable and non-enumerable slots, introducing architectural complexity and requiring additional supervision or feature engineering. There exists no prior method that seamlessly unifies the two modes of prediction—generation from a fixed vocabulary and selection from the input utterance—within a single, coherent neural architecture that learns to decide, at each decoding step, whether to generate a value from a predefined set or to copy a token directly from the input, all without any manual alignment, rule-based preprocessing, or domain-specific lexicons. The absence of such a system represents a critical gap in the state of the art, particularly in applications demanding rapid deployment, low annotation overhead, and robust generalization to unseen entities.

The present invention fills this gap by introducing a novel neural generative model for slot value prediction that operates on unaligned, sentence-level annotated data and combines the strengths of attentional Seq2Seq models with pointer networks to enable hybrid generation and copying of slot values. This model does not require word-level annotations, does not rely on handcrafted rules or external dictionaries, and can dynamically adapt to arbitrary, previously unseen slot values by directly referencing them from the input utterance. It is trained end-to-end using only sentence-level semantic annotations and learns to balance between generating common slot values from a fixed vocabulary and pointing to exact phrases in the input for rare or novel values. The architecture is lightweight, scalable, and domain-agnostic, making it ideal for deployment in diverse conversational systems where data is scarce, annotations are coarse, and the vocabulary of slot values is open-ended and evolving.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a neural generative model for slot value prediction in spoken language understanding systems that operates on utterances annotated only at the sentence level, without requiring any word-level alignment or manual feature engineering. The model is designed to predict sequences of slot values from natural language inputs by dynamically choosing between two complementary modes of prediction: generating a value from a fixed, predefined vocabulary of enumerable slot values, and selecting a contiguous subsequence of words directly from the input utterance via a pointer mechanism. This hybrid architecture enables the model to accurately predict both common, enumerable slot values—such as “moderate,” “Italian,” or “7:00 PM”—and novel, non-enumerable slot values—such as “Blue Dragon Restaurant” or “123 Maple Street”—that are not present in the training vocabulary, thereby overcoming the out-of-vocabulary problem that plagues conventional classification and sequence labeling approaches.

The system comprises a shared encoder-decoder architecture with bidirectional recurrent neural network layers for encoding the input utterance and unidirectional recurrent layers for decoding the output slot value sequence. The encoder processes the input utterance as a sequence of word embeddings and produces a contextualized representation for each word. The decoder generates the output slot value sequence one token at a time, using an attention mechanism to compute relevance scores between the current decoder state and each encoded input word. At each decoding step, the model computes two probability distributions: one over a fixed vocabulary of known slot values, produced by a standard Seq2Seq generator, and another over the positions in the input utterance, produced by a pointer network. A dynamically learned switching mechanism, implemented as a sigmoidal gate function, determines the relative weight between these two distributions at each step, based on the current decoder input, decoder hidden state, and context vector derived from the attention mechanism. This gate is trained end-to-end and adapts automatically to the linguistic context, enabling the model to favor generation for common, predictable values and pointing for rare, context-specific values.

The fixed vocabulary used by the generator component is intentionally limited to slot values that are enumerable and likely to recur across utterances, such as predefined categories of cuisine, price ranges, or time intervals. Non-enumerable slot values, such as names of restaurants, addresses, or personal identifiers, are excluded from this vocabulary, as they are assumed to be unknown in advance and are instead handled exclusively by the pointer mechanism. The union of the fixed vocabulary and all unique words appearing in the input utterances forms an extended vocabulary over which the final prediction is computed. The model’s output at each time step is a weighted combination of the generator’s probability distribution and the pointer’s distribution over input positions, with the weighting determined by the learned gate. This allows the model to produce slot values that are either generated from a lexicon of known terms or copied verbatim from the input, without requiring any prior alignment between the utterance and the semantic annotation.

The system is trained using only sentence-level semantic annotations, meaning that for each utterance, the training data provides a set of slot-value pairs without indicating which words in the utterance correspond to which values. No word-level labels, alignment rules, or manual feature extraction are used during training or inference. The model learns to infer these correspondences implicitly through its attention and pointer mechanisms, enabling it to generalize to new domains with minimal data. The architecture is implemented using recurrent neural networks with gated recurrent units (GRUs), although other sequence modeling components such as LSTMs or transformers may be substituted without altering the core innovation. The entire system is trained end-to-end using maximum likelihood estimation on a corpus of utterance–slot-value pair annotations, with no additional supervision or external knowledge sources.

The invention achieves superior performance compared to prior methods in both standard and challenging conditions. On benchmark datasets such as DSTC2, the model achieves state-of-the-art results for slot value prediction and full spoken language understanding, even when trained on a fraction of the available data. In experiments designed to simulate severe out-of-vocabulary conditions—by subsampling training data to exclude most instances of non-enumerable slot values—the proposed model demonstrates significantly greater robustness than baseline systems, maintaining high accuracy while other models suffer substantial performance degradation. The model’s ability to copy exact phrases from the input allows it to correctly predict slot values that never appeared in the training set, such as newly introduced restaurant names or unfamiliar street addresses, without requiring any retraining or dictionary updates. Furthermore, the system requires no domain-specific rules, lexical mappings, or handcrafted features, making it easily adaptable to new languages, domains, or applications with minimal engineering effort.

The invention also enables the construction of complete spoken language understanding systems that integrate slot value prediction with dialog act classification and slot type assignment, all within a unified, end-to-end trainable framework. The slot value predictions produced by the model serve as input to downstream components that classify the type of each predicted value (e.g., determining whether “Blue Dragon” is a restaurant name or a dish), but these components do not influence the slot value prediction process itself. The entire pipeline operates without any manual intervention, rule-based alignment, or external knowledge bases, making it uniquely suited for deployment in real-world, low-resource, or rapidly evolving conversational systems. The model’s performance is not contingent on the availability of large annotated corpora, and it achieves results comparable to or exceeding those of prior state-of-the-art systems even when trained on as little as 20% of the original data, demonstrating its efficiency and scalability.

In summary, the invention provides a novel, fully neural, and annotation-efficient solution to the problem of slot filling in spoken language understanding, overcoming the limitations of existing methods by unifying generative and pointing mechanisms within a single, trainable architecture that operates without word-level annotations, handcrafted rules, or predefined slot value dictionaries. The model’s ability to dynamically switch between generating and copying slot values enables it to handle both enumerable and non-enumerable slots with high accuracy, robustness, and adaptability, making it a transformative advancement in the field of conversational AI.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The detailed implementation of the invention encompasses a neural architecture that integrates a sequence-to-sequence model with a pointer network to perform slot value prediction from utterances annotated only at the sentence level. The system is designed to operate without any prior knowledge of word-level alignments, without relying on manually constructed rules or lexical mappings, and without requiring a predefined dictionary of all possible slot values. The architecture is composed of three core components: an encoder that contextualizes the input utterance, a decoder that generates the output slot value sequence, and a hybrid prediction mechanism that dynamically selects between generating a token from a fixed vocabulary and pointing to a word in the input utterance. These components are trained end-to-end using only sentence-level semantic annotations, meaning that for each utterance, the training data provides a set of slot-value pairs without indicating which words in the utterance correspond to which values. The model learns to infer these correspondences implicitly through its attention and pointer mechanisms, enabling it to generalize to new domains with minimal data.

The encoder is implemented as a single-layer bidirectional gated recurrent unit (GRU) network, which processes the input utterance as a sequence of word embeddings. Each word in the utterance is first mapped to a dense vector representation using a learned embedding matrix, which is initialized randomly and updated during training. The bidirectional GRU processes the sequence in both forward and backward directions, producing for each word a contextualized hidden state that captures information from both preceding and succeeding words in the utterance. The resulting sequence of hidden states serves as the input to the attention mechanism and forms the basis for all subsequent decoding operations. The use of bidirectional encoding ensures that the model has access to full contextual information when making predictions, which is critical for resolving ambiguities in slot value boundaries—for example, distinguishing between “moderate” as a price range and “moderate” as a descriptor of service quality. The hidden states are not subject to any external constraints or normalization beyond standard recurrent network regularization, and their dimensionality is fixed at 128, although this parameter may be adjusted depending on the complexity of the domain or the size of the training corpus.

The decoder is implemented as a single-layer unidirectional GRU that generates the output slot value sequence one token at a time. At each decoding step, the decoder receives as input the previously generated token (or a special start-of-sequence symbol at the first step), the previous decoder hidden state, and a context vector computed by the attention mechanism. The context vector is derived by computing a weighted sum of the encoder’s hidden states, where the weights are determined by an attention function that measures the relevance of each input word to the current decoding state. The attention function is implemented using a feedforward neural network with a single hidden layer and a tanh activation, followed by a softmax operation to produce a probability distribution over the input positions. This distribution indicates the likelihood that each word in the input utterance is relevant to the next output token. The attention mechanism is computed independently at each decoding step and is conditioned on the current decoder state and the previously generated output, allowing the model to focus on different parts of the input as the slot value sequence unfolds. For example, when predicting the slot value “asian oriental food,” the model may first attend to the word “asian,” then shift attention to “oriental,” and finally to “food,” thereby capturing the sequential structure of the multi-word value.

The core innovation of the invention lies in the hybrid prediction mechanism that combines a standard Seq2Seq generator with a pointer network to produce the final output at each decoding step. The generator component operates over a fixed vocabulary of enumerable slot values, which includes only those values that are likely to recur across utterances and can be reasonably enumerated in advance. This vocabulary is constructed by collecting all unique slot values from the training set that correspond to slots such as “pricerange,” “cuisine,” “time,” or “numberofpeople,” which have a finite and predictable set of possible values. Non-enumerable slot values—such as “restaurantname,” “address,” “contactnumber,” or “specialrequest”—are explicitly excluded from this vocabulary, as they are assumed to be open-ended and context-dependent. The generator produces a probability distribution over this fixed vocabulary using a linear transformation of the decoder’s hidden state, followed by a softmax operation. This distribution represents the model’s belief about which known slot value is most likely to be generated next.

Simultaneously, the pointer network component produces a probability distribution over the positions in the input utterance, indicating the likelihood that the next output token should be copied from a specific word in the input. This distribution is computed using the same attention weights that are used to derive the context vector, but instead of summing the encoder states to form a context, the attention weights are treated as direct probabilities over input positions. This means that the pointer network does not generate new tokens but selects existing ones from the input utterance. For instance, if the input utterance contains the phrase “Blue Dragon Restaurant” and the model is predicting the slot value for “restaurantname,” the pointer network may assign high attention weights to the words “Blue,” “Dragon,” and “Restaurant,” thereby enabling the model to copy the exact phrase as the predicted value. This mechanism inherently handles out-of-vocabulary terms, as any word appearing in the input—even if it was never seen during training—can be selected as a valid output.

The final prediction at each decoding step is a weighted combination of the generator’s distribution and the pointer’s distribution. This combination is controlled by a learned switching parameter, denoted as p_t, which is computed at each time step t based on the current decoder input d_t, the decoder hidden state s_t, and the context vector c_t. The switching parameter is computed using a sigmoidal function applied to a linear combination of these three inputs, with trainable weight vectors w_d, w_s, and w_c. The sigmoid ensures that p_t is constrained between 0 and 1, representing the probability that the model should generate a token from the fixed vocabulary, while 1 − p_t represents the probability that it should copy a token from the input. This gating mechanism is fully differentiable and trained end-to-end alongside the rest of the model, allowing the system to learn, from data, when to generate and when to point. For example, when predicting a common value such as “moderate,” the model learns to assign a high p_t value, favoring generation from the fixed vocabulary. When predicting a novel value such as “The Spice Garden,” the model learns to assign a low p_t value, favoring copying from the input. This adaptive behavior eliminates the need for manual rules or heuristics to determine which strategy to use in different contexts.

The extended vocabulary, which is used to compute the final output probability distribution, is defined as the union of the fixed vocabulary and all unique words that appear in the input utterances of the training corpus. The final probability distribution over the extended vocabulary is computed by summing the generator’s probabilities for words in the fixed vocabulary and the pointer’s probabilities for words that appear in the input. For words that appear in both the fixed vocabulary and the input, their probabilities are combined additively, allowing the model to reinforce predictions that are supported by both generation and pointing. This ensures that common values that are also present in the input are given higher confidence, while novel values that are only present in the input are still assigned non-zero probability. The model is trained using maximum likelihood estimation, where the objective is to maximize the probability of the ground-truth slot value sequence given the input utterance. The loss function is computed as the negative log-likelihood of the correct output sequence, averaged over all training examples. Backpropagation is performed through the entire architecture, including the encoder, decoder, attention mechanism, and switching gate, ensuring that all components are jointly optimized.

During inference, the model generates the output slot value sequence autoregressively, one token at a time. At each step, the model computes the final probability distribution over the extended vocabulary and selects the token with the highest probability as the next output. This process continues until a special end-of-sequence symbol is generated or a maximum length is reached. The output is then parsed into individual slot-value pairs according to the slot types predicted by downstream components, which are not part of the core invention but may be integrated into a complete spoken language understanding system. For example, a separate classifier may be trained to assign slot types to each predicted value, such as determining whether “Blue Dragon” corresponds to “restaurantname” or “dishname,” but this classification does not influence the slot value prediction process itself. The model operates independently of such downstream components, making it modular and reusable across different system architectures.

The training process requires only sentence-level annotations, meaning that for each utterance, the training data provides a set of slot-value pairs without any indication of which words in the utterance correspond to which values. For example, given the utterance “I want a moderately priced restaurant near Central Park,” the training label may be the pair “pricerange=moderate” and “location=Central Park,” without specifying that “moderate” corresponds to the word “moderately” or that “Central Park” corresponds to the phrase “Central Park.” The model learns to infer these alignments implicitly through its attention and pointer mechanisms, without any explicit supervision. This is a critical advantage over prior methods, which require word-level annotations or manual alignment rules to function. The absence of such requirements significantly reduces the cost and effort of data collection, making the system viable for low-resource domains or languages where annotated corpora are scarce.

The model is implemented using the Keras framework with TensorFlow as the backend, and all trainable parameters—including word embeddings, GRU weights, attention weights, and switching gate weights—are initialized randomly and updated using stochastic gradient descent with backpropagation. The dimensionality of the word embeddings is set to 100, the hidden state size is 128, and dropout is not applied during training, as the model achieves sufficient regularization through the attention mechanism and the inherent noise in sentence-level supervision. The training corpus is divided into training, development, and test sets, and hyperparameters are tuned on the development set to maximize performance on a validation metric such as F1 score. The model is trained for a fixed number of epochs, with early stopping applied if performance on the development set plateaus. No external knowledge sources, such as dictionaries, ontologies, or rule-based systems, are used at any stage of training or inference.

The system’s performance is evaluated on benchmark datasets such as DSTC2, which contains multi-turn dialogues in the restaurant search domain, annotated with dialog acts and slot-value pairs. The model is tested on both standard conditions and controlled OOV scenarios, where the training data is subsampled to exclude most instances of non-enumerable slot values, thereby increasing the out-of-vocabulary rate. In these experiments, the proposed model consistently outperforms baseline systems, including CNN-based classifiers, standard Seq2Seq models without pointer networks, and SVM-based slot-value pair classifiers. The model demonstrates superior recall on non-enumerable slots, where baseline systems fail to predict any value, and maintains high precision even under severe data scarcity. In particular, when trained on only 20% of the original training data, the model achieves performance comparable to or exceeding that of prior state-of-the-art systems trained on 100% of the data, demonstrating its data efficiency and robustness.

The model’s ability to handle out-of-vocabulary slot values is further validated through case studies and error analysis. In examples where the input utterance contains a novel restaurant name such as “The Golden Lotus,” the model correctly copies the phrase from the input, whereas the standard Seq2Seq model generates hallucinated values such as “golden restaurant” or “lotus dining,” and the CNN classifier outputs no prediction at all. Similarly, when the utterance contains a multi-word value such as “asian oriental food,” the model correctly copies all three words in sequence, whereas the standard Seq2Seq model may generate only “asian” or “oriental,” and the CNN model may fail to predict any value due to the high cardinality of the slot. The model also demonstrates resilience to speech recognition errors: even when the input utterance contains minor misrecognitions such as “Blue Dragon Resturant,” the model still correctly copies the phrase as-is, preserving the original form for downstream processing. This is in contrast to systems that rely on normalized or cleaned inputs, which would require additional preprocessing steps.

The system’s architecture is inherently scalable and domain-agnostic. Because it does not rely on predefined slot value dictionaries or handcrafted rules, it can be applied to any domain with minimal adaptation. For example, deploying the system in a hotel booking domain requires only a new corpus of utterances annotated with slot-value pairs such as “checkindate=June 5” or “roomtype=deluxe,” without any need to define new rules, update lexicons, or re-engineer features. The same architecture, with the same hyperparameters, can be trained on this new data and achieve high performance immediately. This contrasts sharply with prior systems, which require domain-specific alignment rules, custom feature extractors, or manual mapping tables for each new application. The model’s ability to generalize across domains without architectural changes represents a significant advancement in the field of spoken language understanding.

Furthermore, the system can be extended to handle multiple slot-value pairs within a single utterance by generating a sequence of slot-value tokens in a predefined order, such as “slotname=value slotname=value.” The model does not require explicit segmentation of the output into individual pairs; instead, it learns to generate the correct sequence of tokens that, when parsed, yield the intended slot-value pairs. This approach is more flexible than systems that predict slot-value pairs independently, as it allows for dependencies between slots and enables the model to recover from errors in one slot by leveraging context from others. For example, if the model predicts “pricerange=moderate restaurantname=Blue Dragon,” the sequence structure ensures that the two values are correctly associated with their respective slots, even if the slot names are not explicitly encoded in the input.

The model’s training and inference are computationally efficient, with training times comparable to those of standard Seq2Seq models and inference latency suitable for real-time deployment in conversational systems. The use of GRUs instead of more complex architectures such as transformers ensures low memory consumption and fast computation, making the system suitable for edge devices or low-power environments. The model can be deployed as a standalone component within a larger dialogue system or integrated into end-to-end frameworks that combine intent detection, slot filling, and response generation. Its modular design allows it to be replaced or upgraded without affecting other components of the system.

In summary, the invention provides a novel, fully neural, and annotation-efficient solution to the problem of slot value prediction in spoken language understanding systems. By unifying generative and pointing mechanisms within a single, end-to-end trainable architecture, the model overcomes the limitations of existing methods that rely on word-level annotations, handcrafted rules, or predefined vocabularies. The system’s ability to dynamically switch between generating and copying slot values enables it to handle both enumerable and non-enumerable slots with high accuracy, robustness, and adaptability. It requires no domain-specific knowledge, no manual alignment, and no external dictionaries, making it uniquely suited for deployment in real-world, low-resource, or rapidly evolving conversational systems. The invention represents a transformative advancement in the field of spoken language understanding, enabling the construction of more accurate, scalable, and adaptable dialogue systems with minimal annotation overhead.
