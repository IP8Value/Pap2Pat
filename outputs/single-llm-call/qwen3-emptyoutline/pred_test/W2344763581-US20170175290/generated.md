# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to a novel method for the direct, deterministic growth of single-crystalline III–V semiconductor materials on amorphous or non-lattice-matched substrates without the need for epitaxial alignment, high-temperature epitaxial reactors, or crystalline seed layers. Specifically, the invention encompasses a template-directed liquid-phase crystallization process that enables the formation of user-defined, geometrically complex, single-crystalline semiconductor structures with precise control over shape, size, orientation, and dopant concentration. The method is particularly applicable to indium phosphide (InP), gallium phosphide (GaP), and indium antimonide (InSb), but is fundamentally extendable to other III–V compound semiconductors through appropriate selection of group III metal precursors and group V reactant gases. The resulting structures exhibit optoelectronic properties comparable to those of conventionally epitaxially grown single-crystal wafers, including high carrier mobility, low defect density, sharp band-edge transitions, and tunable doping levels reaching degenerate concentrations. The invention further enables the integration of high-performance semiconductor devices on mechanically flexible, thermally sensitive, or economically scalable substrates such as glass, plastic, and silicon dioxide-coated silicon, thereby overcoming the fundamental limitations of traditional epitaxial growth techniques that require lattice-matched, rigid, and often expensive crystalline substrates. The process operates at relatively low temperatures (500–535 °C), employs a simple cold-wall or hot-wall furnace configuration, and utilizes a self-limiting growth mechanism governed by phosphorus diffusion kinetics and liquid-phase depletion zones, which inherently suppresses multi-nucleation and enforces single-crystal formation within predefined geometric templates. This approach eliminates the need for complex mask alignment, strain engineering, or post-growth transfer processes, and provides a scalable pathway for the monolithic fabrication of integrated photonic, electronic, and optoelectronic systems on non-traditional platforms.

## BACKGROUND

The fabrication of high-quality semiconductor devices has long been constrained by the requirement for crystalline substrates that are lattice-matched to the epitaxial film being grown. Traditional methods for producing III–V semiconductors such as indium phosphide, gallium arsenide, and indium antimonide rely on vapor-phase epitaxy, metalorganic chemical vapor deposition, or molecular beam epitaxy, all of which demand single-crystal substrates such as GaAs, InP, or Si with minimal lattice mismatch and thermal expansion coefficient differences. These constraints severely limit the range of viable substrates, restrict device integration to rigid, brittle, and costly wafers, and preclude the use of flexible, transparent, or low-cost materials such as glass, plastic, or even paper. Furthermore, the high temperatures and ultra-high vacuum environments required for epitaxial growth render these processes incompatible with standard complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) backend processing, limit scalability, and increase manufacturing costs. Attempts to overcome these limitations through heterogeneous integration—such as wafer bonding, lift-off, and transfer printing—introduce additional complexity, yield loss, interfacial defects, and alignment errors, while failing to preserve the single-crystalline integrity of the semiconductor over large areas or complex geometries. 

Existing techniques for patterning semiconductors at the micro- and nanoscale, including lithographic etching and selective area growth, are inherently limited by the underlying substrate’s crystallinity and the thermodynamic instability of non-epitaxial nucleation. Non-epitaxial growth on amorphous substrates typically results in polycrystalline films with numerous grain boundaries, dislocations, and compositional inhomogeneities that degrade carrier mobility, optical efficiency, and device reliability. Even when single-crystalline nanowires or microwires are grown on non-crystalline substrates via vapor-liquid-solid mechanisms, control over crystal orientation, lateral dimensions, and spatial arrangement remains highly stochastic, precluding the deterministic fabrication of complex device arrays or integrated circuits. Moreover, the ability to dope such non-epitaxial structures with precise, spatially controlled concentrations has remained elusive, particularly at levels sufficient to achieve degenerate conductivity without introducing excessive defect states or band-tailing effects that compromise optical performance. 

Recent advances in solution-phase or liquid-phase crystallization have demonstrated the potential for forming large-grain or single-crystalline materials under non-equilibrium conditions, yet none have achieved the combination of geometric determinism, material purity, and electronic quality required for practical device applications. In particular, prior methods lack the ability to confine the liquid precursor within a rigid, chemically inert template that enforces a single nucleation event per feature, thereby preventing secondary nucleation and enabling the growth of isolated, single-crystalline domains with user-defined shapes. Additionally, no prior technique has demonstrated the simultaneous control over crystal orientation, dopant concentration, and three-dimensional stacking in a single, scalable, low-temperature process that operates under ambient pressure conditions using commercially available precursors. The absence of such a method has hindered the realization of high-performance optoelectronic devices on unconventional substrates, limiting applications in flexible displays, wearable sensors, low-cost photodetectors, and monolithic 3D integrated circuits. There exists, therefore, a critical and unmet need for a growth technique that decouples semiconductor crystallinity from substrate crystallinity, enables deterministic shape control at the micron and submicron scale, supports in situ doping to degenerate levels, permits transfer to flexible substrates, and scales to wafer-level production—all while maintaining the optoelectronic quality of single-crystal epitaxial materials.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method for the direct, deterministic growth of single-crystalline III–V semiconductor structures on amorphous or non-lattice-matched substrates through a template-directed liquid-phase crystallization process. The method comprises the sequential deposition of a group III metal layer, a thin nucleation layer, and a conformal group IV oxide cap onto a substrate patterned with a predefined geometric layout, followed by exposure to a group V reactant gas at elevated temperatures under controlled partial pressure conditions. The nucleation layer, comprising a transition metal oxide such as molybdenum oxide, facilitates the initial reaction between the group III metal and the group V species, initiating the formation of a semiconductor nucleus. The group IV oxide cap, typically silicon oxide, mechanically confines the molten group III metal during growth, preventing lateral spreading and ensuring that the resulting semiconductor structure retains the exact shape of the original metal template. Crucially, the diffusion of the group V species through the oxide cap generates a localized supersaturation zone around the initial nucleus, which rapidly depletes the available group V reactant in the surrounding region, thereby suppressing secondary nucleation and enforcing the growth of a single crystal per template feature. The growth temperature is maintained between 500 °C and 535 °C, below the melting point of the group IV oxide and above the melting point of the group III metal, ensuring that the metal remains in a liquid state while the oxide template remains rigid and chemically inert. The partial pressure of the group V reactant is precisely controlled to modulate the nucleation rate and the size of the phosphorus depletion zone, enabling the growth of single-crystalline structures with lateral dimensions ranging from less than 150 nanometers to several tens of micrometers without multi-grain formation. 

The resulting semiconductor structures exhibit high crystalline quality, as confirmed by electron backscatter diffraction, transmission electron microscopy, and X-ray diffraction, which reveal the absence of grain boundaries, twinning, or polycrystallinity within individual features. The stoichiometry of the semiconductor is maintained at a 1:1 ratio of group III to group V elements, as verified by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy. The crystal orientation of the grown structures exhibits preferential texturing along the (1 0 n) family of directions, where n ranges from 1 to 2, indicating a thermodynamically favored growth axis that minimizes interfacial energy. The method further enables the in situ doping of the semiconductor during growth by introducing a gaseous dopant precursor, such as germane, into the growth chamber, allowing for precise control over carrier concentration up to degenerate levels of approximately 1.3 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³, as evidenced by a measurable blue shift in photoluminescence emission due to the Burstein–Moss effect and a corresponding reduction in the Urbach tail parameter, which indicates minimal band-edge disorder. The low growth temperature permits the use of a wide variety of substrates, including borosilicate glass, polyethylene terephthalate, and silicon dioxide-coated silicon, and enables the subsequent transfer of the grown semiconductor structures onto flexible or unconventional substrates using temporary adhesion layers such as polyamic acid. The process is inherently scalable, as the growth mechanism is self-limiting and spatially confined by the template, eliminating the need for complex reactor designs or uniformity control across large areas. Arrays of single-crystalline semiconductor features have been successfully grown across full 4-inch wafers with uniform optical and electrical properties, as demonstrated by photoluminescence imaging and device characterization. Furthermore, the method permits the fabrication of multilayer three-dimensional semiconductor architectures by stacking alternating layers of group III metal and group IV oxide prior to growth, enabling the monolithic integration of vertically stacked electronic or photonic components without the need for intermediate bonding or etching steps. The resulting semiconductor structures are suitable for the fabrication of high-performance electronic and optoelectronic devices, including long-channel metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistors with electron mobilities exceeding 675 cm² V⁻¹ s⁻¹, ON/OFF current ratios greater than 10⁵, and peak transconductance values of 100 S μm⁻¹, as well as photo-MOSFETs with responsivities exceeding 700 A W⁻¹ and specific detectivities approaching 10¹² Jones, rivaling state-of-the-art epitaxial detectors. The invention thus provides a transformative platform for the integration of high-quality III–V semiconductors on virtually any substrate, enabling unprecedented flexibility in device design, material selection, and manufacturing scalability.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention is directed to a method for the deterministic growth of single-crystalline III–V semiconductor structures on non-crystalline substrates through a template-directed liquid-phase crystallization process that operates under controlled thermal and chemical conditions. The process begins with the preparation of a substrate, which may be composed of silicon with a thermally grown silicon dioxide layer, borosilicate glass, or any other amorphous or polycrystalline material that is thermally stable at the growth temperature and chemically inert toward the group III metal and group V reactant. The substrate is first cleaned using standard semiconductor processing techniques, including solvent rinsing, plasma ashing, and deionized water rinsing, to remove organic and particulate contaminants. A photolithographic pattern is then defined on the substrate using conventional photoresist techniques, with the pattern geometry corresponding to the desired shape of the final semiconductor structure. The pattern may take the form of circles, rings, squares, letters, logos, or any other arbitrary two-dimensional geometry, with feature sizes ranging from 150 nanometers to 20 micrometers. After lithographic patterning, a thin nucleation layer is deposited over the entire substrate surface. This nucleation layer is composed of a transition metal oxide, preferably molybdenum oxide, with a thickness between 1 and 10 nanometers. The molybdenum oxide layer serves to reduce the interfacial energy between the group III metal and the underlying substrate, thereby promoting the formation of a uniform and continuous nucleation site upon exposure to the group V reactant. The nucleation layer is deposited by thermal evaporation or electron-beam evaporation under high vacuum conditions, with the substrate temperature maintained below 150 kelvin during deposition to ensure smooth, pinhole-free film formation. Following the deposition of the nucleation layer, a layer of group III metal is deposited on top. In the preferred embodiment, the group III metal is indium, deposited by thermal evaporation to a thickness sufficient to fully convert into the desired semiconductor upon reaction with the group V species. The thickness of the indium layer is typically between 50 and 200 nanometers, depending on the desired final thickness of the semiconductor structure. The indium deposition is performed with the substrate chuck cooled to less than 150 kelvin to minimize surface diffusion and ensure a smooth, continuous film with minimal roughness. After the indium layer is deposited, a conformal cap layer of group IV oxide is applied over the entire structure. In the preferred embodiment, the group IV oxide is silicon oxide, deposited by plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition or thermal evaporation of silicon monoxide in an oxygen-containing atmosphere. The thickness of the silicon oxide cap layer is selected to be between 10 and 100 nanometers, sufficient to mechanically confine the molten indium during growth while allowing slow, controlled diffusion of the group V reactant through its lattice. The silicon oxide cap is deposited such that it fully encapsulates the indium layer within the patterned regions, while leaving the surrounding areas exposed. After deposition, a lift-off process is performed to remove excess material from the non-patterned regions, leaving behind only the desired geometric features composed of the nucleation layer, indium layer, and silicon oxide cap. To ensure complete encapsulation of the indium within the patterned features, angled evaporation is employed to coat the side walls of the indium structures with an additional layer of silicon oxide, with thicknesses ranging from 4 to 50 nanometers. This angled deposition step is critical to prevent lateral diffusion of the molten indium during growth and to ensure that the resulting semiconductor structure retains the exact shape of the original indium template. The entire structure is then transferred to a low-pressure furnace, which may be either a hot-wall or cold-wall chemical vapor deposition system, depending on the scale of production. The furnace is evacuated to a base pressure below 10⁻⁵ torr and then backfilled with a carrier gas, typically hydrogen, at a total pressure between 100 and 300 torr. The growth temperature is then ramped to a value between 500 and 535 degrees Celsius, which is above the melting point of indium (156.6 °C) but well below the melting point of silicon oxide (approximately 1700 °C) and the decomposition temperature of the group V reactant. Once the desired temperature is reached, a group V reactant gas is introduced into the furnace. In the preferred embodiment, phosphine (PH₃) is used as the phosphorus source, diluted in hydrogen to achieve a partial pressure between 0.1 and 10 torr. The phosphine gas diffuses through the silicon oxide cap layer and reacts with the molten indium at the interface, forming indium phosphide. The diffusion rate of phosphorus through the oxide cap is governed by the temperature, cap thickness, and stoichiometry of the oxide, and is carefully controlled to ensure that the phosphorus flux is sufficient to initiate nucleation but not so high as to induce multiple nucleation events. Upon initial contact, a single nucleus forms at the interface between the indium and the nucleation layer, and the indium phosphide crystal begins to grow radially outward. As the crystal grows, it consumes phosphorus from the surrounding region, creating a phosphorus depletion zone that extends radially outward from the growing front. The size of this depletion zone is determined by the phosphorus flux, the growth temperature, and the geometry of the template. For the preferred conditions, the depletion zone extends several hundred micrometers, which is significantly larger than the typical feature size of the patterned structures. As a result, once a single nucleus forms within a template, the surrounding region becomes undersaturated with phosphorus, preventing any secondary nucleation events from occurring within the same template. This phenomenon ensures that each template feature grows as a single crystal, regardless of its size or shape. The growth duration is typically between 10 and 20 minutes, after which the furnace is cooled to room temperature under a hydrogen atmosphere to prevent oxidation of the semiconductor surface. The resulting structure consists of a single-crystalline indium phosphide feature that retains the exact shape of the original indium template, with a thickness corresponding to the initial indium layer thickness, and a smooth, planar interface with the underlying silicon dioxide substrate. The crystallinity of the grown structure is confirmed by electron backscatter diffraction, which reveals a single orientation within each feature, with no evidence of grain boundaries or twin boundaries. The crystal orientation exhibits a preferential texture along the (1 0 n) family of directions, where n ranges from 1 to 2, indicating a thermodynamically favored growth axis that minimizes interfacial strain. The stoichiometry of the indium phosphide is verified by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, which confirms a 1:1 ratio of indium to phosphorus, with no detectable impurities. The absence of polycrystallinity is further confirmed by transmission electron microscopy, which shows a sharp interface between the crystalline indium phosphide and the amorphous silicon dioxide substrate, with no evidence of interfacial voids, dislocations, or secondary phases. The growth process is highly reproducible, with consistent crystal quality observed across multiple batches and across full 4-inch wafers, demonstrating the scalability of the method. The method is further extended to enable the growth of other III–V semiconductors by substituting the group III metal and group V reactant. For example, gallium may be used in place of indium, with phosphine as the group V reactant, to grow gallium phosphide. Similarly, indium may be combined with antimony hydride to grow indium antimonide. The nucleation layer and cap layer remain unchanged, as the mechanism of liquid-phase confinement and depletion zone formation is independent of the specific semiconductor system. The method also enables the in situ doping of the semiconductor during growth by introducing a gaseous dopant precursor into the growth chamber. In the preferred embodiment, germane (GeH₄) is introduced as an n-type dopant, with partial pressures ranging from 0.01 to 1 torr. The germanium atoms incorporate substitutionally into the indium lattice, donating free electrons and increasing the carrier concentration. The electron concentration is directly proportional to the germane partial pressure, with concentrations increasing from 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ at low dopant fluxes to 1.3 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ at the highest fluxes, reaching the degenerate limit for indium phosphide. This doping is achieved without introducing significant lattice distortion or defect formation, as confirmed by the minimal broadening of the photoluminescence peak and the low Urbach tail parameter, which remains comparable to that of high-quality epitaxial wafers. The photoluminescence spectra exhibit a clear blue shift with increasing doping concentration, consistent with the Burstein–Moss effect, and the peak energy shift can be quantitatively correlated with the electron concentration using established theoretical models. The optical quality of the doped structures is further confirmed by photoluminescence imaging, which reveals uniform emission intensity across large-area arrays of patterned features, with no evidence of quenching or non-uniformity. The electronic quality of the grown structures is demonstrated through the fabrication of metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistors. Microwire structures with dimensions of 1 micrometer in width, 50 micrometers in length, and 125 nanometers in thickness are grown using the described method. Source and drain contacts are formed by photolithography and the deposition of a metal stack consisting of germanium, gold, and nickel, followed by annealing at 375 degrees Celsius to form low-resistance ohmic contacts. A gate dielectric of zirconium dioxide, 10 nanometers thick, is deposited by atomic layer deposition at 200 degrees Celsius to ensure pinhole-free coverage and excellent interface quality. A top gate electrode is then deposited, either as nickel for conventional transistors or as indium tin oxide for photo-MOSFETs. The resulting devices exhibit excellent electrical characteristics, including an ON-current of 120 microamperes per micrometer at a gate and drain bias of 2 volts, an ON/OFF current ratio exceeding 10⁵, and a peak extrinsic transconductance of 100 siemens per micrometer. The effective electron mobility is extracted to be 675 cm² V⁻¹ s⁻¹, which is comparable to the best-reported values for unpassivated indium phosphide nanowire transistors. The photo-MOSFETs, fabricated with transparent indium tin oxide gates, exhibit a strong photoresponse under white light illumination, with a peak responsivity of 700 amperes per watt and a specific detectivity of 8.4 × 10¹¹ Jones, surpassing the performance of many commercial photodetectors. The low growth temperature of the process enables the direct growth of semiconductor structures on flexible substrates such as polyethylene terephthalate. After growth on a rigid substrate, the semiconductor structures are transferred to flexible substrates using a temporary adhesive layer composed of polyamic acid, which is selectively dissolved in a mild solvent, leaving the semiconductor structures intact on the new substrate. The transferred structures retain their crystalline quality, as confirmed by electron backscatter diffraction and photoluminescence imaging, and exhibit identical electronic and optical performance to their rigid-substrate counterparts. The method further enables the fabrication of three-dimensional semiconductor architectures by stacking multiple layers of group III metal and group IV oxide prior to growth. By depositing alternating layers of indium and silicon oxide, followed by a final silicon oxide cap, multilayer structures are formed. Upon growth, each indium layer is converted into a separate layer of indium phosphide, separated by the amorphous silicon oxide layers, which act as insulating spacers. The resulting structure is a vertically stacked array of single-crystalline semiconductor layers, each with independent electrical and optical properties, enabling the monolithic integration of 3D electronic or photonic circuits without the need for bonding, etching, or alignment steps. This capability opens new possibilities for high-density, vertically integrated systems such as 3D photodetector arrays, stacked solar cells, and multi-gate transistors. The process is inherently scalable, as the growth mechanism is spatially confined by the template and does not require uniform gas flow or temperature gradients across the wafer. A simple cold-wall furnace with a single gas inlet is sufficient to grow uniform structures across a 4-inch wafer, as demonstrated by photoluminescence mapping, which shows no variation in emission intensity or peak position across the entire wafer. The method is compatible with standard semiconductor manufacturing infrastructure, requiring only minor modifications to existing chemical vapor deposition systems and photolithography tools. The elimination of the need for lattice-matched substrates, high-temperature epitaxy, or post-growth transfer processes significantly reduces manufacturing cost and complexity, while enabling the integration of high-performance semiconductors on virtually any substrate. The method thus provides a transformative platform for the next generation of flexible, wearable, and ultra-low-cost optoelectronic devices, enabling applications in biomedical sensors, large-area photodetectors, transparent electronics, and monolithic 3D integrated circuits that were previously unattainable with conventional semiconductor growth techniques.
