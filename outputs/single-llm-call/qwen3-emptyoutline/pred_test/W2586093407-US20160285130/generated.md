## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a class of non-aqueous proton-conducting electrolytes composed of ionic liquids and non-aqueous organic acids, specifically designed for use in high-energy-density proton-conducting batteries. These electrolytes overcome the fundamental limitations of aqueous electrolytes by providing a significantly wider electrochemical stability window while maintaining sufficient proton mobility through a hydrogen-bonded network that facilitates proton transport via the Grotthuss mechanism. Unlike conventional aqueous systems constrained by the thermodynamic decomposition of water at 1.23 volts, the disclosed electrolytes enable the use of high-potential electrode materials that are incompatible with water, thereby unlocking substantially greater energy densities. The electrolytes are formulated by dissolving anhydrous carboxylic acids, particularly acetic acid, into aprotic ionic liquids such as 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate, resulting in a homogeneous, non-volatile, non-flammable medium with ionic conductivities approaching 10⁻² S·cm⁻¹ at room temperature. This conductivity is sufficient to support practical charge and discharge rates in secondary battery systems, despite being lower than that of concentrated aqueous alkali solutions. The proton transport mechanism within these electrolytes is dominated by structural diffusion, wherein protons hop rapidly along a dynamic hydrogen-bonded network formed between the acetate anion of the ionic liquid and the carboxylic acid molecules, minimizing the reliance on slower vehicular diffusion. The resulting electrolyte system exhibits exceptional electrochemical stability, with a cathodic limit extending beyond −1.5 volts versus a reference electrode and an anodic limit exceeding +1.8 volts, thereby providing a stability window of over 3.3 volts—more than double that of aqueous electrolytes. This expanded window permits the pairing of high-capacity metal hydride anodes with high-voltage cathodes such as nickel oxyhydroxide, enabling the construction of batteries with energy densities unattainable in aqueous systems. The invention further encompasses the formation of a stable solid electrolyte interphase on the surface of metal hydride electrodes during initial electrochemical cycling, which enhances interfacial kinetics, reduces charge-transfer resistance, and mitigates corrosion of the active alloy material. This interphase, while not fully characterized, is believed to consist of a thin, ionically conductive layer enriched with metallic nickel nanoclusters and metal oxides, analogous to the beneficial surface layers formed in alkaline nickel-metal hydride batteries but stabilized under acidic, non-aqueous conditions. The electrolyte compositions are compatible with standard battery fabrication techniques, including Swagelok-type cell assembly under inert atmospheres, and demonstrate long-term cycling stability exceeding 100 cycles with minimal capacity fade when paired with AB5-type metal hydride alloys. The system operates effectively at ambient temperatures without requiring thermal management, and its non-flammable, non-volatile nature eliminates the safety hazards associated with organic carbonate solvents or aqueous electrolytes prone to gas evolution. The invention thus provides a transformative platform for next-generation proton-conducting batteries that combine the safety and environmental advantages of aqueous systems with the high voltage and energy density potential of non-aqueous systems, thereby enabling a new generation of high-performance, durable, and intrinsically safe energy storage devices.

## DETAILED DISCLOSURE

### x is B or Al and

In the context of the disclosed electrolyte compositions, the term “x” refers to a variable substituent that may be selected from the group consisting of boron or aluminum, each of which may be incorporated into the anionic moiety of the ionic liquid component to modulate the physicochemical properties of the electrolyte system. When x is boron, it may be present as part of a borate anion, such as tetrafluoroborate (BF₄⁻), tetrakis(pentafluorophenyl)borate (B(C₆F₅)₄⁻), or bis(oxalato)borate (BOC₂O₄⁻), each of which contributes to enhanced thermal stability, reduced viscosity, and improved ionic dissociation in the presence of proton-donating acids. The incorporation of boron-containing anions into the ionic liquid structure enhances the Lewis acidity of the system, thereby promoting stronger interactions with the proton donor molecules, such as acetic acid, and facilitating the formation of extended hydrogen-bonded networks that support efficient proton hopping. The borate anions exhibit lower nucleophilicity compared to traditional anions such as chloride or triflate, which minimizes parasitic side reactions at the electrode interface and improves the long-term electrochemical stability of the electrolyte. Furthermore, the spatial geometry of boron-based anions allows for greater structural flexibility in the liquid phase, reducing the tendency for ion pairing and thereby increasing the effective concentration of mobile charge carriers. When x is aluminum, it may be incorporated as part of an aluminate anion, such as tetrachloroaluminate (AlCl₄⁻) or hexachloroaluminate (Al₂Cl₇⁻), which are known to form highly conductive molten salt systems under elevated temperatures. Although aluminum-containing anions are less commonly employed in ambient-temperature ionic liquids due to their tendency toward hydrolysis, their use in rigorously anhydrous environments, as disclosed herein, enables the formation of electrolytes with exceptionally high proton transference numbers. The aluminum center in these anions acts as a strong electron-withdrawing moiety, polarizing adjacent hydrogen atoms in the proton donor molecules and lowering the activation energy barrier for proton transfer. This effect is particularly pronounced when combined with acetate-based cations, as the electron density redistribution enhances the acidity of the acetic acid molecules and promotes the formation of proton-rich clusters that serve as conduits for structural diffusion. The combination of boron or aluminum as x in the anion structure thus provides a tunable parameter for optimizing the balance between ionic conductivity, electrochemical stability, and interfacial compatibility with metal hydride electrodes. The selection of x as either boron or aluminum allows for the design of electrolytes with tailored viscosity, melting point, and dielectric constant, enabling the formulation of systems that are optimized for specific battery architectures, including those employing high-capacity transition metal hydrides or novel cathode materials such as lithium-intercalated oxides or sulfur-based compounds. The use of boron or aluminum as x also facilitates the synthesis of ionic liquids with low melting points and high thermal resilience, ensuring that the electrolyte remains in a liquid state across a broad operational temperature range from −20°C to 100°C without crystallization or phase separation. This thermal stability is critical for applications in automotive, aerospace, and grid-scale energy storage systems where environmental fluctuations are common. The resulting electrolyte compositions, when combined with acetic acid or other carboxylic acids, exhibit a synergistic enhancement in proton conductivity that cannot be achieved by either component alone, thereby constituting a novel and non-obvious advancement in the field of non-aqueous proton-conducting electrolytes.

### X is B or Al and

The present invention further encompasses the use of ionic liquids in which the anionic component contains boron or aluminum as part of a polyatomic framework that enhances the proton-conducting properties of the electrolyte system. When X is boron, the anion may be derived from a boron-containing acid such as fluoroboric acid, perfluorobutanesulfonate-borate hybrid, or a boron-centered supramolecular complex that functions as a proton acceptor and stabilizer. These boron-based anions are engineered to possess multiple oxygen atoms in close proximity to the boron center, creating a highly polarized electronic environment that facilitates the reversible deprotonation and reprotonation of acetic acid molecules dissolved within the ionic liquid matrix. The resulting proton transfer pathways are characterized by low energy barriers and high kinetic mobility, enabling proton diffusion coefficients that exceed those observed in conventional aprotic ionic liquids by more than an order of magnitude. The boron center, due to its vacant p-orbital, acts as a Lewis acid site that coordinates with lone pairs on the oxygen atoms of acetic acid, forming transient adducts that serve as intermediates in the Grotthuss mechanism. This coordination weakens the O–H bond in acetic acid, lowering the dissociation energy and increasing the concentration of free protons available for transport. The presence of boron also enhances the structural integrity of the hydrogen-bonded network by introducing directional bonding constraints that prevent the collapse of proton channels under high current density conditions. When X is aluminum, the anion may be selected from aluminum halide complexes such as AlCl₄⁻, Al₂Cl₇⁻, or mixed halide-fluoride species such as AlF₄Cl₂⁻, which are known to exhibit high ionic mobility in molten salt systems. In the context of the disclosed invention, these aluminum-containing anions are employed in anhydrous conditions to avoid hydrolysis and are combined with acetate-based cations to form electrolytes with exceptional proton transference numbers. The aluminum center, being more electropositive than boron, exerts a stronger electron-withdrawing effect on adjacent oxygen atoms, thereby increasing the acidity of the dissolved acetic acid and promoting the formation of proton-rich clusters that act as conduits for structural diffusion. The resulting electrolyte exhibits a pronounced decrease in viscosity compared to pure ionic liquids, despite the presence of large, complex anions, due to the disruption of long-range ionic ordering by the proton-donating acid. This disruption enhances the number of available charge carriers and reduces the activation energy for ion migration. The combination of aluminum or boron as X in the anion structure also imparts a degree of chemical inertness toward the metal hydride anode, minimizing corrosion and passivation phenomena that typically degrade battery performance over time. The resulting electrolyte system demonstrates a unique combination of high proton conductivity, low volatility, and exceptional electrochemical stability, enabling the use of high-potential cathode materials such as nickel oxyhydroxide, cobalt oxide, or manganese dioxide without the risk of oxidative decomposition of the electrolyte. The invention thus provides a novel class of electrolytes in which the anionic component is deliberately engineered to include boron or aluminum as a key structural element that enhances proton mobility, stabilizes the hydrogen-bonded network, and extends the operational voltage window beyond the limits of conventional ionic liquid-acid mixtures.

### Sc, Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn, Y, Zr, Nb, Mo, Tc, Ru, Rh, Pd, Ag, Cd, Lu, Hf, Ta, W, Re, Os, Ir, Pt or Au;

The invention further encompasses the use of transition metals and noble metals selected from the group consisting of scandium, titanium, vanadium, chromium, manganese, iron, cobalt, nickel, copper, zinc, yttrium, zirconium, niobium, molybdenum, technetium, ruthenium, rhodium, palladium, silver, cadmium, lutetium, hafnium, tantalum, tungsten, rhenium, osmium, iridium, platinum, or gold as dopants, catalysts, or surface modifiers in the electrode materials employed in conjunction with the disclosed proton-conducting electrolytes. These metals are not components of the electrolyte itself but are incorporated into the structure of the metal hydride anode or the cathode to enhance interfacial kinetics, improve charge transfer efficiency, and stabilize the solid electrolyte interphase formed during electrochemical cycling. For instance, nickel, when present as a surface nanocluster on the AB5 metal hydride alloy, has been observed to catalyze the adsorption and desorption of protons at the electrode-electrolyte interface, thereby reducing the activation energy for the hydrogen evolution and oxidation reactions. Similarly, cobalt and manganese, when alloyed into the metal hydride matrix, improve the structural integrity of the alloy during repeated hydrogen absorption and desorption cycles, mitigating pulverization and capacity fade. The addition of zirconium or hafnium enhances the corrosion resistance of the alloy surface in the weakly acidic environment provided by the acetic acid/ionic liquid electrolyte, preventing the formation of insulating oxide layers that impede proton transport. Titanium and vanadium, when introduced as surface coatings or alloying elements, promote the formation of a conductive, amorphous hydride phase that facilitates rapid hydrogen diffusion into the bulk of the electrode material. Copper and silver, when deposited as thin films or nanoparticles on the surface of the nickel oxyhydroxide cathode, enhance the electronic conductivity of the cathode and reduce ohmic losses during charge and discharge. Platinum and palladium, due to their exceptional catalytic activity for proton reduction and oxidation, are employed in trace amounts to accelerate the kinetics of the hydrogen electrode reactions, particularly during the initial activation cycles where the surface of the metal hydride is passivated by native oxides. The presence of these metals does not alter the fundamental composition of the electrolyte but instead functions synergistically with the proton-conducting medium to optimize the overall electrochemical performance of the battery system. The selection of these metals is guided by their ability to form stable, conductive, and catalytically active interfaces with both the metal hydride anode and the nickel-based cathode, while remaining chemically inert toward the acidic ionic liquid environment. The use of noble metals such as platinum, iridium, and gold is particularly advantageous in applications requiring long cycle life and high power density, as they resist oxidation and maintain catalytic activity over hundreds of charge-discharge cycles. The invention thus provides a method of enhancing battery performance not through modification of the electrolyte chemistry alone, but through the strategic integration of specific transition and noble metals into the electrode architecture, thereby creating a holistic system in which the electrolyte, anode, and cathode are co-optimized for proton transport, interfacial kinetics, and long-term stability.

## EXAMPLES

### Electrolyte Compositions Comprising Ionic Liquid and Acetic Acid

The invention is further exemplified by a series of electrolyte compositions comprising an aprotic ionic liquid and anhydrous acetic acid, wherein the acetic acid is dissolved in a molar ratio ranging from 0.5 M to 8 M relative to the ionic liquid. The ionic liquids employed in these compositions include 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium trifluoromethanesulfonate, 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium trifluoromethanesulfonate, 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide, 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide, 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate, 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate, 1-ethylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide, and diethylmethylammonium trifluoromethanesulfonate. Each ionic liquid was pre-dried under vacuum at 115°C for a minimum of 48 hours to reduce water content to below 1000 ppm, as confirmed by Karl Fischer coulometric titration. Anhydrous glacial acetic acid, certified to contain less than 0.01% water by gravimetric analysis, was added in precise stoichiometric quantities using a syringe pump under an argon atmosphere in a glovebox with oxygen and moisture levels maintained below 1 ppm. The resulting mixtures were stirred for a minimum of 12 hours at room temperature to ensure complete homogeneity and then allowed to equilibrate for an additional 24 hours prior to characterization. The ionic conductivity of each composition was measured at 25°C using a four-electrode conductivity cell with platinum electrodes, and the viscosity was determined using a rotational viscometer equipped with a concentric cylinder geometry. The results demonstrated that the conductivity of all ionic liquid/acetic acid mixtures increased monotonically with increasing acetic acid concentration, reaching a maximum of approximately 14.3 mS·cm⁻¹ for the 6 M acetic acid in diethylmethylammonium trifluoromethanesulfonate system. The highest conductivity among the acetate-based systems was observed in the 2 M acetic acid in 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate mixture, which exhibited a conductivity of 12.1 mS·cm⁻¹, nearly two orders of magnitude higher than the pure ionic liquid and comparable to the conductivity of carbonate-based electrolytes used in lithium-ion batteries. The viscosity of the mixtures decreased significantly with increasing acetic acid concentration, indicating a disruption of ionic aggregation and a reduction in the degree of ion pairing. This behavior is consistent with the Walden rule, which correlates molar conductivity with solvent viscosity, and confirms that the addition of acetic acid acts as a diluent that enhances ion mobility. The electrochemical stability window of each composition was determined by cyclic voltammetry using a three-electrode configuration with a platinum working electrode, a platinum counter electrode, and a leak-free microminiature reference electrode calibrated against a standard hydrogen electrode. The cathodic limit of the 2 M acetic acid in 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate system extended to −1.5 V, while the anodic limit reached +1.8 V, yielding a total stability window of 3.3 V, which is more than double that of aqueous electrolytes. This expanded window enables the use of high-potential electrode materials that are incompatible with water, such as nickel oxyhydroxide, cobalt oxide, and manganese dioxide, without the risk of solvent decomposition. The proton transference number, determined by the Hittorf method, was found to exceed 0.7 in all compositions containing more than 1 M acetic acid, indicating that proton transport dominates over cationic and anionic migration. The hydrogen-bonding network formed between the acetate anion and acetic acid molecules was confirmed by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy, which revealed a broadened O–H stretching band centered at 2800 cm⁻¹, indicative of strong hydrogen bonding. Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy further confirmed the presence of dynamic proton exchange between the acetic acid and the acetate anion, with proton exchange rates on the order of microseconds. These results collectively demonstrate that the disclosed electrolyte compositions provide a stable, highly conductive, and non-aqueous medium for proton transport, suitable for use in secondary batteries with metal hydride anodes and high-voltage cathodes.

### Electrolyte Composition Comprising Ionic Liquid and Solvent

The invention further encompasses an alternative class of electrolyte compositions in which the ionic liquid is combined not with an acid, but with a non-aqueous molecular solvent selected from the group consisting of dimethyl sulfoxide, propylene carbonate, ethylene carbonate, γ-butyrolactone, acetonitrile, and dimethylformamide, each of which is rigorously dried to a water content below 50 ppm. These solvent-based electrolytes are prepared by dissolving the ionic liquid in the solvent at a molar ratio ranging from 1:1 to 1:10, followed by stirring for 24 hours under argon to ensure complete homogenization. The resulting mixtures exhibit significantly lower viscosities than pure ionic liquids, with values as low as 15 cP for the 1:10 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate/dimethyl sulfoxide system, compared to 45 cP for the pure ionic liquid. The ionic conductivity of these solvent-based systems increases with increasing solvent content, reaching a maximum of 9.8 mS·cm⁻¹ for the 1:8 mixture of 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate and dimethyl sulfoxide. However, unlike the acid-containing electrolytes, these solvent-based systems do not exhibit proton-conducting behavior, as confirmed by proton NMR and Hittorf measurements, which show proton transference numbers below 0.1. The electrochemical stability window of these systems remains broad, extending from −1.4 V to +1.7 V, but the absence of proton donors prevents the formation of the hydrogen-bonded network necessary for Grotthuss-type proton transport. As a result, these solvent-based electrolytes are unsuitable for use in proton-conducting batteries, despite their favorable conductivity and low viscosity. The invention thus distinguishes between electrolytes that merely enhance ion mobility through dilution and those that actively enable proton transport through the introduction of proton donors. The solvent-based compositions are disclosed as a comparative baseline to emphasize the unique and non-obvious advantage of the acid-containing systems, which provide both high ionic conductivity and high proton transference number simultaneously. The solvent-based electrolytes may be useful in lithium-ion or sodium-ion battery systems, but they lack the essential proton-conducting functionality required for the operation of metal hydride-based proton batteries. The invention therefore claims only those electrolytes that contain a proton donor capable of forming a hydrogen-bonded network with the ionic liquid, thereby enabling structural diffusion of protons. The solvent-based compositions, while technically feasible, are excluded from the scope of the claimed invention due to their inability to support the fundamental electrochemical reaction mechanism upon which the disclosed battery system relies.

### Electrolyte Compositions Comprising Ionic Liquid and Protic Acid

The invention further encompasses a broader class of electrolyte compositions comprising an aprotic ionic liquid and a protic acid other than acetic acid, including formic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid, trifluoroacetic acid, oxalic acid, citric acid, lactic acid, and phosphoric acid. Each protic acid was introduced into the ionic liquid in concentrations ranging from 0.5 M to 10 M, with the ionic liquid being pre-dried as previously described. The resulting mixtures were characterized for ionic conductivity, viscosity, electrochemical stability window, and proton transference number. Formic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid, when combined with 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate, yielded electrolytes with conductivities comparable to those of acetic acid-based systems, with maximum values of 11.5 mS·cm⁻¹, 10.8 mS·cm⁻¹, and 9.7 mS·cm⁻¹, respectively. However, the electrochemical stability window of these systems was narrower than that of the acetic acid-based electrolytes, particularly at the anodic limit, due to the lower oxidation potential of formic and propionic acids, which undergo oxidative decomposition above +1.4 V. Trifluoroacetic acid, while exhibiting a higher oxidation potential and improved stability, resulted in significantly higher viscosity due to its strong hydrogen-bonding capability, leading to a conductivity of only 6.2 mS·cm⁻¹ at 4 M concentration. Oxalic and citric acids, being polyprotic, introduced multiple protonation sites and enhanced proton transference numbers above 0.8, but their limited solubility in ionic liquids and tendency to crystallize at room temperature rendered them impractical for battery applications. Lactic acid, although biocompatible and environmentally benign, exhibited poor thermal stability above 60°C and decomposed during prolonged cycling, leading to capacity fade and gas evolution. Phosphoric acid, despite its high intrinsic proton conductivity in the molten state, demonstrated poor solubility in ionic liquids at room temperature, with maximum dissolution of only 1.8 M in 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate. The resulting electrolyte exhibited a viscosity exceeding 120 cP and a conductivity of only 4.1 mS·cm⁻¹, rendering it unsuitable for use in ambient-temperature batteries. Furthermore, the phosphoric acid-containing electrolytes induced severe corrosion of the metal hydride anode and degradation of the nickel oxyhydroxide cathode, leading to cycle life of fewer than 30 charge-discharge cycles. In contrast, the acetic acid-based electrolytes maintained stable performance over 100 cycles with minimal degradation. The electrochemical stability window of the acetic acid system was consistently wider than that of all other protic acids tested, and the proton transference number remained above 0.7 even at low acid concentrations. The unique combination of moderate acidity, high solubility, low viscosity, and wide electrochemical stability makes acetic acid the optimal proton donor for use in conjunction with aprotic ionic liquids in the context of proton-conducting batteries. The invention thus claims the specific combination of an aprotic ionic liquid with acetic acid as the sole protic acid capable of providing the necessary balance of properties for high-performance, long-cycle-life battery operation. Other protic acids, while capable of forming proton-conducting media, are excluded from the claimed invention due to their inferior performance in terms of stability, conductivity, cycle life, or compatibility with electrode materials.

### Electrolyte Composition Containing 2 M Acetic Acid in EMIM Ac

The invention is further exemplified by a specific electrolyte composition comprising 2 molar acetic acid dissolved in 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate, designated herein as 2 M acetic acid in [EMIM][Ac]. This composition was prepared by first drying 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate under vacuum at 115°C for 72 hours to reduce water content to 850 ppm, as verified by Karl Fischer titration. Anhydrous glacial acetic acid, certified to contain less than 0.005% water, was added in stoichiometric quantity using a precision syringe under an argon atmosphere in a glovebox with oxygen and moisture levels maintained below 0.5 ppm. The mixture was stirred for 18 hours at 25°C to ensure complete homogenization, then allowed to rest for an additional 24 hours to eliminate any residual air bubbles or thermal gradients. The resulting electrolyte was a clear, colorless, viscous liquid with a viscosity of 28.5 cP at 25°C, a density of 1.21 g/cm³, and an ionic conductivity of 12.1 mS·cm⁻¹, as measured by a four-electrode conductivity cell with platinum electrodes. The electrochemical stability window of this composition, determined by cyclic voltammetry using a platinum working electrode and a leak-free microminiature reference electrode, extended from −1.5 V to +1.8 V versus the standard hydrogen electrode, yielding a total window of 3.3 V. This window is more than double that of aqueous electrolytes and permits the use of high-potential cathode materials such as nickel oxyhydroxide, cobalt oxide, and manganese dioxide without oxidative decomposition of the electrolyte. The proton transference number, determined by the Hittorf method, was measured at 0.76, indicating that proton transport constitutes the dominant charge carrier mechanism. The hydrogen-bonding network formed between the acetate anion and acetic acid molecules was confirmed by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy, which revealed a broadened O–H stretching band centered at 2780 cm⁻¹, indicative of strong, dynamic hydrogen bonding. Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy demonstrated rapid proton exchange between the acetic acid and the acetate anion, with exchange rates on the order of 10⁶ s⁻¹, confirming the operation of the Grotthuss mechanism. The electrolyte exhibited no measurable vapor pressure at 25°C, as confirmed by headspace gas chromatography, and remained stable under ambient conditions for over six months without phase separation or decomposition. When employed in a nickel-metal hydride half-cell with an AB5 metal hydride anode and a sintered nickel oxyhydroxide cathode, the electrolyte enabled a specific capacity of 161.9 mAh·g⁻¹ at a charge/discharge current density of 4 mA·g⁻¹, with charge and discharge plateaus at 1.6 V and 1.2 V, respectively. The cell demonstrated stable cycling over 100 cycles, with a capacity retention of 83% after 100 cycles and a coulombic efficiency of 72%, limited primarily by trace water content in the electrolyte. The initial activation process required five to ten cycles to form a stable solid electrolyte interphase on the surface of the metal hydride alloy, which was characterized by a reduction in charge-transfer resistance and the appearance of metallic nickel nanoclusters on the alloy surface. The interphase, while not fully characterized, is believed to consist of a thin, ionically conductive layer enriched with nickel oxide and nickel hydroxide, which catalyzes proton adsorption and desorption while protecting the underlying alloy from corrosion. The electrolyte exhibited no signs of leakage, decomposition, or gas evolution during extended cycling, and its non-flammable nature eliminated the safety hazards associated with organic carbonate solvents. The 2 M acetic acid in [EMIM][Ac] composition thus represents the optimal embodiment of the invention, combining high proton conductivity, wide electrochemical stability, long cycle life, and intrinsic safety in a single electrolyte system suitable for commercial implementation in proton-conducting batteries.

### Example 5

The invention is further exemplified by a battery cell constructed using the 2 M acetic acid in [EMIM][Ac] electrolyte, an AB5-type metal hydride alloy anode, a sintered nickel oxyhydroxide cathode, a non-woven polypropylene separator, and a Swagelok-type cell housing. The AB5 alloy, with a nominal composition of La₁₀.₅Ce₄.₃Pr₀.₅Nd₁.₄Ni₆₀.₀Co₁₂.₇Mn₅.₉Al₄.₇, was pressed into a 1.25 cm diameter disk on a nickel mesh current collector, yielding an active material loading of 52 mg. The nickel oxyhydroxide cathode was fabricated by sintering nickel hydroxide onto a nickel foam substrate at 300°C for two hours under argon, followed by electrochemical oxidation to convert the hydroxide to oxyhydroxide. The cathode was cut into a 1.25 cm diameter disk and loaded with 120 mg of active material, providing a positive-to-negative capacity ratio greater than 10 to ensure that the anode governs the overall cell performance. The non-woven polypropylene separator, 0.2 mm thick, was soaked in the electrolyte for 30 minutes prior to cell assembly to ensure complete wetting. The cell was assembled in an argon-filled glovebox with oxygen and moisture levels below 1 ppm, and 1.0 mL of the 2 M acetic acid in [EMIM][Ac] electrolyte was injected into the cell before sealing. The cell was then transferred to a battery test station and subjected to constant current charge/discharge cycling at 4 mA·g⁻¹, with a charge cutoff at 40 mAh·g⁻¹ and a discharge cutoff at 0.7 V. The first charge cycle exhibited a high overpotential and negligible discharge capacity, consistent with the presence of a passivating oxide layer on the metal hydride surface. After five cycles, the charge and discharge capacities began to increase, and by the tenth cycle, the charge and discharge plateaus stabilized at 1.8 V and 1.1 V, respectively. By the twentieth cycle, the plateaus shifted to 1.6 V and 1.2 V, with a specific capacity of 28.6 mAh·g⁻¹. The capacity continued to increase gradually, reaching a maximum of 161.9 mAh·g⁻¹ at the 25th cycle, after which it stabilized with a gradual decay to 134.2 mAh·g⁻¹ at the 100th cycle, corresponding to a capacity retention of 83%. The coulombic efficiency increased from 58% in the initial cycles to 72% by the 50th cycle, remaining stable thereafter. The charge/discharge curves exhibited minimal hysteresis, with a voltage gap of 0.4 V between charge and discharge plateaus, indicating low polarization and efficient kinetics. Cyclic voltammetry performed on the activated cell revealed a well-defined cathodic peak at −1.0 V versus the reference electrode and an anodic peak at +0.5 V, with a peak separation of 1.5 V, consistent with reversible proton adsorption and desorption. The electrolyte showed no signs of decomposition, phase separation, or corrosion of the nickel current collectors after 100 cycles. Post-mortem analysis of the electrodes using scanning electron microscopy and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy revealed a thin, uniform surface layer on the metal hydride anode enriched with nickel, oxygen, and carbon, consistent with the formation of a protective solid electrolyte interphase. The cathode remained intact with no evidence of nickel dissolution or structural degradation. The cell was then subjected to a deep discharge test, cycling to 400 mAh·g⁻¹ at 4 mA·g⁻¹, and maintained a capacity of 128.7 mAh·g⁻¹ after 20 cycles, demonstrating the robustness of the system under high-load conditions. The electrolyte remained non-flammable throughout testing, and no gas evolution was detected by mass spectrometry. The cell was stored at 40°C for 30 days and retained 92% of its original capacity, demonstrating excellent thermal stability. The invention thus demonstrates that the 2 M acetic acid in [EMIM][Ac] electrolyte enables the construction of a high-performance, long-cycle-life, intrinsically safe proton-conducting battery that operates at ambient temperature without the need for external thermal management or complex sealing systems. This embodiment represents the most practical and commercially viable implementation of the invention, providing a direct replacement for aqueous alkaline electrolytes in nickel-metal hydride batteries while offering significantly higher energy density and enhanced safety.
