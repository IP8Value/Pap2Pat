# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to systems and methods for estimating the number of individuals present in a confined environment using radar-based sensing and deep learning techniques, with particular application to automotive cabins, train carriages, and other enclosed transportation spaces where occupancy estimation is critical for environmental control, safety monitoring, and resource optimization. More specifically, the invention encompasses a novel supervised learning loss function, termed Label-Aware Ranked (LAR) loss, designed to enforce a structured, rank-preserving embedding space in deep neural networks trained for regression tasks involving discrete, ordered labels—such as the count of human beings in a scene. The invention further integrates temporal smoothing via exponential moving average filtering to enhance the temporal stability and reliability of occupancy predictions derived from low-resolution, noisy radar data. The system operates on intermediate frequency signals captured by Frequency Modulated Continuous Wave (FMCW) radar sensors, which are preprocessed into Range-Doppler Image representations, and subsequently processed by a convolutional neural network architecture optimized for robustness against signal artifacts, occlusions, and multi-path reflections inherent in dynamic, cluttered interior environments. The combination of label-aware embedding regularization and temporal prediction filtering enables unprecedented accuracy in occupancy estimation under real-world conditions where traditional computer vision approaches are infeasible due to privacy constraints or environmental interference, and where conventional deep learning regression methods fail to exploit the inherent ordinal structure of the target variable.

## BACKGROUND

The accurate estimation of human occupancy in enclosed spaces has long been a critical requirement across numerous domains, including intelligent building management, public transportation systems, automotive climate control, and security surveillance. Traditional approaches to occupancy estimation have relied heavily on visual sensing technologies such as infrared cameras, depth sensors, and visible-light cameras. While these methods have demonstrated success in controlled environments, they suffer from fundamental limitations in real-world applications, particularly in private or sensitive contexts such as vehicle interiors. Visual systems are vulnerable to variations in lighting conditions, occlusions caused by seat structures or personal belongings, and, most critically, significant privacy concerns that render them legally and socially unacceptable in many jurisdictions. Furthermore, environmental factors such as fog, dust, or window tinting can severely degrade image quality, leading to unreliable or incomplete data.

In response to these challenges, radar-based sensing has emerged as a compelling alternative. Radar systems, particularly those operating in the 60 GHz frequency band, offer the ability to detect and localize human presence without capturing identifiable visual information, thereby preserving individual privacy. These sensors are also inherently robust to ambient lighting, weather conditions, and minor obstructions, making them ideal for deployment in vehicles, trains, and other enclosed environments. However, radar signals present their own set of technical challenges. The resolution of low-cost, short-range radar sensors is inherently limited, resulting in sparse and low-fidelity representations of human targets. Reflections from multiple body parts—such as arms, torsos, and legs—produce complex, overlapping Doppler signatures that are difficult to disentangle using conventional signal processing techniques. Additionally, the presence of non-human reflective surfaces—seat belts, metal frames, or even clothing with metallic threads—can generate false targets that further complicate signal interpretation. These factors collectively result in high levels of noise, missed detections, and ambiguous returns, particularly when multiple individuals are present in close proximity.

Conventional signal processing pipelines for radar data typically involve a sequence of operations including Fast Fourier Transforms (FFTs) to extract range and velocity information, windowing to suppress sidelobes, and clutter removal via high-pass filtering to eliminate static or slowly moving objects. The resulting Range-Doppler Images (RDIs) serve as the primary input to downstream machine learning models. While these preprocessing steps are effective in transforming raw intermediate frequency signals into interpretable 2D representations, they do not address the core challenge of accurately mapping these representations to a discrete count of individuals. Traditional machine learning models, such as support vector machines or random forests, applied to hand-engineered features extracted from RDIs, have shown limited generalization capacity and poor performance in dense or dynamic scenarios.

The advent of deep learning has significantly advanced the state of the art in radar-based perception tasks. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), in particular, have demonstrated remarkable ability to learn hierarchical representations directly from raw or preprocessed radar data, bypassing the need for manual feature engineering. Recent work has applied CNN architectures to radar-based people counting, achieving improved accuracy over classical methods. However, these approaches typically treat the counting task as a standard regression problem, minimizing mean squared error (MSE) or mean absolute error (MAE) between predicted and ground-truth counts. This formulation is fundamentally misaligned with the nature of the problem: the number of people in a scene is not a continuous variable but a discrete, ordinal quantity, where the difference between two adjacent counts (e.g., 2 vs. 3 people) is semantically and practically more similar than the difference between distant counts (e.g., 0 vs. 5 people). Standard regression losses do not encode this ordinal structure, leading to suboptimal learning dynamics where the model may assign similar embedding representations to labels that are semantically distant, or conversely, assign dissimilar representations to adjacent labels, thereby increasing the likelihood of prediction errors that are off by one or two units—a common and highly consequential failure mode in occupancy estimation.

Moreover, existing deep learning methods for radar-based perception have largely ignored the temporal continuity inherent in occupancy dynamics. In real-world scenarios, the number of occupants in a vehicle changes infrequently and incrementally—typically by one person at a time, or remains constant over multiple consecutive frames. Yet, most models produce independent predictions for each frame, resulting in erratic, jittery outputs that fluctuate unnecessarily even when the true occupancy remains unchanged. This instability undermines the reliability of downstream applications such as HVAC control, where abrupt changes in predicted occupancy can trigger unnecessary energy consumption or discomfort. While simple temporal filters such as moving averages have been applied post-hoc to smooth predictions, these methods operate on the final output and do not influence the underlying learning process, leaving the model vulnerable to producing highly erroneous individual predictions that are only partially corrected after the fact.

Recent advances in deep metric learning (DML) have introduced powerful frameworks for learning embedding spaces that preserve semantic relationships between data points. Loss functions such as Triplet Loss, Multiclass N-Pair Loss, and Constellation Loss have been successfully employed in classification tasks to ensure that samples from the same class are clustered tightly in the embedding space while samples from different classes are maximally separated. However, these methods are designed for categorical classification, where classes are treated as unordered labels, and do not inherently account for the relative ordering or numerical distance between labels. In the context of people counting, where labels range from 0 to N and represent a natural sequence of increasing quantities, the distance between labels carries critical semantic meaning. A model that learns to separate embeddings of labels 2 and 3 by the same margin as labels 0 and 5 fails to exploit the inherent ordinal structure of the task, leading to inefficient learning and increased susceptibility to misclassification near decision boundaries.

To date, no existing method has successfully integrated the principles of deep metric learning with the ordinal nature of regression tasks involving discrete, ranked labels. There exists a critical gap in the literature between the theoretical potential of embedding space regularization and its practical application to counting problems where the label space is not merely categorical but inherently ordered. Furthermore, no prior work has combined such label-aware embedding constraints with temporal smoothing techniques to jointly optimize for both discriminative power and temporal stability in radar-based occupancy estimation. The present invention fills this gap by introducing a novel loss function—Label-Aware Ranked (LAR) loss—that explicitly encodes the ranking of labels into the geometry of the embedding space, while simultaneously incorporating exponential moving average filtering to stabilize predictions over time. This dual innovation enables a level of accuracy, robustness, and reliability in people counting that far exceeds the capabilities of existing state-of-the-art methods, particularly in challenging, real-world environments such as automotive cabins where signal quality is poor and occupancy dynamics are subtle.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a novel system and method for estimating the number of individuals present in a confined environment using radar sensor data and deep learning techniques, wherein the estimation is rendered highly accurate and temporally stable through the combined use of a label-aware ranked (LAR) loss function and an exponential moving average filter applied to the network’s output. The system comprises a radar sensor configured to emit Frequency Modulated Continuous Wave (FMCW) signals toward an enclosed space and receive reflected signals, a signal preprocessing module that converts the raw intermediate frequency data into a multi-channel Range-Doppler Image representation, a deep convolutional neural network that maps the preprocessed radar data into a normalized embedding space, and a post-processing module that applies an exponential moving average filter to the network’s raw predictions to produce a temporally coherent occupancy estimate. The deep neural network is trained using the LAR loss function, which is defined as a function of the angular distances between normalized embedding vectors of input samples, weighted by a logarithmic function of the absolute difference between their corresponding label values. This formulation ensures that embeddings corresponding to adjacent label values are pulled closer together in angular space than embeddings corresponding to distant label values, thereby enforcing a rank-preserving, uniformly distributed angular structure across the embedding space that aligns with the ordinal nature of the counting task. The LAR loss function minimizes when the angular separation between embeddings of different labels is proportional to the inverse of their label difference, with the optimal configuration occurring when embeddings for all possible labels are arranged on a unit hypersphere with uniform angular spacing, thereby maximizing inter-class discrimination while preserving label ordering. The exponential moving average filter is applied to the raw regression output of the network at inference time, using a smoothing coefficient that assigns exponentially decreasing weights to historical predictions, thereby reducing temporal jitter and enhancing prediction stability without introducing significant latency. The system achieves an accuracy of at least 83.0% in predicting the exact number of individuals and a neighboring-label accuracy of at least 99.9% when allowing for a prediction error of ±1, significantly outperforming conventional regression losses such as mean squared error and state-of-the-art deep metric learning losses such as Constellation Loss, both with and without temporal smoothing. The invention is particularly suited for deployment in automotive cabins, train carriages, and other enclosed transportation environments where privacy-preserving sensing is required, signal quality is low, and occupancy changes are gradual and incremental. By integrating label-aware embedding regularization with temporal filtering, the invention overcomes the fundamental limitations of prior art, delivering a robust, accurate, and reliable solution for real-time occupancy estimation under challenging conditions.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF ILLUSTRATIVE EMBODIMENTS

The present invention is implemented as a complete system for estimating the number of individuals present in an enclosed environment using radar-based sensing and deep learning, with a particular focus on automotive cabin occupancy. The system operates in two distinct phases: a training phase during which a deep neural network is optimized using the novel Label-Aware Ranked (LAR) loss function, and an inference phase during which real-time occupancy estimates are generated and stabilized using an exponential moving average filter. The system architecture is composed of four primary components: a radar sensor subsystem, a signal preprocessing unit, a deep neural network with LAR loss optimization, and a temporal smoothing module. Each component is designed to address specific challenges inherent in radar-based occupancy estimation, and their integration yields a synergistic improvement in performance that neither component could achieve independently.

The radar sensor subsystem comprises a 60 GHz Frequency Modulated Continuous Wave (FMCW) sensor, mounted internally on the upper surface of the front windshield of a vehicle cabin, oriented toward the interior space to capture reflections from occupants seated in the front and rear rows. The sensor emits a sequence of linear frequency-modulated chirps, each lasting a defined duration, and receives the reflected signals from objects within its field of view. The intermediate frequency (IF) signal generated by the mixing of transmitted and received signals is sampled at a high rate, producing a sequence of real-valued samples over time. For each frame of data, the sensor captures N_S samples per chirp and transmits N_C chirps per frame, resulting in a two-dimensional array of complex-valued samples of dimensions N_S × N_C. This raw data is then transmitted to the signal preprocessing unit, which performs a series of operations to extract meaningful physical features from the signal while suppressing noise and artifacts. First, a slow-time frame is constructed by integrating the samples across the chirp dimension to enhance velocity resolution, yielding a second 2D array of the same dimensions. A high-pass filtering operation is then applied along the chirp dimension to remove static clutter and transmitter-receiver leakage, effectively eliminating reflections from the vehicle’s interior structure and any non-moving objects. Subsequently, both the fast-time and slow-time arrays are multiplied by Hamming windows along their respective dimensions to reduce spectral leakage and sidelobe artifacts that arise from the finite sampling window. Following this, a two-dimensional Fast Fourier Transform (2D-FFT) is applied to each array, converting the time-domain samples into the frequency domain and producing two Range-Doppler Images (RDIs), one representing the spatial distribution of targets in range and the other representing their velocity profile. These two RDIs are then decomposed into their real and imaginary components, resulting in four distinct channels. Since the radar sensor is equipped with multiple receive antennas, typically three or six, the process is repeated for each antenna channel, yielding a total of six or twelve complex-valued input channels. These channels are stacked to form a multi-channel input tensor of dimensions N_S × N_C × C, where C is the number of antenna channels, and this tensor constitutes the input to the deep neural network.

The deep neural network is designed as a convolutional encoder architecture, comprising three sequential convolutional layers, each followed by a rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function and a max-pooling layer. Each convolutional layer employs 32 feature maps with a 3×3 kernel size and a stride of one, ensuring spatial feature extraction at multiple scales while preserving resolution. The max-pooling layers, each with a 2×2 kernel and stride of two, progressively reduce the spatial dimensions of the feature maps, enhancing translation invariance and reducing computational load. The output of the final pooling layer is flattened and passed through a fully connected layer with a ReLU activation, which maps the high-dimensional feature representation into a lower-dimensional embedding space of dimensionality D, where D is typically chosen between 64 and 256 to balance discriminative power and computational efficiency. The embedding vectors produced by this layer are then normalized to unit length using L2 normalization, ensuring that all embeddings lie on the surface of a unit hypersphere in D-dimensional space. This normalization is critical to the operation of the LAR loss function, as it ensures that the angular distance between embeddings corresponds directly to their cosine similarity, thereby enabling the loss to operate purely on angular relationships rather than Euclidean magnitudes. The final output of the network is a scalar regression value, computed by applying a linear projection from the embedding space to a single output neuron, followed by rounding to the nearest integer to produce the predicted occupancy count. During training, the network is optimized using the Label-Aware Ranked (LAR) loss function, which is defined as follows:

L_LAR = (1/N) * Σ_{i=1}^{N} Σ_{j=1}^{N} [ log(|l_i - l_j| + ε) * (1 - cos(θ_{ij})) ]

where N is the batch size, l_i and l_j are the ground-truth label values corresponding to the i-th and j-th samples in the batch, θ_{ij} is the angular distance between the normalized embedding vectors f_i and f_j, cos(θ_{ij}) is the cosine similarity between them, and ε is a small positive constant (e.g., 1e-6) introduced to ensure numerical stability when labels are identical. The logarithmic weighting term log(|l_i - l_j| + ε) serves as a label-aware multiplier that scales the contribution of each pairwise angular penalty according to the semantic distance between the corresponding labels. For pairs of samples with identical labels (|l_i - l_j| = 0), the logarithmic term becomes log(ε), which is negligible due to the small value of ε, effectively reducing the loss contribution to zero for intra-class pairs. For pairs with adjacent labels (|l_i - l_j| = 1), the logarithmic term is log(1 + ε) ≈ 0, meaning that the angular penalty is minimized, encouraging the embeddings of adjacent labels to be close in angular space. For pairs with larger label differences, such as |l_i - l_j| = 5, the logarithmic term becomes log(5 + ε), which is significantly larger, thereby imposing a stronger penalty on small angular distances between embeddings of dissimilar labels. This formulation ensures that the embedding space is not merely clustered by label class, but is explicitly ordered: embeddings for label 0 are positioned near those for label 1, which are near label 2, and so on, with increasing angular separation as the label difference increases. Crucially, the loss function is designed to be minimized when the angular separation between embeddings of labels k and k+Δ is proportional to Δ, and when the embeddings of all possible labels are uniformly distributed on the unit hypersphere. This condition is mathematically proven to be the global minimum of the LAR loss under the constraint of normalized embeddings, as demonstrated through the application of Jensen’s inequality and symmetry arguments on the cosine function over the unit circle. The uniform angular distribution ensures that the embedding space is maximally discriminative, with the largest possible angular margins between distant labels while maintaining the smallest necessary margins between adjacent ones. This structure is fundamentally different from conventional metric learning losses, which treat all label pairs equally or use fixed margins, and is uniquely suited to the ordinal nature of the people counting task.

The training process is conducted using a carefully constructed batch sampling strategy to ensure balanced and efficient learning. Each batch contains exactly two samples for each possible label value, ranging from 0 to 5, resulting in a batch size of 12. This ensures that every label is equally represented and that the loss function receives a balanced contribution from all label pairs. The optimizer used is Adam with a learning rate of 1e-4, weight decay of 1e-5, and a batch size of 12, trained over 50,000 iterations on a dataset comprising 76,000 labeled radar frames collected from real vehicle cabins under varying occupancy conditions. The dataset includes recordings of zero to five occupants seated in various configurations, with the number of occupants manually annotated by human observers. The data is split into training and test sets with a 4:1 ratio, and all preprocessing and training procedures are applied identically to both sets to prevent data leakage. The network is trained end-to-end using the LAR loss function, with no additional regularization beyond the inherent constraints imposed by the loss itself. During training, the embeddings evolve such that the angular distances between them increasingly reflect the ordinal relationships of their corresponding labels, resulting in a structured, rank-preserving embedding space that is highly discriminative even in the presence of low-resolution radar data and complex reflections.

At inference time, the trained network processes each new radar frame independently, producing a raw regression output that is a continuous scalar value. This value is not immediately rounded to an integer but is instead passed through an exponential moving average (EMA) filter to stabilize the temporal sequence of predictions. The EMA filter is defined by the recurrence relation:

s[k] = α * y[k] + (1 - α) * s[k - 1]

where s[k] is the smoothed prediction at time step k, y[k] is the raw network output at time step k, and α is a smoothing coefficient between 0 and 1. In the preferred embodiment, α is set to 0.3, which provides a balance between responsiveness to true occupancy changes and suppression of high-frequency noise. The initial value s[0] is set to the first raw prediction. The filtered output s[k] is then rounded to the nearest integer to produce the final occupancy estimate. This temporal filtering is applied solely during inference and does not affect the training process, ensuring that the network learns to produce accurate embeddings without being biased by temporal dependencies. The EMA filter is particularly effective in this application because occupancy changes in a vehicle cabin are inherently slow and incremental—typically occurring when a passenger enters or exits, or when a seated occupant shifts position. The filter effectively suppresses the jitter and oscillations that arise from minor fluctuations in radar reflections due to breathing, limb movement, or signal noise, while preserving the integrity of genuine occupancy transitions. As a result, the final output is a smooth, stable, and highly reliable estimate of the number of occupants, even when the raw network predictions exhibit occasional errors.

The performance of the system is evaluated on a held-out test set of 19,000 frames, using two primary metrics: exact accuracy, defined as the percentage of frames where the predicted count equals the true count, and neighboring-label accuracy, defined as the percentage of frames where the predicted count is within one of the true count (i.e., |predicted - true| ≤ 1). The system achieves an exact accuracy of 83.0% and a neighboring-label accuracy of 99.9%, representing an improvement of 6.7% and 2.1%, respectively, over the best-performing baseline method, which combines the Constellation Loss with EMA filtering. This improvement is statistically significant and consistent across all occupancy levels, with particularly pronounced gains observed in the intermediate ranges (2–4 occupants), where signal overlap and multi-path reflections are most severe. The LAR loss function alone, without EMA filtering, achieves an exact accuracy of 80.8% and a neighboring-label accuracy of 98.5%, demonstrating that the label-aware embedding structure is the primary driver of performance gains. The addition of EMA filtering further improves both metrics, confirming that temporal stability is a critical component of real-world usability. In contrast, baseline methods using MSE or MAE loss achieve exact accuracies below 76.3% and neighboring-label accuracies below 97.8%, even with EMA filtering, highlighting the superiority of the LAR formulation. Ablation studies confirm that removing the logarithmic weighting term from the LAR loss degrades performance significantly, as does replacing it with a linear or constant weighting, validating the necessity of the logarithmic scaling for proper label ranking. Similarly, replacing the EMA filter with a simple moving average or median filter results in either increased latency or reduced noise suppression, confirming the optimality of the exponential formulation for this application.

The system is implemented in software using the PyTorch framework, with the neural network trained on an NVIDIA Tesla P40 GPU with 24 GB of dedicated memory, using CUDA 11.1 and cuDNN 8.0.5. The entire pipeline, from radar data acquisition to final occupancy output, operates at a frame rate of 10 Hz, matching the sensor’s native sampling rate and enabling real-time deployment. The system requires no external visual sensors, requires no calibration beyond initial sensor mounting, and is fully functional under all lighting conditions, including complete darkness, bright sunlight, or foggy windows. It is immune to privacy concerns, as it does not capture or process any visual information, and it is robust to occlusions, clothing materials, and minor environmental changes. The model is lightweight, requiring less than 5 MB of memory for storage and inference, making it suitable for deployment on embedded automotive processors. The invention is not limited to automotive applications; it may be adapted for use in train carriages, elevators, aircraft cabins, or any enclosed space where privacy-preserving, reliable occupancy estimation is required. The LAR loss function is generalizable to any regression task involving discrete, ordinal labels, such as age estimation, crowd density grading, or traffic volume prediction, and may be integrated into any neural network architecture that produces normalized embeddings. The exponential moving average filter is similarly generalizable to any temporal sequence prediction task where stability and low latency are critical. Together, these components form a novel and non-obvious solution to the longstanding problem of accurate, reliable, and privacy-compliant people counting in challenging real-world environments.
