# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

### Technical Field

The present invention relates to a method for producing low-carbon steel with an inherently hardened surface layer without the need for post-casting thermal, mechanical, or chemical treatments. More specifically, the invention pertains to a casting process that, through precise control of solidification kinetics and compositional parameters, induces the formation of a mixed microstructure comprising lath martensite, upper bainite, and acicular ferrite within a shallow surface region of the steel, thereby imparting exceptional surface hardness and yield strength while preserving the inherent ductility and toughness of the bulk material. The invention is particularly applicable to the production of structural components, wear-resistant surfaces, and mechanical parts fabricated from low-carbon steels where conventional surface hardening techniques such as carburizing, induction hardening, laser hardening, or nitriding are either economically prohibitive, environmentally undesirable, or geometrically impractical.

### Description of the Related Art

Low-carbon steels, typically defined as alloys containing less than 0.25 weight percent carbon, are widely employed in structural and automotive applications due to their excellent formability, weldability, and impact toughness. However, their relatively low hardness and yield strength have historically limited their use in applications requiring resistance to abrasion, indentation, or surface fatigue. To overcome these limitations, industry has long relied on post-processing techniques to enhance surface mechanical properties. These methods include carburizing, which diffuses carbon into the surface at elevated temperatures to enable subsequent quenching; induction hardening, which employs electromagnetic heating followed by rapid cooling; laser hardening, which uses focused thermal energy to locally melt and quench the surface; and nitriding, which introduces nitrogen to form hard nitride phases. While effective, each of these approaches introduces significant cost, energy consumption, and process complexity. Moreover, they often require specialized equipment, precise environmental controls, and post-treatment machining to remove surface distortions or oxide layers, thereby diminishing their economic and environmental appeal.

Alternative approaches have sought to achieve surface hardening through alloying with microalloying elements such as titanium, niobium, or vanadium, or through controlled thermomechanical processing involving hot rolling and accelerated cooling. However, these methods still necessitate additional processing steps beyond conventional casting and solidification, and often result in trade-offs between surface hardness and core ductility. Furthermore, the introduction of alloying elements increases material cost and may complicate recycling or welding procedures. In recent years, researchers have explored the potential of sub-rapid solidification to refine microstructures and enhance mechanical properties. Studies have demonstrated that cooling rates in the range of 10² to 10⁴ K/s can suppress the formation of coarse ferrite and promote the development of fine-grained bainitic or martensitic structures. However, prior art has not disclosed a method by which a hardened surface layer with martensitic characteristics can be formed in low-carbon steel without the addition of carbon, alloying elements, or external heat treatment.

Notably, the formation of lath martensite in steels with carbon content below 0.10 wt% has been considered thermodynamically and kinetically improbable under conventional cooling conditions. Martensite formation is typically associated with high carbon concentrations, as carbon atoms are essential to stabilize the body-centered tetragonal lattice distortion that defines martensite’s hardness. Conventional wisdom holds that below a critical carbon threshold—approximately 0.15–0.20 wt%—the driving force for diffusionless transformation is insufficient to overcome the energy barrier for martensite nucleation, and the microstructure instead evolves into ferrite or bainite. Consequently, the presence of lath martensite in a steel with only 0.023 wt% carbon, as disclosed herein, represents a fundamental departure from established metallurgical principles.

Moreover, while prior studies have reported the coexistence of bainite and ferrite in low-carbon steels under controlled cooling, none have demonstrated the simultaneous formation of lath martensite in such a low-carbon matrix, nor have they quantified the resulting mechanical properties to the extent necessary to establish a commercially viable material. The nanohardness values reported in the prior art for martensite in low-carbon steels rarely exceed 6 GPa, even under optimized conditions involving higher carbon content or additional alloying. The achievement of a nanohardness exceeding 9.8 GPa in a steel with 0.023 wt% carbon, as demonstrated herein, is unprecedented and defies conventional strength-hardenability relationships. Furthermore, the absence of any post-casting treatment—such as reheating, quenching, or surface coating—renders the present invention uniquely advantageous in terms of process simplicity, cost efficiency, and scalability.

The present invention resolves these longstanding limitations by demonstrating that a specific combination of casting parameters—namely, the precise control of oxygen content, titanium addition, and the use of copper chill plates to induce sub-rapid solidification—can trigger a cascade of microstructural transformations that culminate in the formation of a hardened surface layer composed of ultrafine lath martensite, upper bainite, and acicular ferrite. This microstructure is formed entirely during solidification and subsequent air cooling, without any external intervention. The resulting material exhibits a surface hardness equivalent to that of medium- to high-carbon steels, while retaining the ductility and toughness characteristic of low-carbon steel cores. This combination of properties has not been previously attainable through any known casting or processing method, and represents a transformative advancement in the field of steel metallurgy.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for manufacturing low-carbon steel having a hardened surface layer without the application of any post-casting heat treatment, surface coating, mechanical deformation, or alloying beyond the minimal additions necessary to control solidification behavior. The method comprises the steps of melting a low-carbon steel composition containing no more than 0.030 weight percent carbon, adjusting the free oxygen content of the molten steel to a range between 35 and 50 parts per million, introducing titanium in an amount sufficient to achieve a final titanium content of between 0.010 and 0.020 weight percent, and subsequently casting the molten steel between two copper plates maintained at ambient temperature, wherein the copper plates are separated by a narrow gap of approximately 2.5 millimeters, and wherein the casting process is completed within a time interval of no more than 0.3 seconds, such that the solidification rate at a depth of 0 to 50 micrometers from the surface exceeds 900 kelvins per second, and at a depth of 50 to 200 micrometers exceeds 400 kelvins per second. Following casting, the steel is allowed to cool in ambient air for a period of at least 20 seconds and then quenched in water to arrest any further phase transformation.

The resulting steel article exhibits a surface layer extending from the outer surface to a depth of at least 200 micrometers, wherein the surface layer contains a mixed microstructure comprising lath martensite, upper bainite, and acicular ferrite. The lath martensite is characterized by ultrafine laths with an average width of less than 150 nanometers, a body-centered tetragonal crystal structure with measurable lattice distortion, and a nanohardness of at least 9.0 gigapascals. The upper bainite is present in two distinct morphological forms: a first form characterized by elongated laths with length-to-width ratios exceeding 30, located within the 0-50 micrometer depth region, and a second form characterized by shorter laths with length-to-width ratios less than 10, located within the 50-200 micrometer depth region. The acicular ferrite is present as needle-like grains with high-angle misorientations and an average width of approximately 800 nanometers, and is confined primarily to the 50-200 micrometer depth region. The presence of cementite precipitates between the laths of the upper bainite is confirmed by electron diffraction analysis, indicating a precipitation strengthening mechanism.

The surface layer exhibits a yield strength of at least 2200 megapascals, attributable primarily to the lath martensite component, which contributes a yield strength of approximately 2378 megapascals. The underlying core of the steel, beyond the 200-micrometer depth, retains the mechanical properties of conventional low-carbon steel, including elongation in excess of 25 percent and Charpy impact energy values comparable to those of unmodified low-carbon steel. The entire article is produced in a single casting operation without any subsequent thermal, mechanical, or chemical processing, and requires no additional surface treatment to achieve the hardened surface layer.

The invention further encompasses the steel article itself, which comprises a low-carbon steel substrate with a surface layer extending from 0 to 200 micrometers in depth, wherein the surface layer contains at least 30 volume percent lath martensite, and wherein the average nanohardness of the surface layer is at least 7.5 gigapascals, and the average yield strength is at least 2000 megapascals. The surface layer is formed entirely during solidification and subsequent cooling, and exhibits no evidence of decarburization, oxidation, or surface contamination. The lath martensite exhibits a lattice distortion parameter of at least 0.3 percent, as determined by the difference in interplanar spacing between the (110) and (011) crystallographic planes, and the carbon content within the martensite laths is estimated to be at least 0.10 weight percent, despite the bulk carbon content of the steel being no greater than 0.030 weight percent. The formation of this carbon-enriched martensite is attributed to the segregation of carbon during the prior formation of bainitic ferrite, which expels carbon into adjacent austenite regions, thereby locally increasing the carbon concentration to levels sufficient to enable diffusionless transformation into martensite upon further cooling.

The invention provides a material that achieves the mechanical performance of medium-carbon steels without the associated drawbacks of high carbon content, such as reduced weldability, increased brittleness, and higher susceptibility to hydrogen embrittlement. The method is scalable, compatible with continuous casting operations, and requires no additional energy input beyond that used for melting and pouring. The resulting product is suitable for applications in automotive components, railway tracks, mining equipment, construction machinery, and tooling surfaces where surface wear resistance is critical but bulk ductility must be preserved. The invention represents a paradigm shift in steel manufacturing by demonstrating that exceptional surface hardness can be achieved not through alloying or post-processing, but through the precise control of solidification kinetics and interfacial heat transfer during casting.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a novel method for producing low-carbon steel with an inherently hardened surface layer, wherein the surface hardness is achieved solely through the control of casting parameters and without any subsequent thermal, mechanical, or chemical treatment. The invention is based on the discovery that, under specific solidification conditions, a low-carbon steel composition can develop a surface microstructure composed of lath martensite, upper bainite, and acicular ferrite, which collectively impart a level of surface hardness and yield strength previously unattainable in steels with carbon content below 0.10 weight percent. The method relies on the synergistic interaction of three critical factors: the precise control of oxygen content in the melt, the intentional addition of titanium to modify nucleation behavior, and the use of copper chill plates to induce sub-rapid solidification with a steep thermal gradient. These factors, when combined in the manner described herein, trigger a sequence of phase transformations during cooling that result in the formation of an ultrafine, carbon-enriched martensitic phase within a shallow surface region, despite the overall carbon content of the steel being exceptionally low.

The steel composition used in the method contains no more than 0.030 weight percent carbon, preferably between 0.020 and 0.028 weight percent, to ensure that the bulk material retains the desired ductility and toughness characteristic of low-carbon steels. The carbon content is deliberately maintained below the threshold at which conventional martensite formation is expected to occur, thereby ensuring that the resulting hardness is not due to bulk carbon enrichment but rather to localized microstructural phenomena. The melt is prepared by melting high-purity iron and controlled additions of manganese, silicon, and other trace elements in an induction furnace, with the free oxygen content of the molten steel being carefully adjusted to a range of 35 to 50 parts per million. This oxygen content is critical, as it influences the formation of oxide inclusions that act as heterogeneous nucleation sites during solidification. Oxygen levels below 35 ppm result in insufficient nucleation density, leading to coarse microstructures and reduced surface hardness, while oxygen levels exceeding 50 ppm promote excessive oxide formation, which can degrade toughness and introduce porosity. The oxygen content is controlled by adding controlled amounts of ferrous oxide (FeO) to the melt during the final stages of melting, followed by degassing to stabilize the concentration.

Following oxygen adjustment, titanium is introduced into the melt in the form of ferrotitanium, with the final titanium content being maintained between 0.010 and 0.020 weight percent. Titanium serves a dual function: it forms fine titanium carbides and nitrides that refine the prior austenite grain structure, and it stabilizes the austenite phase during the initial stages of solidification, thereby delaying the onset of ferrite transformation and allowing for the development of a supersaturated austenitic matrix. The presence of titanium also enhances the solubility of carbon in the austenite phase, which facilitates the subsequent carbon segregation mechanism that leads to localized carbon enrichment. The titanium content must be carefully controlled; levels below 0.010 weight percent are insufficient to significantly influence nucleation kinetics, while levels above 0.020 weight percent promote excessive precipitation of titanium carbides, which can act as stress concentrators and reduce ductility.

The molten steel is then cast between two copper plates, each having a thickness of approximately 9 millimeters, with a narrow gap of 2.5 millimeters maintained between them. The copper plates are held at ambient temperature, typically between 20 and 25 degrees Celsius, and serve as high-conductivity chill molds that rapidly extract heat from the molten steel upon contact. The casting process is completed within a time interval of no more than 0.3 seconds, during which the molten steel fills the gap and begins to solidify from both surfaces inward. The high thermal conductivity of copper, combined with the thin geometry of the cast sheet, results in an extremely steep thermal gradient across the thickness of the solidifying steel. This gradient ensures that the solidification rate at the surface—defined as the region within 50 micrometers of the outer surface—exceeds 900 kelvins per second, while the rate at depths between 50 and 200 micrometers remains above 400 kelvins per second. These cooling rates fall within the sub-rapid solidification regime, which is sufficient to suppress the formation of coarse ferrite and pearlite but not so extreme as to induce full martensitic transformation throughout the entire cross-section.

The rapid cooling at the surface causes the initial solidification to produce a fine, equiaxed austenitic microstructure with an average prior austenite grain size of approximately 150 micrometers, as confirmed by optical and electron microscopy. Upon further cooling, the surface region undergoes a series of sequential phase transformations. First, bainitic ferrite begins to nucleate at austenite grain boundaries and grows inward as laths, expelling carbon into the surrounding austenite due to the lower solubility of carbon in ferrite compared to austenite. As these bainitic laths grow, the carbon concentration in the adjacent austenite increases to levels exceeding 0.10 weight percent, far above the bulk carbon content of the steel. This localized carbon enrichment creates a thermodynamic driving force sufficient to enable the diffusionless transformation of austenite into lath martensite, even though the overall carbon content of the steel remains below 0.030 weight percent. The cooling rate in the 0-50 micrometer region is estimated to be between 50 and 100 kelvins per second, which is sufficient to bypass the bainite nose on the continuous cooling transformation diagram and promote direct martensite formation from the carbon-enriched austenite.

In the deeper region, between 50 and 200 micrometers, the cooling rate slows to between 10 and 30 kelvins per second, which falls within the transition zone between bainite and acicular ferrite formation. Here, acicular ferrite nucleates preferentially on oxide inclusions within the austenite grains, forming needle-like grains that intersect and divide the austenite into smaller sub-regions. These acicular ferrite needles serve as both nucleation sites and growth barriers for the subsequent formation of upper bainite. The resulting microstructure in this region consists of a mixture of acicular ferrite and shorter, less elongated upper bainite laths, with length-to-width ratios below 10. The presence of cementite precipitates between the bainite laths is confirmed by transmission electron microscopy and selected area electron diffraction, which reveal the Isaichev orientation relationship between the ferrite and cementite phases. The nanohardness of this region is lower than that of the surface layer due to the absence of martensite and the larger grain size of the ferritic constituents.

The resulting steel article exhibits a distinct microstructural gradient, with the surface layer (0-50 micrometers) dominated by lath martensite and elongated upper bainite (designated B1), and the intermediate layer (50-200 micrometers) composed of acicular ferrite and shorter upper bainite (designated B2). The lath martensite is characterized by ultrafine laths with an average width of 128 nanometers, well within the range of 100 to 250 nanometers observed in high-carbon steels, but unprecedented in low-carbon steels. The lattice distortion of the martensite, as determined by the difference in interplanar spacing between the (110) and (011) crystallographic planes, is measured at approximately 0.4 percent, consistent with a carbon content of about 0.14 weight percent within the martensite laths. This local enrichment is not a result of bulk carbon addition but rather the consequence of carbon redistribution during prior bainite formation. The nanohardness of the lath martensite is measured at 9.87 ± 0.51 gigapascals, which is comparable to the hardness of medium-carbon steels with 0.40 to 0.45 weight percent carbon. The upper bainite exhibits a nanohardness of 4.18 ± 0.39 gigapascals, attributable to a combination of fine lath morphology and precipitation strengthening from cementite. The acicular ferrite, with an average grain width of 800 nanometers, exhibits a nanohardness of 2.93 ± 0.30 gigapascals, consistent with its ferritic nature and the influence of grain boundary strengthening.

The yield strength of the surface layer is calculated from the nanohardness data using established empirical relationships, yielding a value of 2378 ± 123 megapascals for the lath martensite, 1007 ± 94 megapascals for the upper bainite, and 706 ± 72 megapascals for the acicular ferrite. The composite yield strength of the surface layer, accounting for the volume fractions of each phase, exceeds 2200 megapascals, which is more than double the yield strength of conventional low-carbon steels and approaches that of quenched and tempered medium-carbon steels. Crucially, the core of the steel, beyond the 200-micrometer depth, maintains a yield strength below 400 megapascals and an elongation greater than 25 percent, preserving the ductility and toughness that define low-carbon steels. This combination of a hardened surface and a ductile core is achieved without any post-casting intervention, making the material uniquely suited for applications requiring surface wear resistance without compromising formability or weldability.

The method is fully compatible with industrial casting practices and can be implemented in continuous casting lines by modifying the mold design to incorporate copper chill inserts or by adjusting the mold cooling rate through controlled water flow. The casting process requires no additional energy input beyond that needed for melting and pouring, and the resulting steel article requires no heat treatment, surface coating, or machining to achieve its enhanced surface properties. The absence of surface oxidation or decarburization is confirmed by electron probe microanalysis and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, which show no detectable oxygen or carbon depletion at the surface. The microstructure is homogeneous and continuous from the surface inward, with no evidence of delamination, cracking, or interfacial defects.

The invention further encompasses the steel article produced by the method, which comprises a low-carbon steel substrate with a surface layer extending from 0 to 200 micrometers in depth, wherein the surface layer contains at least 30 volume percent lath martensite, at least 20 volume percent upper bainite, and up to 30 volume percent acicular ferrite. The surface layer exhibits an average nanohardness of at least 7.5 gigapascals, a Vickers hardness of at least 400 HV, and a yield strength of at least 2000 megapascals. The lath martensite component exhibits a lattice distortion parameter of at least 0.3 percent, and the carbon content within the martensite laths is estimated to be between 0.10 and 0.16 weight percent, despite the bulk carbon content being no greater than 0.030 weight percent. The upper bainite is present in two distinct morphologies, with the B1 form occupying the 0-50 micrometer region and exhibiting length-to-width ratios greater than 30, and the B2 form occupying the 50-200 micrometer region and exhibiting length-to-width ratios less than 10. The acicular ferrite is confined to the 50-200 micrometer region and is characterized by needle-like grains with high-angle misorientations and an average width of 700 to 900 nanometers.

The invention provides a material that eliminates the need for energy-intensive surface hardening processes, reduces manufacturing costs, minimizes environmental impact, and enhances product performance. The method is scalable, reproducible, and suitable for high-volume production. The resulting steel articles can be used in a wide range of applications, including but not limited to: railway rails, mining shovel teeth, excavator buckets, gear teeth, bearing surfaces, tooling inserts, and automotive suspension components. The material’s combination of surface hardness and core ductility makes it ideal for components subjected to cyclic loading, impact, and abrasive wear, where traditional low-carbon steels fail and high-carbon steels are too brittle.

## EXAMPLES

The following examples illustrate the implementation of the invention and demonstrate the reproducibility and robustness of the method across multiple casting trials. Each example was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions using the same furnace, mold configuration, and analytical instrumentation as described in the detailed description. The steel composition was prepared using high-purity iron (99.99 percent), with controlled additions of manganese, silicon, titanium, and other trace elements to achieve the specified chemistry. The oxygen content was measured using a Leco oxygen/nitrogen analyzer, and the titanium content was verified by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry. All castings were performed using identical copper chill plates with a 2.5-millimeter gap, and the solidification time was monitored using high-speed thermocouples placed at 10-micrometer intervals from the surface. The resulting microstructures were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy, transmission electron microscopy, and nanoindentation, with all measurements conducted in accordance with standardized protocols.

In Example 1, a steel composition was prepared containing 0.023 weight percent carbon, 0.85 weight percent manganese, 0.25 weight percent silicon, 0.015 weight percent titanium, and the remainder iron, with a free oxygen content of 42 parts per million. The melt was heated to 1600 degrees Celsius and poured between two copper plates separated by a 2.5-millimeter gap. The casting process was completed in 0.24 seconds, and the resulting steel sheet was air-cooled for 30 seconds before being quenched in water. The solidification rate at the surface (0-50 micrometers) was measured at 940 kelvins per second, and at 50-200 micrometers at 820 kelvins per second. Microstructural analysis revealed the presence of lath martensite, upper bainite, and acicular ferrite in the surface layer. The lath martensite exhibited an average lath width of 125 nanometers, with a lattice distortion of 0.38 percent as determined by the difference in interplanar spacing between the (110) and (011) planes. The nanohardness of the lath martensite was measured at 9.72 ± 0.48 gigapascals, while the upper bainite showed a nanohardness of 4.12 ± 0.35 gigapascals and the acicular ferrite a nanohardness of 2.89 ± 0.27 gigapascals. The yield strength of the surface layer was calculated to be 2350 ± 115 megapascals. The core material beyond 200 micrometers exhibited a yield strength of 380 megapascals and an elongation of 28 percent. The surface exhibited no visible oxidation, decarburization, or surface defects.

In Example 2, the same composition was used, but the oxygen content was adjusted to 38 parts per million and the titanium content was increased to 0.018 weight percent. The casting time was 0.22 seconds, with a surface solidification rate of 910 kelvins per second and an intermediate rate of 780 kelvins per second. The resulting microstructure was similar to Example 1, with lath martensite laths averaging 130 nanometers in width and a lattice distortion of 0.41 percent. The nanohardness values were 9.91 ± 0.53 gigapascals for martensite, 4.25 ± 0.41 gigapascals for upper bainite, and 2.97 ± 0.32 gigapascals for acicular ferrite. The calculated yield strength of the surface layer was 2405 ± 128 megapascals. The core retained a yield strength of 375 megapascals and an elongation of 27 percent. The B1 upper bainite in the 0-50 micrometer region exhibited length-to-width ratios exceeding 35, while the B2 upper bainite in the 50-200 micrometer region had ratios below 9. Cementite precipitates were clearly observed between the bainite laths in both regions, confirming the precipitation strengthening mechanism.

In Example 3, the carbon content was reduced to 0.020 weight percent, while the titanium content was maintained at 0.015 weight percent and the oxygen content at 45 parts per million. The casting time was 0.25 seconds, with a surface solidification rate of 905 kelvins per second. The lath martensite laths were slightly finer, averaging 118 nanometers in width, and the lattice distortion was measured at 0.35 percent. The nanohardness of the martensite was 9.56 ± 0.45 gigapascals, still significantly higher than any previously reported value for low-carbon steels. The upper bainite nanohardness was 4.08 ± 0.37 gigapascals, and the acicular ferrite was 2.91 ± 0.29 gigapascals. The calculated yield strength of the surface layer was 2310 ± 110 megapascals. The core material exhibited a yield strength of 390 megapascals and an elongation of 29 percent. The absence of any surface treatment and the retention of high hardness confirm that the mechanism is intrinsic to the casting process and not dependent on minor compositional variations.

In Example 4, the titanium content was reduced to 0.008 weight percent, while all other parameters were held constant. The resulting microstructure showed a significant reduction in the volume fraction of lath martensite, with the surface layer dominated by upper bainite and acicular ferrite. The nanohardness of the surface region dropped to 6.12 ± 0.58 gigapascals, and the yield strength was reduced to 1550 ± 130 megapascals. The lattice distortion of the martensite was negligible, indicating that the carbon enrichment mechanism was insufficient without adequate titanium to stabilize the austenite and promote carbon segregation. This example demonstrates that titanium within the specified range is essential to the formation of the hardened surface layer.

In Example 5, the oxygen content was increased to 60 parts per million, resulting in excessive oxide formation and porosity in the surface layer. The solidification rate remained high, but the microstructure was discontinuous, with large oxide clusters acting as crack initiation sites. The nanohardness of the martensite was inconsistent, ranging from 5.8 to 8.2 gigapascals, and the yield strength of the surface layer was reduced to 1720 ± 150 megapascals. This example confirms that oxygen content must be tightly controlled within the 35-50 ppm range to avoid detrimental effects on microstructural integrity.

In Example 6, the casting gap was increased to 5 millimeters, resulting in a slower solidification rate of 420 kelvins per second at the surface. The resulting microstructure consisted primarily of coarse ferrite and pearlite, with no detectable martensite. The nanohardness of the surface was 3.2 ± 0.3 gigapascals, and the yield strength was 850 ± 75 megapascals. This example demonstrates that the sub-rapid solidification rate is critical to the formation of the hardened layer, and that conventional casting rates are insufficient to achieve the desired microstructure.

In Example 7, the copper plates were replaced with steel plates of identical geometry. The solidification rate dropped to 120 kelvins per second at the surface, and the microstructure was entirely ferritic with no bainite or martensite. The nanohardness was 2.1 ± 0.2 gigapascals, confirming that the high thermal conductivity of copper is essential to achieving the necessary cooling gradient.

In Example 8, the same composition and casting parameters as Example 1 were used, but the air-cooling time was reduced to 10 seconds before water quenching. The resulting microstructure was nearly identical, with no significant difference in nanohardness or yield strength, indicating that the phase transformations are complete within 20 seconds of casting and that the water quench is primarily for stabilization rather than transformation control.

In Example 9, a larger-scale casting was performed using a continuous casting machine modified with copper chill inserts along the mold walls. The casting speed was adjusted to maintain a solidification rate of 900 kelvins per second at the surface. A 10-meter-long slab was produced, and samples were taken at intervals of 1 meter. Microstructural analysis showed consistent lath martensite formation throughout the entire length, with average lath widths of 120 to 135 nanometers and nanohardness values between 9.6 and 9.9 gigapascals. This example demonstrates the scalability of the invention to industrial production levels.

In Example 10, the steel article from Example 1 was subjected to a series of wear tests using a pin-on-disk apparatus under a load of 50 newtons and a sliding speed of 0.5 meters per second. The wear rate was measured at 1.2 × 10⁻⁶ mm³/N·m, which is comparable to that of quenched and tempered 1045 steel and significantly lower than that of unmodified low-carbon steel (8.7 × 10⁻⁶ mm³/N·m). The surface retained its hardness after 10,000 cycles, with no visible spalling or cracking. This confirms the practical utility of the invention in wear-resistant applications.

The examples collectively demonstrate that the invention is reproducible, robust, and scalable. The method consistently produces a hardened surface layer with nanohardness exceeding 9.0 gigapascals and yield strength exceeding 2200 megapascals in low-carbon steel without any post-casting treatment. The critical parameters—carbon content, oxygen content, titanium content, copper chill plates, and solidification rate—are interdependent and must be maintained within the specified ranges to achieve the desired outcome. Deviations from these parameters result in the loss of martensite formation and a corresponding reduction in surface hardness. The invention thus provides a novel, industrially viable method for producing high-performance low-carbon steel with an inherently hardened surface, representing a significant advancement over all prior art.
