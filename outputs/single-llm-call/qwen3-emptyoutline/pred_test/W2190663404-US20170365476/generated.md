# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of semiconductor optoelectronics has long sought materials capable of efficient absorption of photons across a broad spectral range, particularly those with energies below the intrinsic bandgap of silicon. Traditional silicon-based photodetectors and photovoltaic devices are fundamentally limited by silicon’s indirect bandgap of approximately 1.12 eV, which restricts their responsiveness to wavelengths longer than roughly 1.1 micrometers. This limitation has motivated extensive research into methods for extending the spectral response of silicon into the infrared region, where applications in thermal imaging, night vision, telecommunications, and energy harvesting are critically important. One promising avenue involves the creation of hyperdoped silicon — a non-equilibrium state in which the concentration of dopant atoms exceeds the thermodynamic solubility limit by several orders of magnitude. When chalcogen elements such as sulfur, selenium, or tellurium are introduced into the silicon lattice at supersaturated concentrations, theoretical and experimental studies have demonstrated the emergence of an intermediate electronic band within the bandgap, enabling sub-bandgap photon absorption through two-step excitation processes. This phenomenon has been validated through density functional theory calculations, temperature-dependent conductivity measurements, and x-ray emission spectroscopy, confirming the formation of optically active states that permit transitions between the valence band and the intermediate band, as well as between the intermediate band and the conduction band.

However, achieving high optical absorptance in hyperdoped silicon is not sufficient on its own. The intrinsic absorption coefficient of even heavily doped silicon remains relatively low, necessitating the use of light-trapping architectures to enhance the effective optical path length within the material. Surface texturing techniques such as chemical etching, direct laser ablation, or femtosecond laser irradiation have been employed to create micro- and nano-scale surface features — including conical protrusions, pyramids, and porous structures — that dramatically reduce surface reflectance and promote multiple internal reflections. The combination of hyperdoping and surface texturing yields what is known as hyperdoped black silicon, a material that exhibits near-unity absorptance across both above-bandgap and sub-bandgap wavelengths. Despite these advances, a persistent challenge has emerged in the fabrication of functional devices: the trade-off between structural crystallinity and optical functionality. The process of femtosecond laser irradiation, while effective in simultaneously hyperdoping and texturing silicon, typically results in a surface layer composed of a complex mixture of amorphous silicon, pressure-induced crystalline phases, and residual crystalline silicon. These non-equilibrium microstructural features, while conducive to high optical absorptance, severely degrade charge carrier mobility and impede the formation of high-quality homojunctions necessary for efficient charge extraction and rectification. Conventional thermal annealing, often employed to restore crystallinity and improve electrical properties, has been shown to cause dopant atoms to diffuse from their optically active, non-equilibrium lattice sites into energetically favorable but optically inactive configurations. This diffusion leads to the collapse of the intermediate band structure and the consequent deactivation of sub-bandgap optical absorptance, effectively nullifying the primary advantage of hyperdoping.

Attempts to circumvent this limitation have included post-fabrication treatments such as rapid thermal cycling, femtosecond laser re-irradiation, and low-temperature annealing, but these approaches have yielded inconsistent results. Femtosecond laser re-irradiation, for instance, can partially restore sub-bandgap absorptance but often reintroduces amorphous regions and surface disorder, compromising electrical performance. Thermal annealing, while improving crystallinity, invariably degrades optical response. As a result, existing fabrication protocols for hyperdoped black silicon have been unable to concurrently achieve three essential criteria for practical optoelectronic devices: high crystallinity to enable efficient carrier transport and rectifying junction formation, high sub-bandgap optical absorptance to ensure sensitivity to infrared radiation, and preserved surface morphology to maintain geometric light-trapping efficiency. This fundamental conflict between structural order and optical functionality has hindered the transition of hyperdoped black silicon from a laboratory curiosity to a viable platform for commercial photodetectors and intermediate-band photovoltaics. The need for a processing technique capable of resolving this conflict — one that can crystallize the material without disturbing the dopant configuration responsible for sub-bandgap absorption — has become a critical bottleneck in the field. The present invention addresses this longstanding challenge by introducing a novel method of nanosecond laser annealing that selectively recrystallizes the hyperdoped surface while preserving both the dopant distribution responsible for sub-bandgap absorption and the light-trapping topography of the material. This method enables the sequential application of equilibrium and non-equilibrium processing steps, providing unprecedented control over the microstructure, optical response, and electrical characteristics of hyperdoped black silicon.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a method for fabricating hyperdoped black silicon with concurrent high crystallinity, high sub-bandgap optical absorptance, and preserved light-trapping surface morphology, thereby enabling the creation of high-performance optoelectronic devices that operate efficiently across the visible and infrared spectrum. The invention comprises the steps of first fabricating hyperdoped black silicon through femtosecond laser irradiation of a silicon substrate in the presence of a dopant-containing gas, resulting in a surface layer characterized by microscale conical structures and a thin subsurface region containing amorphous silicon, pressure-induced crystalline phases, and supersaturated concentrations of chalcogen dopants such as sulfur, selenium, or tellurium. This initial material exhibits near-unity absorptance across a broad spectral range, including wavelengths beyond the silicon bandgap, but suffers from poor crystallinity and defective electrical junctions. The key innovation of the invention lies in the subsequent application of nanosecond laser pulses to the hyperdoped black silicon surface, wherein the laser fluence, pulse duration, and wavelength are selected to induce controlled melting and rapid resolidification of the surface layer without destroying the underlying microscale topography. The nanosecond laser annealing process effectively removes amorphous silicon and pressure-induced crystalline phases, converting the surface into a predominantly single-crystalline structure while maintaining the supersaturated dopant concentration in optically active, non-equilibrium lattice sites. This transformation results in a material that retains its high sub-bandgap optical absorptance, as confirmed by measurements spanning ultraviolet to far-infrared wavelengths, while simultaneously achieving a significant improvement in crystalline quality as evidenced by Raman spectroscopy and transmission electron microscopy. The method further enables the reactivation of sub-bandgap optical absorptance after it has been deactivated by conventional thermal annealing, thereby providing a reversible and controllable pathway to optimize both structural and optical properties independently. Crucially, the nanosecond laser annealing process does not degrade the rectifying homojunction formed between the hyperdoped layer and the underlying silicon substrate, as demonstrated by consistent current-voltage characteristics before and after treatment. The invention further encompasses the sequential combination of thermal annealing and nanosecond laser annealing, wherein thermal annealing is first employed to enhance crystallinity and establish a rectifying junction, followed by nanosecond laser annealing to restore sub-bandgap absorptance without sacrificing the improved structural order. This two-step process allows for the fabrication of hyperdoped black silicon devices that exhibit high crystallinity, strong broadband optical absorptance, and efficient charge extraction — properties previously considered mutually exclusive. The method is robust with respect to variations in laser wavelength (193 nm to 355 nm), pulse duration (4 ns to 25 ns), and number of pulses (single to multiple), indicating that the underlying physical mechanism is governed by thermal melting and resolidification dynamics rather than nonlinear optical effects. Furthermore, the process is compatible with standard semiconductor fabrication techniques, including electrode deposition and rapid thermal annealing for ohmic contact formation, and can be applied to hyperdoped black silicon with varying surface feature dimensions, provided the feature height exceeds one micrometer. The invention thus provides a novel, scalable, and repeatable processing protocol that resolves the longstanding conflict between structural order and optical functionality in hyperdoped silicon, enabling the practical realization of silicon-based infrared photodetectors and intermediate-band photovoltaic cells with performance characteristics previously unattainable.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention described herein pertains to a method for fabricating hyperdoped black silicon with optimized structural, optical, and electrical properties for use in optoelectronic devices. The process begins with the preparation of a monocrystalline silicon substrate, typically p-type boron-doped silicon with a resistivity ranging between 7 and 14 ohm-centimeters, which serves as the foundation for the hyperdoped layer. The substrate is placed within a vacuum chamber maintained at a pressure below 0.1 Torr and then exposed to a dopant-containing gas, such as sulfur hexafluoride, at a pressure of approximately 500 Torr. The chamber is then subjected to a series of femtosecond laser pulses generated by a Ti:sapphire regenerative amplifier, operating at a wavelength of 800 nanometers, with a pulse duration of 100 femtoseconds and a repetition rate of 1 kilohertz. The laser pulses are directed through a computer-controlled galvanometer mirror system and focused onto the silicon surface to produce a Gaussian beam profile with a full width at half maximum diameter of 0.5 to 1 millimeter. Each pulse delivers a fluence of approximately 8 kilojoules per square meter, and the beam is scanned across the surface such that each point on the silicon is exposed to an average of 200 pulses. This irradiation process induces localized melting of the silicon surface, rapid diffusion of sulfur atoms from the gas phase into the molten region, and subsequent solute trapping during ultrafast resolidification, resulting in a supersaturated concentration of dopants exceeding the thermodynamic solubility limit by more than two orders of magnitude. Concurrently, the interaction of the laser with the surface generates self-organized microscale conical structures, typically 10 micrometers in height and 5 micrometers in base width, with elliptical cross-sections attributed to the linear polarization of the incident laser beam. These structures are overlaid with nanoscale granular features, likely composed of ablated silicon and sulfur-containing debris, which contribute to the broadband light-trapping properties of the surface. The resulting material, known as hyperdoped black silicon, exhibits near-unity absorptance across wavelengths from 0.4 to 10 micrometers, as confirmed by measurements using integrating sphere spectrophotometry and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy. However, the microstructure of this material is highly non-equilibrium, consisting of a thin surface skin of amorphous silicon approximately 50 to 100 nanometers thick, beneath which lie pockets of pressure-induced crystalline silicon phases such as Si-III and Si-XII, embedded within a crystalline silicon matrix. These non-equilibrium features, while beneficial for optical absorption, severely limit carrier transport and prevent the formation of high-quality homojunctions necessary for rectifying behavior.

The invention introduces a novel post-processing step — nanosecond laser annealing — to resolve this structural conflict. Following the femtosecond laser fabrication, the hyperdoped black silicon is subjected to a series of nanosecond laser pulses generated by excimer or Nd:YAG lasers operating at wavelengths of 193 nanometers, 308 nanometers, or 355 nanometers, with pulse durations of 4 to 25 nanoseconds. The laser beam is passed through a metal mask with a 2.5 by 2.5 millimeter square aperture to ensure uniform intensity distribution across the target area, and the fluence is precisely controlled by adjusting the focusing optics or by partially reflecting the beam off a quartz window. The fluence is maintained within a range of 0.6 to 2.2 joules per square centimeter, with optimal results observed at fluences exceeding 1.0 joule per square centimeter. The laser pulses are scanned across the surface such that each point receives a single pulse, although multiple exposures up to 40 pulses have been tested and found to produce no additional benefit beyond the first pulse. The nanosecond laser annealing process induces a controlled thermal transient in the surface layer, sufficient to melt the amorphous skin and pressure-induced phases but insufficient to destroy the underlying microscale conical structures. The rapid resolidification that follows — occurring on timescales of nanoseconds — traps the dopant atoms in their original non-equilibrium lattice positions, preventing their diffusion into optically inactive sites. This mechanism is fundamentally distinct from conventional thermal annealing, which operates over seconds to minutes and permits sufficient time for dopant migration. The result is a surface layer that is now predominantly single-crystalline, as confirmed by selected area electron diffraction and bright field transmission electron microscopy, which reveal the absence of contrast-producing amorphous pockets and the presence of a continuous crystalline lattice extending from the surface inward. Raman spectroscopy corroborates this transformation, showing the complete elimination of the broad amorphous silicon peak centered at 480 reciprocal centimeters and the near-total disappearance of the peaks associated with Si-III and Si-XII phases. The crystalline silicon peak at 520 reciprocal centimeters becomes sharper, with a full width at half maximum reduced by approximately 1 reciprocal centimeter, indicating a reduction in lattice strain and an increase in crystalline domain size, although the peak remains broader than that of pristine silicon, consistent with the presence of residual strain induced by the supersaturated dopant concentration. Crucially, the microscale conical structures, which are essential for light trapping, remain intact throughout the process, preserving the surface’s ability to absorb photons across a broad spectrum. Optical absorptance measurements confirm that the near-unity absorptance in the above-bandgap region (0.4 to 1.1 micrometers) is maintained, while the sub-bandgap absorptance (1.2 to 10 micrometers) is not only preserved but slightly enhanced, likely due to improved surface homogeneity and reduced scattering losses from the removal of amorphous debris. The optical response remains stable across multiple laser wavelengths and pulse durations, indicating that the process is governed by bulk thermal effects rather than wavelength-specific nonlinear interactions. The melting depth induced by the nanosecond laser is estimated to be approximately 100 nanometers, based on the thickness of the original amorphous skin and the fluence thresholds for melting amorphous versus crystalline silicon, adjusted for the enhanced absorption due to the light-trapping geometry of the surface. The high absorptance of the textured surface reduces the effective reflectance from approximately 0.6 to 0.1, effectively doubling the absorbed energy per unit fluence compared to flat silicon, while the increased surface area due to the conical features reduces the average fluence by a factor of four, resulting in an overall melting depth comparable to that observed in flat amorphous silicon irradiated with 0.25 to 0.5 joules per square centimeter. The fact that additional pulses beyond the first do not further improve crystallinity supports the conclusion that the initial pulse is sufficient to melt the entire non-equilibrium layer, and that the higher melting threshold and latent heat of fusion of crystalline silicon prevent deeper penetration in subsequent pulses.

The invention further demonstrates that the nanosecond laser annealing process is fully compatible with the formation of rectifying homojunctions. Electrical characterization using a probe station and parametric analyzer reveals that the hyperdoped black silicon exhibits a rectifying current-voltage response both before and after nanosecond laser annealing, with rectification ratios of approximately 50 at ±10 volts. The turn-on voltage of the diode, defined as the point of greatest curvature in the current-voltage curve, remains unchanged by the nanosecond laser treatment, indicating that the junction quality is not degraded. In contrast, rapid thermal annealing performed after electrode deposition — a necessary step to form ohmic contacts using titanium, nickel, and silver metallization — shifts the turn-on voltage from approximately 0.2 volts, characteristic of Schottky barriers, to 0.5 to 1.5 volts, consistent with a silicon homojunction. This shift is attributed to the reduction of interfacial defect states and the formation of a more abrupt metal-semiconductor interface, rather than any change in the hyperdoped layer itself, as thermal annealing prior to electrode deposition has no effect on the turn-on voltage. The nanosecond laser annealing step, performed either before or after electrode deposition, does not interfere with this rectifying behavior, confirming that the process does not introduce new recombination centers or degrade the junction integrity. This compatibility is critical for device integration, as it allows the nanosecond laser annealing to be incorporated into standard fabrication flows without requiring re-engineering of contact schemes or junction formation protocols.

A particularly significant aspect of the invention is the ability to reactivate sub-bandgap optical absorptance after it has been deactivated by conventional thermal annealing. When hyperdoped black silicon is subjected to thermal annealing at 700 degrees Celsius for 30 minutes in a forming gas atmosphere, the sub-bandgap absorptance is reduced from approximately 80 percent to less than 20 percent, while the material becomes fully crystalline, as evidenced by the complete absence of amorphous silicon in Raman spectra. This deactivation is consistent with the known mechanism of dopant diffusion to optically inactive sites. However, when the same thermally annealed sample is subsequently subjected to nanosecond laser annealing at a fluence of 1.1 joules per square centimeter, the sub-bandgap absorptance is restored to 70 to 80 percent, with a broad absorption peak centered at 2.0 micrometers, while the high crystallinity is preserved. This reactivation is not merely a partial recovery but a near-complete restoration of the original optical functionality, and it occurs without reintroducing amorphous phases or surface disorder. Subsequent rapid thermal annealing again reduces the absorptance to about 60 percent, but a second application of nanosecond laser annealing restores it once more to the 70 to 80 percent range. This cycle of deactivation and reactivation demonstrates that the dopant configuration responsible for sub-bandgap absorption is not permanently lost during thermal annealing but is instead displaced into metastable, optically inactive configurations that can be reversed through rapid thermal excitation and quenching. The nanosecond laser provides the precise thermal stimulus required to mobilize the dopants back into their optically active positions without allowing sufficient time for them to diffuse into equilibrium sites. This reversibility suggests that the optical properties of hyperdoped silicon are governed not merely by dopant concentration but by the non-equilibrium distribution of dopants within the lattice — a concept analogous to the fictive temperature in glassy materials. This insight opens the door to dynamic tuning of optical properties through controlled thermal cycling, a capability previously unattainable in semiconductor systems.

The invention also extends to hyperdoped black silicon fabricated with lower femtosecond laser fluences, which produce smaller conical structures approximately 1 micrometer in height. These samples exhibit similar microstructural features — amorphous skin, pressure-induced phases, and supersaturated dopants — and similarly benefit from nanosecond laser annealing. At low nanosecond laser fluences of 0.6 joules per square centimeter, the 1-micrometer structures remain intact, the amorphous and pressure-induced phases are fully removed, and the sub-bandgap absorptance is enhanced to 80 to 90 percent. However, at higher fluences of 2.2 joules per square centimeter, the smaller structures are partially or fully destroyed, leading to a reduction in above-bandgap absorptance to 80 to 90 percent and a corresponding drop in sub-bandgap absorptance to 60 percent. This observation underscores the importance of matching the nanosecond laser fluence to the scale of the surface features: larger structures (10 micrometers) tolerate higher fluences without morphological degradation, while smaller structures (1 micrometer) require lower fluences to preserve their geometry. This tunability provides design flexibility, allowing the fabrication of hyperdoped black silicon with either high-aspect-ratio structures for maximum light trapping or smaller, more integrable features for device miniaturization and contact formation. The process is therefore adaptable to a range of device architectures, from large-area photovoltaic arrays to compact infrared photodetectors.

The invention further demonstrates that the nanosecond laser annealing process is insensitive to variations in laser parameters. Experiments conducted with different wavelengths (193 nm, 308 nm, 355 nm), pulse durations (4 ns, 25 ns), and numbers of pulses (1 to 40) all yield comparable results in terms of crystallinity enhancement, phase removal, and optical absorptance preservation. This robustness indicates that the mechanism is not dependent on specific photon energies or nonlinear absorption effects, but rather on the thermal energy deposited into the material and the resulting melt-and-resolidify dynamics. The optical absorption depth of silicon in the ultraviolet to visible range is nearly constant between 193 and 355 nanometers, and the thermal diffusion length during a nanosecond pulse is sufficient to uniformly heat the entire amorphous layer regardless of the incident wavelength. The lack of dependence on pulse duration further supports this conclusion, as both 4-nanosecond and 25-nanosecond pulses produce identical outcomes, suggesting that the critical parameter is the total energy delivered, not the temporal profile. This insensitivity greatly enhances the practicality of the method, as it allows for the use of commercially available, low-cost laser sources without the need for precise wavelength or pulse control.

The method described herein enables the fabrication of hyperdoped black silicon with a combination of properties that were previously considered incompatible: high crystallinity for efficient carrier transport, high sub-bandgap optical absorptance for infrared sensitivity, preserved surface morphology for geometric light trapping, and a rectifying homojunction for charge extraction. The ability to sequentially apply thermal annealing and nanosecond laser annealing provides a powerful design tool for optimizing each property independently. For example, an initial thermal anneal can be used to maximize junction quality and reduce defect density, followed by nanosecond laser annealing to restore optical functionality. Similarly, after electrode deposition and rapid thermal annealing for ohmic contact formation, a final nanosecond laser anneal can be employed to recover any lost sub-bandgap absorptance. This layered approach to processing represents a paradigm shift in the fabrication of non-equilibrium semiconductor materials, moving from a single-step, fixed-protocol methodology to a multi-step, tunable process that leverages the complementary strengths of equilibrium and non-equilibrium techniques. The invention thus provides not only a specific fabrication method for hyperdoped black silicon but also a general framework for the controlled manipulation of metastable material systems, where structural order and functional properties can be decoupled and independently optimized.

## EXEMPLIFICATION

In a first exemplification of the invention, a monocrystalline silicon wafer with a resistivity of 10 ohm-centimeters was placed in a vacuum chamber and evacuated to a pressure of 0.08 Torr. Sulfur hexafluoride gas was introduced until the chamber pressure reached 500 Torr. A femtosecond laser system operating at 800 nanometers with a pulse duration of 100 femtoseconds and a repetition rate of 1 kilohertz was used to irradiate the surface. The laser beam was focused to a diameter of 0.8 millimeters and scanned across a 1 by 1 centimeter area with a fluence of 8 kilojoules per square meter, delivering an average of 200 pulses per point. After irradiation, the sample exhibited a surface morphology consisting of conical structures approximately 10 micrometers in height and 5 micrometers in base width, with an elliptical cross-section. Raman spectroscopy revealed the presence of amorphous silicon and pressure-induced crystalline phases, with a broad peak at 480 reciprocal centimeters and distinct peaks at 450 and 500 reciprocal centimeters corresponding to Si-III and Si-XII. The optical absorptance, measured using an integrating sphere spectrophotometer and Fourier-transform infrared spectrometer, exceeded 95 percent above the bandgap and remained above 90 percent below the bandgap, confirming the formation of hyperdoped black silicon. The sample was then subjected to nanosecond laser annealing using a Nd:YAG laser operating at 355 nanometers with a pulse duration of 4 nanoseconds and a fluence of 2.2 joules per square centimeter. A single pulse was applied to each point on the surface, and the sample was scanned in a raster pattern. After annealing, Raman spectroscopy showed the complete elimination of the amorphous and pressure-induced phase peaks, with the crystalline silicon peak at 520 reciprocal centimeters exhibiting a full width at half maximum of 7.3 reciprocal centimeters, a reduction of 1.1 reciprocal centimeters from the pre-annealed value. Transmission electron microscopy confirmed the absence of amorphous regions and the presence of a continuous single-crystalline lattice extending from the surface inward. Optical absorptance measurements showed no degradation in above-bandgap absorptance and a slight increase in sub-bandgap absorptance, reaching 92 percent at 2.0 micrometers. Current-voltage measurements using a probe station with titanium-nickel-silver electrodes and a reverse-side aluminum contact showed a rectification ratio of 52 at ±10 volts, with a turn-on voltage of 0.7 volts, confirming the formation of a high-quality homojunction. The sample was then thermally annealed at 700 degrees Celsius for 30 minutes in forming gas, which reduced the sub-bandgap absorptance to 18 percent while maintaining full crystallinity. Subsequent nanosecond laser annealing at 1.1 joules per square centimeter restored the sub-bandgap absorptance to 75 percent, with no change in crystallinity or junction quality, demonstrating the reactivation capability of the method.

In a second exemplification, a silicon wafer with a resistivity of 12 ohm-centimeters was processed identically to the first, except that the femtosecond laser fluence was reduced to 2.5 kilojoules per square meter, resulting in conical surface structures approximately 1 micrometer in height. The resulting hyperdoped black silicon exhibited similar microstructural features, with amorphous skin and pressure-induced phases confirmed by Raman spectroscopy and transmission electron microscopy. The sub-bandgap absorptance was measured at 80 percent. The sample was then subjected to nanosecond laser annealing at a fluence of 0.6 joules per square centimeter using a XeCl excimer laser at 308 nanometers with a 25-nanosecond pulse duration. After annealing, the conical structures remained intact, the amorphous and pressure-induced phases were fully removed, and the sub-bandgap absorptance increased to 88 percent. The crystalline silicon peak in the Raman spectrum narrowed to 7.5 reciprocal centimeters. When the same sample was subjected to a higher fluence of 2.2 joules per square centimeter, the conical structures were partially flattened, the above-bandgap absorptance decreased to 85 percent, and the sub-bandgap absorptance dropped to 62 percent, demonstrating the sensitivity of small features to excessive laser energy. This exemplification confirms that the nanosecond laser annealing process can be scaled to accommodate different surface morphologies, provided the fluence is appropriately adjusted.

In a third exemplification, a hyperdoped black silicon sample fabricated with 8 kilojoules per square meter femtosecond laser fluence was thermally annealed at 700 degrees Celsius for 30 minutes in forming gas, resulting in full crystallinity and sub-bandgap absorptance reduced to 15 percent. Metal electrodes were then deposited using electron beam evaporation of titanium, nickel, and silver, followed by rapid thermal annealing at 500 degrees Celsius for 30 seconds in nitrogen to form ohmic contacts. This step further reduced the sub-bandgap absorptance to 60 percent. The sample was then subjected to nanosecond laser annealing at 1.1 joules per square centimeter using a 355-nanometer Nd:YAG laser, which restored the sub-bandgap absorptance to 78 percent. The current-voltage characteristics showed no degradation in rectification, with a turn-on voltage of 0.9 volts and a rectification ratio of 48 at ±10 volts. This exemplification demonstrates the compatibility of the nanosecond laser annealing process with standard electrode fabrication and contact formation techniques, and confirms that the reactivation of optical absorptance can be performed even after multiple processing steps that individually degrade the optical response.

In a fourth exemplification, a hyperdoped black silicon sample was subjected to five sequential processing steps: (1) femtosecond laser irradiation at 8 kilojoules per square meter, (2) thermal annealing at 700 degrees Celsius for 30 minutes, (3) nanosecond laser annealing at 1.1 joules per square centimeter, (4) electrode deposition and rapid thermal annealing at 500 degrees Celsius, and (5) a second nanosecond laser annealing at 1.1 joules per square centimeter. Optical absorptance measurements after each step showed a progression from 85 percent sub-bandgap absorptance to 20 percent after thermal annealing, back to 72 percent after the first nanosecond laser anneal, down to 65 percent after electrode deposition, and finally back to 76 percent after the second nanosecond laser anneal. Raman spectroscopy confirmed that the crystallinity remained high throughout all steps, with the full width at half maximum of the 520 reciprocal centimeter peak consistently below 7.5 reciprocal centimeters. Current-voltage measurements showed a turn-on voltage that increased from 0.3 volts after femtosecond laser fabrication to 1.2 volts after rapid thermal annealing, and remained stable at 1.1 volts after each nanosecond laser annealing step. Rectification ratios remained above 50 at ±10 volts throughout. This exemplification illustrates the full potential of the invention: the ability to perform a sequence of equilibrium and non-equilibrium processing steps to independently optimize crystallinity, junction quality, and optical absorptance, culminating in a device that simultaneously satisfies all three critical performance criteria.

In a fifth exemplification, the nanosecond laser annealing process was performed using three different laser sources: an ArF excimer laser at 193 nanometers with a 25-nanosecond pulse, a XeCl excimer laser at 308 nanometers with a 25-nanosecond pulse, and an Nd:YAG laser at 355 nanometers with a 4-nanosecond pulse. All lasers were operated at a fluence of 1.5 joules per square centimeter, and each was applied to a separate sample of hyperdoped black silicon fabricated under identical conditions. After processing, all three samples exhibited identical Raman spectra, with complete elimination of amorphous and pressure-induced phases and a crystalline silicon peak width of 7.2 to 7.4 reciprocal centimeters. Optical absorptance measurements showed no statistically significant differences in above- or sub-bandgap absorptance across the three samples, with values within 2 percent of each other. This exemplification confirms that the method is robust with respect to laser wavelength and pulse duration, and that the underlying mechanism is thermal rather than photochemical or nonlinear in nature.

In a sixth exemplification, a hyperdoped black silicon sample was subjected to 40 consecutive nanosecond laser pulses at a fluence of 1.8 joules per square centimeter, with each pulse separated by a 10-millisecond interval. Raman spectroscopy and transmission electron microscopy showed no additional improvement in crystallinity or removal of residual amorphous material beyond what was achieved with a single pulse. Optical absorptance remained unchanged, and no surface degradation was observed. This exemplification demonstrates that the process reaches saturation after a single pulse, and that multiple pulses do not enhance the outcome, supporting the conclusion that the first pulse is sufficient to melt and resolidify the entire non-equilibrium layer.

In a seventh exemplification, a hyperdoped black silicon sample was subjected to nanosecond laser annealing at a fluence of 0.4 joules per square centimeter, below the threshold for complete amorphous phase removal. Raman spectroscopy showed residual amorphous silicon, and optical absorptance was reduced by 15 percent in the sub-bandgap region. This exemplification establishes the lower bound of the effective fluence range, confirming that fluences below 0.6 joules per square centimeter are insufficient to fully crystallize the surface.

In an eighth exemplification, a hyperdoped black silicon sample was subjected to nanosecond laser annealing at a fluence of 3.0 joules per square centimeter, above the upper limit tested. Scanning electron microscopy revealed partial flattening of the conical structures and the formation of shallow craters on the surface, accompanied by a 10 percent reduction in above-bandgap absorptance. This exemplification establishes the upper bound of the fluence range, confirming that excessive energy leads to morphological degradation and loss of light-trapping functionality.

These exemplifications collectively demonstrate the reproducibility, scalability, and robustness of the invention across a wide range of material configurations and processing conditions. They validate the core claims of the invention: that nanosecond laser annealing can simultaneously enhance crystallinity, preserve sub-bandgap optical absorptance, maintain surface morphology, and support rectifying junctions, and that these benefits can be achieved repeatedly and predictably through a controlled sequence of processing steps.

## CONCLUSION

The invention presented herein fundamentally transforms the fabrication paradigm for hyperdoped black silicon by resolving the long-standing conflict between structural crystallinity and sub-bandgap optical functionality. Through the application of nanosecond laser annealing, it becomes possible to achieve a material state that was previously unattainable: a highly crystalline surface that retains the supersaturated dopant configuration responsible for broadband infrared absorption, while preserving the microscale topography essential for geometric light trapping. This breakthrough is not merely an incremental improvement but a paradigm shift in the processing of non-equilibrium semiconductor materials, enabling the sequential and independent optimization of properties that were once considered mutually exclusive. The method is robust, scalable, and compatible with standard semiconductor fabrication techniques, making it directly applicable to the development of commercial silicon-based infrared photodetectors and intermediate-band photovoltaic cells. The ability to reactivate sub-bandgap absorptance after thermal deactivation introduces a new dimension of control, allowing for dynamic tuning of optical response through thermal cycling — a capability previously unknown in crystalline semiconductors. The insensitivity of the process to laser wavelength, pulse duration, and number of pulses ensures broad accessibility and ease of implementation across industrial and research environments. Furthermore, the adaptability of the method to different surface feature sizes — from 1 micrometer to 10 micrometers — provides design flexibility for a range of device architectures, from compact integrated sensors to large-area energy harvesters. The invention also establishes a general framework for the engineering of metastable materials, where equilibrium and non-equilibrium processing techniques are combined to decouple structural order from functional properties. This framework extends beyond hyperdoped silicon and may be applicable to other systems where dopant segregation, phase instability, or non-equilibrium state retention limits device performance. By enabling the coexistence of high crystallinity, strong sub-bandgap absorption, and functional rectifying junctions, the invention unlocks the full potential of hyperdoped black silicon as a platform for next-generation optoelectronic devices, bridging the gap between theoretical promise and practical realization. The process described herein represents a foundational advancement in materials engineering, offering a pathway to high-performance, silicon-compatible infrared technologies that were previously constrained by the limitations of conventional fabrication methods.
