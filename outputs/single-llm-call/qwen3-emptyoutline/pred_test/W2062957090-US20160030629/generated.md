# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The field of in vitro cell culture has long relied on two-dimensional substrates such as tissue culture polystyrene to support the growth and study of mammalian cells. These platforms have served as the foundational framework for decades of biological research, drug screening, and toxicological evaluation. However, it is now well established that the flat, rigid, and homogeneously nutrient-exposed environment provided by such surfaces fails to recapitulate the complex three-dimensional architecture of the native extracellular matrix in which cells reside in vivo. In this artificial context, cells adopt an unnatural flattened and stretched morphology, deprived of the spatial constraints and mechanical cues that govern their behavior in living tissues. This discrepancy leads to profound alterations in fundamental cellular processes including proliferation, apoptosis, differentiation, gene expression profiles, migratory behavior, and responses to pharmacological agents. As a consequence, data derived from two-dimensional cultures often exhibit poor translational relevance when applied to in vivo systems, contributing significantly to the high failure rate of drug candidates in clinical trials. The limitations of two-dimensional models have prompted a paradigm shift in cell biology, driving the development of advanced three-dimensional culture systems that more faithfully emulate the physiological microenvironment.

Among the most promising approaches to bridge this gap are hydrogel-based scaffolds—three-dimensional networks of cross-linked polymer chains capable of retaining large volumes of water while providing structural support for cell adhesion, migration, and organization. These materials offer a unique combination of biocompatibility, tunable mechanical properties, and porous architecture that facilitates the diffusion of oxygen, nutrients, metabolic waste, and soluble signaling molecules. Hydrogels can be derived from either natural or synthetic sources. Natural hydrogels, such as those composed of collagen, fibrin, hyaluronic acid, chitosan, or alginate, possess inherent bioactive motifs that promote cell survival, proliferation, and functional differentiation by mimicking the molecular composition of the native extracellular matrix. However, their use is often hindered by batch-to-batch variability, undefined biochemical composition, and inconsistent mechanical behavior, all of which introduce significant experimental noise and reduce reproducibility across laboratories. Synthetic hydrogels, on the other hand, offer superior control over chemical structure, cross-linking density, and mechanical rigidity, enabling precise engineering of the cellular microenvironment. Yet, many synthetic polymers, such as poly(lactic acid) and poly(glycolic acid), exhibit fiber diameters and pore sizes that are orders of magnitude larger than those found in native tissues, resulting in scaffolds that lack the nanoscale topographical features necessary to guide cell behavior with physiological fidelity.

In recent years, peptide-based hydrogels have emerged as a compelling alternative that bridges the advantages of both natural and synthetic systems. These materials are constructed from short amino acid sequences designed to self-assemble under physiological conditions into nanofibrous networks that closely resemble the structural and mechanical properties of endogenous extracellular matrices. Their modular design allows for precise control over sequence, charge, hydrophobicity, and secondary structure, enabling the incorporation of specific cell-adhesive motifs, enzymatic cleavage sites, or bioactive domains. Importantly, peptide hydrogels can be synthesized with high purity and consistency, eliminating the variability inherent in biological extracts. Despite these advantages, existing peptide hydrogel systems face critical practical limitations that impede their widespread adoption in cell culture and drug discovery applications. Most notably, the majority of current systems require non-physiological triggers for gelation—such as extreme pH shifts, low temperatures, or the addition of cross-linking agents like calcium ions or enzymatic substrates. These conditions are often incompatible with live cells, as they induce osmotic stress, protein denaturation, or metabolic arrest during the encapsulation process. For example, some systems necessitate pre-cooling of both the peptide solution and the cell culture medium to below 10°C to initiate gelation, forcing cells to remain in suboptimal or nutrient-deprived environments for extended periods before the hydrogel forms. Others require the addition of specialized buffers or solvents that must be carefully removed or neutralized prior to cell exposure, adding multiple labor-intensive steps that increase the risk of contamination, cell death, or experimental inconsistency.

Moreover, the isolation of cells from these hydrogel matrices remains a persistent challenge. Conventional methods for recovering cells typically involve reversing the gelation trigger—such as raising the temperature, adjusting the pH, or introducing proteolytic enzymes or chelating agents—that are themselves cytotoxic or disruptive to cellular integrity. These procedures often result in significant cell loss, reduced viability, or altered phenotypic states, thereby compromising downstream analyses such as transcriptomics, proteomics, or functional assays. In many cases, the very act of extracting cells from the scaffold introduces artifacts that obscure the true biological response being studied. Furthermore, the inability to reversibly manipulate the physical state of the hydrogel limits its utility in dynamic experimental protocols, such as repeated sampling, drug dosing, or sequential imaging. There is therefore an urgent and unmet need for a hydrogel system that enables cell encapsulation under fully physiological conditions—without requiring pH adjustment, temperature modulation, or exogenous additives—while simultaneously allowing for gentle, non-destructive, and highly efficient cell recovery through simple mechanical means. Such a system would represent a transformative advancement in three-dimensional cell culture, enabling robust, reproducible, and biologically relevant models for drug screening, tissue engineering, and fundamental cell biology research.

The present invention addresses these longstanding challenges through the discovery and application of a novel peptide, designated h9e, which forms a hydrogel matrix under ambient physiological conditions when mixed with standard cell culture media such as Minimum Essential Medium supplemented with fetal bovine serum. Unlike prior systems, the h9e peptide does not require any external stimulus to initiate gelation; it self-assembles spontaneously upon dilution into the culture medium at neutral pH and room temperature. This unique property allows cells to be suspended directly in their native growth medium during encapsulation, eliminating the need for pre-conditioning, buffer exchanges, or chilling procedures that compromise cell viability. The resulting hydrogel exhibits a nanofibrous architecture with mechanical properties that are both stable under physiological temperatures and dynamically responsive to mechanical perturbation. Crucially, the hydrogel can be reversibly disrupted through repeated pipetting or dilution, transitioning from a solid-like gel to a low-viscosity liquid without the use of chemical agents, proteases, or extreme environmental conditions. This reversible phase behavior enables the safe and efficient isolation of viable cells from the matrix using standard centrifugation techniques, preserving cellular integrity and functionality for subsequent analysis. The combination of mild encapsulation, physiological compatibility, and facile cell recovery distinguishes this invention from all previously described hydrogel systems and establishes a new standard for three-dimensional cell culture platforms.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a novel peptide-based hydrogel system for three-dimensional cell culture that enables the encapsulation of live cells under fully physiological conditions and permits their subsequent isolation without the use of cytotoxic agents, enzymatic digestion, or environmental manipulation. The invention comprises a self-assembling peptide, designated h9e, which forms a stable, nanofibrous hydrogel matrix upon mixing with standard cell culture media such as Minimum Essential Medium supplemented with fetal bovine serum, sodium pyruvate, non-essential amino acids, and sodium bicarbonate. The hydrogel forms spontaneously at neutral pH and ambient temperature, without requiring the addition of cross-linking agents, pH buffers, or temperature changes. The peptide concentration required for gelation is as low as one millimolar, corresponding to a weight concentration of approximately 0.17 percent, making the system highly efficient and cost-effective. The resulting hydrogel exhibits a three-dimensional architecture composed of entangled nanofibers with a diameter of approximately twenty nanometers, providing a structurally supportive scaffold that enables cells to adopt a physiologically relevant, rounded, clustered morphology distinct from the flattened, monolayer phenotype observed in two-dimensional culture.

A key feature of the invention is the unique rheological behavior of the h9e hydrogel, which demonstrates reversible shear-thinning and rapid self-reassembly properties. When subjected to mechanical shear forces such as those generated by pipetting, the hydrogel transitions from a solid-like state to a low-viscosity liquid without permanent structural damage. Upon cessation of shear stress, the hydrogel rapidly reassembles into its original gel state, often recovering more than 80 percent of its initial mechanical strength within minutes. This dynamic behavior allows the hydrogel to be delivered through standard laboratory equipment such as micropipettes, syringes, or microfluidic channels without compromising its structural integrity. Furthermore, the hydrogel can be diluted with cell culture medium to reduce its peptide concentration below the critical gelation threshold, resulting in a stable, low-viscosity liquid suspension that permits the efficient separation of embedded cells via centrifugation. The diluted solution remains in a non-gelling state throughout the centrifugation process, ensuring that cells can be pelleted without interference from residual gel structure. Upon removal from centrifugation, the peptide can be re-concentrated to reform the hydrogel, enabling the re-encapsulation of recovered cells for subsequent experiments or longitudinal studies.

The invention further provides a method for three-dimensional cell culture comprising the steps of: preparing an aqueous solution of the h9e peptide at a concentration sufficient to form a hydrogel upon dilution; mixing the peptide solution with a cell suspension suspended in standard cell culture medium; allowing the mixture to incubate at ambient temperature for a period of time sufficient to permit hydrogel formation; and maintaining the resulting hydrogel in a culture environment under standard physiological conditions. The method further includes a step for isolating cells from the hydrogel matrix by diluting the hydrogel with cell culture medium to reduce the peptide concentration below the critical gelation threshold, mixing the diluted suspension by pipetting to ensure homogeneity, and centrifuging the suspension at a temperature between zero and ten degrees Celsius to pellet the cells. The recovered cells retain high viability and proliferative capacity, and can be re-seeded for further culture, analyzed by flow cytometry, subjected to molecular profiling, or used in downstream functional assays without requiring enzymatic digestion or exposure to harsh chemical agents.

The invention also encompasses the use of the h9e hydrogel system for drug screening and pharmacological testing. The hydrogel permits the uniform diffusion of small molecule therapeutics, proteins, and other bioactive agents throughout its three-dimensional structure, enabling the assessment of drug efficacy, penetration kinetics, and dose-response relationships in a physiologically relevant context. Cells encapsulated within the hydrogel exhibit altered sensitivity to pharmacological agents compared to their two-dimensional counterparts, reflecting the in vivo behavior of tumor spheroids or tissue aggregates. For example, when exposed to the chemotherapeutic agent cisplatin, cells within the h9e hydrogel demonstrate a dose- and time-dependent reduction in viability that is consistent with known in vivo responses, and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of cisplatin in this system closely matches published values for three-dimensional tumor models. The ability to recover intact cells from the hydrogel after drug exposure enables direct molecular analysis of drug-induced changes in gene expression, protein signaling, and apoptotic pathways using techniques such as Western blotting, immunofluorescence, and quantitative PCR.

The h9e hydrogel system further supports the formation of multicellular tumor-like clusters that recapitulate key features of in vivo tumor architecture, including cell-cell adhesion, spatial heterogeneity, and nutrient gradients. These clusters exhibit distinct morphological, biochemical, and biomechanical characteristics compared to two-dimensional monolayers, including altered cytoskeletal organization, differential expression of proliferation and apoptosis markers, and reduced proliferative rates that mirror the behavior of solid tumors in vivo. The hydrogel does not induce apoptosis or cellular stress in the absence of exogenous stimuli, as evidenced by the absence of cleaved caspase-3 and the maintenance of survivin expression in untreated cultures. The system is compatible with standard laboratory techniques including microscopy, flow cytometry, immunostaining, and molecular biology assays, and can be used with a wide variety of mammalian cell types, including epithelial, mesenchymal, and cancer cell lines. The invention thus provides a robust, reproducible, and biologically faithful platform for three-dimensional cell culture that overcomes the limitations of existing systems and enables accurate modeling of in vivo cellular behavior for applications in drug discovery, toxicology, regenerative medicine, and fundamental biological research.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention disclosed herein is directed to a novel peptide-based hydrogel system for three-dimensional cell culture that enables the encapsulation of live cells under fully physiological conditions and permits their subsequent isolation through simple mechanical means without the use of cytotoxic agents or environmental manipulation. The core component of the system is a synthetic peptide, designated h9e, which is composed of a specific sequence of amino acids designed to self-assemble into a nanofibrous hydrogel upon dilution into standard cell culture media. The peptide is synthesized using solid-phase peptide synthesis techniques with a 9-fluorenylmethoxycarbonyl (Fmoc) strategy on Rink amide resin, followed by cleavage and purification via high-performance liquid chromatography and matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry to ensure high purity and structural fidelity. The resulting lyophilized peptide is stored under dry, inert conditions and dissolved in a dilute aqueous buffer such as 100 millimolar sodium bicarbonate immediately prior to use. The peptide solution is then mixed directly with cell culture medium, such as Minimum Essential Medium supplemented with 10 percent fetal bovine serum, 1 millimolar sodium pyruvate, 17.9 millimolar sodium bicarbonate, 0.01 milligrams per milliliter insulin, and 1 percent non-essential amino acids, without any prior adjustment of pH, temperature, or ionic composition.

Upon mixing, the h9e peptide undergoes spontaneous self-assembly into a three-dimensional network of entangled nanofibers, forming a stable hydrogel within fifteen minutes at room temperature and physiological pH. The gelation process is concentration-dependent, with storage modulus values increasing from approximately one hundred pascals at one millimolar peptide concentration to seven hundred pascals at three millimolar, as measured by dynamic rheological testing under oscillatory shear strain at 37 degrees Celsius. Scanning electron microscopy confirms that the hydrogel architecture is composed of uniformly distributed nanofibers with a diameter of approximately twenty nanometers, creating a porous, interconnected matrix that permits the free diffusion of oxygen, nutrients, and soluble signaling molecules while providing structural support for cell adhesion and organization. The nanofiber density and interstitial spacing vary with peptide concentration, with higher concentrations yielding more compact, densely packed networks that restrict cell migration and promote tighter cluster formation, while lower concentrations permit greater cell motility and the formation of more dispersed, loosely connected aggregates.

The hydrogel exhibits remarkable rheological properties that distinguish it from all previously described peptide-based or synthetic hydrogel systems. Under dynamic oscillatory shear testing, the hydrogel behaves as a viscoelastic solid at low strain amplitudes, with a storage modulus (G′) consistently exceeding the loss modulus (G″), indicating dominant elastic behavior. However, when subjected to high shear strain (greater than 500 percent), the hydrogel undergoes a reversible transition to a liquid-like state, with G′ dropping below 0.2 pascals and G″ becoming dominant. This shear-thinning behavior occurs without permanent disruption of the peptide’s self-assembling units, and upon cessation of shear, the hydrogel rapidly reassembles into its original gel state. The recovery kinetics are concentration-dependent, with three millimolar hydrogels recovering up to 100 percent of their initial modulus within ten minutes of rest, while one millimolar hydrogels recover approximately 83 percent under the same conditions. This reassembly capability is maintained over multiple cycles of shear disruption and recovery, demonstrating that the hydrogel retains its structural integrity even after repeated mechanical perturbation. This property enables the hydrogel to be handled using standard laboratory equipment such as micropipettes, allowing for multiple rounds of mixing, transfer, and seeding without loss of structural function. The hydrogel can be injected, dispensed, or aspirated through narrow-bore pipette tips without permanent degradation, making it suitable for use in automated high-throughput screening platforms, microfluidic devices, or 3D bioprinting applications.

A critical innovation of the invention lies in the ability to convert the hydrogel into a non-gelling, low-viscosity liquid state for the purpose of cell isolation. This is achieved by diluting the hydrogel with an equal or greater volume of cell culture medium, reducing the peptide concentration below the critical gelation threshold. For example, a three millimolar hydrogel can be diluted fifteen-fold with culture medium to yield a final concentration of 0.2 millimolar, at which point the storage modulus drops to near zero and the system behaves as a Newtonian fluid. Rheological measurements confirm that both G′ and G″ remain below five pascals for over one hour under standard oscillatory conditions, indicating a stable liquid state that resists re-gelation during the time frame required for centrifugation. This state is further stabilized at low temperatures, such as four degrees Celsius, where peptide mobility is reduced and reassembly kinetics are slowed. Under these conditions, the diluted suspension can be centrifuged at 2,000 revolutions per minute for five minutes to pellet the cells, while the peptide remains in solution as a non-viscous supernatant. The recovered cell pellet exhibits high viability, with no statistically significant difference observed between pre-encapsulation and post-isolation viability, as determined by trypan blue exclusion and automated cell counting. The cells retain their proliferative capacity, morphological integrity, and responsiveness to pharmacological stimuli, demonstrating that the isolation process does not induce stress, apoptosis, or phenotypic drift.

The h9e hydrogel supports the growth of a wide variety of mammalian cell types, including epithelial, mesenchymal, and cancer cell lines. In particular, the human breast cancer cell line MCF-7 was used as a model system to demonstrate the functionality of the invention. When encapsulated within the hydrogel, MCF-7 cells form three-dimensional, tumor-like clusters with diameters exceeding fifty micrometers, characterized by rounded morphology, multi-layered organization, and cell-cell adhesion that closely resemble in vivo tumor spheroids. These clusters are distinct from the flat, monolayer morphology observed in two-dimensional culture, and exhibit altered gene expression profiles, cytoskeletal organization, and drug sensitivity. Immunofluorescence staining reveals that actin filaments in three-dimensional cultures are localized primarily at the cell periphery, forming a cortical ring that contrasts with the elongated, stress-fiber-dominated cytoskeleton of two-dimensional cells. The expression of proliferation markers such as Ki67 is robust in untreated clusters, while apoptosis markers such as cleaved caspase-3 remain undetectable unless cells are exposed to cytotoxic agents. The hydrogel itself does not induce cellular stress or apoptosis, as confirmed by the absence of cleaved caspase-3 and the maintenance of survivin expression in untreated cultures, indicating that the material is biologically inert and does not interfere with normal cellular physiology.

The hydrogel permits the uniform diffusion of small molecule therapeutics, proteins, and other bioactive agents throughout its three-dimensional structure. When the chemotherapeutic agent cisplatin is introduced into the system via three distinct methods—top surface application, transwell diffusion, or pre-mixing with the hydrogel prior to gelation—it is delivered effectively to all cells within the matrix, resulting in a dose- and time-dependent reduction in viability. The IC50 value for cisplatin in the h9e hydrogel system is determined to be 31.25 micromolar, a value consistent with published data for three-dimensional tumor models and significantly higher than the IC50 observed in two-dimensional cultures, reflecting the increased resistance conferred by the three-dimensional architecture. Confocal microscopy of cisplatin-treated clusters reveals nuclear fragmentation, disruption of actin organization, downregulation of Ki67 and survivin, and upregulation of cleaved caspase-3, confirming the induction of apoptosis in a manner consistent with in vivo responses. The ability to recover intact cells from the hydrogel after drug exposure enables direct molecular analysis of drug-induced signaling pathways using techniques such as Western blotting, which confirms the presence of cleaved caspase-3, reduced survivin, and altered procaspase-3 processing in treated samples, while untreated controls remain negative for apoptotic markers.

The invention further enables the re-encapsulation of isolated cells for longitudinal studies or repeated experimental cycles. Recovered cells can be re-suspended in fresh h9e peptide solution and re-encapsulated into a new hydrogel matrix, allowing for the study of cell behavior across multiple generations or under varying experimental conditions. The hydrogel is compatible with standard cell culture protocols, including medium changes, passaging, and cryopreservation. It can be sterilized by ultraviolet irradiation without loss of function, and is stable for extended periods under refrigerated conditions. The system is scalable, with hydrogels formed in multi-well plates, microfluidic chambers, or even larger culture vessels, and is compatible with automated imaging systems, high-content screening platforms, and robotic liquid handlers. The hydrogel does not interfere with optical clarity, permitting high-resolution microscopy, live-cell imaging, and fluorescence-based assays without the need for specialized equipment or matrix-clearing agents.

The invention represents a significant advancement over existing three-dimensional culture systems by eliminating the need for non-physiological triggers, chemical additives, or enzymatic digestion in both cell encapsulation and isolation. Unlike systems that require cold temperatures or pH shifts to initiate gelation, the h9e hydrogel forms spontaneously under ambient conditions, ensuring that cells are never exposed to suboptimal or stressful environments. Unlike systems that rely on proteolytic enzymes or chelating agents for cell recovery, the h9e hydrogel can be disrupted through simple dilution and centrifugation, preserving cell surface proteins, membrane integrity, and intracellular signaling molecules. The system is highly reproducible, as the peptide is chemically synthesized with defined sequence and purity, eliminating the batch-to-batch variability inherent in natural hydrogels. The mechanical properties of the hydrogel can be tuned by varying peptide concentration, enabling the creation of microenvironments that mimic the stiffness of different tissue types, from soft brain tissue to stiffer tumor stroma. The ability to reversibly transition between gel and liquid states provides unprecedented flexibility for experimental design, allowing for dynamic manipulation of the cellular microenvironment, sequential drug exposure, and repeated sampling without the need for cell lysis or destruction of the scaffold.

The invention has broad applicability across multiple domains of biomedical research. In drug discovery, it provides a physiologically relevant platform for high-throughput screening of compound libraries, enabling the identification of agents that are effective against three-dimensional tumor models but may fail in two-dimensional assays. In toxicology, it allows for the assessment of chemical toxicity in a context that more accurately reflects human tissue architecture. In regenerative medicine, it offers a scaffold for the growth and differentiation of stem cells in three dimensions, with the added advantage of facile cell recovery for transplantation or analysis. In fundamental cell biology, it enables the study of cell-cell communication, tissue morphogenesis, and mechanotransduction in a controlled, reproducible, and biologically faithful environment. The h9e peptide hydrogel system thus represents a transformative tool that bridges the gap between traditional two-dimensional culture and complex in vivo models, providing a simple, robust, and scalable solution to one of the most persistent challenges in modern cell biology.

### EXAMPLES

The invention is further illustrated by the following detailed examples, which describe the preparation, characterization, and application of the h9e peptide hydrogel system in three-dimensional cell culture and drug testing. These examples are provided to demonstrate the practical utility and reproducibility of the invention, and are not intended to limit its scope.

In Example 1, the h9e peptide was synthesized using solid-phase peptide synthesis on Rink amide MBHA resin with Fmoc-protected amino acids and a microwave-assisted CEM Liberty synthesizer. After synthesis, the peptide was cleaved from the resin using a mixture of trifluoroacetic acid, triisopropylsilane, and water in a 95:2.5:2.5 volume ratio. The crude peptide was precipitated with anhydrous ether, washed three times, dissolved in a 50:50 mixture of acetonitrile and deionized water, and lyophilized. The molecular weight and purity of the peptide were confirmed by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry and high-performance liquid chromatography, respectively, with a purity exceeding 95 percent. The lyophilized peptide was stored at -20 degrees Celsius until use. For hydrogel formation, the peptide was dissolved in 100 millimolar sodium bicarbonate at a concentration of 10 millimolar and stirred magnetically for three hours to ensure complete solubilization. The solution was then filtered through a 0.22-micrometer sterile filter and stored at 4 degrees Celsius until use.

In Example 2, the hydrogel was formed by mixing 100 microliters of the 10 millimolar h9e peptide solution with 900 microliters of Minimum Essential Medium supplemented with 10 percent fetal bovine serum, 1 millimolar sodium pyruvate, 17.9 millimolar sodium bicarbonate, 0.01 milligrams per milliliter insulin, and 1 percent non-essential amino acids. The mixture was gently hand-shaken for ten seconds and incubated at room temperature for fifteen minutes. The resulting hydrogel exhibited a stable, self-supporting structure, as confirmed by inverting the culture plate without flow of the material. Rheological testing using a C-VOR 150 rheometer with a 20-millimeter parallel plate geometry and a 500-micrometer gap showed that the storage modulus (G′) reached 100 pascals for the one millimolar formulation, 400 pascals for the two millimolar formulation, and 700 pascals for the three millimolar formulation within fifteen minutes of mixing. Scanning electron microscopy revealed a uniform nanofibrous network with fiber diameters of approximately twenty nanometers, with increased fiber density and reduced interstitial spacing observed at higher peptide concentrations.

In Example 3, the shear-thinning and reassembly properties of the hydrogel were evaluated using dynamic rheological testing. A three millimolar hydrogel was prepared and allowed to equilibrate overnight at room temperature. The hydrogel was then subjected to a single frequency test at 1 hertz and 1 percent strain for ten minutes to establish baseline mechanical properties. A shear-thinning event was induced by increasing the strain amplitude to 500 percent for one minute, resulting in a drop in G′ to less than 0.2 pascals. After a one-minute rest period, the strain was returned to 1 percent and the recovery of G′ was monitored for one hour. The hydrogel recovered 88 percent of its initial modulus within one hour. The experiment was repeated four times, with a 10-minute rest period between each cycle, and the hydrogel retained its ability to recover over 90 percent of its modulus after the fourth cycle. Amplitude sweep tests conducted at 4 degrees Celsius showed that the hydrogel remained in a liquid state during centrifugation when diluted fifteen-fold with culture medium, with G′ and G″ both below five pascals for the duration of a one-hour test.

In Example 4, MCF-7 human breast cancer cells were cultured in two-dimensional monolayers on tissue culture polystyrene or encapsulated within the h9e hydrogel. Cells were detached from flasks using TrypLE Express trypsin solution, pelleted by centrifugation, and resuspended in Minimum Essential Medium at a density of 2.8 × 10⁵ cells per well. The cell suspension was mixed with the h9e peptide solution at concentrations of one, two, or three millimolar to form hydrogels in 12-well plates. After hydrogelation, one milliliter of culture medium was added to the top of each well to prevent desiccation. Cell morphology was monitored daily using an inverted light microscope. In two-dimensional culture, cells adhered to the substrate and adopted a flattened, spread morphology within 24 hours, with progressive monolayer confluence by day 7. In contrast, cells in the hydrogel formed rounded, three-dimensional clusters that increased in size and density over time, with distinct multicellular aggregates visible by day 3 and tumor-like spheroids exceeding 50 micrometers in diameter by day 7. The clusters exhibited no signs of necrosis or detachment, and remained suspended within the hydrogel matrix throughout the observation period.

In Example 5, cells were isolated from the hydrogel using a dilution-based method. After five days of culture, two milliliters of culture medium was added to each well containing the hydrogel, and the mixture was thoroughly pipetted up and down ten times to ensure homogenization. An additional four milliliters of medium was added to wash the well, and the entire volume was transferred to a 15-milliliter centrifuge tube. One milliliter of TrypLE Express trypsin solution was added to the original well, incubated for five minutes at 37 degrees Celsius, and collected into the same tube. The final volume was adjusted to 15 milliliters with culture medium, and the tube was placed on ice. The suspension was centrifuged at 2,000 revolutions per minute for five minutes at four degrees Celsius. The supernatant was carefully removed, and the cell pellet was resuspended in fresh medium. Cell viability was assessed using a trypan blue exclusion assay and automated cell counting, revealing no significant difference between pre-encapsulation and post-isolation viability, with viability exceeding 95 percent in all cases. The recovered cells were re-seeded into fresh hydrogel and demonstrated continued proliferation, confirming their functional integrity.

In Example 6, the distribution of cells within the hydrogel was visualized using confocal microscopy. MCF-7 cells were encapsulated in a three millimolar hydrogel and cultured for five days. The cell clusters were isolated and stained with Hoechst 33342 fluorescent dye for 30 minutes at 37 degrees Celsius, washed three times with culture medium, and re-encapsulated into a fresh hydrogel. Z-stack imaging using a LSM 700 confocal microscope revealed homogeneous distribution of nuclei throughout the hydrogel, with no evidence of clustering at the periphery or sedimentation at the bottom. Three-dimensional reconstructions confirmed the formation of multicellular spheroids with diameters ranging from 30 to 80 micrometers, each containing multiple layers of cells with intact intercellular junctions.

In Example 7, the effect of the chemotherapeutic agent cisplatin on MCF-7 cells was evaluated using three different delivery methods. In the top surface method, cisplatin at a concentration of 40 micromolar was added to the medium above the hydrogel. In the transwell method, hydrogel-embedded cell clusters were placed on a transwell insert, with cisplatin added to both the upper and lower chambers. In the pre-mixed method, cisplatin was added directly to the cell-peptide-medium mixture prior to hydrogelation. Cell viability was assessed at days 0, 1, 3, 5, and 10 using trypan blue exclusion. All three methods resulted in a dose-dependent reduction in viability, with the pre-mixed method showing the most pronounced effect, achieving an 85 percent reduction in viability by day 10. The IC50 value for cisplatin in the h9e hydrogel system was determined to be 31.25 micromolar, consistent with published values for three-dimensional tumor models.

In Example 8, immunofluorescence staining was performed on cells isolated from the hydrogel after cisplatin treatment. Cells were fixed, permeabilized, and stained with antibodies against actin, Ki67, survivin, and cleaved caspase-3, followed by Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAPI. Confocal microscopy revealed that untreated clusters exhibited cortical actin organization, high Ki67 expression, and strong survivin staining, with minimal cleaved caspase-3 signal. In contrast, cisplatin-treated clusters showed disrupted actin networks, reduced Ki67 and survivin expression, and intense cleaved caspase-3 staining localized to fragmented nuclei. Z-stack reconstructions confirmed that these changes were uniform throughout the spheroid, indicating that cisplatin penetrated the entire three-dimensional structure.

In Example 9, Western blot analysis was performed on cells harvested from two-dimensional culture and from the h9e hydrogel, with and without cisplatin treatment. Whole cell lysates were prepared, separated by SDS-PAGE, and probed with antibodies against survivin, procaspase-3, and cleaved caspase-3. Untreated cells from both two-dimensional and three-dimensional cultures showed expression of survivin and procaspase-3, but no detectable cleaved caspase-3. In cisplatin-treated samples from the hydrogel, all three markers were clearly detected, with a marked increase in cleaved caspase-3 and a corresponding decrease in survivin. These results confirm that cells recovered from the hydrogel retain their molecular integrity and can be used for downstream proteomic analysis.

In Example 10, the h9e hydrogel system was tested for compatibility with cryopreservation. Cells were encapsulated in the hydrogel, cultured for five days, and then isolated using the dilution-centrifugation method. The recovered cells were resuspended in cryopreservation medium containing 10 percent dimethyl sulfoxide and frozen at -80 degrees Celsius overnight before transfer to liquid nitrogen. Upon thawing, cells were re-encapsulated in fresh hydrogel and monitored for viability and proliferation. The cells retained high viability and resumed proliferation within 24 hours, demonstrating that the h9e hydrogel system is compatible with long-term storage and cryopreservation protocols.

These examples collectively demonstrate the robustness, versatility, and biological fidelity of the h9e peptide hydrogel system as a platform for three-dimensional cell culture and drug testing. The invention provides a simple, scalable, and physiologically relevant method for encapsulating and recovering live cells without the use of cytotoxic agents or environmental manipulation, establishing a new standard for in vitro cell modeling.
