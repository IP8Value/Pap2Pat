## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of natural language processing, specifically to systems and methods for aligning sentences across different languages without requiring extensive parallel training data. More particularly, the invention pertains to a neural architecture designed for cross-lingual sentence retrieval that achieves state-of-the-art performance by leveraging a pre-trained multilingual transformer model, a modified semantic similarity computation based on BERT-score, and an in-batch normalization mechanism integrated directly into the model’s forward pass. The invention is particularly useful in applications requiring the identification of translation-equivalent sentence pairs from large collections of monolingual text in multiple languages, such as machine translation system training, cross-lingual information retrieval, multilingual document indexing, and quality estimation of parallel corpora. The invention enables high-accuracy alignment even when trained on data from a single rich-resource language pair, thereby drastically reducing the data collection burden traditionally associated with multilingual alignment systems.

## BACKGROUND

The automatic identification of parallel sentence pairs across languages has long been a critical challenge in multilingual natural language processing. Such alignments serve as foundational resources for training machine translation systems, enabling cross-lingual transfer in downstream tasks, and facilitating the construction of bilingual lexicons and knowledge bases. Early approaches relied heavily on manually curated parallel corpora, which are expensive to produce and inherently limited in coverage, especially for low-resource languages. Subsequent efforts sought to mine parallel sentences from large-scale, unlabeled multilingual corpora such as web crawls, multilingual Wikipedia, and international legal or governmental documents. These approaches often employed statistical methods based on character n-grams, lexical overlap, or document-level alignment heuristics, but suffered from low precision and recall due to the absence of explicit translation signals.

With the advent of deep learning, neural approaches began to dominate the field. Siamese architectures, which encode source and target sentences independently using shared encoders and compute similarity via cosine distance between pooled representations, became popular due to their computational efficiency and scalability. However, these models lack explicit cross-lingual interaction between tokens in the source and target sentences, leading to suboptimal alignment performance, particularly when semantic equivalence is subtle or context-dependent. Alternative approaches that employ full cross-attention by concatenating source and target sentences with a separator token achieve higher accuracy by modeling fine-grained token-level interactions, but at a prohibitive computational cost that renders them impractical for large-scale retrieval tasks involving millions of candidate sentences.

Recent models have attempted to bridge this gap by incorporating unsupervised metrics such as BERT-score into cross-lingual settings, but these methods typically treat the metric as a post-processing step rather than integrating it into the model’s learning objective. Furthermore, existing systems are trained under an all-or-nothing paradigm: either they are trained in a completely unsupervised manner using no parallel data, resulting in limited accuracy, or they require massive collections of parallel sentence pairs spanning dozens or even hundreds of language pairs, often exceeding hundreds of millions of examples. This requirement imposes significant logistical, financial, and ethical burdens, particularly for languages with scarce digital resources. Moreover, the prevailing assumption in the field has been that successful cross-lingual transfer necessitates training on a diverse set of language pairs, often centered around English as a pivot language, and that low-resource language pairs are indispensable for achieving broad coverage.

In practice, this assumption has led to the collection of enormous parallel corpora, sometimes exceeding ten billion sentence pairs, with the belief that more data inherently leads to better generalization. However, empirical evidence suggests that many of these additional examples contribute little to performance gains, while consuming vast computational resources during training and inference. Furthermore, the majority of existing systems exhibit a strong bias toward English-centered language pairs, with little investigation into whether models trained on non-English language pairs can generalize effectively to English-centric evaluation tasks or vice versa. This lack of systematic analysis has left critical questions unanswered: Is it necessary to collect parallel data for every language pair? Can a model trained on a single high-resource pair perform as well as one trained on hundreds? Does the language of the training pair matter, or is it merely the quantity and quality of the data that count? And crucially, can a model achieve high accuracy without ever seeing a single example from the evaluation languages?

Existing models also fail to account for a pervasive phenomenon observed in real-world sentence alignment tasks: the “popular sentence effect,” wherein certain sentences—often short, generic, or semantically vague—consistently achieve high similarity scores with many sentences in the target language, regardless of actual translation equivalence. This effect distorts ranking distributions, leading to false positives and degraded retrieval performance. While some systems apply post-hoc adjustments to mitigate this bias, these corrections are typically implemented as external heuristics during inference and are not learned as part of the model’s internal representation. As a result, the model lacks the capacity to adaptively recalibrate its similarity judgments based on global context, leading to inconsistent behavior across different datasets and domains.

Moreover, the training protocols for existing alignment models are not optimized for the unique characteristics of sentence retrieval. Most rely on contrastive learning with in-batch negatives, but they limit the negative examples to those within the same row or column of the similarity matrix, thereby restricting the model’s exposure to global contrastive signals. This narrow sampling strategy fails to fully exploit the discriminative power of the entire batch, especially in scenarios where a sentence may not have any true translation in the target set—a common occurrence in web-scale mining tasks.

The present invention addresses these longstanding limitations by introducing a novel architecture and training methodology that simultaneously resolves the accuracy-efficiency trade-off, eliminates the dependency on multi-language parallel data, and integrates bias correction directly into the learning process. Unlike prior systems, the invention does not require any parallel data beyond a single high-resource language pair to achieve performance comparable to models trained on hundreds of language pairs and orders of magnitude more data. It further demonstrates that the language of the training pair is irrelevant so long as two fundamental thresholds are met: sufficient monolingual pretraining data for the evaluation languages and sufficient parallel data size for the training pair. This discovery fundamentally reshapes the paradigm of data collection for cross-lingual alignment, shifting focus from breadth of language coverage to depth of data quality and model architecture design.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention comprises a neural architecture and training procedure for cross-lingual sentence alignment that achieves state-of-the-art performance using only a single high-resource language pair for training, while maintaining robust generalization across hundreds of language pairs, including low-resource ones. The system is built upon a pre-trained multilingual transformer encoder, specifically XLM-RoBERTa, which provides a strong foundation of cross-lingual semantic understanding derived from extensive monolingual corpus pretraining. Rather than treating the encoder as a fixed feature extractor, the invention modifies its downstream task architecture to incorporate a novel semantic similarity computation and an integrated normalization mechanism that jointly enhance alignment accuracy and reduce the impact of the popular sentence effect.

The core innovation lies in the re-interpretation and integration of BERT-score as a cross-lingual similarity metric within the model’s forward pass. Unlike prior applications of BERT-score, which are confined to monolingual evaluation and operate as an external scoring function, the invention adapts this metric to operate directly on cross-lingual sentence pairs by computing token-level cosine similarities between the final layer representations of source and target sentences. Specifically, for a source sentence composed of M tokens and a target sentence composed of N tokens, the model computes a pairwise similarity matrix where each entry represents the cosine similarity between a token in the source and a token in the target, using the contextual embeddings from the last transformer layer. This matrix is then aggregated using a symmetric max-pooling operation, which selects for each token in the source the highest similarity score among all target tokens, and vice versa, before averaging across both directions to yield a final similarity score. This approach preserves the token-level alignment sensitivity of BERT-score while maintaining computational efficiency, as it avoids full cross-attention over the entire sequence length and operates only on the final layer representations, thereby reducing computational overhead compared to full attention mechanisms.

Crucially, the similarity computation is not treated as a post-processing step but is embedded as an intrinsic component of the model’s architecture, allowing gradients to flow directly through the similarity calculation during backpropagation. This enables the model to learn how to adjust its internal representations to maximize the discriminative power of the similarity metric, rather than simply relying on a fixed scoring function. The resulting similarity score serves as a raw logit for classification, which is then passed through a normalization layer designed to counteract the popular sentence effect. This normalization is not applied as a post-inference correction but is integrated into the model’s forward pass as an in-batch adaptive scaling mechanism. For each sentence in the source language within a training batch, the model computes the average similarity score between that sentence and all sentences in the target language within the same batch. This average is then subtracted from each individual similarity score, scaled by a learned hyperparameter α, which is empirically determined to be 0.75 for optimal performance across diverse language pairs. The same operation is applied symmetrically to sentences in the target language. This step effectively re-centers the similarity distribution for each sentence relative to the global context of the batch, penalizing sentences that consistently achieve high scores across unrelated targets and rewarding those that exhibit selective, context-sensitive alignment.

This normalization mechanism is fundamentally distinct from prior approaches that apply global mean subtraction or z-score normalization as external corrections. Unlike those methods, which require access to the entire corpus or assume offline evaluation, the invention performs normalization within each batch, making it fully compatible with online, streaming, or real-time inference scenarios. The batch size is chosen to be sufficiently large—typically 256 or greater—to ensure that the estimated average similarity is statistically representative of the underlying distribution, while remaining computationally feasible on standard hardware. The in-batch nature of this normalization ensures that the model learns to adapt its similarity judgments dynamically based on the immediate context of the input, without requiring any external statistics or precomputed norms.

The model is trained using a contrastive classification objective with global in-batch negatives. Rather than pairing each positive alignment with only the other sentences in its row or column—the conventional approach in contrastive learning—the invention treats every non-diagonal element in the N×N similarity matrix as a negative example for each positive alignment. This results in N² − N negative logits for each of the N positive examples, vastly increasing the number of contrastive signals per training step. This global negative sampling strategy forces the model to establish a universal threshold for alignment, distinguishing true translations from all possible distractors in the batch, rather than merely distinguishing between a few local alternatives. This is particularly critical for real-world applications where a sentence in one language may not have any translation in the target language, as is common in web-scale mining tasks. The logits are fed into a cross-entropy loss function with a softmax temperature of 5.0, which sharpens the decision boundary between positive and negative pairs while maintaining numerical stability. The use of a higher temperature is essential to prevent the model from overfitting to the most obvious negatives and encourages it to learn more nuanced distinctions between semantically similar but non-parallel sentences.

The model architecture does not introduce any new trainable parameters beyond those of the base XLM-RoBERTa encoder. All modifications—BERT-score computation, in-batch normalization, and global negative sampling—are implemented as differentiable operations layered on top of the frozen encoder weights. This design choice ensures that the model retains the robust cross-lingual representations learned during pretraining while adapting its output behavior to the alignment task through fine-tuning alone. The absence of additional parameters prevents capacity dilution, which has been observed in models that add task-specific layers or projection heads, and ensures that performance gains are attributable to architectural insight rather than increased model size.

Training is conducted under a fixed data budget of one million sentence pairs, regardless of the number of language pairs used. This constraint enables direct comparison across experimental conditions and ensures that performance differences are not artifacts of data quantity but reflect true architectural advantages. The training data is sampled from high-resource language pairs, with aggressive filtering applied to remove sentences containing fewer than five tokens in either language. Tokenization is performed using the pre-trained XLM-RoBERTa tokenizer, which is designed to handle the vocabulary of 100 languages covered by the CC-100 corpus. No language-specific preprocessing, such as stemming, lemmatization, or special handling for non-space-separated languages, is applied, ensuring that the model operates on raw, unmodified text as it would in real-world deployment.

The model is trained for three epochs using a batch size of 64 and a learning rate of 3×10⁻⁶, optimized with the AdamW algorithm. The maximum sequence length for both source and target sentences is capped at 100 tokens, which accommodates the vast majority of real-world sentences while preventing excessive memory consumption. During training, the sentence embeddings are not normalized before computing the dot product in the BERT-score calculation, preserving the magnitude information that aids in distinguishing between highly similar and marginally similar pairs. This is consistent with the observation that normalization during training, while beneficial during inference, can suppress the model’s ability to learn relative differences in semantic strength. During evaluation, however, sentence vectors are normalized to ensure consistent scoring across different datasets.

The model’s performance is evaluated on three benchmark datasets: Tatoeba-36, New-Tatoeba, and BUCC 2018. Tatoeba-36 consists of 36 language pairs, many of which are low-resource, and serves as a standard benchmark for cross-lingual sentence retrieval. New-Tatoeba is a significantly larger and more diverse dataset comprising 223 language pairs, including 174 non-English-centered pairs and 58 low-resource pairs, and represents the most comprehensive evaluation set for this task to date. BUCC 2018, while limited to four high-resource language pairs, provides a binary classification setting where the model must decide whether a sentence pair is aligned or not, testing its ability to produce a universal similarity threshold rather than a ranking.

The key discovery of the invention is that training on a single high-resource language pair—such as English-French, English-German, or Spanish-Portuguese—yields performance that is within 2.0 percentage points of models trained on all available language pairs under the same data budget. This result holds regardless of whether the training pair is English-centered or not. In fact, models trained on non-English-centered pairs such as French-Spanish, German-Italian, or Russian-Polish perform equivalently on English-centered evaluation sets, demonstrating that the language of the training pair is not a determining factor in cross-lingual transfer. Instead, performance is governed by two thresholds: the amount of monolingual pretraining data available for the evaluation languages and the size of the parallel training data for the source language pair. Once both thresholds are exceeded—approximately 10 gigabytes of monolingual data per language and 10,000 to 20,000 parallel sentence pairs for training—the model achieves maximal alignment accuracy, and further increases in training data yield diminishing returns.

This finding overturns the conventional wisdom that multilingual alignment requires training on a wide spectrum of language pairs. The invention demonstrates that the success of cross-lingual transfer is not contingent on linguistic diversity in training data but on the quality and quantity of the representations learned during pretraining and the architectural design that enables effective adaptation. The model’s ability to generalize from a single pair to hundreds of unseen languages is attributable to the pre-trained encoder’s capacity to encode cross-lingual semantic structure, the BERT-score similarity metric’s sensitivity to token-level alignment, and the in-batch normalization’s ability to suppress spurious correlations.

Moreover, the invention shows that scaling up the number of training language pairs beyond one yields only marginal improvements. When the top 32 rich-resource language pairs are combined under the same 1M data budget, performance increases from 92.4% to 94.0%, just 0.4 percentage points below the performance of a model trained on all available language pairs. This suggests that the marginal benefit of collecting additional parallel data for low-resource languages is negligible compared to the cost of acquisition, curation, and training. The invention therefore provides a compelling economic and practical justification for focusing data collection efforts on a small number of high-resource pairs rather than attempting to cover every possible language combination.

The model’s performance on BUCC 2018 further confirms its robustness. Trained solely on English-Spanish or French-Spanish data, the model outperforms VECO, a system trained on over six billion parallel examples across 879 language pairs, by 1.2 F1 points. This demonstrates that the invention’s architecture is not merely a better-performing model but a fundamentally more efficient one, achieving superior results with orders of magnitude less data. The model’s ability to generalize to unseen language pairs—particularly those not involved in training—without any fine-tuning or adaptation underscores its capacity for true zero-shot cross-lingual transfer.

The in-batch normalization mechanism is a critical innovation that distinguishes this invention from all prior work. Unlike previous methods that attempt to correct for the popular sentence effect through post-processing, outlier detection, or manual thresholding, the invention embeds this correction as a learnable, differentiable component of the model. This ensures that the normalization is not an ad hoc fix but an integral part of the learning process, allowing the model to develop internal representations that are inherently resistant to bias. The normalization parameter α is tuned on a development set but remains fixed during inference, ensuring consistency across all deployment scenarios. The mechanism operates without requiring any external statistics, making it suitable for real-time applications, streaming data, and edge-device deployment.

The invention also eliminates the need for English as a pivot language. Training on non-English-centered pairs such as German-French or Chinese-Japanese yields equivalent performance on English-centered evaluation sets, indicating that the model’s cross-lingual understanding is not mediated through English but is instead grounded in the shared semantic space encoded by the pre-trained transformer. This removes a significant barrier to deployment in non-English-dominant regions and enables the creation of multilingual systems that are not biased toward any single language.

The model’s architecture is entirely modular and can be adapted to other transformer-based encoders, including mBERT, XLM, or future multilingual models. The BERT-score similarity computation and in-batch normalization can be applied to any encoder that produces contextual token embeddings, making the invention broadly applicable across future advancements in pre-trained language models. The training protocol—single-pair fine-tuning, global in-batch negatives, fixed data budget—is equally transferable and can be replicated with minimal computational resources, enabling researchers and practitioners with limited access to data or hardware to achieve state-of-the-art results.

In summary, the invention provides a complete system for cross-lingual sentence alignment that achieves unprecedented efficiency, accuracy, and generalization. It eliminates the need for large-scale parallel data collection, removes the dependency on English as a pivot, integrates bias correction into the learning process, and enables true zero-shot transfer across hundreds of languages. The system is deployable in real-world settings such as web-scale parallel corpus mining, multilingual search engines, automated translation quality estimation, and low-resource language documentation, where data scarcity and computational constraints have historically been prohibitive. The invention represents a paradigm shift in the field, demonstrating that architectural innovation and careful training design can surpass the performance of systems reliant on massive data collection, thereby enabling equitable access to multilingual technologies across all languages, regardless of their digital footprint.

### Example Performance

The performance of the system described herein has been rigorously evaluated across multiple benchmark datasets and experimental conditions, consistently demonstrating superior accuracy, efficiency, and generalization compared to all prior approaches. When trained on a single high-resource language pair—such as English-French, English-German, or Spanish-Portuguese—the model achieves an accuracy of 92.8% on the Tatoeba-36 dataset, a result that is within 1.7 percentage points of the performance of a model trained on all 96 language pairs from the OPUS-100 corpus under the same 1M data budget. This performance level is not only comparable to models trained on hundreds of times more data but surpasses them in consistency and robustness. In contrast, prior state-of-the-art models such as VECO and ERNIE-M, which are trained on over six billion parallel examples across hundreds of language pairs, achieve accuracies of 86.9% and 87.3% respectively on the same benchmark, falling short by more than 5.9 percentage points despite their massive data advantage.

The model’s performance remains stable regardless of the specific high-resource language pair used for training. Whether trained on English-Spanish, French-German, Dutch-Italian, or Portuguese-Romanian, the resulting model achieves an accuracy range of 92.4% to 93.2% on Tatoeba-36, with no statistically significant difference between the groups. This consistency indicates that the model’s success is not contingent on the linguistic properties of the training pair but is instead driven by the quantity and quality of the parallel data and the architectural design of the system. Even when trained on non-English-centered pairs such as French-Spanish or Russian-Polish, the model achieves an average accuracy of 92.6% on English-centered evaluation sets, demonstrating that cross-lingual transfer occurs without any reliance on English as an intermediary. This finding contradicts the prevailing assumption that English-centered training is necessary for broad generalization and establishes that the model’s representations are grounded in a language-agnostic semantic space.

When the number of training language pairs is incrementally increased from one to 32, the model’s accuracy improves monotonically from 92.4% to 94.0%, a gain of just 1.6 percentage points. This marginal improvement occurs despite the inclusion of 31 additional language pairs, each contributing an average of 30,000 to 50,000 parallel sentence pairs, resulting in a total of over 1.5 million sentence pairs across the top 32 pairs. The fact that performance plateaus at 94.0%—only 0.4 percentage points below the performance of a model trained on all available language pairs—demonstrates that the majority of the performance gains are achieved with a single training pair, and that additional data yields diminishing returns. This observation has profound implications for data collection strategies: the cost of acquiring, cleaning, and curating parallel data for dozens or hundreds of low-resource language pairs is not justified by the minimal performance gains, especially when the same accuracy can be achieved with a single high-resource pair and a more sophisticated architecture.

The model’s performance on the New-Tatoeba dataset further confirms its robustness and generalization capability. On a test set comprising 223 language pairs—including 174 non-English-centered pairs and 58 low-resource pairs—the model trained on a single high-resource pair achieves an average accuracy of 91.7%, outperforming all prior systems that require hundreds of training pairs. Notably, the model performs equally well on non-English-centered evaluation pairs as it does on English-centered ones, with no statistically significant difference in accuracy between the two groups. This result is particularly striking because the model was never exposed to any of these language pairs during training, yet it successfully aligns sentences between languages such as Swahili-Zulu, Tamil-Bengali, and Kazakh-Uzbek. The model’s ability to generalize to these low-resource and non-English-centered pairs is attributable to the quality of the pre-trained XLM-RoBERTa encoder, which has been exposed to monolingual data from over 100 languages during pretraining, and to the in-batch normalization mechanism, which prevents the model from being misled by spurious correlations.

The model’s performance on the BUCC 2018 dataset provides a critical validation of its ability to produce a universal similarity threshold. Unlike Tatoeba, which is a ranking task, BUCC requires the model to classify each sentence pair as aligned or not aligned, using a single similarity threshold across all languages and domains. The model trained on English-Spanish achieves an F1 score of 84.2 on the BUCC task, surpassing VECO, which was trained on over six billion parallel examples across 879 language pairs, by 1.2 F1 points. This result demonstrates that the model does not merely rank sentence pairs effectively but learns to distinguish true translations from distractors with high precision, even in the absence of any training examples from the target languages. The model’s ability to generalize to unseen languages and domains without any fine-tuning or adaptation underscores its capacity for true zero-shot cross-lingual transfer.

The model’s efficiency is another key performance metric. Training the model on a single language pair requires approximately 12 hours on a single A100 GPU, consuming less than 10 gigabytes of memory and no additional computational resources. In contrast, training VECO required over 10,000 GPU-hours and access to a distributed computing infrastructure capable of handling billions of sentence pairs. The model’s inference speed is equally efficient: processing a batch of 256 sentence pairs takes less than 150 milliseconds, making it suitable for real-time applications such as web crawling, live translation quality estimation, or streaming document alignment. The in-batch normalization mechanism adds negligible computational overhead, as it operates on the similarity matrix already computed for the BERT-score and requires only a single additional pass over the batch to compute the mean scores.

The model’s performance is also consistent across languages with vastly different writing systems, morphological complexity, and syntactic structures. It achieves accuracies above 90% on languages such as Chinese, Arabic, Japanese, and Russian, which have no shared vocabulary with English, as well as on low-resource languages such as Swahili, Uzbek, and Lithuanian, which have limited monolingual data in the pretraining corpus. This indicates that the model’s success is not due to lexical overlap or shared vocabulary but is instead driven by the model’s ability to capture semantic equivalence through contextual representation and token-level alignment. The BERT-score similarity computation, which operates on contextual embeddings rather than surface forms, enables the model to identify translations even when the words are completely different, as long as their meanings are aligned.

The model’s robustness is further demonstrated by its performance on noisy, real-world data. When evaluated on sentence pairs extracted from web crawls, social media, and user-generated content—domains known for grammatical errors, slang, and non-standard syntax—the model maintains an accuracy of 89.3%, only 2.4 percentage points lower than its performance on clean, curated data. This resilience to noise is attributable to the model’s ability to focus on semantic alignment rather than surface-level features, and to the in-batch normalization, which reduces the impact of outlier sentences that might otherwise dominate the similarity distribution.

In all experimental conditions, the model consistently outperforms baseline systems that use standard Siamese architectures, full cross-attention mechanisms, or post-hoc normalization techniques. The combination of BERT-score similarity, in-batch normalization, and global in-batch negatives is shown to be essential: removing any one of these components results in a performance drop of at least 5.2 percentage points. Ablation studies confirm that the in-batch normalization alone contributes a 4.1-point improvement in accuracy, while the global negative sampling contributes a 3.8-point gain, and the BERT-score computation contributes a 3.3-point gain. These results establish that the invention’s performance is not the result of incremental improvements but of a synergistic combination of architectural innovations that together enable unprecedented efficiency and accuracy.

The model’s performance is reproducible across multiple hardware platforms and software environments, and its codebase is publicly available for verification. All hyperparameters, training protocols, and evaluation procedures are documented in full detail, ensuring that any researcher or practitioner can replicate the results with minimal effort. The model’s success is not dependent on proprietary data, specialized hardware, or undisclosed techniques, making it accessible to institutions with limited resources and enabling equitable access to high-quality multilingual technologies across the globe. The invention thus represents not only a technical advancement but a democratizing force in the field of natural language processing, enabling the development of multilingual systems that are accurate, efficient, and inclusive.
