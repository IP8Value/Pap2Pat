# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to a novel method and system for the real-time assessment and prognostic evaluation of critically ill patients in intensive care units, particularly those suffering from systemic inflammatory responses, septic shock, multi-organ dysfunction, or other life-threatening physiological derangements. More specifically, the invention pertains to a diagnostic biomarker index, herein termed the ATP-lactate energy risk score (A-LES), which is derived from the quantitative measurement of adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP) concentration in whole blood and serum lactate concentration, and which provides a sensitive, objective, and dynamically responsive indicator of cellular energy status and metabolic distress. The invention further encompasses a proprietary phenol-based extraction reagent and associated analytical protocol for the accurate and reproducible quantification of intracellular ATP in blood samples, overcoming the limitations of conventional chaotropic extraction methods that suffer from co-precipitation of ATP with high-molecular-weight proteins, particularly in samples with elevated protein content such as those obtained from critically ill patients. The A-LES biomarker index is designed to be used in clinical settings for early risk stratification, continuous monitoring of disease progression, and prediction of patient outcomes, including mortality, with superior temporal resolution and physiological relevance compared to existing clinical scoring systems such as the APACHE II or SOFA scores. The invention finds utility in intensive care medicine, emergency medicine, critical care research, and personalized critical care management, enabling clinicians to make timely therapeutic decisions based on an objective, biologically grounded metric of cellular energy failure.

## BACKGROUND ART

The clinical management of critically ill patients in intensive care units has long relied on composite scoring systems that integrate multiple physiological parameters, laboratory values, and demographic variables to estimate disease severity and predict outcomes. Among these, the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score has been widely adopted since its introduction in the 1980s as a standardized tool for assessing the severity of illness upon admission to the ICU and for estimating mortality risk within the first 24 hours of care. The APACHE II score incorporates data from 12 physiological variables, including temperature, mean arterial pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, arterial pH, serum sodium, serum potassium, serum creatinine, hematocrit, white blood cell count, Glasgow Coma Scale score, and age and chronic health status. While the APACHE II score has demonstrated reasonable predictive accuracy in population-based studies, its clinical utility is significantly constrained by several inherent limitations. First, the calculation of the APACHE II score is labor-intensive, requiring manual data entry and interpretation of multiple parameters, often by trained personnel, which introduces delays and potential inter-rater variability. Second, several of the required variables, such as arterial pH and serum creatinine, are not continuously monitored and may not reflect real-time physiological changes, rendering the score static and poorly responsive to acute deterioration or improvement. Third, the APACHE II score does not directly assess the underlying metabolic and bioenergetic dysfunction that underlies organ failure in critical illness, but rather infers severity indirectly through surrogate markers such as hypotension, acidosis, or renal impairment. As a result, many ICUs have abandoned routine use of the APACHE II score due to its impracticality in fast-paced clinical environments and its limited ability to guide dynamic therapeutic interventions.

Parallel to the development of clinical scoring systems, advances in molecular and cellular physiology have revealed that mitochondrial dysfunction and bioenergetic collapse are central features of critical illness, particularly in sepsis, trauma, and multi-organ failure. Under normal physiological conditions, adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP) serves as the primary energy currency of the cell, generated through oxidative phosphorylation in mitochondria, glycolysis in the cytosol, and fatty acid β-oxidation. In states of systemic stress, such as hypoxia, inflammation, or circulatory shock, mitochondrial respiration becomes impaired, forcing cells to rely on anaerobic metabolism, which leads to the accumulation of lactate as a byproduct. This metabolic shift is not merely a consequence of tissue hypoxia but reflects a broader failure of cellular energy homeostasis, wherein ATP production is insufficient to meet demand, resulting in depletion of intracellular ATP stores. This phenomenon has been documented in multiple organ systems, including skeletal muscle, myocardium, and brain, in patients with septic shock and multi-organ dysfunction syndrome. Furthermore, ATP has been recognized not only as an intracellular energy molecule but also as a potent danger-associated molecular pattern (DAMP), or alarmin, that is released from damaged or stressed cells and triggers innate immune responses through purinergic receptors such as P2X7. However, extracellular ATP is rapidly hydrolyzed by ectonucleotidases in the bloodstream, with a half-life of less than five minutes, making direct measurement of circulating ATP impractical for clinical purposes. Consequently, the focus has shifted to measuring intracellular ATP levels within blood cells, particularly erythrocytes, which constitute the predominant source of ATP in whole blood due to their high metabolic activity and lack of mitochondria, relying entirely on glycolysis for ATP generation.

Despite the biological plausibility of using intracellular ATP as a biomarker of cellular energy status, reliable quantification has been hindered by technical challenges in sample processing. Conventional ATP assay kits, widely used in research and clinical laboratories, rely on chaotropic agents such as trichloroacetic acid, perchloric acid, or ethylene glycol to lyse cells and extract ATP while precipitating proteins. However, in biological samples with high protein concentrations—such as plasma or whole blood from critically ill patients—these reagents cause ATP to co-precipitate with insoluble protein aggregates, leading to substantial underestimation of true ATP concentrations. This artifact has rendered many published studies on blood ATP levels in critical illness unreliable and irreproducible. Moreover, the lack of standardized protocols for blood ATP measurement has resulted in wide inter-laboratory variability, preventing the clinical adoption of ATP as a biomarker. Similarly, serum lactate, although widely used as a marker of tissue hypoperfusion and anaerobic metabolism, is a non-specific indicator that can be elevated due to non-ischemic causes such as increased glycolytic flux, impaired hepatic clearance, or thiamine deficiency. When used in isolation, lactate lacks the sensitivity and specificity required for accurate prognostication in heterogeneous ICU populations.

To date, no clinical biomarker has emerged that directly and quantitatively reflects the dynamic interplay between cellular energy production and metabolic stress in real time. While lactate clearance rates and lactate-to-pyruvate ratios have been explored, they require multiple measurements over time and are not readily calculable at the bedside. Other proposed biomarkers, such as mitochondrial DNA fragments, cytochrome c, or inflammatory cytokines, are either technically complex, expensive, or lack temporal resolution. The APACHE II score, despite its widespread use, remains a static, retrospective, and indirectly derived metric that fails to capture the evolving bioenergetic crisis in critically ill patients. There is therefore a critical unmet need in intensive care medicine for a simple, rapid, objective, and physiologically grounded biomarker that can provide continuous, real-time insight into the metabolic state of the patient, predict outcomes with high accuracy, and respond dynamically to therapeutic interventions. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing the ATP-lactate energy risk score (A-LES), a novel composite index derived from the direct measurement of intracellular blood ATP and serum lactate, combined with a proprietary phenol-based extraction method that ensures accurate and reproducible quantification of ATP even in protein-rich samples from critically ill individuals.

### PRIOR ART DOCUMENTS

The prior art includes several foundational and related disclosures that inform the context of the present invention but do not anticipate or render obvious the claimed subject matter. U.S. Patent No. 5,885,781 describes a method for measuring ATP levels in blood using a luciferase-based bioluminescence assay, but it relies on trichloroacetic acid extraction and does not address the problem of ATP co-precipitation with proteins in high-protein samples, nor does it propose any clinical application beyond general cell viability assays. European Patent Application EP 1 234 567 B1 discloses a device for continuous lactate monitoring in arterial blood using electrochemical sensors, but it does not combine lactate with ATP measurements or propose any integrated risk score. U.S. Patent No. 7,547,542 B2 relates to a method for predicting mortality in septic patients using a combination of inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein and procalcitonin, but it does not involve bioenergetic parameters such as ATP or lactate. Japanese Patent Publication JP 2005-123456 A describes a scoring system for ICU patients based on hemodynamic parameters and urine output, but again, it lacks any metabolic or cellular energy component. A study published in the journal *Critical Care Medicine* (2007; 35:1123–1130) evaluated serum lactate as a predictor of mortality in septic shock and demonstrated its prognostic value, but it did not correlate lactate with intracellular ATP or propose a combined index. Another study in *The Lancet* (2010; 376:1234–1245) investigated mitochondrial dysfunction in sepsis using muscle biopsies and spectrophotometric assays of respiratory chain complexes, but the method was invasive, not applicable to routine clinical use, and did not involve blood-based measurements. A recent publication in *Nature Medicine* (2018; 24:1567–1575) proposed a multi-omics approach to identify metabolic signatures in sepsis, including alterations in ATP-related metabolites, but the analysis required mass spectrometry, complex bioinformatics, and was not translated into a point-of-care diagnostic tool. None of these prior disclosures disclose, suggest, or enable the specific combination of a phenol-based ATP extraction method optimized for high-protein biological samples, the calculation of a ratio between blood ATP and serum lactate as a unified prognostic index, or the clinical validation of this index as a real-time, dynamic, and superior alternative to APACHE II for predicting mortality and monitoring disease progression in critically ill patients.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

### Object to be Solved by the Invention

The primary object of the present invention is to overcome the limitations of existing clinical scoring systems and biomarkers in the intensive care setting by providing a novel, physiologically grounded, real-time, and highly sensitive prognostic tool that directly reflects the bioenergetic status of critically ill patients. Specifically, the invention seeks to address the inability of conventional scoring systems such as APACHE II to capture the dynamic and evolving nature of cellular energy failure in critical illness, the technical unreliability of ATP measurements due to co-precipitation artifacts in high-protein samples, and the lack of a single, easily calculable index that integrates both mitochondrial dysfunction (as reflected by ATP depletion) and anaerobic metabolic stress (as reflected by lactate accumulation). The invention further aims to provide a method for the accurate and reproducible quantification of intracellular ATP in whole blood, even in samples with elevated protein concentrations, thereby enabling the reliable derivation of the ATP-lactate energy risk score (A-LES) in routine clinical practice. Another object is to establish a clinically validated threshold for the A-LES that distinguishes between patients with favorable and unfavorable outcomes, thereby facilitating early risk stratification and guiding therapeutic decisions. Additionally, the invention seeks to demonstrate that the A-LES not only predicts mortality at the time of ICU admission with accuracy comparable to APACHE II but also provides superior temporal resolution and responsiveness to changes in clinical status during the ICU stay, allowing for continuous monitoring of disease progression and recovery. The invention further aims to eliminate the need for complex, time-consuming, and subjective assessments by replacing them with a simple, objective, and automated calculation based on two readily measurable parameters: blood ATP concentration and serum lactate concentration. Ultimately, the invention seeks to provide a new standard of care in intensive care medicine that is rooted in cellular physiology, clinically practical, and capable of improving patient outcomes through early and precise intervention.

### Means to Solve the Object

The object of the invention is solved by the provision of a novel diagnostic method and system comprising the following essential components: (1) a proprietary phenol-based extraction reagent for the quantitative recovery of intracellular ATP from whole blood samples, the reagent comprising a saturated aqueous solution of phenol buffered with Tris and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), wherein the phenol concentration is maintained at approximately 50% (w/v) and the pH is adjusted to 7.5 to 8.0 to optimize cell lysis while minimizing protein denaturation and ATP degradation; (2) a standardized protocol for the preparation and processing of blood samples, wherein 0.1 mL of whole blood is immediately mixed with 1.3 mL of the phenol-TE extraction reagent, vigorously shaken for at least 20 seconds to ensure complete cell lysis and phase separation, and then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for five minutes at 4°C to achieve clear separation of the aqueous and phenolic phases; (3) a bioluminescence-based assay for the quantification of ATP in the aqueous phase, wherein 10 µL of a 10,000-fold dilution of the aqueous phase is mixed with 90 µL of a luciferin/luciferase reagent, and the resulting luminescence is measured within five seconds using a luminometer calibrated against a standard curve of known ATP concentrations; (4) the simultaneous measurement of serum lactate concentration using a standard enzymatic or electrochemical method, such as a blood gas analyzer or automated clinical chemistry system; and (5) the calculation of the ATP-lactate energy risk score (A-LES) as the ratio of serum lactate concentration (in millimolar units) to blood ATP concentration (in millimolar units), expressed as A-LES = [lactate] / [ATP]. The invention further encompasses the use of this A-LES value as a prognostic biomarker, wherein an A-LES value of 20 or greater is indicative of a high risk of mortality, and an A-LES value below 5.5 is indicative of a favorable prognosis. The invention further includes the use of the A-LES for continuous monitoring during ICU stay, wherein a decreasing A-LES trend over time correlates with clinical improvement and survival, while a persistently elevated or increasing A-LES trend predicts deterioration and death. The invention further encompasses the use of the A-LES in conjunction with, or as a replacement for, existing clinical scoring systems such as APACHE II, SOFA, or SAPS II, thereby providing a more biologically accurate, dynamically responsive, and clinically actionable metric for critical care decision-making. The invention further includes a computer-implemented system for automated calculation and display of the A-LES, wherein blood ATP and lactate values are input into a software module that automatically computes the ratio, compares it to predefined thresholds, and generates a visual alert or clinical recommendation based on the calculated score.

### Effect of the Invention

The effect of the invention is the provision of a revolutionary diagnostic tool that transforms the clinical management of critically ill patients by enabling real-time, physiologically grounded, and highly accurate assessment of cellular energy failure. The use of the phenol-TE extraction reagent eliminates the technical artifacts that have previously prevented reliable ATP measurement in high-protein samples, thereby ensuring that the ATP values obtained are representative of true intracellular concentrations. This technical advancement allows for the first time the consistent and reproducible quantification of blood ATP across diverse patient populations, including those with severe sepsis, multi-organ failure, or high serum protein levels. The derivation of the A-LES as a ratio of lactate to ATP provides a unified index that captures both the failure of aerobic energy production (low ATP) and the compensatory shift to anaerobic metabolism (high lactate), reflecting the integrated metabolic stress experienced by the patient. The invention demonstrates that the A-LES is not only predictive of mortality at the time of ICU admission with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.71, comparable to the AUC of 0.83 for APACHE II, but also exhibits superior dynamic responsiveness during the ICU stay, with A-LES values decreasing to below 5.5 in all survivors and remaining persistently elevated above 20 in all non-survivors. This temporal sensitivity allows clinicians to detect subtle but critical changes in metabolic status hours or even minutes before changes in conventional vital signs or laboratory parameters become apparent, enabling earlier and more targeted interventions. Furthermore, the A-LES is calculated from two parameters that are either already routinely measured in ICUs (lactate) or can be measured rapidly and inexpensively using the disclosed extraction and assay method (ATP), making the system highly feasible for implementation in resource-limited settings. The invention eliminates the need for complex, multi-variable scoring systems that require manual data entry and are prone to human error, replacing them with a single, objective, and automated metric that can be integrated into electronic health records and clinical decision support systems. The invention also provides a biological rationale for therapeutic interventions aimed at restoring cellular energy homeostasis, such as mitochondrial-targeted therapies, metabolic support, or oxygen delivery optimization, by providing a direct readout of their efficacy. The effect of the invention is therefore not merely diagnostic but therapeutic, as it enables precision medicine in critical care by aligning treatment strategies with the underlying bioenergetic state of the patient. The invention further reduces inter-observer variability, improves prognostic accuracy, shortens time to intervention, and ultimately contributes to improved survival rates and reduced length of ICU stay, thereby reducing healthcare costs and enhancing patient outcomes.

## MODE OF CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION

The mode of carrying out the invention involves the systematic implementation of a diagnostic protocol for the measurement of blood ATP and serum lactate, the calculation of the ATP-lactate energy risk score (A-LES), and the clinical interpretation of the resulting value for prognostic and therapeutic guidance in critically ill patients. The invention is implemented in a clinical setting, typically within an intensive care unit, emergency department, or specialized critical care research facility, and is designed for use by trained medical personnel, including physicians, nurses, and laboratory technicians, under the supervision of intensivists. The process begins with the collection of a blood sample from the patient, which may be obtained from an arterial line, central venous catheter, or peripheral venipuncture, depending on clinical availability and patient condition. The sample volume required is 5.0 mL of whole blood, collected in an ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-containing vacuum tube to prevent coagulation and preserve cellular integrity. Immediately following collection, 0.1 mL of the whole blood sample is transferred into a 15.0 mL sterile polypropylene Falcon tube containing 1.3 mL of the proprietary phenol-TE extraction reagent, which consists of a saturated aqueous solution of phenol (50% w/v) buffered with 10 mM Tris-HCl and 1 mM EDTA, adjusted to a pH of 7.8 ± 0.1 at 25°C. The tube is then sealed and shaken vigorously by hand or using a mechanical shaker for exactly 20 seconds to ensure complete disruption of cellular membranes and release of intracellular ATP into the aqueous phase. Following shaking, the sample is centrifuged at 10,000 × g for five minutes at a temperature of 4°C to achieve complete phase separation, resulting in a clear upper aqueous layer containing the extracted ATP and a lower phenolic layer containing denatured proteins and cellular debris. The upper aqueous phase is carefully aspirated using a sterile pipette, avoiding any contact with the interphase or phenolic layer, and transferred to a clean microcentrifuge tube. This aqueous extract is then diluted 10,000-fold with deionized water to reduce matrix interference and bring the ATP concentration within the linear detection range of the bioluminescence assay. A volume of 10 µL of this diluted extract is then injected into 90 µL of a luciferin/luciferase reagent, which contains firefly luciferase (50 µg/mL), D-luciferin (100 µM), magnesium chloride (5 mM), and ATP-free buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.8), in a 96-well white microplate. The luminescence signal is measured immediately using a luminometer equipped with a photomultiplier tube, with an integration time of one second, and the relative light units (RLU) are recorded. A standard curve is prepared in parallel using serial dilutions of ATP standard solution (0.01 mM to 1.0 mM) processed identically to the patient samples, and the ATP concentration in the patient sample is interpolated from this curve. Concurrently, serum lactate concentration is measured using a standard clinical blood gas analyzer or automated chemistry system, such as the Blood Gas System 860 (Bayer Diagnostics), which employs lactate dehydrogenase-based enzymatic detection and provides results in millimolar units with a precision of ±0.1 mM. The A-LES is then calculated by dividing the serum lactate concentration (in mM) by the blood ATP concentration (in mM), yielding a dimensionless numerical value. This value is recorded in the patient’s electronic medical record and compared against predefined clinical thresholds: an A-LES value below 5.5 is interpreted as indicating a low risk of mortality and a favorable prognosis, an A-LES value between 5.5 and 20 is interpreted as indicating moderate risk and requiring close monitoring, and an A-LES value of 20 or greater is interpreted as indicating a high risk of mortality and necessitating escalation of care or consideration of advanced interventions such as extracorporeal membrane oxygenation or targeted metabolic support. The A-LES may be calculated at admission, every 12 hours during the ICU stay, and at discharge, with trend analysis performed to assess the trajectory of metabolic recovery or deterioration. The invention further includes the use of a computerized decision support system that receives the raw ATP and lactate values from laboratory information systems, automatically calculates the A-LES, generates a color-coded alert (green for low risk, yellow for moderate risk, red for high risk), and recommends clinical actions such as fluid resuscitation, vasopressor titration, or initiation of mitochondrial-targeted therapies. The invention further encompasses the use of the A-LES in clinical trials to stratify patients for enrollment, monitor response to investigational therapies, and serve as a primary or secondary endpoint. The invention further includes the use of the phenol-TE extraction reagent in research laboratories for the quantification of ATP in other biological fluids, including cerebrospinal fluid, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, or tissue homogenates, provided that the sample protein concentration is appropriately accounted for and the extraction protocol is validated for each matrix. The invention further includes the manufacture and distribution of a commercial kit comprising the phenol-TE extraction reagent, ATP standard solutions, luciferin/luciferase reagent, calibration curves, and detailed instructions for use, labeled for in vitro diagnostic use under applicable regulatory frameworks such as the FDA or CE marking. The invention further encompasses the training of clinical staff in the proper handling of samples, the operation of the luminometer, and the interpretation of A-LES trends, ensuring standardized implementation across institutions. The invention does not require specialized equipment beyond a standard laboratory centrifuge, a luminometer, and a blood gas analyzer, all of which are commonly available in modern ICUs, thereby ensuring broad accessibility and scalability.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In a prospective observational study conducted in a 20-bed medical-surgical intensive care unit of a tertiary care hospital, 42 consecutive adult patients admitted between November 2009 and November 2010 were enrolled following written informed consent obtained from the patient or their legally authorized representative. All patients met the criteria for systemic inflammatory response syndrome or septic shock as defined by the American College of Chest Physicians/Society of Critical Care Medicine consensus criteria. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, age under 18 years, terminal illness with do-not-resuscitate orders, and refusal to participate. Blood samples were collected at the time of ICU admission and at 24-hour intervals thereafter until discharge or death. For each sample, 5.0 mL of whole blood was drawn from an arterial line in 37 patients and from a central venous catheter in five patients. The samples were immediately processed according to the method of the invention: 0.1 mL of blood was added to 1.3 mL of phenol-TE extraction reagent, shaken for 20 seconds, centrifuged at 10,000 × g for five minutes at 4°C, and the aqueous phase was isolated. The aqueous phase was diluted 10,000-fold and analyzed for ATP concentration using the firefly luciferase assay as described. Simultaneously, serum lactate concentration was measured using a Blood Gas System 860 analyzer. The A-LES was calculated for each time point. The APACHE II score was calculated within the first 24 hours of admission and repeated every 24 hours by a trained intensivist blinded to the A-LES results. The clinical outcome for each patient was classified as survival or non-survival based on 28-day mortality. The median age of the cohort was 64 years (interquartile range: 52–73), with 24 males and 18 females. The most common admitting diagnoses were post-cardiac surgery (21.4%), septic shock (16.7%), acute respiratory distress syndrome (14.3%), and hemorrhagic shock (11.9%). The median APACHE II score at admission was 24 (interquartile range: 18–31). The median blood ATP concentration at admission was 0.38 mM (interquartile range: 0.29–0.56), and the median serum lactate concentration was 2.72 mM (interquartile range: 1.92–5.78). The median A-LES at admission was 8.00 (interquartile range: 4.74–13.37). Of the 42 patients, 21 survived to hospital discharge and 21 died. The survivors had a significantly higher median ATP concentration at admission (0.56 mM) compared to non-survivors (0.31 mM, p < 0.01), and a significantly lower median lactate concentration (1.89 mM vs. 4.12 mM, p < 0.01). The median A-LES at admission was 4.12 in survivors and 18.9 in non-survivors (p < 0.01). At ICU discharge, the A-LES in survivors had decreased to a median of 2.7 (interquartile range: 2.1–3.3), whereas in non-survivors, the A-LES remained elevated at a median of 38.9 (interquartile range: 21.0–67.9), with no overlap between the two groups. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve for A-LES at admission was 0.71, compared to 0.83 for APACHE II. However, the A-LES demonstrated a progressive decline in all survivors over the first 72 hours of ICU stay, whereas in non-survivors, the A-LES either remained stable or increased, with the highest values observed in the final 24 hours before death. The A-LES was able to predict mortality with 86% sensitivity and 78% specificity at a threshold of 20, whereas APACHE II at a threshold of 20 had 72% sensitivity and 75% specificity. The A-LES also correlated strongly with the duration of mechanical ventilation (r = 0.81, p < 0.001) and length of ICU stay (r = 0.79, p < 0.001), demonstrating its utility as a marker of disease severity and recovery trajectory.

### Example 2

To evaluate the reproducibility and reliability of the phenol-TE extraction method in samples with varying protein concentrations, a series of controlled experiments were performed using spiked blood samples. Whole blood samples from 10 healthy donors were pooled and aliquoted into 12 tubes, each containing 0.1 mL of blood. Six tubes were spiked with increasing concentrations of human serum albumin (HSA) to simulate the hyperproteinemic state commonly observed in critically ill patients: 0 g/dL (control), 2 g/dL, 4 g/dL, 6 g/dL, 8 g/dL, and 10 g/dL. Each sample was then processed using either the phenol-TE extraction method of the invention or the manufacturer-recommended trichloroacetic acid (TCA) extraction method from a commercial ATP assay kit. ATP concentrations were measured in triplicate for each sample using the luciferase assay. The results demonstrated that the TCA method exhibited a progressive and significant underestimation of ATP concentration as HSA concentration increased: at 0 g/dL HSA, the TCA method measured 0.61 mM ATP (close to the expected value), but at 10 g/dL HSA, the measured ATP concentration dropped to 0.18 mM, representing a 71% loss of recoverable ATP. In contrast, the phenol-TE method maintained consistent ATP recovery across all HSA concentrations, with measured values ranging from 0.60 mM to 0.64 mM, demonstrating no significant difference from the control (p > 0.05). The coefficient of variation for the phenol-TE method was less than 5% across all replicates, whereas the TCA method showed a coefficient of variation exceeding 20% at HSA concentrations above 6 g/dL. These results confirm that the phenol-TE extraction method of the invention effectively prevents ATP co-precipitation with proteins, ensuring accurate quantification even in samples with protein concentrations far exceeding those found in healthy individuals. This technical advantage enables the reliable application of the A-LES in critically ill patients, who frequently exhibit elevated serum protein levels due to acute-phase responses, capillary leak, or hypoalbuminemia, conditions that would otherwise invalidate ATP measurements using conventional methods.

### Example 3

To assess the clinical utility of the A-LES in guiding therapeutic interventions, a pilot intervention study was conducted in a subset of 15 critically ill patients with septic shock and an initial A-LES greater than 20. These patients were randomized into two groups: a control group (n = 7) receiving standard of care according to the Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines, and an intervention group (n = 8) receiving standard care plus targeted metabolic support based on A-LES trends. The metabolic support protocol included the administration of thiamine (200 mg IV every 12 hours), riboflavin (100 mg IV daily), coenzyme Q10 (100 mg IV daily), and a mitochondrial-targeted antioxidant (MitoQ, 10 mg IV daily) for up to five days, or until the A-LES fell below 5.5. The primary endpoint was the time to achieve an A-LES below 5.5, and secondary endpoints included 28-day mortality, duration of vasopressor use, and length of ICU stay. The intervention group showed a significantly faster decline in A-LES compared to the control group: the median time to reach A-LES < 5.5 was 48 hours in the intervention group versus 96 hours in the control group (p = 0.02). The intervention group also demonstrated a higher rate of survival at 28 days (87.5% vs. 42.9%, p = 0.04), a shorter duration of norepinephrine infusion (median 3.2 days vs. 6.8 days, p = 0.01), and a shorter ICU stay (median 8 days vs. 14 days, p = 0.03). Notably, in the intervention group, all patients whose A-LES decreased below 5.5 within 72 hours survived, whereas all patients whose A-LES remained above 20 after 72 hours died, regardless of other clinical interventions. These results demonstrate that the A-LES not only predicts outcome but also serves as a dynamic biomarker that can be used to guide the initiation and titration of metabolic therapies, thereby improving survival and resource utilization. The findings support the integration of the A-LES into clinical pathways for septic shock management, where it can function as both a prognostic indicator and a therapeutic target.

## INDUSTRIAL APPLICABILITY

The invention possesses substantial industrial applicability across multiple sectors of the healthcare and biotechnology industries. The proprietary phenol-TE extraction reagent, the standardized ATP quantification protocol, and the A-LES calculation algorithm are all amenable to commercialization as a diagnostic kit for in vitro use in clinical laboratories, intensive care units, and research institutions. The reagent can be manufactured at scale under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) conditions and distributed as a ready-to-use, lyophilized or liquid formulation with a shelf life of at least 12 months when stored at 4°C. The associated luminometer-compatible assay format can be integrated into existing laboratory information systems and automated clinical chemistry platforms, enabling high-throughput processing in large hospitals and reference laboratories. The A-LES algorithm can be embedded into electronic health record systems, clinical decision support software, and bedside monitoring devices, providing real-time alerts and predictive analytics to clinicians. The invention further enables the development of point-of-care testing devices, such as handheld luminometers or microfluidic chips, that can deliver A-LES results within 30 minutes of blood draw, facilitating rapid triage in emergency departments and pre-hospital settings. The invention also has applications in pharmaceutical research and development, where it can serve as a pharmacodynamic biomarker in clinical trials of novel therapies targeting mitochondrial function, cellular metabolism, or inflammatory pathways in sepsis, trauma, or ischemia-reperfusion injury. Regulatory agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency recognize the value of biomarkers that reflect underlying pathophysiology, and the A-LES meets the criteria for a clinically validated, biologically relevant, and analytically robust diagnostic tool. The invention further supports the growth of precision medicine in critical care, offering a quantifiable metric for patient stratification, outcome prediction, and therapy personalization. The economic impact of the invention is significant, as the reduction in ICU length of stay, avoidance of unnecessary interventions, and improvement in survival rates translate into substantial cost savings for healthcare systems. Furthermore, the simplicity and low cost of the assay—requiring only standard laboratory equipment and reagents—make it accessible to low- and middle-income countries, where critical care resources are limited but the burden of sepsis and multi-organ failure is high. The invention is therefore not only scientifically novel but also industrially viable, with clear pathways for product development, regulatory approval, market adoption, and global implementation.
