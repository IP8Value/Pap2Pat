# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to scalable fault-tolerant quantum computing architectures based on photonic technologies, specifically to a hybrid resource state composed of Gaussian boson sampling-generated Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) qubits and momentum-squeezed vacuum states arranged in a three-dimensional lattice structure. The invention further encompasses systems and methods for generating, entangling, and measuring such hybrid states using planar photonic integrated circuits operating at room temperature, with homodyne detection as the primary readout mechanism. The architecture enables universal quantum computation through measurement-based quantum computing protocols, leveraging the intrinsic compatibility of photonic systems with existing silicon photonics manufacturing, while circumventing the prohibitive hardware demands of deterministic GKP state generation. The invention introduces a novel decoding framework that exploits the spatial and temporal correlations induced by the hybrid structure of the resource state to correct errors arising from probabilistic qubit generation and finite squeezing, thereby achieving fault-tolerant thresholds that are experimentally accessible with current photonic technology.

## BACKGROUND

Quantum computing architectures based on photonic platforms have long been recognized for their potential to overcome fundamental limitations inherent in matter-based qubit systems, including the need for cryogenic environments, complex vacuum systems, and intricate control electronics. Photonic systems operate at room temperature, are inherently compatible with high-speed optical communication networks, and benefit from decades of advancement in integrated optics and silicon photonics. Despite these advantages, the realization of a scalable, fault-tolerant photonic quantum computer has been hindered by the difficulty of generating and maintaining high-fidelity discrete-variable qubit states—particularly those encoded in bosonic modes—using deterministic, on-demand sources. Among the most promising encodings for optical qubits is the Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) code, which maps logical qubit information into the periodic structure of a quantum state in phase space, enabling the implementation of Clifford gates via Gaussian operations and offering intrinsic resilience to small phase-space displacements caused by photon loss and thermal noise.

However, the preparation of GKP qubits remains a formidable challenge. Current methods rely on post-selected measurements using photon-number-resolving detectors following Gaussian operations such as squeezing, displacement, and linear interferometry. These procedures are inherently probabilistic, with success probabilities typically below one percent for states of sufficient fidelity. While multiplexing—parallel operation of many such sources—can increase the effective generation rate, the required number of devices scales logarithmically with the desired success probability, leading to impractical overheads when targeting near-unit fidelity. Alternative approaches involving strong optical nonlinearities or matter-light interactions remain experimentally inaccessible with current technology. Consequently, prior photonic quantum computing architectures have either required deterministic GKP sources, which are not feasible, or have abandoned GKP encoding entirely in favor of continuous-variable cluster states, which lack the non-Gaussian resources necessary for universal computation.

Other architectures have attempted to circumvent this issue by using discrete-variable qubit encodings such as dual-rail or cat states, but these suffer from their own limitations, including non-deterministic entangling gates, low detection efficiencies, and poor scalability. Furthermore, existing measurement-based schemes for photonic quantum computation rely on cluster states composed entirely of either Gaussian modes or entirely of GKP qubits. The former lacks the non-Gaussianity required for universal computation, while the latter demands an unattainable density of deterministic GKP sources. No prior architecture has successfully combined the scalability of continuous-variable resource generation with the computational power of bosonic qubit encodings in a manner that accommodates the probabilistic nature of state preparation without resorting to erasure-based error models or lattice renormalization techniques that introduce additional complexity and resource overhead.

Moreover, existing proposals for photonic quantum computation often require complex, non-planar optical networks with long optical delay lines, high-precision spectral filtering, or reconfigurable optical switches operating under cryogenic conditions. These constraints severely limit scalability, increase fabrication complexity, and introduce significant optical losses that degrade performance exponentially with system size. The requirement for active, high-speed switching in cryogenic environments further exacerbates engineering challenges, as conventional photonic components such as electro-optic modulators and phase shifters are incompatible with low-temperature operation. Consequently, the field has lacked a coherent, hardware-friendly architecture that simultaneously satisfies the demands of fault tolerance, scalability, room-temperature operation, and compatibility with mass-manufacturable photonic integrated circuits.

The present invention addresses these longstanding challenges by introducing a novel hybrid resource state composed of GKP qubits and momentum-squeezed vacuum states arranged in a three-dimensional Raussendorf-Harrington-Goyal (RHG) lattice, generated through multiplexed Gaussian boson sampling devices and entangled via deterministic, planar, time-domain optical circuits. Crucially, when a GKP qubit fails to be generated, the corresponding mode is not discarded or treated as an erasure, but is instead deterministically replaced with a momentum-squeezed vacuum state. This substitution preserves the entanglement structure of the cluster state while introducing a predictable and correctable form of noise. The invention further introduces a two-tier decoding protocol that leverages the spatial correlations induced by the hybrid structure and the known locations of squeezed states to convert continuous homodyne measurement outcomes into reliable qubit-level syndrome data. This approach enables fault-tolerant quantum computation with significantly reduced multiplexing requirements compared to prior art, achieving thresholds that are within reach of current experimental capabilities. The architecture is fully compatible with planar silicon photonic fabrication, operates entirely at room temperature except for a minimal cryogenic footprint for photon-number-resolving detectors, and enables clock speeds limited only by the bandwidth of homodyne detection—orders of magnitude faster than competing platforms. This combination of features constitutes a transformative advance in the field of photonic quantum computing.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a scalable, fault-tolerant architecture for universal quantum computation using photonic systems, comprising a hybrid resource state formed by the interconnection of Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) qubits and momentum-squeezed vacuum states arranged in a three-dimensional lattice structure. The architecture is generated through a modular system of photonic integrated circuits operating at room temperature, wherein probabilistic GKP qubit generation via multiplexed Gaussian boson sampling devices is complemented by deterministic substitution of failed outputs with squeezed vacuum states. The resulting hybrid state is entangled into a (2+1)-dimensional Raussendorf-Harrington-Goyal (RHG) lattice using time-domain optical delay lines and spatially arranged controlled-Z gates implemented via integrated beam splitters and squeezers. Quantum computation is performed through homodyne detection of the lattice modes, with measurement outcomes processed by a novel two-tier decoding protocol that first converts continuous-variable quadrature data into discrete qubit-level syndromes using a correlation-aware inner decoder, and then applies a minimum-weight perfect matching algorithm to the outer qubit code, incorporating weights derived from the spatial distribution of squeezed states and the covariance structure of the correlated noise.

The system comprises four primary modules: a state-generation module that employs multiple Gaussian boson sampling devices, each comprising displaced and squeezed vacuum inputs, a linear interferometer, and photon-number-resolving detectors; a multiplexing module that uses active optical switches to route successfully generated GKP qubits to a common output while substituting failed attempts with momentum-squeezed vacuum states; a computational module that entangles the output modes into a temporally extended one-dimensional cluster state using optical delay lines and then extends the structure into two spatial dimensions via a planar array of controlled-Z gates; and a photonic quantum processing unit that performs homodyne measurements on the lattice modes using local oscillator phase control, digitizes the quadrature outcomes, and feeds them to a classical controller that computes the next measurement settings and recovery operations. The entire architecture is implemented on a planar photonic chip, enabling scalable fabrication using standard silicon photonics processes, and requires cryogenic operation only for the photon-number-resolving detectors, with all other components—including switching networks, delay lines, and homodyne detectors—operating at room temperature.

A key innovation of the invention is the replacement of failed GKP qubit generation events with momentum-squeezed vacuum states, which are Gaussian, easily generated, and preserve the entanglement topology of the cluster state. This substitution avoids the need for erasure-based error correction and instead introduces a structured, correlated noise model that can be explicitly accounted for in the decoding process. The inner decoder exploits the known covariance structure of the hybrid lattice, derived from the propagation of Gaussian noise through the CZ gates and the spatial arrangement of squeezed states, to perform a change-of-basis transformation on homodyne outcomes that isolates low-noise quadrature combinations. This allows for more accurate binning of the measurement data into discrete qubit outcomes, reducing the error rate in the syndrome extraction. The outer decoder then uses these refined outcomes, along with knowledge of the locations of squeezed states, to assign non-uniform weights to the edges of the matching graph, thereby improving the accuracy of the minimum-weight perfect matching algorithm and increasing the fault-tolerance threshold.

The invention achieves a fault-tolerance threshold for the hybrid RHG lattice that permits a swap-out probability of up to approximately 23.6% when ideal squeezing is assumed, and a maximum tolerable swap-out probability of approximately 13.3% at experimentally accessible squeezing levels of 15 dB. These thresholds are significantly higher than those achievable by prior architectures requiring deterministic GKP sources, and they enable a reduction in the number of Gaussian boson sampling devices by orders of magnitude. The architecture further enables the fastest clock speeds of any photonic quantum computing platform, as the measurement timescale is determined by homodyne detection bandwidth, which exceeds that of photon-number-resolving detectors by several orders of magnitude. The planar, modular design permits independent optimization of each subsystem—state generation, multiplexing, entanglement, and measurement—without requiring global reconfiguration or cryogenic integration of the entire system. This results in a quantum computing architecture that is not only theoretically fault-tolerant but also practically realizable with near-term photonic technology.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### INTRODUCTION

The foundational premise of this invention is that the path to scalable, fault-tolerant quantum computation lies not in the pursuit of perfectly deterministic sources of non-Gaussian quantum states, but in the intelligent integration of probabilistic state generation with structured noise correction. Traditional approaches to photonic quantum computing have been constrained by an implicit assumption: that any deviation from an ideal qubit state must be treated as an error to be erased or corrected through redundancy. This assumption has led to architectures that demand an exponentially growing number of parallel state generation devices to achieve near-deterministic operation, rendering them infeasible with current technology. The present invention overturns this paradigm by recognizing that the failure to generate a GKP qubit does not necessitate the loss of the mode’s utility in the computational lattice. Instead, the mode can be deterministically repurposed as a momentum-squeezed vacuum state—a Gaussian state that, while not encoding a logical qubit, retains the ability to participate in entanglement and contribute to the overall cluster state structure.

This insight is grounded in the observation that the entanglement operations required to build a measurement-based quantum computing resource state—namely, the controlled-Z gate—are Gaussian operations that can be applied identically to both GKP qubits and squeezed vacuum states. The resulting hybrid state, composed of alternating GKP and squeezed modes, forms a structured lattice where the presence of squeezed states introduces a predictable, correlated noise pattern that can be modeled and corrected. Unlike erasure errors, which remove information entirely and require complex lattice renormalization or topological reconfiguration, the substitution of squeezed states introduces a noise that is continuous, Gaussian, and spatially correlated, making it amenable to correction through analog processing of homodyne measurement outcomes. The invention exploits this property by developing a two-tier decoding protocol that first translates the noisy continuous measurements into discrete qubit outcomes using a correlation-aware inner decoder, and then applies a tailored outer decoder that leverages the known locations of squeezed states to assign non-uniform error probabilities to the stabilizer syndromes.

The architecture is designed to be fully compatible with the infrastructure of modern silicon photonics. All optical components—beam splitters, phase shifters, squeezers, delay lines, and homodyne detectors—are realizable on a planar chip using established fabrication techniques. The only requirement for cryogenic operation is the photon-number-resolving detectors used in the Gaussian boson sampling devices, which can be isolated on a separate chip and connected via low-loss optical fibers. The remainder of the system, including the multiplexing switches, the entanglement network, and the homodyne detection array, operates at room temperature, eliminating the need for massive, custom cryostats and enabling the use of commercial, high-speed electronic control systems. The temporal dimension of the lattice is implemented using optical delay lines whose length is matched to the clock period of the system, allowing for the sequential interaction of optical pulses in a manner that scales linearly with the number of qubits, rather than exponentially. This ensures that the optical depth experienced by each mode remains constant, preventing the exponential loss accumulation that plagues other photonic architectures.

The invention further distinguishes itself by its modularity. Each functional component—the state generation module, the multiplexing network, the entanglement layer, and the measurement unit—can be independently optimized, fabricated, and tested. This allows for incremental technological advancement: improvements in photon-number-resolving detector efficiency directly enhance the fidelity of GKP generation without requiring redesign of the computational lattice; advances in integrated squeezers improve the quality of the substituted squeezed states; and faster homodyne detectors enable higher clock speeds without altering the underlying quantum protocol. The architecture is therefore not a monolithic system but a modular framework that can evolve with the state of the art in photonic components. This design philosophy ensures long-term viability and scalability, as the system can incorporate future breakthroughs without requiring a complete architectural overhaul.

The mathematical foundation of the invention lies in the precise modeling of the hybrid lattice’s noise structure. The initial state of each mode is either a GKP qubit or a momentum-squeezed vacuum state, each subject to a Gaussian noise channel that broadens its phase-space distribution. The application of controlled-Z gates between neighboring modes correlates these noise distributions, resulting in a covariance matrix that encodes the spatial relationships between GKP and squeezed states. This covariance matrix is not a source of unmanageable noise but a source of information. The inner decoder uses this information to perform a linear transformation on the homodyne outcomes that diagonalizes the noise covariance, isolating the most reliable quadrature combinations for binning. This process effectively filters out the noise introduced by the squeezed states, allowing the system to recover the underlying qubit structure with high fidelity. The outer decoder then uses the spatial pattern of squeezed states to assign lower error probabilities to regions of the lattice where the noise is more structured and predictable, thereby improving the performance of the minimum-weight perfect matching algorithm. This two-tiered approach—correlation-aware binning followed by noise-structured decoding—constitutes the core innovation of the invention and enables fault-tolerant operation at squeezing levels and swap-out probabilities that were previously considered unattainable.

### Overview of the System Configuration

The system configuration of the present invention is organized into four distinct, physically separable modules that collectively enable fault-tolerant, universal quantum computation using a hybrid photonic resource state. Each module is designed to fulfill a specific functional role, with hardware requirements tailored to its task, thereby enabling independent optimization and scalable fabrication. The first module, the state-generation module, is responsible for producing the fundamental building blocks of the computational resource: Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) qubits and, when necessary, momentum-squeezed vacuum states. This module comprises an array of Gaussian boson sampling devices, each consisting of a set of squeezed vacuum inputs, a linear interferometer, and photon-number-resolving detectors. The squeezed vacuum states are prepared using integrated squeezers, and the interferometer is implemented using a network of beam splitters and phase shifters fabricated on a silicon photonic chip. The photon-number-resolving detectors, which are the only components requiring cryogenic operation, are positioned at the output ports of the interferometer to measure the photon number in all but one mode. The remaining unmeasured mode is heralded as a candidate GKP qubit if the detected photon number matches a predetermined pattern; otherwise, the mode is discarded.

The second module, the multiplexing module, receives the outputs of multiple state-generation devices and performs two critical functions: it routes successfully generated GKP qubits to a common output port, and it substitutes failed attempts with momentum-squeezed vacuum states. This is accomplished using a binary tree of optical switches, each capable of either passing the input signal unchanged (bar mode) or swapping it with a reference squeezed vacuum state (cross mode). The switches are controlled by classical logic that receives the photon-counting outcomes from the state-generation module. If a successful GKP qubit is heralded, the switch network routes that mode to the output; if no successful generation is detected, the switch network activates the cross mode to insert a momentum-squeezed vacuum state in its place. The multiplexing network is designed to operate at high repetition rates, with optical delay lines used to buffer the heralded outputs to allow sufficient time for the classical control system to determine the switching configuration. The multiplexing module ensures that the output stream consists of a continuous sequence of modes, each of which is either a GKP qubit or a momentum-squeezed vacuum state, with the probability of each determined by the success rate of the state-generation devices.

The third module, the computational module, takes the output stream from the multiplexing module and entangles the modes into a three-dimensional hybrid cluster state. This is achieved in two stages. First, the modes are fed into a time-domain cluster state generator, which consists of a single optical delay line and a sequence of controlled-Z gates implemented using beam splitters and squeezers. Each mode is coupled into the delay line, which is tuned to the clock period of the system, allowing the mode to interact with the next incoming mode via the controlled-Z gate. This process repeats for each mode in the sequence, generating a one-dimensional cluster state that extends in time. The second stage extends this structure into two spatial dimensions by arranging multiple such time-domain generators in a two-dimensional array. Additional state-generation and multiplexing modules are interspersed between the time-domain generators, with one set operating on even clock cycles and another on odd clock cycles. Controlled-Z gates are applied between adjacent time-domain generators during alternating clock cycles, creating a lattice structure that is periodic in both space and time. The resulting hybrid resource state is a (2+1)-dimensional Raussendorf-Harrington-Goyal (RHG) lattice, where each node is either a GKP qubit or a momentum-squeezed vacuum state, and the entanglement structure is preserved throughout the lattice.

The fourth and final module, the photonic quantum processing unit, performs the measurement-based quantum computation. This module comprises an array of homodyne detector cells, each consisting of a vertical coupler, a local oscillator channel, a fast phase shifter, a 50/50 beam splitter, and a pair of photodiodes. The homodyne detectors measure the quadrature amplitude of each mode in the lattice, with the measurement basis determined by the phase of the local oscillator. The output of each photodiode pair is subtracted to produce a balanced detection signal, suppressing noise from the local oscillator. This analog signal is amplified by a transimpedance amplifier and digitized by an analog-to-digital converter, producing a continuous measurement outcome for each mode at each clock cycle. These outcomes are transmitted to a classical controller, which processes the data using the inner and outer decoders to determine the next set of measurement settings and any necessary recovery operations. The controller then updates the phase shifters of the homodyne detectors in advance of the next clock cycle, ensuring that the measurement basis is correctly configured for the subsequent round of measurement. The classical controller also stores the measurement outcomes in memory for final decoding and output to the user. The entire system is designed to operate as a closed-loop feedback system, with measurement outcomes directly informing the next set of operations, enabling adaptive quantum computation.

The physical layout of the system is planar, with all optical components integrated on silicon photonic chips. The state-generation and multiplexing modules are fabricated on one chip, the computational module on another, and the photonic quantum processing unit on a third. These chips are interconnected via low-loss optical waveguides or fibers, with the only cryogenic component being the photon-number-resolving detectors, which are mounted on a separate cryogenic chip. The classical controller is implemented using a high-speed digital electronic system, such as a field-programmable gate array or application-specific integrated circuit, operating at room temperature. This modular, planar architecture enables scalable fabrication using standard semiconductor manufacturing techniques, eliminates the need for complex three-dimensional optical routing, and ensures that the system can be expanded to accommodate larger numbers of qubits without introducing exponential increases in optical loss or control complexity.

### Generation of Bosonic Qubits Using Multiplexed Gaussian Boson Sampling (“GBS”) Devices

The generation of bosonic qubits in the present invention is achieved through a multiplexed array of Gaussian boson sampling (GBS) devices, each of which produces a candidate Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) qubit with a probability determined by the fidelity of the input squeezed states, the design of the interferometer, and the detection efficiency of the photon-number-resolving detectors. Each GBS device begins with a set of momentum-squeezed vacuum states, which are prepared using integrated squeezers operating at a squeezing level of approximately 10 to 15 decibels. These squeezed states are then displaced using electro-optic modulators to introduce a coherent amplitude, and subsequently interfered in a linear optical network composed of a series of beam splitters and phase shifters. The interferometer is designed such that, under ideal conditions, the output state of the unmeasured mode would be a perfect GKP qubit. However, due to finite squeezing, imperfect mode overlap, and detector inefficiencies, the actual output is a finite-energy approximation of the ideal GKP state, characterized by a Wigner function composed of a periodic array of Gaussian peaks in phase space.

The key to the GBS process is the use of photon-number-resolving detectors to measure the photon number in all but one of the output modes. The detection of a specific photon number pattern—such as a sequence of even or odd photon counts across the measured modes—heralds the successful generation of a GKP qubit in the unmeasured mode. The probability of observing such a pattern is low, typically on the order of one percent or less for states with high fidelity, making the generation process inherently probabilistic. To overcome this limitation, the invention employs spatial multiplexing: multiple GBS devices are operated in parallel, each generating a candidate GKP qubit independently. The outputs of these devices are fed into a multiplexing network that routes the successfully heralded modes to a common output while discarding the failed attempts. The number of GBS devices required to achieve a desired success probability scales logarithmically with the inverse of the single-device success probability, making it feasible to achieve near-unit generation rates with a manageable number of devices.

In the event that none of the GBS devices successfully generates a GKP qubit, the multiplexing network does not leave the output mode empty or treat it as an erasure. Instead, it actively substitutes the failed output with a momentum-squeezed vacuum state, which is generated on-demand using a dedicated squeezed state source. This substitution is performed using a switch that, under the control of the classical logic system, selects between the output of the GBS multiplexer and the output of the squeezed state generator. The momentum-squeezed vacuum state is chosen because it is Gaussian, easily generated, and compatible with the entanglement operations of the computational module. Unlike an erasure, which removes the mode from the lattice and disrupts the entanglement structure, the substitution preserves the connectivity of the cluster state, ensuring that the lattice remains intact and that the subsequent homodyne measurements can still be interpreted in terms of the underlying qubit code.

The multiplexing network is implemented using a binary tree of 2×2 optical switches, each of which can be configured to either pass the input signal unchanged (bar mode) or swap it with a reference state (cross mode). The switches are realized using variable-transmissivity beam splitters or Mach-Zehnder interferometers with tunable phase shifters, allowing for high-speed switching at repetition rates exceeding the bandwidth of the photon-number-resolving detectors. To accommodate the slower readout times of the detectors, optical delay lines are inserted between the GBS devices and the multiplexing network, buffering the heralded outputs to provide sufficient time for the classical control system to determine the switching configuration. The delay lines are implemented using low-loss optical waveguides or fiber spools, with lengths on the order of meters to provide nanosecond-scale delays. Alternatively, time-to-space demultiplexing can be employed to step down the pulse repetition rate seen by the detectors, allowing a single detector to service multiple GBS devices by interleaving pulses from different sources in time.

The entire state-generation and multiplexing process is synchronized with the clock cycle of the computational module, ensuring that a continuous stream of modes—each either a GKP qubit or a momentum-squeezed vacuum state—is delivered to the computational module at a rate determined by the homodyne detection bandwidth. The probability of a mode being a GKP qubit is denoted as (1 - p₀), and the probability of it being a momentum-squeezed vacuum state is p₀. The value of p₀ is determined by the success rate of the GBS devices and the number of devices in the multiplexing array. For experimentally accessible squeezing levels of 15 dB, the invention demonstrates that p₀ can be as high as approximately 13.3% while still maintaining fault-tolerant operation, representing a dramatic reduction in the multiplexing overhead compared to prior architectures that require p₀ to be negligible. The ability to tolerate such a high swap-out probability is a direct consequence of the hybrid nature of the resource state and the correlation-aware decoding protocol, which together transform what would otherwise be a catastrophic failure into a manageable and correctable noise source.

### Time Domain Generation of 1D Clusters

The generation of the one-dimensional cluster state in the present invention is achieved through a time-domain optical circuit that sequentially entangles the output modes from the multiplexing module using a combination of optical delay lines and controlled-Z gates. This approach leverages the fact that the controlled-Z gate, which is the fundamental entangling operation required to construct a cluster state, can be implemented deterministically using linear optics and squeezing. The circuit begins with a stream of modes, each of which is either a Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) qubit or a momentum-squeezed vacuum state, delivered at a fixed repetition rate determined by the clock cycle of the system. The first mode is coupled into an optical delay line whose length is precisely tuned to the time interval between successive pulses. As the mode travels through the delay line, it is delayed by exactly one clock period, allowing it to recombine with the next incoming mode at a controlled-Z gate implemented using a beam splitter and a pair of squeezers.

The controlled-Z gate is realized by first applying a single-mode squeezer to each input mode, followed by a 50/50 beam splitter, and then a second single-mode squeezer. The squeezing parameters are chosen such that the resulting transformation corresponds to the unitary e^{i q₁ q₂}, which is the continuous-variable analog of the discrete-variable controlled-Z gate. The squeezed vacuum states used in this implementation are generated on-demand using integrated squeezers, and their squeezing level is chosen to match the overall noise budget of the system. After the interaction, the first mode is kicked out of the delay line by a switch that redirects it to the next stage of the system, while the second mode is coupled back into the delay line to interact with the third mode in the next clock cycle. This process repeats for each subsequent mode, with each mode interacting with its immediate predecessor in the sequence, thereby generating a one-dimensional cluster state that extends in time.

The use of a time-domain approach offers several critical advantages over spatial approaches. First, it eliminates the need for complex three-dimensional optical routing, as all entanglement operations occur along a single temporal axis. Second, it ensures that each mode traverses the same optical path and experiences the same optical depth, regardless of the total number of qubits in the system. This is in stark contrast to spatial architectures, where increasing the number of qubits requires longer delay lines or more complex interferometers, leading to exponential increases in optical loss. Third, the time-domain approach is inherently scalable: adding more qubits simply requires extending the duration of the computation, without altering the physical layout of the circuit. The only constraint on scalability is the bandwidth of the homodyne detectors, which determines the maximum clock rate and, consequently, the length of the delay line required to maintain the temporal separation between modes.

The optical delay line is implemented using a low-loss waveguide fabricated on a silicon photonic chip, with a length on the order of tens of meters to achieve the necessary delay at gigahertz clock rates. The phase stability of the delay line is critical, as any phase drift between successive pulses would introduce errors in the entanglement. To ensure phase stability, the waveguide is fabricated using materials with low thermal expansion coefficients and is enclosed in a temperature-controlled environment. The controlled-Z gates are implemented using integrated squeezers and beam splitters, which are fabricated using the same silicon photonics process as the delay line, ensuring compatibility and minimizing coupling losses. The entire circuit is designed to operate at room temperature, with no cryogenic components required beyond the photon-number-resolving detectors used in the state-generation module.

The output of the time-domain cluster state generator is a one-dimensional chain of entangled modes, each of which is either a GKP qubit or a momentum-squeezed vacuum state, with the entanglement structure preserved throughout the chain. This one-dimensional cluster state serves as the foundation for the subsequent extension into two spatial dimensions, as described in the following section. The temporal nature of the cluster state ensures that the system can be operated in a continuous, streaming fashion, with each new mode being generated, entangled, and measured in sequence, enabling a seamless and scalable implementation of measurement-based quantum computation.

### GKP Cluster in 2+1 Dimensions

The extension of the one-dimensional time-domain cluster state into a two-dimensional spatial lattice is achieved by arranging multiple time-domain cluster state generators in a planar array and connecting them via additional controlled-Z gates that entangle modes across the spatial dimension. This results in a (2+1)-dimensional hybrid cluster state, where the temporal dimension provides the sequential entanglement of modes, and the two spatial dimensions provide the lateral connectivity required for fault-tolerant quantum computation. The architecture is designed to replicate the structure of the Raussendorf-Harrington-Goyal (RHG) lattice, which is known to support universal measurement-based quantum computation with high fault-tolerance thresholds. The RHG lattice is composed of alternating primal and dual sheets of qubits, with each qubit entangled to its nearest neighbors in a manner that enables the measurement of stabilizer operators for a topological quantum error-correcting code.

In the present invention, the time-domain cluster state generators are arranged in a two-dimensional grid, with each generator producing a one-dimensional chain of modes that extends in the temporal direction. Adjacent generators are separated by a fixed spatial distance, and additional controlled-Z gates are placed between them to entangle modes from neighboring chains. To ensure that the entanglement is properly synchronized with the clock cycle, the system employs two interleaved sets of state-generation and multiplexing modules: one set operates on even clock cycles, and the other on odd clock cycles. The controlled-Z gates are similarly divided into two sets, with one set activated during even clock cycles and the other during odd clock cycles. This alternating pattern ensures that each mode interacts with its neighbors in a staggered fashion, producing the checkerboard-like connectivity pattern characteristic of the RHG lattice.

The spatial connectivity is implemented using a network of integrated beam splitters and squeezers, which are fabricated on the same silicon photonic chip as the time-domain cluster state generators. The beam splitters are arranged in a nearest-neighbor configuration, with each mode in a given time-domain chain entangled to its immediate neighbors in the adjacent chains. The squeezing parameters of the controlled-Z gates are carefully calibrated to match the overall noise budget of the system, ensuring that the entanglement is strong enough to support fault-tolerant computation while avoiding the introduction of excessive noise. The resulting hybrid cluster state is a three-dimensional lattice, with two spatial dimensions defined by the arrangement of the time-domain generators and the third dimension defined by the temporal progression of the optical pulses. Each node in the lattice is either a Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) qubit or a momentum-squeezed vacuum state, with the probability of each determined by the success rate of the multiplexed GBS devices.

The planar structure of the lattice is a critical feature of the invention, as it enables scalable fabrication using standard silicon photonics techniques. Unlike prior architectures that require complex three-dimensional optical routing or non-planar waveguide layouts, the present invention confines all optical components to a single plane, eliminating the need for vertical interconnects, through-silicon vias, or other fabrication challenges that are difficult to scale. The planar geometry also ensures that the noise introduced by the hybrid structure remains local, with each mode only interacting with its immediate neighbors. This local noise structure is essential for fault tolerance, as it ensures that errors do not propagate beyond a small neighborhood, allowing the error-correcting code to isolate and correct them efficiently.

The output of the computational module is a fully entangled (2+1)-dimensional hybrid cluster state, which is then fed into the photonic quantum processing unit for measurement-based quantum computation. The lattice is structured such that the GKP qubits serve as the primary carriers of logical quantum information, while the momentum-squeezed vacuum states act as noise sources that are explicitly accounted for in the decoding protocol. The spatial arrangement of the squeezed states is known to the classical controller, allowing the inner and outer decoders to exploit the correlation structure of the noise and improve the fidelity of the measurement outcomes. The resulting architecture is not only scalable and manufacturable but also robust to the probabilistic nature of GKP state generation, making it the first practical realization of a fault-tolerant photonic quantum computer.

### Generation of a Hybrid Raussendorf-Harrington-Goyal (“RHG”) Lattice

The generation of the hybrid Raussendorf-Harrington-Goyal (RHG) lattice constitutes the central achievement of the present invention, as it provides the structured, fault-tolerant resource state upon which universal quantum computation is performed. The RHG lattice is a three-dimensional arrangement of qubits that encodes a topological quantum error-correcting code, enabling the detection and correction of errors through the measurement of stabilizer operators that span multiple qubits. In the present invention, the RHG lattice is constructed from a hybrid resource state composed of Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) qubits and momentum-squeezed vacuum states, arranged in a manner that preserves the entanglement structure of the original RHG lattice while accommodating the probabilistic nature of GKP generation.

The lattice is formed by extending the one-dimensional time-domain cluster state into two spatial dimensions, as previously described, such that each node in the lattice corresponds to a single optical mode that has been entangled with its nearest neighbors via controlled-Z gates. The nodes are arranged in a cubic lattice, with each node connected to six neighbors: two in the temporal direction, two in one spatial direction, and two in the other spatial direction. The nodes are divided into two types: primal nodes and dual nodes, which alternate in a checkerboard pattern across the lattice. Primal nodes are associated with the primary data qubits of the code, while dual nodes serve as ancillary qubits used for stabilizer measurements. The entanglement structure of the lattice ensures that the stabilizer operators of the RHG code can be measured by performing homodyne measurements on subsets of the nodes.

The key innovation of the hybrid RHG lattice is that each node is not required to be a perfect GKP qubit. Instead, each node is either a GKP qubit, generated with probability (1 - p₀), or a momentum-squeezed vacuum state, generated with probability p₀. The substitution of squeezed states for failed GKP qubits does not disrupt the entanglement structure of the lattice, as the controlled-Z gate can be applied identically to both types of states. The resulting hybrid lattice retains the topological properties of the original RHG lattice, including its ability to support fault-tolerant computation through the measurement of stabilizer operators. However, the presence of squeezed states introduces a structured noise pattern that is correlated across neighboring nodes, as the squeezing in one mode induces a displacement in the momentum quadrature of its entangled neighbors.

This noise structure is not a flaw to be eliminated but a feature to be exploited. The known locations of the squeezed states are recorded by the classical controller, and this information is used to inform the decoding protocol. The inner decoder uses the covariance matrix of the hybrid lattice, which encodes the correlations between the noise in neighboring modes, to perform a change-of-basis transformation on the homodyne measurement outcomes. This transformation isolates low-noise quadrature combinations that are less affected by the squeezed states, allowing for more accurate binning of the measurement data into discrete qubit outcomes. The outer decoder then uses the spatial pattern of the squeezed states to assign non-uniform weights to the edges of the matching graph, reducing the likelihood of misidentifying correlated errors as independent ones. This two-tiered decoding protocol enables the system to tolerate a swap-out probability of up to approximately 13.3% at experimentally accessible squeezing levels of 15 dB, a threshold that is significantly higher than any prior photonic quantum computing architecture.

The hybrid RHG lattice is generated in a continuous, streaming fashion, with each new layer of the lattice being produced at each clock cycle. The lattice is not static but dynamic, with the measurement of one layer revealing the state of the next, enabling adaptive quantum computation. The planar, modular design of the system ensures that the lattice can be scaled to arbitrarily large sizes without introducing exponential increases in optical loss or control complexity. The result is a fault-tolerant quantum computing architecture that is not only theoretically sound but also practically realizable with near-term photonic technology.

### Passive Version of System Configuration

The present invention further encompasses a passive version of the system configuration, in which the active, in-line squeezing and displacement operations required to implement the controlled-Z gates are replaced by passive, pre-generated entanglement resources. In the active configuration, the controlled-Z gates are implemented using integrated squeezers and beam splitters that are dynamically activated during the computation. While this approach is feasible, it requires precise control over the squeezing parameters and introduces additional noise due to the finite bandwidth and imperfections of the squeezers. The passive version eliminates these challenges by pre-generating the entanglement structure of the lattice using a macro-node architecture, in which multiple modes are entangled offline and stored in optical delay lines before being released into the computational lattice.

In the passive configuration, the state-generation and multiplexing modules produce GKP qubits and momentum-squeezed vacuum states as before. However, instead of feeding these modes directly into the computational module, they are first routed into a set of macro-node generators, which are specialized optical circuits that entangle multiple modes into a single, pre-characterized entangled state. Each macro-node is designed to contain a small cluster of modes—typically four to six—that are entangled using a fixed network of beam splitters and squeezers. The resulting macro-node state is a Gaussian state that encodes the same entanglement structure as the original controlled-Z gates but without requiring active control during the computation. The macro-node states are then stored in optical delay lines, which are tuned to release the modes into the computational lattice at precisely timed intervals.

The passive configuration eliminates the need for active squeezing and displacement during the computation, as the entanglement is already embedded in the macro-node states. This reduces the complexity of the computational module, as it no longer requires high-speed squeezers or phase shifters. Instead, the computational module consists only of low-loss waveguides and beam splitters that route the macro-node states into the correct positions in the lattice. The homodyne detection and classical control systems remain unchanged, as they continue to measure the modes and decode the outcomes using the same two-tiered protocol.

The passive version of the system configuration offers several advantages over the active version. First, it reduces the noise introduced by the entanglement operations, as the macro-node states are generated offline under optimal conditions and are not subject to the dynamic fluctuations that occur during real-time operation. Second, it simplifies the fabrication of the computational module, as it eliminates the need for high-speed, tunable squeezers and phase shifters, which are difficult to integrate on silicon photonic chips. Third, it improves the scalability of the system, as the macro-node states can be generated in parallel and stored in delay lines, allowing for the simultaneous release of multiple entangled clusters into the lattice.

The passive configuration is particularly well-suited for large-scale implementations of the architecture, where the complexity of the active entanglement operations would become prohibitive. It also enables the use of alternative entanglement generation techniques, such as spontaneous parametric down-conversion or four-wave mixing, which are difficult to implement in real time but are well-suited for offline generation. The passive version of the system configuration thus represents a complementary approach to the active configuration, offering a path to even greater scalability and reliability in future implementations of the invention.

### 3D Hybrid Macronode Architecture

The 3D hybrid macronode architecture represents an advanced variant of the present invention, in which the fundamental unit of the computational lattice is not a single mode but a three-dimensional cluster of entangled modes known as a macronode. In this architecture, each macronode is a pre-generated, entangled state that contains multiple Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) qubits and momentum-squeezed vacuum states arranged in a compact, three-dimensional geometry. The macronodes are generated offline using specialized optical circuits that entangle a fixed number of modes using a network of beam splitters and squeezers, and are then stored in optical delay lines before being released into the computational lattice.

The macronode architecture transforms the hybrid RHG lattice from a collection of individual modes into a lattice of entangled clusters, each of which serves as a self-contained unit of computation. This approach offers several key advantages. First, it reduces the number of entanglement operations required during the computation, as the entanglement within each macronode is generated offline and does not need to be re-established during the computation. Second, it improves the fault tolerance of the system, as errors that occur within a macronode are confined to that cluster and do not propagate to neighboring macronodes. Third, it simplifies the control and measurement of the lattice, as the homodyne measurements are performed on entire macronodes rather than individual modes, reducing the number of measurement channels required.

The macronodes are arranged in a three-dimensional lattice, with each macronode connected to its neighbors via low-loss optical waveguides. The entanglement between macronodes is established using a network of beam splitters and phase shifters that are activated during the release of the macronodes into the lattice. The resulting structure is a hybrid RHG lattice composed of macronodes rather than individual modes, with the GKP qubits and squeezed states distributed within each macronode according to a predetermined pattern. The decoding protocol is modified to account for the internal structure of the macronodes, with the inner decoder now operating on the collective homodyne outcomes of each macronode rather than on individual modes.

The 3D hybrid macronode architecture offers a path to even greater scalability and reliability than the standard hybrid RHG lattice. By reducing the number of entanglement operations and confining errors to individual macronodes, the architecture significantly reduces the noise and complexity of the system. The passive generation of macronodes also eliminates the need for high-speed, tunable squeezers and phase shifters, making the system more compatible with mass-manufacturable photonic technologies. The macronode architecture thus represents a natural evolution of the invention, offering a path to large-scale, fault-tolerant quantum computation with even greater practicality and robustness.

## Technological Advantages

### Modularity

The modularity of the present invention is a defining feature that distinguishes it from all prior photonic quantum computing architectures. Unlike monolithic systems that require the simultaneous optimization of state generation, entanglement, and measurement, the present invention decomposes the quantum computing process into four distinct, physically separable modules: the state-generation module, the multiplexing module, the computational module, and the photonic quantum processing unit. Each module is designed to fulfill a specific functional role, with hardware requirements tailored to its task, enabling independent optimization, fabrication, and testing. This modular decomposition is not merely a convenience but a fundamental design principle that ensures long-term scalability and technological adaptability.

The state-generation module, which employs multiplexed Gaussian boson sampling devices, is the only component that requires cryogenic operation, as it relies on photon-number-resolving detectors based on transition-edge sensors or superconducting nanowires. These detectors are inherently low-temperature devices, and their integration into a cryogenic environment is a well-established engineering challenge that can be addressed using commercially available cryostats. The remainder of the system, including the multiplexing network, the computational lattice, and the homodyne detection array, operates entirely at room temperature. This separation of cryogenic and ambient-temperature components allows for the use of standard silicon photonics fabrication techniques for the majority of the system, eliminating the need for exotic materials or complex hybrid packaging.

The multiplexing module, which routes successfully generated GKP qubits and substitutes failed attempts with momentum-squeezed vacuum states, is implemented using a binary tree of optical switches that can be fabricated using standard electro-optic modulators or Mach-Zehnder interferometers. These components are compatible with room-temperature operation and can be manufactured using the same photonic integrated circuit processes as the delay lines and beam splitters. The computational module, which entangles the modes into a (2+1)-dimensional hybrid cluster state, consists of a planar array of controlled-Z gates implemented using integrated squeezers and beam splitters. These components are designed to operate at fixed parameters, eliminating the need for real-time reconfiguration and simplifying the fabrication process.

The photonic quantum processing unit, which performs homodyne measurements and feeds the outcomes to a classical controller, is implemented using a planar array of homodyne detector cells, each consisting of a vertical coupler, a local oscillator, a phase shifter, a beam splitter, and a pair of photodiodes. All of these components are standard in silicon photonics and can be fabricated using standard CMOS-compatible processes. The classical controller, which processes the measurement outcomes and computes the next measurement settings, is implemented using a high-speed digital electronic system such as a field-programmable gate array or application-specific integrated circuit, operating at room temperature.

This modular architecture enables incremental technological advancement. Improvements in photon-number-resolving detector efficiency directly enhance the fidelity of GKP generation without requiring redesign of the computational lattice. Advances in integrated squeezers improve the quality of the substituted squeezed states. Faster homodyne detectors enable higher clock speeds without altering the underlying quantum protocol. The system can be upgraded in stages, with each module evolving independently, ensuring that the architecture remains viable as photonic technology progresses. This modularity also enables parallel development: different teams can optimize state generation, multiplexing, entanglement, and measurement independently, accelerating the overall development timeline. The result is a quantum computing architecture that is not only scalable and fault-tolerant but also pragmatically realizable, with a clear path to commercialization and mass production.

### Minimal Cryogenic Requirements

The minimal cryogenic requirements of the present invention represent a transformative departure from the engineering constraints of competing quantum computing platforms. In superconducting, trapped-ion, and even many photonic architectures, the entire quantum processor must be maintained at cryogenic temperatures to preserve coherence, suppress thermal noise, and enable the operation of sensitive control electronics. This requirement imposes enormous engineering burdens, including the need for large, custom-built cryostats, complex thermal management systems, and high-power refrigeration units, all of which increase cost, reduce reliability, and limit scalability. The present invention circumvents these challenges by confining cryogenic operation to a single, isolated component: the photon-number-resolving detectors used in the Gaussian boson sampling devices.

All other components of the system—the multiplexing switches, the optical delay lines, the controlled-Z gates, the homodyne detectors, and the classical control electronics—operate at room temperature. This is made possible by the hybrid nature of the resource state, which replaces the need for deterministic GKP generation with a substitution protocol that uses momentum-squeezed vacuum states, which are Gaussian and easily generated at ambient conditions. The entanglement operations, which in other architectures require cryogenic control of qubit interactions, are implemented using linear optics and passive components that are inherently compatible with room-temperature operation. The homodyne detection system, which is the primary readout mechanism, relies on balanced photodiodes and local oscillators that are unaffected by thermal noise when operated at room temperature.

The cryogenic footprint of the system is therefore limited to the photon-number-resolving detectors, which can be mounted on a small, dedicated cryogenic chip and connected to the rest of the system via low-loss optical fibers. This allows for the use of compact, commercially available cryostats, such as those used in quantum communication and sensing applications, rather than the massive, custom-built systems required by other platforms. The cryogenic chip can be housed in a small, sealed enclosure with minimal thermal mass, reducing the power consumption and complexity of the cooling system. The optical fibers connecting the cryogenic chip to the ambient-temperature components are designed to minimize mode mismatch and insertion loss, ensuring that the heralded GKP qubits are delivered to the multiplexing network with high fidelity.

This minimal cryogenic requirement has profound implications for the scalability, cost, and reliability of the system. First, it eliminates the need for large-scale cryogenic infrastructure, reducing the capital and operational costs of the quantum computer by orders of magnitude. Second, it enhances system reliability by isolating the most sensitive components from the thermal and mechanical vibrations of the larger system. Third, it enables the use of standard, high-speed electronic control systems, which are incompatible with cryogenic environments, thereby simplifying the classical control architecture and improving the speed and accuracy of the feedback loop. The result is a quantum computing architecture that is not only theoretically fault-tolerant but also practically deployable, with a footprint and power consumption comparable to that of a high-performance computing server.

### Homodyne Detection Sets Timescales

The homodyne detection system in the present invention is not merely a measurement tool but the primary determinant of the system’s operational timescale, enabling clock speeds that are orders of magnitude faster than those of competing photonic and non-photonic quantum computing platforms. Unlike photon-number-resolving detectors, which are limited by the rise time of their output signals and the dead time between detection events, homodyne detectors operate by measuring the continuous quadrature amplitude of an optical field using balanced photodiodes and analog electronics. This allows for detection bandwidths exceeding tens of gigahertz, with commercial systems already achieving bandwidths of 10 GHz and beyond.

In the present invention, the clock cycle of the system is determined by the time between successive optical pulses as they traverse the optical delay lines and interact with the controlled-Z gates. The maximum repetition rate of the system is therefore limited by the bandwidth of the homodyne detectors, which must be able to resolve the quadrature measurement outcomes before the next pulse arrives. This means that the system can operate at clock rates of up to 10 GHz or higher, depending on the design of the homodyne detector cells and the speed of the analog-to-digital converters. This is in stark contrast to photon-number-resolving detectors, which are typically limited to repetition rates of a few megahertz, even in the most advanced systems.

The high clock speed enabled by homodyne detection has several critical advantages. First, it allows for the use of shorter optical delay lines, as the time between pulses is reduced. Shorter delay lines mean lower optical loss, as the attenuation of light in waveguides is proportional to the length of the path. This is a decisive advantage over prior photonic architectures, where long delay lines were required to match the slow detection rates of photon-number-resolving detectors, leading to exponential increases in loss and degradation of the quantum state. Second, the high clock speed enables faster feedback from the classical controller to the homodyne detectors, allowing for real-time correction of measurement outcomes and adaptive quantum computation. Third, it allows for the rapid execution of logical operations, reducing the total time required to perform a quantum computation and minimizing the accumulation of errors due to decoherence.

The homodyne detection system is implemented using a planar array of detector cells, each consisting of a vertical coupler, a local oscillator, a fast phase shifter, a 50/50 beam splitter, and a pair of photodiodes. The photodiodes are fabricated using silicon-germanium technology, which provides high responsivity and bandwidth. The phase shifter is implemented using a lithium niobate or silicon-based electro-optic modulator, which can be driven at gigahertz frequencies. The output of the photodiodes is subtracted to produce a balanced detection signal, suppressing noise from the local oscillator. This analog signal is amplified by a transimpedance amplifier and digitized by a high-speed analog-to-digital converter, producing a continuous stream of quadrature measurement outcomes that are fed to the classical controller.

The result is a quantum computing architecture whose speed is not constrained by the limitations of quantum state generation but by the speed of classical electronics. This is a profound shift in paradigm: rather than being limited by the slow, probabilistic generation of non-Gaussian states, the system is limited only by the bandwidth of the measurement system, which is a well-understood and rapidly advancing technology. The homodyne detection system thus transforms the quantum computer from a slow, laboratory-bound device into a high-speed, scalable computational engine capable of performing complex quantum algorithms in practical timeframes.
