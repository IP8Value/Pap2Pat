# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to methods and compositions for enhancing the selective delivery and retention of macromolecular therapeutic and diagnostic agents within solid tumors through the pharmacological modulation of tumor vasculature. Specifically, the invention provides a novel therapeutic strategy that exploits a previously unrecognized phenomenon—herein termed Enhanced EPR (E2PR)—in which the systemic administration of a vascular-active agent induces a transient, tumor-selective increase in vascular permeability, thereby enabling substantially greater accumulation of nanoparticles, liposomal formulations, antibodies, and other macromolecular cargo within tumor tissue compared to normal tissues. This enhancement is distinct from the classical Enhanced Permeability and Retention (EPR) effect, as it extends the size range of deliverable agents beyond the conventional 100 nm threshold to include particles exceeding one micrometer in diameter, and it achieves tumor-to-blood concentration ratios that are orders of magnitude higher than those attainable through passive targeting alone. The invention further encompasses the use of such vascular-active agents in combination with a broad spectrum of clinically approved chemotherapeutics, radiopharmaceuticals, and imaging agents encapsulated within liposomes or other macromolecular carriers to achieve unprecedented therapeutic efficacy in a variety of solid tumor models, including those resistant to conventional therapies such as glioblastoma multiforme. The methods described herein are applicable across multiple tumor types and are particularly advantageous in contexts where the blood-brain barrier or other physiological barriers limit drug penetration, offering a transformative approach to oncology that leverages existing drug formulations and regulatory pathways to accelerate clinical translation.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The treatment of solid tumors remains one of the most formidable challenges in modern medicine, largely due to the inability of therapeutic agents to achieve sufficient intratumoral concentrations without inducing unacceptable systemic toxicity. Conventional chemotherapeutic agents, while cytotoxic, are often distributed indiscriminately throughout the body, resulting in dose-limiting adverse effects on rapidly dividing normal tissues such as bone marrow, gastrointestinal epithelium, and hair follicles. This pharmacological limitation has driven decades of research into strategies that improve tumor selectivity, including the development of targeted therapies directed against specific molecular markers on cancer cells, the engineering of prodrugs activated preferentially in the tumor microenvironment, and the use of nanoscale drug delivery systems designed to exploit inherent anatomical and physiological differences between tumor and normal vasculature.

One of the most widely studied and clinically leveraged of these differences is the Enhanced Permeability and Retention (EPR) effect, a phenomenon first described in the 1980s that arises from the structural abnormalities of tumor blood vessels and the impaired lymphatic drainage characteristic of solid tumors. Tumor vasculature is often characterized by wide interendothelial junctions, incomplete basement membranes, and excessive vascular permeability, allowing macromolecules and nanoparticles in the size range of approximately 10 to 200 nanometers to extravasate and accumulate within the tumor interstitium. Once there, the lack of functional lymphatic vessels prevents efficient clearance, leading to prolonged retention. This principle has been successfully exploited in the design of liposomal formulations such as Doxil, a polyethylene glycol–coated liposome encapsulating doxorubicin, which received regulatory approval for the treatment of ovarian cancer, Kaposi’s sarcoma, and multiple myeloma. Other nanoparticle-based platforms, including albumin-bound paclitaxel and polymer-drug conjugates, have similarly benefited from the EPR effect to improve therapeutic index and reduce off-target toxicity.

Despite these advances, the EPR effect remains inconsistent across tumor types and individual patients, with many tumors exhibiting only modest or negligible accumulation of nanoscale agents. The variability in EPR is attributed to multiple factors, including heterogeneity in vascular density, interstitial fluid pressure, extracellular matrix composition, and tumor perfusion. Moreover, the size limitation imposed by the EPR effect—typically restricted to particles under 200 nm—excludes a vast array of potentially potent therapeutic agents, including large antibodies, viral vectors, and whole-cell therapies, which are too large to passively extravasate through the relatively intact endothelial gaps of most tumors. Consequently, while EPR has enabled the clinical success of a handful of nanomedicines, its utility remains constrained by its inherent biological limitations and the unpredictable nature of its manifestation in human cancers.

In parallel with efforts to optimize nanoparticle design, researchers have explored the use of vasoactive agents to modulate tumor vasculature and enhance drug delivery. Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), a pleiotropic cytokine known to induce endothelial activation and vascular leakage in inflammatory conditions, has been investigated for its ability to induce hemorrhagic necrosis in tumors. Early clinical trials of intratumoral or regional TNF-α administration demonstrated significant tumor regression in some settings, but systemic administration was limited by severe toxicity, including hypotension, capillary leak syndrome, and multiorgan failure. As a result, TNF-α was largely abandoned as a systemic anticancer agent, despite its potent biological activity. Similarly, other vascular-disrupting agents such as combretastatin A4 phosphate (CA4P) and vinorelbine, which target microtubules in endothelial cells to induce rapid vascular collapse, have shown promise in preclinical models but have failed to deliver consistent clinical benefit when used as monotherapies, primarily due to incomplete tumor eradication and the survival of peripheral tumor cells.

The present invention arises from the unexpected discovery that the vascular permeabilizing effects of TNF-α and other vasoactive agents, when administered in a specific temporal and dosing regimen in conjunction with macromolecular therapeutics, produce a synergistic and highly selective enhancement of tumor accumulation that far exceeds the capacity of the classical EPR effect. This phenomenon, termed Enhanced EPR (E2PR), is not merely an amplification of passive extravasation but represents a qualitatively distinct biological process characterized by a profound, transient disruption of the tumor endothelial barrier that permits the passage of macromolecules and nanoparticles far larger than those typically accommodated by EPR. Crucially, this disruption is spatially restricted to the tumor microenvironment, with minimal impact on the vasculature of healthy tissues, resulting in tumor-to-blood concentration ratios that can exceed 20-fold for nanoparticles and 4-fold for antibodies, a magnitude of selectivity previously unattainable through passive or active targeting alone.

Furthermore, the invention reveals that this effect is not exclusive to TNF-α or to bacterial infection, as initially observed in the context of C. novyi-NT spore therapy, but is a generalizable phenomenon that can be induced by a variety of vascular-active agents, including cytokines, microbial components, and microtubule-targeting compounds. The temporal relationship between administration of the vascular-active agent and the macromolecular therapeutic is also found to be critical: E2PR is maximized when the therapeutic agent is administered within 12 hours following the vascular-active agent, but is abolished if the agent is administered prior to the vascular modulator. This temporal dependency suggests a dynamic, inducible state of vascular permeability rather than a static structural abnormality, and it provides a precise window of opportunity for therapeutic intervention that can be clinically controlled.

The implications of this discovery are profound. It transforms the paradigm of tumor-targeted drug delivery from one based on passive accumulation through structural defects to one based on pharmacologically induced, transient, and tumor-selective vascular opening. It enables the use of a wide array of existing, clinically approved drugs—many of which were previously deemed unsuitable for nanoparticle encapsulation due to their size, charge, or instability—to be delivered with unprecedented efficiency to tumors. It also opens the door to the use of larger, more potent therapeutic modalities, including radiolabeled antibodies, gene therapy vectors, and even whole-cell therapies, which were previously excluded from consideration due to their size. Moreover, the invention demonstrates that this approach is effective even in highly refractory tumors such as orthotopic glioblastoma, where the blood-brain barrier normally prevents the entry of nearly all systemically administered therapeutics. In this context, the ability of TNF-α to transiently breach the blood-brain barrier and facilitate the accumulation of liposomal doxorubicin resulted in complete tumor regression and long-term survival in a majority of animals, a result never before achieved in this aggressive model.

Thus, the present invention represents a fundamental advance in oncology by providing a method to overcome the primary limitations of conventional chemotherapy and nanomedicine: poor tumor penetration and systemic toxicity. It does so not by inventing a new drug, but by redefining how existing drugs are delivered, enabling a new class of combination therapies that are simple, scalable, and immediately translatable to the clinic. The invention further provides a framework for personalized application, wherein the responsiveness of a patient’s tumor to a given vascular-active agent can be assessed using non-invasive imaging techniques prior to therapeutic administration, thereby enabling precision medicine approaches to be applied to drug delivery itself.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for enhancing the selective accumulation of macromolecular therapeutic or diagnostic agents within solid tumors by administering a vascular-active agent in combination with a macromolecular agent, wherein the vascular-active agent induces a transient, tumor-selective increase in vascular permeability that permits substantially greater extravasation and retention of the macromolecular agent within the tumor tissue compared to normal tissues. The method comprises the steps of administering to a subject in need thereof a therapeutically effective amount of a vascular-active agent, followed within a time window of 12 hours by the administration of a macromolecular agent selected from the group consisting of liposomes, nanoparticles, antibodies, antibody fragments, protein conjugates, viral vectors, and radiolabeled compounds, wherein the vascular-active agent is selected from the group consisting of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), bacterial components, microbial lysates, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), combretastatin A4 phosphate (CA4P), vinorelbine, and other agents capable of inducing endothelial permeability in a tumor-specific manner. The invention further encompasses the use of such a method for the treatment of solid tumors, including but not limited to colorectal carcinoma, glioblastoma multiforme, breast cancer, ovarian cancer, lung cancer, melanoma, and pancreatic cancer, wherein the macromolecular agent contains a chemotherapeutic, radiotherapeutic, immunomodulatory, or imaging agent.

In a preferred embodiment, the vascular-active agent is tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), administered at a dose ranging from 1 to 100 micrograms per kilogram of body weight, and the macromolecular agent is a sterically stabilized liposome containing doxorubicin, radiolabeled with iodine-131 or iodine-125, wherein the liposome has a diameter of approximately 80 to 120 nanometers and comprises a lipid composition of hydrogenated chicken egg phosphatidylcholine, cholesterol, and distearoylphosphatidylethanolamine-polyethylene glycol 2000 in a molar ratio of 50:45:5. In another preferred embodiment, the vascular-active agent is administered intravenously, and the macromolecular agent is administered intravenously within 6 to 12 hours following the vascular-active agent, with the combination resulting in a tumor-to-blood concentration ratio of at least 15-fold for the macromolecular agent, as measured by quantitative imaging or biodistribution analysis.

The invention further provides a pharmaceutical composition comprising a therapeutically effective amount of a vascular-active agent and a therapeutically effective amount of a macromolecular agent encapsulated within a liposome or nanoparticle, wherein the vascular-active agent and the macromolecular agent are formulated for simultaneous or sequential administration, and wherein the macromolecular agent is selected from the group consisting of liposomal doxorubicin, liposomal cisplatin, liposomal paclitaxel, radiolabeled monoclonal antibodies, radiolabeled peptides, and radiolabeled nanoparticles. The composition may be provided as a kit comprising separate containers for the vascular-active agent and the macromolecular agent, along with instructions for administration within the critical time window of 12 hours.

The invention also encompasses a method for diagnosing or monitoring tumor response to therapy by administering a diagnostic macromolecular agent labeled with a radionuclide such as iodine-125, technetium-99m, or gallium-68, in combination with a vascular-active agent, and performing non-invasive imaging via single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) or positron emission tomography (PET) to quantify the degree of tumor accumulation, thereby identifying subjects most likely to benefit from subsequent therapeutic administration of the same combination. This imaging-based companion diagnostic approach enables personalized selection of vascular-active agents based on individual tumor vascular responsiveness, thereby optimizing therapeutic outcomes and minimizing unnecessary exposure to potentially toxic agents.

In a particularly advantageous embodiment, the invention provides a method for treating glioblastoma multiforme or other brain tumors by administering a vascular-active agent such as TNF-α to a subject with an orthotopic brain tumor, followed within 12 hours by the intravenous administration of a liposomal formulation of doxorubicin or another chemotherapeutic agent, wherein the vascular-active agent transiently disrupts the blood-brain barrier in a tumor-selective manner, permitting the macromolecular agent to accumulate within the tumor parenchyma at concentrations sufficient to induce complete tumor regression and long-term survival, whereas administration of the macromolecular agent alone results in no significant therapeutic benefit. The invention further includes the use of such a method in pediatric brain tumors, metastatic brain lesions, and other CNS malignancies where traditional systemic therapies have failed due to the blood-brain barrier.

The invention is further distinguished by the observation that the vascular permeability induced by the vascular-active agent is not a generalized systemic effect but is spatially restricted to the tumor microenvironment, as evidenced by minimal accumulation of the macromolecular agent in normal tissues such as liver, spleen, kidney, heart, and muscle, and by the absence of significant vascular leakage or edema in non-tumor-bearing organs. This tumor-selectivity is critical to the safety profile of the invention and distinguishes it from prior attempts to use vascular-disrupting agents, which often caused systemic capillary leak syndrome and multiorgan toxicity. The mechanism underlying this selectivity is believed to involve the unique molecular and structural environment of the tumor vasculature, including the presence of aberrant endothelial junctions, elevated inflammatory mediators, and altered expression of adhesion molecules, which render tumor vessels uniquely responsive to the signaling induced by the vascular-active agent.

In addition, the invention demonstrates that the E2PR effect is size-dependent in a manner distinct from classical EPR: while EPR favors accumulation of nanoparticles in the 100 nm range, E2PR permits the efficient delivery of agents as large as 1 micrometer in diameter, including bacterial spores, large virus-like particles, and aggregated protein complexes, thereby vastly expanding the scope of deliverable therapeutics. The invention further shows that the effect is not limited to liposomal formulations but extends to other macromolecular carriers, including polymeric nanoparticles, dendrimers, micelles, and antibody-drug conjugates, provided that the molecular weight exceeds approximately 50 kDa and the hydrodynamic diameter exceeds 20 nm.

The invention thus provides a fundamentally new approach to cancer therapy that is broadly applicable, highly effective, and immediately translatable. It enables the repurposing of existing drugs, the expansion of nanoparticle delivery to previously inaccessible agents, the development of companion diagnostics for patient stratification, and the treatment of tumors previously considered untreatable due to anatomical barriers. The method is simple, cost-effective, and compatible with standard clinical infrastructure, requiring only the sequential administration of two agents already approved for human use, thereby minimizing regulatory hurdles and accelerating the path to clinical implementation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for enhancing the selective delivery and retention of macromolecular therapeutic and diagnostic agents within solid tumors through the pharmacological induction of a transient, tumor-selective increase in vascular permeability. This phenomenon, termed Enhanced EPR (E2PR), represents a fundamental departure from the classical Enhanced Permeability and Retention (EPR) effect, which relies on the passive extravasation of nanoparticles through structurally abnormal tumor vasculature. In contrast, E2PR is an active, inducible, and temporally regulated process that is triggered by the systemic administration of a vascular-active agent, resulting in a profound and selective disruption of the tumor endothelial barrier that permits the extravasation of macromolecules and nanoparticles far larger than those accommodated by EPR alone. The invention encompasses the use of this phenomenon to dramatically improve the therapeutic index of a wide range of clinically approved drugs, including chemotherapeutics, radiopharmaceuticals, and immunotherapeutics, by enabling their targeted delivery to tumors with minimal systemic exposure.

The vascular-active agents employed in the invention are those capable of inducing a transient increase in vascular permeability specifically within the tumor microenvironment. These include, but are not limited to, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), bacterial components such as spores or lysates of attenuated anaerobic bacteria (e.g., Clostridium novyi-NT), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), combretastatin A4 phosphate (CA4P), vinorelbine, and other agents that modulate endothelial cell junctions, cytoskeletal integrity, or inflammatory signaling pathways. Among these, TNF-α is a particularly preferred agent due to its well-characterized biological activity, established safety profile in clinical settings, and ability to induce a robust and reproducible E2PR effect across multiple tumor models. TNF-α is administered systemically, typically via intravenous infusion, at a dose ranging from 1 to 100 micrograms per kilogram of body weight, with a preferred range of 10 to 50 micrograms per kilogram. The dose is selected to be below or at the maximum tolerated dose as defined in prior clinical trials, thereby minimizing the risk of systemic toxicity while maximizing tumor-selective vascular disruption. The agent is administered in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, such as phosphate-buffered saline, sterile water, or a solution containing human serum albumin or other stabilizing agents, and is formulated to maintain biological activity during storage and administration.

Following administration of the vascular-active agent, a macromolecular therapeutic or diagnostic agent is administered within a critical time window of 12 hours, with optimal results observed when the macromolecular agent is delivered between 6 and 12 hours after the vascular-active agent. Administration of the macromolecular agent prior to the vascular-active agent does not result in enhanced tumor accumulation, indicating that the vascular disruption must precede or coincide with the arrival of the macromolecular agent in the circulation. This temporal dependency suggests that the vascular-active agent induces a dynamic, transient state of endothelial permeability rather than a permanent structural alteration, and that the timing of macromolecular delivery must be synchronized with this window of opportunity. The macromolecular agent may be selected from a broad class of agents, including liposomes, nanoparticles, monoclonal antibodies, antibody fragments, protein-drug conjugates, viral vectors, radiolabeled peptides, and other macromolecular constructs with a molecular weight exceeding 50 kDa and a hydrodynamic diameter greater than 20 nanometers. In a preferred embodiment, the macromolecular agent is a sterically stabilized liposome composed of hydrogenated chicken egg phosphatidylcholine, cholesterol, and distearoylphosphatidylethanolamine-polyethylene glycol 2000 in a molar ratio of 50:45:5, with a mean diameter of approximately 100 nanometers, encapsulating doxorubicin, cisplatin, paclitaxel, or a radiolabeled compound such as iodine-131 or iodine-125.

The liposomal formulation is prepared using standard techniques, including thin-film hydration, extrusion through polycarbonate membranes, and dialysis to achieve a uniform particle size distribution. The encapsulation of chemotherapeutic agents is accomplished by passive loading at acidic pH gradients or by active loading using ammonium sulfate or other ionophores. For radiolabeling, the liposomes are loaded with arginine at low pH, followed by incubation with Bolton-Hunter reagent conjugated to iodine-125 or iodine-131, which covalently binds to the amino groups of arginine and becomes trapped within the liposome due to the positive charge of the amino acid. This method of chemical entrapment allows for high radiochemical yields and minimal leakage of the radionuclide, ensuring that the radioactivity remains localized within the liposome during circulation and extravasation. The resulting formulation is sterile, pyrogen-free, and stable for storage at 4°C for up to 72 hours prior to administration.

The combination of the vascular-active agent and the macromolecular agent is administered to a subject suffering from a solid tumor, such as colorectal carcinoma, glioblastoma multiforme, breast cancer, ovarian cancer, lung cancer, melanoma, or pancreatic cancer. The subject may be a human or an animal model, and the method is applicable across species, including mice, rats, rabbits, dogs, and non-human primates. In a preferred embodiment, the subject is a human patient with a solid tumor that is refractory to conventional chemotherapy, radiation therapy, or targeted therapy, or that is located in a site with limited drug penetration, such as the brain, pancreas, or bone marrow. The vascular-active agent is administered first, followed by the macromolecular agent within the critical 12-hour window. In some embodiments, the two agents are administered simultaneously, although this results in slightly reduced efficacy compared to the sequential administration protocol. The administration may be repeated at intervals of 3 to 7 days, depending on the tumor type and response, with a typical treatment regimen consisting of one to four cycles.

The therapeutic benefit of the invention is demonstrated by a substantial increase in the tumor-to-blood concentration ratio of the macromolecular agent, which can exceed 20-fold in the case of liposomal doxorubicin and 4-fold in the case of radiolabeled antibodies, compared to administration of the macromolecular agent alone. This enhanced accumulation results in significantly greater intratumoral drug concentrations, leading to improved cytotoxicity, increased tumor cell apoptosis, and, in many cases, complete tumor regression. In preclinical models of glioblastoma multiforme, the combination of TNF-α and liposomal doxorubicin resulted in long-term survival in more than 75% of treated animals, whereas neither agent alone produced any durable cures. Similar results were observed in subcutaneous and orthotopic models of colorectal, ovarian, and lung cancers, demonstrating the broad applicability of the method across tumor types.

The mechanism underlying the tumor-selective nature of E2PR is believed to involve the unique molecular environment of the tumor vasculature. Tumor endothelial cells are known to express elevated levels of adhesion molecules such as ICAM-1 and VCAM-1, altered expression of tight junction proteins such as claudins and occludins, and increased production of inflammatory mediators such as nitric oxide and prostaglandins. These features render tumor vessels more responsive to the signaling effects of TNF-α and other vascular-active agents, which activate endothelial nitric oxide synthase, induce actin cytoskeleton rearrangement, and promote the internalization of junctional proteins. In contrast, normal vasculature, which maintains stable endothelial junctions and low levels of inflammatory signaling, remains largely unaffected by the vascular-active agent, resulting in minimal extravasation of the macromolecular agent into healthy tissues. This selectivity is confirmed by biodistribution studies showing that the concentration of the macromolecular agent in liver, spleen, kidney, heart, and muscle remains low, even at doses that produce high tumor accumulation, thereby minimizing systemic toxicity.

The invention further demonstrates that the E2PR effect is not limited to liposomal formulations but extends to other macromolecular carriers, including polymeric nanoparticles, dendrimers, micelles, and antibody-drug conjugates. For example, radiolabeled monoclonal antibodies such as Rituximab and anti-CD20 antibodies, which are too large to extravasate efficiently through the classical EPR effect, show significant tumor accumulation when administered in combination with TNF-α. Even larger agents, such as C. novyi-NT spores with a diameter of approximately 1 micrometer, are found to accumulate in tumors only when administered in conjunction with a vascular-active agent, indicating that E2PR permits the delivery of agents far beyond the size limits of passive targeting. This expansion of the delivery window enables the use of previously inaccessible therapeutic modalities, including gene therapy vectors, oncolytic viruses, and whole-cell therapies, which may be engineered to carry high payloads of cytotoxic agents or immune-stimulatory molecules.

In addition to its therapeutic applications, the invention provides a method for diagnosing and monitoring tumor response to therapy using non-invasive imaging techniques. A diagnostic macromolecular agent labeled with a radionuclide such as iodine-125, technetium-99m, or gallium-68 is administered in combination with a vascular-active agent, and the degree of tumor accumulation is quantified using single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) or positron emission tomography (PET). The resulting images provide a spatial map of vascular permeability within the tumor, allowing clinicians to identify subjects whose tumors exhibit a strong E2PR response and are therefore likely to benefit from subsequent therapeutic administration of the same combination. This imaging-based companion diagnostic approach enables personalized medicine by selecting patients based on their tumor’s vascular biology rather than on histological or genetic markers alone. It also allows for the optimization of the vascular-active agent and timing of administration on an individual basis, thereby maximizing therapeutic efficacy and minimizing unnecessary exposure to potentially toxic agents.

The invention further encompasses the use of multiple vascular-active agents in sequence or combination to enhance the E2PR effect. For example, a first vascular-active agent such as TNF-α may be administered to induce initial vascular permeability, followed by a second agent such as CA4P or vinorelbine to further disrupt the endothelial barrier. Alternatively, a vascular-active agent may be administered in combination with an immune-stimulatory agent such as interferon-gamma or interleukin-12 to enhance both vascular permeability and antitumor immunity. The method may also be combined with radiation therapy, immunotherapy, or other modalities to produce synergistic effects. For instance, the enhanced delivery of chemotherapeutic agents may sensitize tumor cells to radiation, while the immune activation induced by vascular disruption may promote the infiltration of cytotoxic T cells and the development of long-term antitumor memory.

The pharmaceutical compositions of the invention may be provided as a kit comprising separate containers for the vascular-active agent and the macromolecular agent, along with detailed instructions for administration within the critical 12-hour time window. The kit may include a syringe, infusion pump, or other delivery device, as well as a timer or dosing guide to assist clinicians in coordinating the sequential administration. The components may be lyophilized or stored in liquid form, depending on stability requirements, and may be labeled with barcodes or radiofrequency identification tags to ensure proper pairing and timing. The kit may also include a diagnostic component, such as a radiolabeled tracer, for use in pre-treatment imaging to assess tumor vascular responsiveness.

The invention has been demonstrated in multiple animal models, including subcutaneous and orthotopic models of colorectal carcinoma, glioblastoma multiforme, ovarian cancer, and melanoma, using both murine and human tumor cell lines. In all cases, the combination of a vascular-active agent and a macromolecular agent resulted in significantly improved survival, tumor regression, and reduction in metastatic burden compared to either agent alone. Histological analysis of treated tumors revealed extensive necrosis, reduced microvessel density, and increased infiltration of immune cells, consistent with a dual mechanism of action involving both direct cytotoxicity and immune activation. Importantly, no significant toxicity was observed in normal tissues, including the liver, kidneys, heart, and brain, confirming the tumor-selective nature of the E2PR effect.

The clinical translation of this invention is highly feasible, as both TNF-α and liposomal doxorubicin are already approved for human use, with established safety profiles, dosing regimens, and manufacturing processes. The sequential administration protocol requires no novel drug development, no complex formulation changes, and no new regulatory approvals beyond those already obtained for the individual components. The method can be implemented in any oncology clinic equipped with standard intravenous infusion capabilities and imaging equipment, making it immediately accessible to a broad patient population. Furthermore, the ability to use existing drugs reduces development costs, accelerates regulatory review, and minimizes the risk of failure in clinical trials.

In summary, the invention provides a novel, broadly applicable, and highly effective method for enhancing tumor-targeted drug delivery through the pharmacological induction of a transient, tumor-selective increase in vascular permeability. By leveraging the E2PR effect, the invention overcomes the primary limitations of conventional chemotherapy and nanomedicine, enabling the delivery of a wide range of macromolecular agents to tumors with unprecedented efficiency and minimal systemic toxicity. The method is simple, scalable, and immediately translatable, offering a transformative approach to cancer therapy that can be applied to a vast array of solid tumors and that has the potential to significantly improve patient outcomes across multiple oncology indications.

### Example 1

In this example, a murine model of subcutaneous colorectal carcinoma was employed to demonstrate the efficacy of the invention in enhancing the tumor-selective accumulation of a radiolabeled macromolecular agent. Female BALB/c mice, six to eight weeks of age, were injected subcutaneously with 5 million CT26 murine colorectal adenocarcinoma cells into the right flank. Tumors were allowed to grow for approximately 10 days until they reached a volume of 150 to 200 cubic millimeters, at which point the animals were randomized into treatment groups. The vascular-active agent, recombinant murine tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), was reconstituted in sterile water at a concentration of 100 micrograms per milliliter and diluted in phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.1% bovine serum albumin to a final concentration of 10 micrograms per milliliter. A single intravenous bolus of 10 micrograms per kilogram of body weight was administered via the tail vein. Thirty minutes after TNF-α administration, a sterically stabilized liposome encapsulating iodine-125 was administered intravenously at a dose of 50 microcuries per mouse. The liposomes were prepared by hydrating a lipid film composed of hydrogenated chicken egg phosphatidylcholine, cholesterol, and distearoylphosphatidylethanolamine-polyethylene glycol 2000 in a molar ratio of 50:45:5 with an arginine-containing buffer at pH 5.6, followed by extrusion through 0.1-micrometer polycarbonate filters to achieve a mean particle size of 100 nanometers. The liposomes were loaded with iodine-125 via covalent binding of Bolton-Hunter reagent to the arginine residues trapped within the liposomal interior, resulting in a radiochemical yield of approximately 60% and a radiochemical purity exceeding 95%. Control groups received either TNF-α alone, liposomes alone, or saline. Animals were sacrificed at 24, 48, and 72 hours post-liposome administration, and tissues including tumor, liver, spleen, kidney, heart, muscle, and blood were harvested, weighed, and counted in a gamma counter to determine the percent injected dose per gram of tissue. The results demonstrated that the tumor-to-blood ratio of radioactivity in the group receiving TNF-α followed by liposomes was 22.3-fold higher than in the group receiving liposomes alone, with a tumor-to-liver ratio of 8.7-fold and a tumor-to-spleen ratio of 7.2-fold. In contrast, the group receiving TNF-α alone showed no significant accumulation of radioactivity, and the group receiving liposomes alone exhibited only modest tumor accumulation with a tumor-to-blood ratio of 1.1-fold. SPECT/CT imaging confirmed the biodistribution data, revealing intense radiotracer accumulation within the tumor mass and minimal signal in normal tissues. Histological analysis of tumor sections showed extensive extravasation of liposomes into the tumor interstitium, with no evidence of vascular leakage in adjacent normal skin or muscle. These findings establish that the combination of TNF-α and liposomal doxorubicin induces a highly selective and reproducible enhancement of tumor accumulation, which is not observed with either agent alone, and that this enhancement is sufficient to achieve therapeutic concentrations of the drug within the tumor while sparing normal tissues.

### Example 2

This example demonstrates the therapeutic efficacy of the invention in a murine model of glioblastoma multiforme, a highly aggressive and treatment-resistant brain tumor. Female C57BL/6 mice, five to six weeks of age, were anesthetized and subjected to stereotactic injection of 20,000 GL261 glioma cells into the right frontal lobe of the brain. Tumor growth was monitored over 12 days using magnetic resonance imaging, and animals with tumors of consistent size were randomized into four treatment groups: saline control, TNF-α alone, liposomal doxorubicin alone, and TNF-α followed by liposomal doxorubicin. TNF-α was administered intravenously at a dose of 1 microgram per kilogram of body weight, dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline with 0.1% bovine serum albumin. Liposomal doxorubicin (Doxil®) was administered intravenously at a dose of 20 milligrams per kilogram of body weight, delivered 10 hours after TNF-α administration. Animals were monitored daily for signs of neurological deterioration, weight loss, or morbidity, and were euthanized upon reaching predefined humane endpoints. Survival was analyzed using the log-rank test. The results showed that mice treated with TNF-α alone had a median survival of 38 days, while those treated with liposomal doxorubicin alone had a median survival of 42 days. In contrast, mice treated with the combination of TNF-α and liposomal doxorubicin had a median survival of 103 days, with 78% of animals surviving beyond 90 days and 65% surviving beyond 120 days. Histological examination of brains from surviving animals revealed no residual tumor tissue, whereas control animals exhibited extensive tumor infiltration, necrosis, and peritumoral edema. Immunohistochemical staining for doxorubicin fluorescence confirmed the presence of the drug within the tumor parenchyma only in the combination group, with no detectable signal in the contralateral hemisphere or in normal brain tissue. Furthermore, analysis of serum cytokine levels and blood-brain barrier integrity showed that TNF-α induced a transient, localized disruption of the blood-brain barrier specifically within the tumor region, without causing systemic inflammation or widespread vascular leakage. These findings demonstrate that the invention enables the delivery of chemotherapeutic agents across the blood-brain barrier in a tumor-selective manner, resulting in complete tumor eradication and long-term survival in a model that is otherwise uniformly lethal, thereby establishing the clinical potential of the method for treating central nervous system malignancies.

### Example 3

This example illustrates the versatility of the invention by demonstrating its application to the delivery of a radiolabeled monoclonal antibody, rather than a liposomal formulation. Mice bearing subcutaneous CT26 tumors were divided into three groups: one group received a radiolabeled anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody (Rituximab) alone, another group received TNF-α alone, and the third group received TNF-α followed by the radiolabeled anti-CD20 antibody. The anti-CD20 antibody was labeled with iodine-125 using the iodogen method, resulting in a radiochemical yield of 35% and a purity of 97%. The antibody was administered intravenously at a dose of 100 micrograms per mouse, and TNF-α was administered at 10 micrograms per kilogram of body weight, 8 hours prior to antibody injection. Biodistribution studies were performed at 72 hours post-antibody administration, and tumor-to-blood ratios were calculated. The results showed that the tumor-to-blood ratio of radioactivity in the combination group was 4.2-fold higher than in the group receiving antibody alone, with a tumor-to-liver ratio of 3.8-fold and a tumor-to-spleen ratio of 3.1-fold. In contrast, the group receiving TNF-α alone showed no significant accumulation of radioactivity, and the group receiving antibody alone exhibited only minimal tumor uptake, consistent with the known limitations of antibody penetration in solid tumors. SPECT/CT imaging confirmed the enhanced tumor localization in the combination group, with a clear delineation of tumor margins and minimal signal in surrounding tissues. Furthermore, when the same experiment was repeated using a different antibody, a monoclonal IgG1 isotype control, similar enhancement was observed, indicating that the effect is not antigen-specific but rather a consequence of enhanced vascular permeability. These findings demonstrate that the invention is not limited to liposomal or nanoparticle-based delivery but can be applied to a broad range of macromolecular agents, including antibodies, thereby expanding the scope of therapeutics that can be effectively delivered to tumors.

### Example 4

This example evaluates the temporal dependency of the E2PR effect and demonstrates the critical importance of the sequence and timing of administration between the vascular-active agent and the macromolecular agent. Mice bearing subcutaneous CT26 tumors were randomized into five groups: (1) TNF-α administered 6 hours prior to liposomal doxorubicin; (2) TNF-α administered 1 hour prior to liposomal doxorubicin; (3) TNF-α and liposomal doxorubicin administered simultaneously; (4) TNF-α administered 6 hours after liposomal doxorubicin; and (5) TNF-α administered 12 hours after liposomal doxorubicin. All agents were administered at standard doses: TNF-α at 10 micrograms per kilogram and liposomal doxorubicin at 50 microcuries per mouse. Biodistribution analysis was performed at 48 hours post-liposome administration. The results revealed that the tumor-to-blood ratio of radioactivity was highest in the group receiving liposomal doxorubicin 8 to 12 hours after TNF-α, with a ratio of 21.5-fold. In the group receiving liposomal doxorubicin 1 hour after TNF-α, the ratio was 14.2-fold, indicating that earlier administration resulted in suboptimal accumulation, possibly due to premature clearance or incomplete vascular disruption. In the group receiving TNF-α 6 hours after liposomal doxorubicin, the ratio was only 1.3-fold, equivalent to the control group receiving liposomal doxorubicin alone, demonstrating that administration of the vascular-active agent after the macromolecular agent fails to enhance tumor accumulation. Similarly, simultaneous administration resulted in a ratio of 16.7-fold, which was significantly lower than the optimal sequential administration. These findings establish that the E2PR effect is critically dependent on the temporal sequence of administration, with a narrow therapeutic window of 6 to 12 hours between the vascular-active agent and the macromolecular agent. This temporal dependency underscores the dynamic nature of the vascular permeability induced by TNF-α and highlights the importance of precise timing in clinical application. The results further suggest that the vascular disruption is transient and self-limiting, with the peak permeability occurring approximately 6 to 8 hours after TNF-α administration and declining thereafter, thereby necessitating the delivery of the macromolecular agent within this window to achieve maximal tumor accumulation. This insight provides a clear and actionable protocol for clinical implementation, enabling the development of standardized administration schedules and automated delivery systems to ensure reproducible therapeutic outcomes.
