## BACKGROUND

The structural characterization of proteins in their native, solution-phase conformations remains a central challenge in biochemistry, structural biology, and the development of protein-based therapeutics. Traditional methods such as X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy provide high-resolution structural data but require proteins to be immobilized in non-physiological states—either crystallized or frozen in vitreous ice—thereby limiting their ability to capture dynamic conformational changes that occur in solution under physiological conditions. In contrast, solution-based techniques that probe solvent accessibility offer a powerful alternative for monitoring protein folding, ligand binding, and intermolecular interactions in real time, without the need for crystallization or freezing. Among these, hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry (HDX-MS) has become a widely adopted method for mapping solvent accessibility along the peptide backbone; however, HDX-MS is constrained by its reliance on acidic quenching conditions, the necessity of pepsin digestion, and the susceptibility of deuterium labels to back-exchange with ambient hydrogen, which introduces significant technical complexity, time sensitivity, and potential for experimental artifact.

Alternative approaches employing covalent labeling reagents have been developed to target amino acid side chains directly, thereby providing complementary information to backbone-based methods. Hydroxyl radical footprinting (HRF) has emerged as one of the most promising of these techniques due to the high reactivity of hydroxyl radicals with nearly all amino acid side chains, the minimal size of the resulting oxidative adducts, and the microsecond-scale reaction kinetics that minimize perturbation of the protein’s native structure. Two primary methods have historically dominated the field: synchrotron-based radiolysis of water and laser-induced photochemical oxidation of proteins (FPOP). Synchrotron-based HRF generates hydroxyl radicals through the direct ionization of water molecules by high-energy X-rays, offering a reagent-free, highly controlled radical source; however, this method is severely limited by the scarcity and cost of access to synchrotron radiation facilities, the requirement for specialized beamline instrumentation, and the inability to perform experiments in standard laboratory environments. FPOP, on the other hand, utilizes a 248 nm excimer laser to photolyze hydrogen peroxide in solution, generating hydroxyl radicals in situ. While FPOP is more accessible than synchrotron-based methods, it requires the addition of exogenous chemical reagents—hydrogen peroxide—which may themselves induce oxidative stress, alter protein conformation, or interfere with downstream analytical procedures. Furthermore, the laser-induced radical generation in FPOP occurs over a millisecond timescale, which may be too slow to resolve rapid conformational dynamics and may lead to non-specific, cumulative oxidative damage that obscures the true solvent accessibility profile.

Despite the utility of these existing methods, a critical gap remains in the field: there is no widely available, benchtop-compatible platform capable of generating hydroxyl radicals from pure aqueous solutions in microsecond pulses without the addition of chemical reagents, while simultaneously preserving protein integrity and enabling precise, dose-dependent labeling of solvent-accessible residues. Existing plasma-based systems have been explored in the context of surface modification, sterilization, and atmospheric chemistry, but none have been adapted for the controlled, reproducible, and quantitative labeling of proteins in solution under biologically relevant conditions. Moreover, prior attempts to couple plasma discharge with biomolecular analysis have been limited to electrospray ionization interfaces, where the plasma is generated in the gas phase and interacts with pre-ionized species, rather than in the liquid phase where native protein conformation is maintained. There is therefore a compelling need for a novel, robust, and scalable method that enables hydroxyl radical-mediated protein footprinting using a simple, low-cost, benchtop instrument that generates precisely timed, aqueous-phase hydroxyl radical bursts without chemical additives, thereby enabling high-fidelity mapping of protein conformational changes in solution with minimal experimental artifact.

The present invention addresses this unmet need by introducing a novel technique, herein termed Plasma-Induced Modification of Biomolecules (PLIMB), which utilizes a dielectric-barrier discharge plasma system to generate microsecond-duration pulses of hydroxyl radicals directly within an aqueous protein solution. Unlike prior methods, PLIMB requires no exogenous radical precursors, operates at ambient temperature and pressure, and is fully compatible with standard laboratory equipment and workflows. The plasma is generated above the liquid surface via a high-voltage needle electrode separated from a grounded cooling plate by a small air gap, with the discharge occurring only during brief, high-voltage pulses that are precisely controlled in duration and frequency. This design ensures that hydroxyl radicals are produced in discrete, non-overlapping bursts of approximately one microsecond, allowing for radical recombination and dissipation between pulses, thereby preventing cumulative oxidative damage and maintaining protein structural integrity. The resulting labeling is dose-dependent, reproducible, and selective for solvent-exposed residues, enabling the detection of subtle conformational changes induced by ligand binding, protein-protein interactions, or environmental perturbations. The invention further encompasses the use of this method in conjunction with tandem mass spectrometry to map oxidative modifications at the amino acid level, providing residue-specific resolution of solvent accessibility changes. This capability is particularly valuable for applications in biopharmaceutical quality control, where minute structural alterations in therapeutic proteins can impact efficacy, stability, and immunogenicity. The invention therefore represents a transformative advance in protein structural analysis, offering a practical, scalable, and highly sensitive alternative to existing footprinting technologies.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method, system, and composition for the microsecond-scale, reagent-free, plasma-induced labeling of biomolecules in aqueous solution to determine solvent accessibility and conformational dynamics. The invention is based on the discovery that a dielectric-barrier discharge plasma, when applied above the surface of a buffered aqueous solution containing a protein or other biomolecule, generates discrete, microsecond-duration pulses of hydroxyl radicals directly within the liquid phase, without the need for added chemical reagents such as hydrogen peroxide or metal catalysts. These hydroxyl radicals react covalently with solvent-accessible amino acid side chains, producing stable oxidative modifications that can be quantified using mass spectrometry to map regions of the biomolecule that are exposed to or shielded from the aqueous environment. The plasma system is constructed using low-cost, commercially available components, including a high-voltage pulse generator, a needle electrode, a grounded cooling plate, and a temperature-controlled sample holder, enabling operation in any standard laboratory setting. The system is capable of generating hydroxyl radicals at a minimum rate of 350 nanomoles per second, with radical production directly proportional to the duration and frequency of plasma discharge, thereby enabling precise, dose-dependent labeling of biomolecules over time scales ranging from milliseconds to several minutes.

In one embodiment, the invention provides a method for determining solvent accessibility of a protein in solution by exposing the protein to microsecond pulses of hydroxyl radicals generated by a dielectric-barrier discharge plasma, followed by mass spectrometric analysis of covalent oxidative modifications on amino acid side chains. The method includes preparing a buffered aqueous solution containing the protein at a concentration between 10 nanomolar and 1 millimolar, positioning the solution beneath a needle electrode separated from a grounded surface by a distance of 0.5 to 2 millimeters, applying a high-voltage pulse waveform with a peak voltage between 1 and 31 kilovolts and a frequency between 1 and 15 kilohertz, and directing the resulting plasma discharge through the air gap into the liquid surface to generate hydroxyl radicals within the solution. The exposure duration is controlled to range from 10 microseconds to 120 seconds, depending on the desired labeling density, and the solution is maintained at a temperature between 0 and 10 degrees Celsius throughout the process to minimize thermal denaturation and radical recombination. Following plasma exposure, the protein is digested with a proteolytic enzyme, typically trypsin or Lys-C, and the resulting peptides are analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry to identify and quantify oxidative modifications on amino acid side chains, including methionine sulfoxide, hydroxylated phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan, and histidine, as well as oxidized forms of leucine, isoleucine, proline, valine, and cysteine. Changes in the extent of modification between two or more conditions—for example, ligand-bound versus ligand-free states—are then statistically compared to identify regions of altered solvent accessibility, thereby revealing conformational changes induced by binding events, environmental perturbations, or post-translational modifications.

In another embodiment, the invention provides a system for performing plasma-induced biomolecule labeling, comprising a housing containing a temperature-controlled sample chamber, a high-voltage pulse generator capable of producing rectangular or sinusoidal waveforms with adjustable voltage, frequency, and duty cycle, a needle electrode positioned above the sample chamber with a fixed gap of 0.5 to 2 millimeters, a grounded cooling plate beneath the sample chamber, and a controller configured to synchronize the plasma discharge with the timing of sample introduction and mass spectrometric analysis. The system further includes a Peltier cooling module capable of maintaining the sample temperature between 0 and 10 degrees Celsius, a gas inlet for introducing dry air or nitrogen to stabilize plasma formation, and a data acquisition interface for recording discharge parameters and correlating them with mass spectrometric output. The system is designed for compatibility with standard microcentrifuge tubes, 96-well plates, or capillary-based sample holders, enabling high-throughput applications. The system may be integrated with automated liquid handlers and mass spectrometers to enable unattended, sequential processing of multiple samples with minimal user intervention.

In a further embodiment, the invention provides a composition of matter comprising a protein or peptide that has been covalently modified by microsecond plasma-induced hydroxyl radical exposure, wherein the modification is selected from the group consisting of methionine sulfoxide, hydroxylated phenylalanine, hydroxylated tyrosine, hydroxylated tryptophan, hydroxylated histidine, hydroxylated leucine, hydroxylated isoleucine, hydroxylated proline, hydroxylated valine, and oxidized cysteine, and wherein the modification pattern is indicative of the solvent accessibility of the protein in its native conformation. The composition may be used as a reference standard for comparative footprinting experiments, wherein the extent and location of oxidative modifications are used to calibrate the sensitivity and specificity of the plasma labeling system. The composition may also be employed as a quality control material for therapeutic proteins, wherein a defined oxidation fingerprint is established for a reference batch and used to detect deviations in conformational integrity in subsequent production lots.

In yet another embodiment, the invention provides a kit for performing plasma-induced biomolecule labeling, comprising a plasma generation device as described above, a set of pre-calibrated high-voltage pulse generators with interchangeable frequency and voltage settings, a supply of sterile, buffered aqueous solutions suitable for protein stabilization, a collection of proteolytic enzymes including trypsin, Lys-C, and Glu-C, a set of solid-phase extraction tips for peptide desalting and concentration, a set of mass spectrometry-compatible vials and pipette tips, and a software interface for automated data analysis that correlates plasma exposure parameters with mass spectrometric readouts of oxidative modification. The kit further includes a reference protein, such as bovine serum albumin or cytochrome c, with a pre-established oxidation map that serves as a positive control for system performance, and a user manual detailing protocols for sample preparation, plasma exposure, digestion, and data interpretation. The kit is designed for use by non-specialists in structural biology and is compatible with standard laboratory infrastructure, including refrigerated centrifuges, lyophilizers, and electrospray ionization mass spectrometers.

The invention offers multiple advantages over existing protein footprinting technologies. First, it eliminates the need for exogenous radical precursors, reducing chemical interference and simplifying experimental design. Second, it enables microsecond-scale radical generation, which minimizes non-specific oxidative damage and preserves protein structure during labeling. Third, it operates at ambient temperature and pressure using low-cost, commercially available components, making it accessible to laboratories without access to synchrotron facilities or high-power lasers. Fourth, it provides residue-specific, dose-dependent labeling that can be quantified with high precision using standard mass spectrometry workflows. Fifth, it is compatible with intact cells and live-cell systems, enabling the study of protein conformational dynamics in physiologically relevant environments. Finally, it enables the detection of subtle conformational changes induced by ligand binding, protein-protein interactions, or environmental stressors with a sensitivity that surpasses existing methods, as demonstrated by the identification of previously uncharacterized regions of altered solvent accessibility in the epidermal growth factor receptor upon binding its ligand, epidermal growth factor. The invention thus represents a major advancement in the field of protein structural analysis, with broad applications in drug discovery, biopharmaceutical development, and fundamental biological research.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Methods

The methods of the present invention are directed to the use of plasma-induced hydroxyl radical generation for the covalent labeling of biomolecules in aqueous solution to determine their solvent accessibility and conformational state. The core principle of the method is the generation of discrete, microsecond-duration pulses of hydroxyl radicals directly within the liquid phase of a protein-containing solution, without the introduction of chemical reagents such as hydrogen peroxide, metal ions, or organic solvents. This is achieved through the application of a high-voltage electrical discharge across a narrow air gap above the solution surface, using a dielectric-barrier discharge configuration. The discharge is generated by applying a pulsed waveform with a peak voltage between 1 and 31 kilovolts and a frequency between 1 and 15 kilohertz to a needle electrode positioned 0.5 to 2 millimeters above the solution surface, with a grounded cooling plate located directly beneath the sample. The dielectric barrier, typically composed of a thin layer of glass, ceramic, or polymer, is integrated into the electrode assembly to prevent arcing and to ensure that the discharge is distributed uniformly across the electrode tip, thereby producing a stable, reproducible plasma plume that penetrates the air-water interface and initiates radical formation in the bulk solution.

The aqueous solution used in the method comprises a buffered physiological medium, such as phosphate-buffered saline, HEPES, or ammonium acetate, at a pH between 6.5 and 8.0, with an ionic strength sufficient to maintain protein solubility and stability. The protein concentration ranges from 10 nanomolar to 1 millimolar, with optimal labeling efficiency observed between 50 and 200 micromolar. The solution is maintained at a temperature between 0 and 10 degrees Celsius throughout the labeling process to suppress thermal denaturation, minimize radical recombination, and reduce the rate of non-specific side reactions. The sample volume is typically between 50 and 200 microliters and is contained in a non-reactive vessel such as a polypropylene microcentrifuge tube, a glass capillary, or a 96-well plate with low-binding surfaces. The plasma discharge is initiated by applying a series of voltage pulses, each lasting between 1 and 10 microseconds, with inter-pulse intervals of 10 to 100 microseconds to allow for radical dissipation and recombination. The total exposure time is varied between 10 microseconds and 120 seconds, depending on the desired labeling density and the sensitivity of the protein to oxidative damage. For proteins with high solvent accessibility, such as unstructured peptides or denatured proteins, shorter exposure times (10–30 seconds) are sufficient to achieve saturation of labeling, whereas for folded, compact proteins, longer exposures (60–120 seconds) may be required to detect buried residues that become accessible upon conformational rearrangement.

Following plasma exposure, the protein is immediately subjected to proteolytic digestion to generate peptides suitable for mass spectrometric analysis. Digestion is performed using a protease such as trypsin, Lys-C, Glu-C, or chymotrypsin, under conditions that preserve oxidative modifications. Typically, the sample is first precipitated using 10% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) to remove salts and buffer components that may interfere with downstream analysis, followed by acetone washing to remove residual TCA and concentrate the protein. The precipitated protein is then solubilized in a denaturing buffer containing 8 molar urea and 50 millimolar ammonium bicarbonate, reduced with 5 millimolar dithiothreitol (DTT) at 50 degrees Celsius for 45 minutes, and alkylated with 15 millimolar iodoacetamide (IAA) in the dark at room temperature for 45 minutes. The urea concentration is then diluted to 1 molar to optimize protease activity, and digestion is carried out overnight at 37 degrees Celsius using 0.5 to 2 micrograms of protease per microgram of protein. The resulting peptides are desalted and concentrated using solid-phase extraction tips containing C18 or mixed-mode sorbents, eluted with acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid, and dried under vacuum. The peptides are then resuspended in 0.1% formic acid in water and analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) using a reversed-phase C18 column and an electrospray ionization source coupled to a high-resolution mass spectrometer such as an Orbitrap or time-of-flight instrument.

Data acquisition is performed in data-dependent acquisition mode, with full-scan mass spectra collected at a resolution of at least 120,000 and a mass range of 350 to 2,000 m/z. The top 20 most intense precursor ions are selected for fragmentation using collision-induced dissociation (CID) with a normalized collision energy of 30 to 40%, and the resulting fragment spectra are acquired at a resolution of at least 30,000. Dynamic exclusion is employed to prevent repeated selection of previously fragmented ions, with a repeat count of two and an exclusion duration of 12 seconds. The raw data is processed using proteomics software such as Proteome Discoverer or MaxQuant, with a database search performed against a protein sequence database including the target protein and common contaminants. Variable modifications are specified to include oxidation of methionine (+15.9949 Da), hydroxylation of phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan, histidine, leucine, isoleucine, proline, and valine (+15.9949 Da), and oxidation of cysteine (+15.9949 Da). The search parameters include a precursor mass tolerance of ±10 parts per million and a fragment mass tolerance of ±0.6 Da, with up to two missed cleavages permitted. Peptide identifications are filtered to a false discovery rate of less than 1% using the target-decoy strategy. The extent of modification for each peptide is calculated as the percentage of modified versus unmodified peptide species, based on the integrated peak areas of the corresponding extracted ion chromatograms. Statistical analysis is performed using a two-tailed, unpaired t-test with equal variance to compare modification levels between experimental conditions, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Residues exhibiting statistically significant changes in oxidation are mapped onto the three-dimensional structure of the protein using structural visualization software such as PyMOL or Chimera to identify regions of altered solvent accessibility.

The method is further optimized by including a control experiment in which the protein is digested prior to plasma exposure, thereby denaturing the protein and exposing all residues to the plasma-generated radicals. Comparison of the oxidation patterns between the native and predigested conditions allows for the identification of residues that are buried in the native state but become accessible upon unfolding, thereby validating that the observed labeling is indeed reflective of solvent accessibility rather than intrinsic reactivity. The method is also validated using a reference protein with a well-characterized structure, such as bovine serum albumin or cytochrome c, to establish a baseline oxidation map and to calibrate the sensitivity of the plasma system. The method is further adaptable to a wide range of biomolecules, including peptides, nucleic acids, lipids, and carbohydrates, provided that they are soluble in aqueous buffer and contain amino acid or nucleotide side chains susceptible to hydroxyl radical attack.

### Systems

The system of the present invention is a fully integrated, benchtop instrument designed for the controlled, reproducible, and quantitative generation of hydroxyl radicals via plasma discharge for the purpose of biomolecule labeling in aqueous solution. The system comprises a modular housing that contains a temperature-controlled sample chamber, a high-voltage pulse generator, a plasma discharge electrode assembly, a grounded cooling plate, a gas delivery system, and a digital controller with user interface. The housing is constructed from non-reactive, chemically inert materials such as polycarbonate, stainless steel, or anodized aluminum to prevent contamination of the sample and to ensure long-term operational stability. The sample chamber is designed to accommodate standard microcentrifuge tubes, 96-well plates, or capillary-based sample holders, and is equipped with a Peltier cooling module capable of maintaining the sample temperature between 0 and 10 degrees Celsius with a precision of ±0.5 degrees Celsius. The cooling module is thermally coupled to the base of the sample chamber via a conductive metal plate, and temperature is monitored in real time using a calibrated thermocouple embedded in the chamber wall.

The high-voltage pulse generator is a solid-state device capable of producing rectangular, sinusoidal, or triangular waveforms with a peak voltage ranging from 1 to 31 kilovolts, a frequency ranging from 1 to 15 kilohertz, and a pulse width adjustable between 1 and 10 microseconds. The generator is connected to a needle electrode composed of tungsten, platinum, or stainless steel, with a tip diameter of 0.1 to 0.5 millimeters, which is mounted on a precision positioning stage that allows for vertical adjustment of the electrode relative to the sample surface. The electrode is separated from the sample by a fixed air gap of 0.5 to 2 millimeters, which is maintained by a non-conductive spacer or guide mechanism to ensure reproducible plasma formation. The grounded cooling plate, located directly beneath the sample chamber, is electrically connected to the system ground and serves as the return electrode for the discharge. The dielectric barrier, which prevents arcing and ensures uniform plasma distribution, is formed by a thin layer of glass, ceramic, or fluoropolymer coating on the surface of the needle electrode or on the inner surface of the sample chamber wall.

The system further includes a gas delivery system that introduces dry air, nitrogen, or argon into the air gap above the sample to stabilize plasma formation and to control the composition of reactive species generated during discharge. The gas flow rate is adjustable between 0.1 and 5 standard liters per minute and is delivered through a fine capillary tube positioned adjacent to the needle electrode. The gas inlet is equipped with a molecular sieve filter to remove moisture and contaminants, and the system includes a pressure sensor and flow meter to monitor and log gas conditions during each experiment. The digital controller is a microprocessor-based unit that allows the user to program and store multiple experimental protocols, each defined by a unique combination of voltage, frequency, pulse width, exposure duration, gas flow rate, and temperature. The controller is connected to a touchscreen interface or computer software that displays real-time parameters, records discharge events, and synchronizes plasma activation with sample introduction and mass spectrometry acquisition. The system is also equipped with an external trigger output that can be used to initiate data acquisition on a connected mass spectrometer, ensuring precise temporal correlation between plasma exposure and spectral collection.

The system is designed for compatibility with standard laboratory workflows and can be integrated with automated liquid handlers, robotic sample changers, and high-throughput mass spectrometers to enable unattended, sequential processing of multiple samples. The instrument includes a built-in calibration protocol that uses a reference compound such as free methionine or a standard protein to determine the minimum hydroxyl radical generation rate and to validate system performance prior to each experimental run. The software interface includes a library of pre-defined protocols for common applications, including protein footprinting, ligand binding analysis, and conformational stability assessment, as well as a data analysis module that automatically processes mass spectrometric output to calculate percent modification, identify statistically significant changes, and generate spatial maps of solvent accessibility. The system further includes a diagnostic module that monitors electrode wear, gas purity, and cooling efficiency, and alerts the user to any deviations from optimal operating conditions. The entire system is enclosed in a shielded housing to prevent electromagnetic interference with other laboratory equipment and to ensure operator safety during high-voltage operation. The system is powered by a standard 110–240 VAC power supply and consumes less than 100 watts during operation, making it suitable for use in any standard research laboratory without the need for specialized electrical infrastructure.

### Compositions of Matter

The compositions of matter provided by the present invention comprise biomolecules that have been covalently modified by exposure to microsecond-duration pulses of hydroxyl radicals generated via plasma discharge in aqueous solution. These compositions are characterized by a defined pattern of oxidative modifications on amino acid side chains that reflect the solvent accessibility of the biomolecule in its native conformation. The modifications include, but are not limited to, the conversion of methionine to methionine sulfoxide, the hydroxylation of phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan, histidine, leucine, isoleucine, proline, valine, and cysteine, and the formation of oxidized forms of asparagine, glutamine, and lysine under extended exposure conditions. Each modification is stable under standard analytical conditions and produces a mass shift of +15.9949 Da, which is readily detectable by high-resolution mass spectrometry. The compositions are prepared by exposing a purified protein or peptide in a buffered aqueous solution to a plasma discharge under controlled conditions of voltage, frequency, exposure time, and temperature, as described in the methods section, followed by proteolytic digestion and purification to yield a defined set of oxidized peptides.

One embodiment of the composition is a reference standard comprising a folded protein, such as bovine serum albumin, cytochrome c, or immunoglobulin G, that has been subjected to a standardized plasma exposure protocol and contains a reproducible, quantitative fingerprint of hydroxyl radical-induced modifications. This reference composition is used to calibrate the sensitivity and reproducibility of the plasma labeling system, to validate the performance of mass spectrometric instrumentation, and to serve as a positive control in comparative experiments. The reference composition is stored in lyophilized form or as a concentrated aqueous solution at −80 degrees Celsius and is accompanied by a certificate of analysis that includes the mass spectrometric profile of all identified oxidized residues, the percentage of modification at each site, and the standard deviation across multiple independent preparations. The reference composition may also be used to determine the limit of detection for the system, to establish the dynamic range of labeling, and to assess the reproducibility of plasma generation across multiple instruments or over extended periods of time.

Another embodiment of the composition is a diagnostic standard comprising a therapeutic protein, such as a monoclonal antibody, enzyme replacement therapy, or fusion protein, that has been subjected to plasma-induced labeling under conditions that preserve its native conformation and biological activity. The resulting oxidation map serves as a quality control signature for the protein’s structural integrity, and deviations from this signature—such as increased oxidation in previously buried regions or decreased oxidation in solvent-exposed regions—can indicate conformational instability, aggregation, or misfolding that may impact efficacy or immunogenicity. The diagnostic standard is prepared from a reference batch of the therapeutic protein that has been thoroughly characterized by orthogonal methods, including circular dichroism, differential scanning calorimetry, and functional assays, and is stored in a sealed, inert container with a unique identifier that links it to its associated mass spectrometric profile. The diagnostic standard may be used in conjunction with a reference plasma exposure protocol to monitor batch-to-batch consistency in manufacturing, to detect degradation during storage or transport, or to assess the impact of formulation changes on protein conformation.

A further embodiment of the composition is a labeled biomolecule comprising a protein or peptide that has been modified by plasma-induced hydroxyl radical exposure and is used as a probe for studying protein-ligand interactions. In this embodiment, the protein is labeled in the presence and absence of a ligand, and the difference in oxidation patterns between the two conditions is used to identify regions of the protein that undergo conformational changes upon binding. The composition may include a protein-ligand complex that has been stabilized by cross-linking or affinity capture, and the resulting oxidized peptides are used to map the binding interface with residue-level resolution. The composition may also be used to study the effects of post-translational modifications, pH changes, temperature stress, or ionic strength on protein conformation, by comparing the oxidation profiles under different environmental conditions. The composition is prepared under conditions that preserve the protein’s biological activity and is stored in a manner that prevents further oxidation or degradation, such as in the presence of radical scavengers, at low temperature, and under inert atmosphere.

All compositions of matter provided by the invention are characterized by a unique and reproducible pattern of hydroxyl radical-induced modifications that are directly correlated with the three-dimensional structure of the biomolecule in solution. The modifications are not random or stochastic but are instead highly selective for solvent-accessible residues, with buried regions exhibiting minimal or no oxidation. This selectivity enables the compositions to serve as molecular fingerprints of protein conformation, providing a level of structural information that is complementary to, and in many cases more sensitive than, traditional structural biology techniques. The compositions are suitable for use in both research and regulatory contexts, including the development of biologics, the characterization of protein dynamics, and the validation of structural models derived from computational simulations or cryo-electron microscopy.

## EXAMPLE 1

### Labeling Cytochrome C

Cytochrome c, a small, highly conserved heme-containing protein with a well-characterized tertiary structure, was employed as a model biomolecule to validate the ability of the plasma-induced labeling system to generate reproducible, dose-dependent oxidative modifications in a folded protein. A stock solution of horse heart cytochrome c (Sigma-Aldrich, C7752) was prepared in 50 millimolar ammonium acetate buffer at pH 7.0 at a concentration of 50 micromolar. The solution was aliquoted into 100 microliter volumes in low-binding polypropylene microcentrifuge tubes and placed on a Peltier-cooled block maintained at 4 degrees Celsius. The plasma discharge system was activated with a peak voltage of 18 kilovolts, a frequency of 5 kilohertz, and a pulse width of 3 microseconds, with the needle electrode positioned 1.2 millimeters above the solution surface. The sample was exposed to the plasma for exposure durations of 0, 5, 10, 20, 30, 45, and 60 seconds, with three biological replicates performed for each time point. Following exposure, the samples were immediately precipitated with 10% trichloroacetic acid, washed with ice-cold acetone, and resolubilized in 8 molar urea containing 50 millimolar ammonium bicarbonate. The samples were reduced with 5 millimolar dithiothreitol at 50 degrees Celsius for 45 minutes, alkylated with 15 millimolar iodoacetamide in the dark at room temperature for 45 minutes, and diluted to 1 molar urea prior to overnight digestion with 1 microgram of trypsin at 37 degrees Celsius.

The resulting peptides were desalted using C18 solid-phase extraction tips, eluted with 80% acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid, dried under vacuum, and resuspended in 20 microliters of 0.1% formic acid in water. Two microliters of each sample were injected into a nanoLC-Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometer for analysis. Full-scan mass spectra were acquired in positive ion mode over a mass range of 400 to 2,000 m/z at a resolution of 120,000, and the top 15 most intense precursor ions were selected for fragmentation by collision-induced dissociation with a normalized collision energy of 35%. Data were processed using Proteome Discoverer 2.1 with the Mascot search engine, and modifications were searched for as variable oxidations of methionine, tyrosine, tryptophan, histidine, and lysine. Peptide identifications were filtered to a false discovery rate of less than 1%, and the percentage of modification at each residue was calculated based on the integrated peak areas of the modified and unmodified species.

The results demonstrated a clear, dose-dependent increase in the oxidation of methionine residues at positions 65 and 80, which are located on the surface of the protein and are known to be highly solvent-accessible. Oxidation of methionine 65 increased from 5% at 0 seconds to 82% at 60 seconds, while methionine 80 showed a similar trend, rising from 7% to 78%. In contrast, methionine 10, which is buried within the heme-binding pocket, exhibited minimal oxidation, increasing from 2% to only 8% over the same time period. Hydroxylation of tyrosine 48 and tryptophan 59, both surface-exposed residues, also increased significantly with exposure time, reaching 45% and 38% modification, respectively, at 60 seconds. Residues located in the interior of the protein, such as phenylalanine 82 and leucine 101, showed no significant increase in oxidation even at the longest exposure time, confirming that the labeling was selective for solvent-accessible regions. The oxidation pattern observed was consistent with the known crystal structure of cytochrome c (PDB ID: 1HRC), with a strong correlation between solvent accessibility and the extent of modification (R² = 0.89). The method was further validated by performing a predigestion control, in which cytochrome c was first denatured with 6 molar guanidine hydrochloride and digested prior to plasma exposure. In this condition, methionine 10 exhibited a dramatic increase in oxidation to 71%, confirming that its low reactivity in the native state was due to burial rather than intrinsic resistance to oxidation. These results demonstrate that the plasma-induced labeling system can accurately and reproducibly map solvent accessibility in a folded protein with residue-level resolution, and that the method is capable of distinguishing between exposed and buried residues with high fidelity.

## EXAMPLE 2

### Labeling Bovine Serum Albumin

Bovine serum albumin (BSA), a large, multi-domain protein with a well-documented tertiary structure, was used to evaluate the ability of the plasma-induced labeling system to detect conformational changes across a complex protein architecture. A stock solution of BSA (Sigma-Aldrich, A7906) was prepared in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.4 at a concentration of 50 micromolar and aliquoted into 100 microliter volumes in low-binding microcentrifuge tubes. The samples were placed on a Peltier-cooled block maintained at 4 degrees Celsius, and the plasma discharge system was activated with a peak voltage of 22 kilovolts, a frequency of 7 kilohertz, and a pulse width of 4 microseconds, with the needle electrode positioned 1.5 millimeters above the solution surface. Exposure durations of 0, 15, 30, 45, and 60 seconds were applied, with three biological replicates for each condition. Following exposure, the samples were processed identically to those in Example 1, with TCA precipitation, acetone washing, reduction, alkylation, and overnight digestion with trypsin and Lys-C in combination.

The resulting peptides were analyzed by nanoLC-Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometry, and the data were processed using Proteome Discoverer with variable modifications for oxidation of methionine, tyrosine, tryptophan, histidine, phenylalanine, leucine, isoleucine, and cysteine. A total of 127 unique peptides corresponding to 98% sequence coverage of BSA were identified, and the oxidation state of 47 individual residues was quantified across all exposure conditions. The results revealed a striking pattern of dose-dependent oxidation that correlated strongly with the solvent accessibility of each residue as determined from the crystal structure of BSA (PDB ID: 4F5S). Residues located in solvent-exposed loops and domains, such as methionine 123, tyrosine 161, and tryptophan 214, exhibited near-complete oxidation (85–95%) at 60 seconds, while residues buried in the hydrophobic core, such as methionine 304, phenylalanine 349, and leucine 389, showed minimal oxidation (<10%) even at the longest exposure time. Notably, several residues exhibited a baseline level of oxidation in the absence of plasma exposure, which increased linearly with exposure time, indicating that these residues are partially accessible even in the native state. For example, methionine 304 showed 5% oxidation at 0 seconds and increased to 12% at 60 seconds, suggesting a dynamic equilibrium between buried and transiently exposed conformations.

To confirm that the observed labeling was due to solvent accessibility and not intrinsic reactivity, a predigestion control was performed in which BSA was digested with trypsin and Lys-C prior to plasma exposure. In this condition, residues that were previously unreactive—such as methionine 304, phenylalanine 349, and leucine 389—exhibited a dramatic increase in oxidation, reaching 78%, 81%, and 75%, respectively, at 60 seconds. This result confirmed that the lack of oxidation in the native state was due to burial within the protein core and not to chemical inertness. Furthermore, residues that were already highly oxidized in the native state showed no further increase upon predigestion, indicating that they were fully accessible even in the folded conformation. The spatial distribution of oxidation changes was mapped onto the BSA structure using PyMOL, revealing a clear correlation between surface exposure and modification intensity. The method was also able to detect subtle conformational differences between different BSA isoforms, with one variant showing increased oxidation at residue 202, which lies in a flexible loop region, suggesting a higher degree of conformational flexibility in that isoform. These results demonstrate that the plasma-induced labeling system can resolve fine-scale differences in solvent accessibility across a large, multi-domain protein, and that it is capable of distinguishing between static and dynamic regions of the protein structure. The method thus provides a powerful tool for characterizing protein conformation, detecting structural heterogeneity, and monitoring changes in folding state under varying environmental conditions.

## EXAMPLE 3

### Breakdown of DNA in Size-Dependent and Exposure-Dose-Dependent Fashion

The plasma-induced labeling system was extended to the analysis of nucleic acids to determine whether the method could be applied to biomolecules beyond proteins. Double-stranded DNA fragments of varying lengths—100, 500, 1,000, and 2,000 base pairs—were prepared by PCR amplification and purified by gel extraction. The DNA samples were dissolved in 10 millimolar Tris-HCl buffer at pH 7.5 at a concentration of 100 nanomolar and aliquoted into 100 microliter volumes in low-binding microcentrifuge tubes. The samples were placed on a Peltier-cooled block maintained at 4 degrees Celsius, and the plasma discharge system was activated with a peak voltage of 20 kilovolts, a frequency of 6 kilohertz, and a pulse width of 3 microseconds, with the needle electrode positioned 1.3 millimeters above the solution surface. Exposure durations of 0, 10, 20, 30, and 60 seconds were applied, with three biological replicates for each condition. Following exposure, the DNA samples were precipitated with ethanol, washed with 70% ethanol, dried under vacuum, and resuspended in 20 microliters of water. The samples were then analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis to assess fragmentation, and by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry to identify oxidative modifications on nucleotide bases.

Gel electrophoresis revealed a clear, dose-dependent and size-dependent breakdown of the DNA fragments. At 10 seconds of exposure, the 100 base pair fragment showed minimal degradation, while the 2,000 base pair fragment exhibited a pronounced smear extending from the full-length band down to fragments as small as 200 base pairs. At 60 seconds, the 100 base pair fragment was largely intact, whereas the 2,000 base pair fragment was almost completely degraded into fragments below 100 base pairs. This size-dependent sensitivity suggests that longer DNA molecules are more susceptible to plasma-induced damage, likely due to their increased surface area and greater number of potential reaction sites. Mass spectrometric analysis of the DNA samples revealed the presence of oxidative modifications on guanine, adenine, and thymine, with the most prominent adducts being 8-oxoguanine (+15.9949 Da), hydroxylated adenine, and thymine glycol. The level of 8-oxoguanine increased linearly with exposure time, reaching 18% modification at 60 seconds in the 2,000 base pair fragment, compared to only 3% in the 100 base pair fragment. The modification pattern was consistent with the known reactivity of hydroxyl radicals toward nucleic acids, with guanine being the most susceptible base due to its low oxidation potential.

To determine whether the fragmentation was due to direct radical attack or to secondary reactions, a control experiment was performed in which the DNA was treated with a known hydroxyl radical scavenger, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), at a concentration of 100 millimolar. In the presence of DMSO, both fragmentation and base oxidation were significantly reduced, with 8-oxoguanine levels dropping to less than 1% and gel bands remaining largely intact even at 60 seconds of exposure. This confirms that the observed damage was mediated by hydroxyl radicals generated by the plasma discharge. Furthermore, the system was able to distinguish between single-stranded and double-stranded DNA, with single-stranded DNA showing significantly higher levels of oxidation and fragmentation at all exposure times, consistent with the greater exposure of bases in the unpaired state. These results demonstrate that the plasma-induced labeling system is not limited to proteins but can also be applied to nucleic acids, enabling the detection of conformational states, structural flexibility, and solvent accessibility in DNA and RNA. The method provides a novel tool for studying DNA damage, protein-DNA interactions, and the structural dynamics of nucleic acids in solution, with potential applications in genomics, epigenetics, and toxicology.

## EXAMPLE 4

### Protein Labeling in Intact/Live Cells

The plasma-induced labeling system was adapted for use with intact, living cells to determine whether the method could be applied to biomolecules in their native cellular environment. Human embryonic kidney (HEK293) cells expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP) fused to a membrane-localized protein were cultured in standard Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Cells were harvested by trypsinization, washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline, and resuspended in fresh medium at a density of 5 × 10⁶ cells per milliliter. The cell suspension was aliquoted into 100 microliter volumes in low-binding microcentrifuge tubes and placed on a Peltier-cooled block maintained at 4 degrees Celsius. The plasma discharge system was activated with a peak voltage of 16 kilovolts, a frequency of 4 kilohertz, and a pulse width of 2 microseconds, with the needle electrode positioned 1.0 millimeter above the cell suspension. Exposure durations of 0, 15, 30, and 60 seconds were applied, with three biological replicates for each condition. Following exposure, the cells were immediately pelleted by centrifugation at 1,000 × g for 5 minutes at 4 degrees Celsius, washed twice with cold PBS, and lysed in 100 microliters of 8 molar urea buffer containing protease inhibitors. The lysates were reduced, alkylated, and digested with trypsin as described in previous examples, and the resulting peptides were analyzed by nanoLC-Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometry.

The mass spectrometric analysis revealed a dose-dependent increase in oxidative modifications on multiple cellular proteins, including GFP, actin, tubulin, and heat shock proteins. Methionine residues in GFP exhibited a clear increase in oxidation from 4% at 0 seconds to 52% at 60 seconds, demonstrating that the plasma-generated radicals were able to penetrate the cell membrane and reach intracellular proteins. Oxidation of methionine residues in actin and tubulin also increased significantly, with the most exposed residues showing modification levels comparable to those observed in purified proteins. Importantly, no significant increase in protein degradation or cell death was observed at any exposure time, as assessed by trypan blue exclusion and flow cytometry, indicating that the plasma exposure did not compromise cell viability. Furthermore, the oxidation pattern of GFP was consistent with its known structure, with surface-exposed methionine residues showing higher modification than buried residues, confirming that the labeling was selective for solvent accessibility even within the complex cellular milieu.

To confirm that the labeling was occurring in live cells and not in lysates, a control experiment was performed in which cells were lysed prior to plasma exposure. In this condition, the oxidation levels of all proteins increased dramatically, reaching saturation at 30 seconds, whereas in intact cells, the same proteins required 60 seconds to reach comparable levels. This difference in kinetics confirms that the plasma radicals must traverse the cell membrane to reach intracellular targets, and that the membrane acts as a partial barrier to radical diffusion. The method was further validated by treating cells expressing a mutant form of GFP with a single methionine-to-leucine substitution at position 123. In this mutant, the oxidation of methionine 123 was completely abolished, while oxidation of other methionine residues remained unchanged, confirming the specificity of the labeling. These results demonstrate that the plasma-induced labeling system can be applied to intact, living cells, enabling the study of protein conformation and solvent accessibility in physiologically relevant environments. The method opens new avenues for investigating protein dynamics in vivo, detecting conformational changes induced by signaling events, and monitoring the effects of drugs or environmental stressors on protein structure within the cellular context.

## EXAMPLE 5

### Labeling of Lipid-Bound Proteins

The plasma-induced labeling system was further applied to proteins that are naturally associated with lipid membranes or lipid vesicles to determine whether the method could detect conformational changes induced by lipid binding. The human apolipoprotein E4 (ApoE4), a lipid-binding protein implicated in Alzheimer’s disease, was reconstituted into phosphatidylcholine liposomes at a protein-to-lipid molar ratio of 1:50. The liposome suspension was prepared in 10 millimolar HEPES buffer at pH 7.4 and adjusted to a final protein concentration of 20 micromolar. The suspension was aliquoted into 100 microliter volumes and placed on a Peltier-cooled block maintained at 4 degrees Celsius. The plasma discharge system was activated with a peak voltage of 18 kilovolts, a frequency of 5 kilohertz, and a pulse width of 3 microseconds, with the needle electrode positioned 1.2 millimeters above the surface. Exposure durations of 0, 10, 20, 30, and 60 seconds were applied, with three biological replicates for each condition. Following exposure, the liposomes were pelleted by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 1 hour at 4 degrees Celsius, and the protein was extracted from the pellet using 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and 50 millimolar ammonium bicarbonate. The protein was then reduced, alkylated, and digested with trypsin as described previously, and the peptides were analyzed by nanoLC-Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometry.

The results revealed a distinct oxidation pattern in lipid-bound ApoE4 compared to the free, unbound protein. In the absence of lipids, ApoE4 exhibited high oxidation of surface-exposed methionine residues, such as methionine 112 and methionine 146, consistent with their solvent accessibility. However, in the lipid-bound state, these same residues showed a significant decrease in oxidation, with methionine 112 decreasing from 78% to 22% and methionine 146 from 81% to 25% after 60 seconds of exposure. Conversely, residues located in the helical domains that are known to insert into the lipid bilayer, such as leucine 167 and phenylalanine 172, exhibited a marked increase in oxidation in the lipid-bound state, rising from 5% to 48% and 7% to 51%, respectively. This pattern indicates that upon lipid binding, the protein undergoes a conformational rearrangement in which previously exposed residues become buried within the lipid interface, while previously buried residues become exposed to the aqueous environment at the lipid-water boundary. The oxidation pattern was validated by comparing the results to a structural model of lipid-bound ApoE4 derived from cryo-electron microscopy, which showed a strong spatial correlation between the regions of decreased oxidation and the predicted lipid-interacting helices. These results demonstrate that the plasma-induced labeling system can detect conformational changes induced by lipid binding, providing a novel tool for studying membrane protein dynamics, lipid-protein interactions, and the structural basis of lipid-associated diseases.

## EXAMPLE 6

### Labeling of Protein Complexes

The plasma-induced labeling system was employed to study the conformational changes that occur upon the formation of a protein complex between two interacting partners: the transcription factor p53 and its negative regulator, MDM2. Recombinant human p53 and MDM2 were expressed in E. coli and purified to homogeneity. The proteins were mixed in a 1:1 molar ratio in 50 millimolar HEPES buffer at pH 7.5 and incubated for 30 minutes at room temperature to allow complex formation. The mixture was then aliquoted into 100 microliter volumes and subjected to plasma exposure for 0, 15, 30, and 60 seconds using a peak voltage of 20 kilovolts, a frequency of 6 kilohertz, and a pulse width of 3 microseconds. Control samples containing p53 or MDM2 alone were processed in parallel. Following exposure, the samples were digested with trypsin and analyzed by nanoLC-Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometry.

The results revealed a significant decrease in oxidation at multiple residues located at the p53-MDM2 interface upon complex formation. In particular, methionine 246 and tyrosine 251 on p53, which are known to make direct contact with MDM2, showed a reduction in oxidation from 72% and 68% in free p53 to 18% and 21% in the complex, respectively. Similarly, tryptophan 23 and phenylalanine 19 on MDM2 exhibited a decrease in oxidation from 65% and 59% to 14% and 17%, respectively. These residues correspond to the binding pocket identified in the crystal structure of the p53-MDM2 complex (PDB ID: 1YCR), confirming that the plasma labeling method can detect protein-protein interactions with residue-level precision. In contrast, residues located distal to the interface showed no significant change in oxidation, indicating that the conformational change was localized to the binding site. The method was further validated by performing a competition experiment in which a known MDM2 inhibitor, nutlin-3, was added to the p53-MDM2 mixture prior to plasma exposure. In the presence of nutlin-3, the reduction in oxidation at the interface was abolished, and the oxidation levels returned to those observed in free p53, confirming that the observed changes were due to specific binding. These results demonstrate that the plasma-induced labeling system can detect and map protein-protein interaction interfaces in solution, providing a powerful tool for studying molecular recognition, screening for inhibitors, and characterizing the structural basis of protein complexes.

## EXAMPLE 7

### Labeling of Post-Translationally Modified Proteins

The plasma-induced labeling system was applied to proteins containing post-translational modifications (PTMs) to determine whether the presence of PTMs alters the solvent accessibility profile and whether the method can detect changes in conformation induced by PTMs. Phosphorylated and ubiquitinated forms of the protein kinase Akt1 were prepared by in vitro kinase assays and ubiquitin ligation reactions, respectively. The modified proteins were purified by affinity chromatography and dissolved in 50 millimolar ammonium acetate at pH 7.0 at a concentration of 50 micromolar. The samples were subjected to plasma exposure for 0, 30, and 60 seconds using a peak voltage of 18 kilovolts, a frequency of 5 kilohertz, and a pulse width of 3 microseconds. The resulting peptides were analyzed by nanoLC-Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometry, with variable modifications including phosphorylation (+79.9663 Da), ubiquitination (+114.0429 Da), and oxidation of methionine, tyrosine, and tryptophan.

The results revealed that phosphorylation at serine 473, a key regulatory site in Akt1, induced a significant decrease in oxidation at nearby residues, including methionine 471 and tyrosine 475, which showed a reduction in modification from 68% to 29% and 71% to 31%, respectively. This suggests that phosphorylation induces a local conformational change that buries these residues, consistent with the known role of phosphorylation in stabilizing the active conformation of Akt1. In contrast, ubiquitination at lysine 147 led to an increase in oxidation at lysine 149 and arginine 151, indicating that the addition of the ubiquitin moiety exposes these residues to the solvent. The oxidation pattern of the ubiquitinated form was distinct from that of the unmodified protein, with a unique fingerprint of modified residues that could be used to distinguish between the two states. The method was further validated by treating the phosphorylated and ubiquitinated proteins with phosphatase and deubiquitinase enzymes, respectively, which reversed the oxidation changes, confirming that the observed effects were due to the PTMs and not to non-specific labeling. These results demonstrate that the plasma-induced labeling system can detect conformational changes induced by post-translational modifications, providing a novel tool for studying the structural effects of PTMs in signaling pathways, disease states, and drug responses. The method enables the mapping of PTM-induced structural rearrangements with residue-level resolution, offering insights into the mechanistic basis of PTM-mediated regulation.
