# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of plant pathology and agricultural biotechnology, specifically to novel methods for enhancing plant resistance to plant-parasitic nematodes through the targeted inhibition of the enzyme CINNAMIC ACID-4-HYDROXYLASE, hereinafter referred to as C4H. The invention further encompasses the use of specific chemical inhibitors of C4H to modulate the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway in crop plants, thereby inducing a state of enhanced resistance to sedentary endoparasitic nematodes such as root-knot nematodes (Meloidogyne spp.) and cyst nematodes (Heterodera spp. and Globodera spp.), without compromising plant growth, development, or reproductive capacity. The invention is particularly applicable to monocot and dicot crop species including but not limited to wheat, rice, soybean, potato, and cotton, and provides a non-transgenic, chemically mediated strategy for sustainable nematode management in agricultural systems.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Plant-parasitic nematodes represent one of the most persistent and economically damaging groups of agricultural pests worldwide, causing annual yield losses estimated at 10 to 25% across major crops. Among these, sedentary endoparasitic nematodes such as Meloidogyne and Heterodera species are particularly destructive due to their ability to establish long-term feeding sites within plant roots, diverting nutrients and disrupting vascular function. Conventional control strategies—chemical nematicides, crop rotation, and resistant cultivars—have proven inadequate due to environmental toxicity, evolving nematode virulence, and limited genetic resistance in key crop species. The search for novel, sustainable, and environmentally benign approaches to nematode control has intensified, with increasing focus on the plant’s own biochemical defense systems.

Plants defend against nematode invasion through a combination of physical barriers, inducible immune responses, and the production of secondary metabolites with anti-nematode properties. Among these, the phenylpropanoid pathway has emerged as a central hub for the biosynthesis of a diverse array of defense compounds, including hydroxycinnamic acids, flavonoids, lignin, and other phenolic phytoalexins. The pathway begins with the deamination of L-phenylalanine by phenylalanine ammonia-lyase to form cinnamic acid, which is subsequently hydroxylated at the para position by the enzyme CINNAMIC ACID-4-HYDROXYLASE (C4H) to yield p-coumaric acid. This step is universally conserved across vascular plants and serves as the gateway to downstream branches of the pathway that lead to the formation of lignin, flavonoids, coumarins, and other phenolic compounds. The importance of C4H in plant development and defense has been well documented, and its activity is known to be upregulated in response to biotic stress, including nematode infection.

Historically, the accumulation of phenolic compounds such as chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid, and flavonoids has been correlated with resistance to nematodes in multiple plant-nematode systems. For instance, in soybean, the phytoalexin glyceollin I accumulates in response to Heterodera glycines infection and is associated with resistance; in banana, phenylphenalenones such as anigorufone accumulate at infection sites and exhibit direct nematicidal activity; in rice, sakuranetin is induced upon infection by Ditylenchus angustus and is absent in susceptible varieties. These observations have led to the widely held assumption that enhancing phenylpropanoid flux through C4H activation would bolster plant resistance. However, recent studies have revealed a more complex relationship between phenylpropanoid metabolism and nematode susceptibility. In particular, it has been observed that certain nematode species, notably Meloidogyne incognita, actively suppress the expression of genes encoding C4H and other phenylpropanoid enzymes within their feeding sites, suggesting that the nematode may exploit or require specific phenylpropanoid intermediates for successful parasitism.

Notably, in poplar plants infected with M. incognita, chlorogenic acid—a downstream product of the C4H-dependent pathway—was found to be significantly repressed in galls compared to surrounding uninfected tissue, despite elevated levels of upstream precursors. This paradoxical suppression of phenolic metabolites at the site of infection implies that the nematode may depend on the availability of early pathway intermediates, such as cinnamic acid or its derivatives, for feeding site establishment or suppression of host defenses. Furthermore, in several resistant plant genotypes, the accumulation of late-stage phenolic compounds like flavonoids and lignin occurs only after the nematode has already established its feeding site, suggesting that these compounds may be part of a delayed, secondary defense rather than a primary barrier.

This body of evidence challenges the conventional paradigm that phenylpropanoid activation is uniformly beneficial for plant resistance. Instead, it suggests that the early steps of the pathway—particularly those preceding C4H—may serve as metabolic signals or substrates that nematodes co-opt for their own benefit. The hypothesis that C4H inhibition could disrupt nematode parasitism by depriving the parasite of essential precursors, while simultaneously allowing the accumulation of upstream metabolites with anti-nematode properties, has not been previously explored or tested in a controlled, systematic manner. No prior art describes the deliberate pharmacological or genetic suppression of C4H as a strategy to enhance nematode resistance, nor has any study demonstrated that such inhibition can be achieved without impairing plant viability, growth, or yield.

Existing nematicidal strategies rely either on direct toxicity to nematodes (e.g., chemical fumigants, biofumigants like glucosinolate-derived isothiocyanates) or on the induction of broad-spectrum plant immunity (e.g., via salicylic acid or jasmonate signaling). These approaches often suffer from non-specific effects, environmental persistence, or unintended suppression of beneficial microbes. Moreover, transgenic approaches to enhance phenylpropanoid biosynthesis have focused on overexpressing upstream enzymes such as phenylalanine ammonia-lyase or chalcone synthase, with no evidence that such manipulations improve nematode resistance in a consistent or commercially viable manner. The present invention introduces a novel and counterintuitive strategy: the inhibition of C4H to selectively block the flow of metabolites into the downstream phenylpropanoid network, thereby disrupting nematode feeding site formation and development while preserving plant health and productivity.

The invention is further distinguished by its applicability across diverse crop species, its compatibility with conventional agricultural practices, and its lack of requirement for genetic modification. The chemical inhibitors employed are structurally distinct from existing herbicides or fungicides, act with high specificity toward C4H, and do not interfere with primary metabolism or photosynthetic function. Importantly, the method does not rely on the accumulation of downstream phenolic compounds, which have shown inconsistent or even detrimental effects in some systems, but rather exploits the accumulation of upstream intermediates—particularly cinnamic acid and its esters—that are inherently toxic to nematode juveniles and interfere with their ability to establish permanent feeding sites.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and non-obvious method for enhancing plant resistance to plant-parasitic nematodes by inhibiting the enzymatic activity of CINNAMIC ACID-4-HYDROXYLASE (C4H), a key enzyme in the phenylpropanoid biosynthetic pathway. The invention is based on the discovery that pharmacological or biochemical suppression of C4H activity leads to a significant reduction in nematode infection success, feeding site formation, and reproductive output, without adversely affecting plant growth, development, or yield. This effect is achieved not through the induction of classical defense responses, but by the strategic accumulation of early phenylpropanoid intermediates—primarily cinnamic acid and its derivatives—which are toxic to second-stage juvenile nematodes and interfere with their ability to migrate through root tissues and establish syncytia or giant cells.

In one embodiment, the invention comprises the application of a C4H inhibitor to a plant or plant part prior to or during the early stages of nematode infection. The inhibitor is selected from a group of structurally defined compounds that bind to the active site of C4H with high affinity, thereby preventing the conversion of cinnamic acid to p-coumaric acid. As a consequence of this inhibition, cinnamic acid and its esters accumulate in root tissues at concentrations sufficient to impair nematode motility, induce paralysis, and ultimately prevent successful parasitism. The accumulation of these upstream metabolites is accompanied by a marked reduction in the levels of downstream phenolic compounds such as flavonoids, lignin monomers, and hydroxycinnamic acid esters, which are known to be manipulated by nematodes to facilitate feeding site development.

The invention further provides that the C4H inhibitor may be applied as a seed treatment, soil drench, foliar spray, or through controlled-release formulations, and is effective against a broad spectrum of sedentary endoparasitic nematodes, including Meloidogyne incognita, Meloidogyne javanica, Heterodera glycines, Heterodera schachtii, Globodera rostochiensis, and Globodera pallida. The method is particularly effective in monocot crops such as wheat and rice, where conventional resistance genes are scarce or ineffective, and in dicot crops such as soybean and potato, where existing resistance mechanisms are frequently overcome by virulent nematode populations.

Crucially, the invention demonstrates that C4H inhibition does not impair plant fertility, biomass accumulation, or grain yield, even under high nematode pressure. In fact, in multiple field trials, plants treated with C4H inhibitors exhibited equal or greater yield compared to untreated controls, despite significantly reduced nematode populations. This contrasts sharply with other nematicidal strategies, which often result in phytotoxicity or reduced photosynthetic efficiency. The invention further shows that C4H inhibition does not trigger a generalized stress response, nor does it alter the plant’s ability to respond to other biotic or abiotic stresses, indicating a highly targeted mode of action.

The invention also provides a method for identifying suitable C4H inhibitors through high-throughput screening of chemical libraries using recombinant C4H enzyme assays, followed by validation in planta using nematode infection bioassays. The inhibitors identified are structurally distinct from known cytochrome P450 inhibitors such as piperonyl butoxide or triadimefon, and exhibit no cross-reactivity with other plant P450 enzymes involved in hormone biosynthesis or detoxification. The chemical scaffolds of the preferred inhibitors include substituted benzoxazoles, pyridine derivatives, and aryl-substituted acetylenes, each of which demonstrates nanomolar affinity for C4H and low toxicity to non-target organisms.

The invention further encompasses the use of C4H inhibition as a resistance biomarker in breeding programs, wherein plants exhibiting elevated endogenous levels of cinnamic acid or reduced C4H transcript abundance are selected for enhanced nematode resistance. This allows for the development of nematode-resistant cultivars without genetic modification, through conventional selection methods guided by metabolic profiling.

In summary, the invention represents a paradigm shift in plant-nematode interaction biology by demonstrating that the inhibition of a key biosynthetic enzyme, previously assumed to be beneficial for plant defense, can instead be exploited to confer robust, broad-spectrum resistance to economically devastating plant-parasitic nematodes. The method is safe, scalable, compatible with existing agricultural systems, and provides a sustainable alternative to chemical nematicides and transgenic approaches.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention is based on the unexpected and counterintuitive discovery that the inhibition of CINNAMIC ACID-4-HYDROXYLASE (C4H), a central enzyme in the phenylpropanoid pathway, results in a dramatic enhancement of plant resistance to plant-parasitic nematodes, without compromising plant growth or reproductive capacity. This finding overturns decades of conventional wisdom, which held that the activation of the phenylpropanoid pathway was universally beneficial for plant defense against pathogens and pests. Instead, the present invention reveals that the early steps of this pathway—specifically the conversion of cinnamic acid to p-coumaric acid by C4H—are exploited by sedentary endoparasitic nematodes to facilitate their establishment within host root tissues.

C4H is a cytochrome P450 monooxygenase encoded by a family of genes in most vascular plants. It catalyzes the para-hydroxylation of cinnamic acid to form p-coumaric acid, a reaction that is essential for the biosynthesis of lignin, flavonoids, coumarins, and other phenolic defense compounds. In the context of nematode infection, this enzyme has been assumed to be a component of the plant’s immune arsenal, given that its expression is often upregulated in response to biotic stress. However, detailed transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses of nematode-infected roots have revealed a different pattern: in susceptible plants, C4H expression is suppressed precisely within the developing feeding sites of Meloidogyne and Heterodera species, while upstream metabolites such as cinnamic acid and its derivatives accumulate in the surrounding tissue. This spatial and temporal pattern suggests that the nematode actively suppresses C4H activity to prevent the formation of downstream phenolic compounds that may be toxic, while simultaneously allowing the accumulation of cinnamic acid and related molecules that may serve as signaling molecules or metabolic substrates for the nematode itself.

The present invention demonstrates that this suppression is not merely a passive consequence of infection, but an active requirement for successful parasitism. When C4H activity is pharmacologically inhibited using highly specific small-molecule inhibitors, the accumulation of cinnamic acid and its esters—such as cinnamoyl glucose, cinnamoyl malate, and cinnamoyl ascorbate—is significantly enhanced in root tissues. These compounds, which are normally transient intermediates rapidly converted to p-coumaric acid, reach concentrations that are directly toxic to second-stage juvenile nematodes (J2s). In controlled in vitro assays, exposure to cinnamic acid at concentrations as low as 50 μM resulted in complete immobilization of M. incognita J2s within 24 hours, with no recovery observed upon transfer to clean water, indicating irreversible nematidal activity. Similar effects were observed with cinnamoyl glucose, which demonstrated potent nematidal activity at concentrations fivefold lower than those required for cinnamic acid alone.

In planta, the application of C4H inhibitors led to a 70–95% reduction in the number of nematodes successfully penetrating root tissues and a corresponding 80–98% reduction in the formation of feeding sites. Histological examination of treated roots revealed that nematodes that did manage to enter root tissue were unable to establish functional syncytia or giant cells. Instead, they remained arrested in the cortex or epidermis, exhibiting stunted development, reduced body size, and failure to molt beyond the J2 stage. In contrast, untreated control plants showed normal nematode migration, feeding site formation, and subsequent reproduction, with cysts or galls developing in abundance.

Importantly, the inhibition of C4H did not result in any observable phytotoxicity. Plants treated with C4H inhibitors exhibited normal leaf coloration, root architecture, photosynthetic rates, and biomass accumulation. Growth parameters such as plant height, stem diameter, and number of tillers or branches were statistically indistinguishable from untreated controls. Furthermore, reproductive output—measured as seed number, seed weight, and fruit yield—was either unchanged or, in some cases, significantly increased in treated plants. This increase in yield was attributed to the reduced nutrient drain caused by nematode suppression, rather than any direct stimulatory effect of the inhibitor on plant physiology.

The specificity of the inhibitors used in the invention was rigorously validated. A panel of 12 structurally diverse compounds was screened for C4H inhibition using recombinant C4H protein expressed in yeast. Only four compounds demonstrated nanomolar binding affinity (IC50 < 100 nM) and no cross-reactivity with other plant P450 enzymes involved in auxin biosynthesis, gibberellin metabolism, or defense compound detoxification. These four compounds were then tested in vivo across five crop species: wheat (Triticum aestivum), rice (Oryza sativa), soybean (Glycine max), potato (Solanum tuberosum), and cotton (Gossypium hirsutum). All four compounds consistently reduced nematode infection by 75% or more, regardless of crop species or nematode type. The most effective compound, designated herein as C4Hi-1, is a substituted 2-phenyl-4-oxo-1,2,3,4-tetrahydroquinoline derivative that binds to the heme iron of C4H, preventing oxygen activation and substrate hydroxylation.

The mode of action of C4H inhibition is distinct from that of other nematicidal agents. Unlike chemical nematicides, which act through neurotoxicity or mitochondrial disruption, C4H inhibitors do not directly kill nematodes upon contact. Instead, they act indirectly by altering the chemical environment of the root apoplast and cortex, rendering it inhospitable for nematode migration and feeding site establishment. The accumulation of cinnamic acid and its esters appears to interfere with nematode chemotaxis, as J2s exposed to C4H-inhibited root exudates showed reduced attraction to root tips and failed to orient toward the vascular cylinder. Additionally, these metabolites disrupt the nematode’s cuticle integrity, as evidenced by scanning electron microscopy showing increased surface roughness and pore formation in treated J2s.

The invention further demonstrates that C4H inhibition does not compromise the plant’s ability to mount other forms of defense. Transcriptomic analysis of treated plants revealed no upregulation of pathogenesis-related genes, salicylic acid-responsive genes, or jasmonic acid pathway components. This indicates that the resistance conferred by C4H inhibition is independent of classical immune signaling pathways. Instead, resistance is conferred through a metabolic barrier—a biochemical shield created by the accumulation of specific, nematode-toxic intermediates that are naturally produced by the plant but normally rapidly consumed by C4H.

The invention also provides a method for identifying and selecting plant genotypes with naturally elevated levels of cinnamic acid or reduced C4H expression as candidates for breeding programs. Using high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS), root extracts from diverse germplasm collections were analyzed for cinnamic acid content. Plants exhibiting constitutive cinnamic acid levels above 1.2 μmol/g fresh root weight were found to be significantly less susceptible to nematode infection than those with lower levels, even in the absence of chemical treatment. This discovery enables the development of nematode-resistant cultivars through conventional breeding, without the need for genetic modification.

The application methods for C4H inhibitors are versatile and adaptable to existing agricultural practices. The inhibitors may be applied as seed coatings, soil drenches, foliar sprays, or through controlled-release formulations embedded in biodegradable polymers. Seed treatment is particularly effective, as it ensures that the inhibitor is present in the rhizosphere during the critical window of nematode invasion, which typically occurs within the first 72 hours after seed germination. Soil drench applications are suitable for perennial crops or high-value horticultural systems, while foliar sprays may be used in conjunction with systemic translocation to deliver the inhibitor to root tissues via phloem transport.

Field trials conducted over three growing seasons in multiple geographic regions confirmed the efficacy and safety of the invention. In wheat, C4H inhibition reduced Heterodera avenae populations by 92% and increased grain yield by 18% compared to untreated controls. In rice, treatment with C4Hi-1 reduced Ditylenchus angustus infestation by 87% and maintained panicle number and grain weight at levels equivalent to nematode-free controls. In soybean, treatment suppressed Heterodera glycines cyst formation by 95% and increased seed protein content by 6%, without affecting oil content. In potato, treatment reduced Globodera pallida egg production by 90% and eliminated tuber cracking associated with nematode feeding. In cotton, treatment reduced Meloidogyne incognita galls by 89% and improved fiber quality indices.

The invention further demonstrates that C4H inhibition is compatible with integrated pest management strategies. When combined with low-dose applications of biological control agents such as Paecilomyces lilacinus or Bacillus subtilis, the efficacy of nematode suppression was synergistic, reducing the required chemical dose by up to 70%. Moreover, the inhibitors showed no adverse effects on beneficial soil microbes, earthworms, or pollinators, and degraded rapidly in soil with a half-life of less than 14 days, minimizing environmental persistence.

The invention also provides a method for monitoring the efficacy of C4H inhibition in real time. A diagnostic assay based on the ratio of cinnamic acid to p-coumaric acid in root exudates was developed, with a ratio above 3.5 indicating effective inhibition and a high probability of nematode resistance. This assay can be performed using portable HPLC-MS devices in the field, enabling farmers to verify treatment success and optimize application timing.

In conclusion, the invention provides a novel, broadly applicable, and environmentally sustainable method for controlling plant-parasitic nematodes by targeting a previously unrecognized vulnerability in the nematode’s parasitic strategy: its dependence on the timely conversion of cinnamic acid to p-coumaric acid. By inhibiting C4H, the plant’s own metabolic machinery is harnessed to generate a localized, non-toxic, and highly effective barrier against nematode invasion, without compromising yield or requiring genetic modification. This represents a transformative advance in plant protection science and offers a viable alternative to the declining arsenal of chemical nematicides.

## EXAMPLES

### Effect of C4H Inhibition on Growth and Development in Uninfected Crops

To evaluate the potential phytotoxic effects of C4H inhibition on plant physiology in the absence of nematode pressure, a series of controlled growth experiments were conducted across five major crop species: wheat (Triticum aestivum), rice (Oryza sativa), soybean (Glycine max), potato (Solanum tuberosum), and cotton (Gossypium hirsutum). Each species was grown under standardized greenhouse conditions with controlled light, temperature, humidity, and nutrient supply. Plants were treated at the two-leaf stage with a single application of the C4H inhibitor C4Hi-1 at a concentration of 10 μM, delivered via soil drench. Control plants received an equivalent volume of solvent (0.1% DMSO in water). Treatments were applied in triplicate, with 30 plants per treatment group, and growth parameters were monitored weekly for 60 days.

No signs of chlorosis, necrosis, wilting, or abnormal leaf morphology were observed in any of the treated plants. Leaf area, measured using digital image analysis, remained statistically identical between treated and control groups across all species. Photosynthetic efficiency, assessed via chlorophyll fluorescence (Fv/Fm ratio), showed no significant difference, with mean values of 0.81 ± 0.02 in treated plants versus 0.82 ± 0.01 in controls. Root architecture, including total root length, root volume, and root hair density, was quantified using X-ray microtomography and found to be indistinguishable between groups. Biomass accumulation, measured as dry weight of shoots and roots at day 60, showed no significant variation: wheat shoot biomass was 12.3 ± 0.8 g per plant in treated versus 12.1 ± 0.7 g in controls; rice shoot biomass was 8.9 ± 0.6 g versus 8.8 ± 0.5 g; soybean shoot biomass was 15.7 ± 1.1 g versus 15.5 ± 1.0 g; potato shoot biomass was 22.4 ± 1.5 g versus 22.1 ± 1.4 g; and cotton shoot biomass was 18.6 ± 1.2 g versus 18.4 ± 1.1 g.

Stem elongation, tiller number in wheat, and node formation in soybean were also unaffected. In rice, panicle initiation occurred at the same developmental stage in both treated and control plants, with no delay in flowering. In potato, tuber initiation was observed at 35 days post-emergence in both groups, with no difference in tuber number or size distribution. In cotton, flowering began on day 48 in both groups, with identical numbers of flowers per plant and no aberrant floral development.

Metabolomic profiling of root tissues at day 30 confirmed the biochemical specificity of the inhibitor. HPLC-MS analysis revealed a 4.7-fold increase in cinnamic acid concentration in treated roots compared to controls, while p-coumaric acid levels were reduced by 89%. Levels of downstream metabolites—including ferulic acid, sinapic acid, caffeic acid, and the flavonoid quercetin—remained unchanged or showed only minor fluctuations within the range of biological variability. This confirmed that C4H inhibition was highly specific and did not trigger compensatory metabolic shifts in other branches of the phenylpropanoid pathway.

Gene expression analysis using RNA sequencing showed no significant differential expression of genes involved in hormone biosynthesis (auxin, cytokinin, gibberellin, abscisic acid), defense signaling (PR genes, WRKY transcription factors), or primary metabolism (carbohydrate synthesis, nitrogen assimilation). This indicated that the inhibition of C4H did not induce a generalized stress response or alter the plant’s endogenous signaling networks.

In summary, these experiments demonstrate that pharmacological inhibition of C4H using C4Hi-1 has no detectable adverse effects on plant growth, development, or metabolic homeostasis in the absence of nematode infection. The inhibitor acts with high specificity, altering only the targeted metabolic node without triggering pleiotropic consequences. This establishes the foundation for the safe and sustainable use of C4H inhibitors in agricultural systems, where long-term plant health and yield stability are paramount.

### C4H Inhibitors have No Effect on Yield and Fertility in Wheat

To determine whether C4H inhibition affects reproductive output and grain yield in a major cereal crop, a comprehensive field trial was conducted over three consecutive growing seasons in three distinct agroecological zones of the United States: the Great Plains (Kansas), the Midwest (Iowa), and the Pacific Northwest (Washington). The spring wheat cultivar ‘Trego’ was selected for its uniform agronomic performance and susceptibility to the cereal cyst nematode Heterodera avenae. The trial consisted of four treatments: (1) untreated control; (2) application of C4Hi-1 at 10 μM via seed coating; (3) application of a commercial nematicide (fluopyram) at the manufacturer’s recommended rate; and (4) application of a non-specific P450 inhibitor (piperonyl butoxide) at an equivalent concentration.

Each treatment was replicated in 10 randomized blocks, with 20 plants per block, for a total of 200 plants per treatment. Soil nematode populations were monitored prior to planting and confirmed to be uniformly high across all plots. Plants were grown under standard agronomic practices, including fertilizer application, irrigation, and pest management, with no additional interventions.

At maturity, grain yield was measured as kilograms per hectare, and fertility parameters—including tiller number per plant, spikelet number per spike, kernel weight, and thousand-kernel weight—were recorded. Nematode infection was quantified by extracting cysts from root systems at harvest and counting them microscopically.

In the first season, the untreated control yielded 4,210 kg/ha, while the fluopyram-treated plots yielded 4,980 kg/ha, representing a 18.3% increase attributable to nematode suppression. The piperonyl butoxide-treated plots yielded 4,150 kg/ha, showing no significant improvement over the control, and in some blocks, a slight reduction in kernel weight was observed, suggesting potential phytotoxicity. In contrast, the C4Hi-1-treated plots yielded 5,120 kg/ha, a 21.6% increase over the control and a 2.8% increase over fluopyram. Tiller number per plant was 12.3 ± 0.7 in C4Hi-1-treated plants versus 11.8 ± 0.6 in controls; spikelet number per spike was 23.1 ± 0.9 versus 21.7 ± 0.8; and thousand-kernel weight was 41.2 ± 0.5 g versus 39.8 ± 0.4 g.

In the second season, similar results were obtained. The C4Hi-1 treatment yielded 4,890 kg/ha compared to 4,020 kg/ha in the control, a 21.6% increase. Nematode cyst counts were reduced by 93% in C4Hi-1-treated plots, compared to 85% in fluopyram-treated plots. Crucially, no reduction in kernel viability or germination rate was observed in seeds harvested from C4Hi-1-treated plants. Germination assays conducted under controlled conditions showed 96% germination in C4Hi-1-treated seeds versus 95% in controls.

In the third season, a drought stress event occurred during flowering. Despite this, C4Hi-1-treated plots maintained a yield advantage of 19.7% over controls, while fluopyram-treated plots showed a reduced yield benefit (12.4%), suggesting that the C4H inhibition strategy confers resilience under abiotic stress. Kernel weight remained stable, and no reduction in protein or gluten content was detected in flour analysis.

Histological examination of flag leaves and developing spikes revealed no differences in vascular bundle density, stomatal conductance, or pollen viability between treated and control plants. Pollen germination rates in vitro were 94% in both groups. Seed set per spike was identical, and no evidence of abnormal embryo development or seed abortion was observed.

These results conclusively demonstrate that C4H inhibition using C4Hi-1 not only suppresses nematode infection but also enhances yield and fertility in wheat without any detrimental effects on reproductive physiology. The absence of yield penalty, even under environmental stress, distinguishes this method from conventional nematicides, which often impair plant vigor or reduce grain quality. The mechanism underlying this yield enhancement is attributed to the elimination of nutrient drain by nematodes, allowing the plant to allocate resources more efficiently to grain filling. The fact that this benefit is achieved without genetic modification, chemical toxicity, or disruption of plant metabolism underscores the agricultural value of the invention.

### The Early Phenylpropanoid Pathway and Rice Defense

To investigate the role of the early phenylpropanoid pathway in rice resistance to the stem nematode Ditylenchus angustus, a series of experiments were conducted using both chemical inhibition and genetic analysis. Rice cultivars differing in susceptibility to D. angustus were selected: the resistant variety ‘Nipponbare’ and the susceptible variety ‘Koshihikari’. Plants were grown in hydroponic systems under controlled conditions and inoculated with 500 J2s of D. angustus per plant at the three-leaf stage. Treatments included: (1) no inhibitor; (2) application of C4Hi-1 at 5 μM; (3) application of a phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) inhibitor (2-aminoindan-2-phosphonic acid, AIP) at 20 μM; and (4) application of p-coumaric acid at 100 μM to rescue the pathway downstream of C4H.

Root exudates and tissue extracts were collected at 24, 48, and 72 hours post-inoculation and analyzed by HPLC-MS. In untreated susceptible plants, cinnamic acid levels remained low, while p-coumaric acid and its derivatives accumulated rapidly. In resistant ‘Nipponbare’, cinnamic acid levels were constitutively higher than in ‘Koshihikari’, and p-coumaric acid accumulation was delayed. When C4Hi-1 was applied to susceptible ‘Koshihikari’, cinnamic acid levels increased 6.3-fold within 48 hours, and nematode penetration was reduced by 88%. Nematodes that did enter the roots failed to migrate beyond the epidermis and exhibited severe morphological abnormalities, including body curling and loss of cuticular integrity.

In contrast, when PAL was inhibited using AIP, cinnamic acid levels dropped by 90%, and nematode infection increased by 40% compared to untreated controls, indicating that the presence of cinnamic acid is essential for resistance. When p-coumaric acid was exogenously supplied to C4Hi-1-treated plants, the protective effect was completely abolished: nematode penetration rates returned to control levels, and feeding site formation resumed normally. This demonstrates that the accumulation of cinnamic acid, not its downstream products, is the key determinant of resistance.

RNA sequencing of root tissues revealed that the expression of genes encoding C4H was significantly downregulated in resistant ‘Nipponbare’ compared to susceptible ‘Koshihikari’ at 48 hours post-inoculation, whereas genes encoding PAL and 4CL remained unchanged. This suggests that resistance in ‘Nipponbare’ is mediated not by enhanced phenylpropanoid flux, but by the natural suppression of C4H activity, allowing cinnamic acid to accumulate.

To confirm this, a CRISPR-Cas9 knockout line of the C4H gene (OsC4H1) was generated in the susceptible ‘Koshihikari’ background. The knockout plants showed a 7.1-fold increase in cinnamic acid levels and exhibited complete resistance to D. angustus, with no feeding sites observed in 100% of plants. These plants were phenotypically indistinguishable from wild-type controls in terms of growth, flowering time, and grain yield.

These findings establish that in rice, resistance to D. angustus is conferred by the accumulation of cinnamic acid resulting from the suppression of C4H activity, rather than by the production of downstream phenolics. The invention leverages this natural mechanism by pharmacologically mimicking the resistant phenotype through targeted C4H inhibition, thereby conferring resistance to susceptible cultivars without genetic modification.

### Second Infection Experiment in Rice

A second, independent infection experiment was conducted to validate the findings from the previous example under more ecologically realistic conditions. The experiment was carried out in a greenhouse with natural soil inoculum containing field-collected populations of Ditylenchus angustus and Meloidogyne graminicola, two of the most economically damaging nematodes in Asian rice systems. The resistant cultivar ‘Nipponbare’ and the susceptible cultivar ‘Koshihikari’ were planted in 100-liter pots filled with sterilized field soil amended with 500 nematode eggs per pot. At the three-leaf stage, half of the plants in each cultivar were treated with C4Hi-1 at 5 μM via soil drench; the other half received solvent only.

Plants were maintained for 60 days, after which nematode populations were quantified by extracting cysts and galls from root systems and counting them under a dissecting microscope. Root tissue was also analyzed for cinnamic acid and p-coumaric acid levels using HPLC-MS.

In untreated ‘Koshihikari’, 94% of plants developed visible galls, with an average of 18 galls per plant and 1,200 cysts per pot. In C4Hi-1-treated ‘Koshihikari’, only 8% of plants developed galls, with an average of 0.7 galls per plant and 89 cysts per pot—a 93% reduction. Nematode reproduction was nearly eliminated, with egg counts reduced by 96%. In contrast, untreated ‘Nipponbare’ showed only 3% gall formation and 112 cysts per pot, confirming its natural resistance. C4Hi-1-treated ‘Nipponbare’ showed no further reduction in infection, consistent with the hypothesis that resistance in this cultivar is already mediated by endogenous C4H suppression.

Metabolomic analysis confirmed that cinnamic acid levels in treated ‘Koshihikari’ reached 4.8 μmol/g fresh root weight, compared to 0.7 μmol/g in untreated controls. p-Coumaric acid levels dropped from 2.1 μmol/g to 0.2 μmol/g. In ‘Nipponbare’, cinnamic acid levels were constitutively high at 4.1 μmol/g, and p-coumaric acid was low at 0.3 μmol/g, even without inhibitor treatment.

To assess yield impact, plants were harvested at maturity. Grain yield in untreated ‘Koshihikari’ was 2,150 kg/ha, while in treated ‘Koshihikari’ it was 3,890 kg/ha—a 81% increase. In ‘Nipponbare’, yield was 4,020 kg/ha in both treated and untreated plants, confirming that the inhibitor does not enhance yield beyond the level conferred by natural resistance.

The experiment was repeated with a second nematode species, Meloidogyne graminicola, with identical results: C4Hi-1 reduced gall formation by 89% and increased yield by 78% in ‘Koshihikari’, with no effect on ‘Nipponbare’. These results confirm the broad-spectrum efficacy of C4H inhibition against multiple nematode species in rice and demonstrate that the method can convert a susceptible cultivar into a resistant one without altering its genetic background.

### Example 5

To evaluate the long-term efficacy and environmental compatibility of C4H inhibition in a commercial agricultural setting, a multi-year field trial was conducted across 12 commercial farms in the United States, India, and Brazil, representing diverse cropping systems, soil types, and nematode pressures. Protein content in soybean increased by 5.2%, while oil content remained unchanged. Test weight in wheat increased by 3.1%, indicating improved grain filling. No reduction in germination rate or seed viability was observed in seeds harvested from treated plants.
