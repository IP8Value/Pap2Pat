# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Derivative contracts represent a cornerstone of modern financial markets, serving as instruments through which parties hedge risk, speculate on price movements, or gain leveraged exposure to underlying assets without direct ownership. These financial instruments derive their value from the performance of one or more underlying assets—such as equities, currencies, commodities, interest rates, or indices—and are typically governed by contractual terms that specify the conditions under which payments are made, the timing of those payments, and the final settlement mechanism. The global notional value of outstanding derivative contracts is estimated to exceed tens of trillions of dollars, underscoring their pervasive role in institutional finance and macroeconomic stability. While some derivatives, such as European call or put options, admit closed-form analytical solutions under simplified assumptions like geometric Brownian motion, the majority of commercially significant derivatives exhibit path-dependent structures that render analytical pricing intractable. These include autocallable structures, target accrual redemption forwards (TARFs), barrier options, and other structured products whose payoffs depend on the entire trajectory of the underlying asset’s price over the life of the contract, not merely its terminal value. As a result, financial institutions routinely rely on Monte Carlo simulation techniques to estimate the fair value of such instruments, generating thousands or even millions of simulated price paths under a stochastic model of the underlying dynamics. Each path represents a possible evolution of asset prices over discrete time steps, and the expected discounted payoff is approximated by averaging the payoffs across all simulated scenarios. The convergence rate of this classical approach is governed by the central limit theorem, yielding an error that diminishes proportionally to the inverse square root of the number of sampled paths. Consequently, achieving high precision in pricing requires an enormous computational burden, particularly when the underlying model incorporates multiple correlated assets, stochastic volatility, or complex payoff structures with conditional triggers. The computational cost escalates rapidly with the number of time steps, the dimensionality of the underlying asset basket, and the granularity of discretization required to capture the fine details of the payoff function. This persistent computational bottleneck has motivated the search for alternative computational paradigms capable of delivering superior scaling properties. Quantum computing, with its capacity to encode and manipulate high-dimensional probability distributions in superposition, offers a theoretically compelling pathway to accelerate such simulations. Quantum algorithms based on amplitude estimation can, in principle, achieve a quadratic speedup over classical Monte Carlo methods, reducing the number of required samples from O(1/ε²) to O(1/ε) for a target accuracy ε. However, the practical realization of such an advantage has been hindered by the absence of a coherent, end-to-end framework that accounts for the full computational overhead of preparing the quantum state representing the underlying stochastic process, evaluating the path-dependent payoff function, and executing the amplitude estimation procedure with sufficient fidelity under fault-tolerant constraints. Previous proposals have either assumed idealized state preparation mechanisms that ignore the resource cost of encoding complex multivariate distributions, or have relied on methods that introduce exponentially growing normalization errors, rendering them infeasible for realistic financial applications. Moreover, the transition from theoretical complexity analysis to concrete resource estimation—factoring in the number of logical qubits, T-gate depth, error correction overhead, and gate synthesis fidelity—has been largely unaddressed in prior literature. This gap between theoretical promise and practical feasibility has left the financial industry without a clear roadmap for when, or if, quantum computing could deliver tangible value in derivative pricing. The present invention bridges this gap by introducing a novel, fully realized quantum algorithm for derivative pricing that systematically accounts for all computational components, from the loading of correlated stochastic processes to the evaluation of intricate path-dependent payoffs, while eliminating the fatal normalization issues that plagued earlier approaches. It provides the first comprehensive set of fault-tolerant resource estimates for real-world financial derivatives, demonstrating a viable path to quantum advantage through a re-parameterization technique that leverages the independence of log-returns in Gaussian models to decompose the problem into reusable, variational subroutines. This invention does not merely propose a theoretical speedup; it delivers a complete, implementable architecture for quantum financial computation, grounded in the physical constraints of error-corrected quantum hardware and calibrated against the precision requirements of institutional finance.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a quantum computational method for pricing path-dependent financial derivatives with a quadratic speedup over classical Monte Carlo simulation, achieved through a novel re-parameterization technique that transforms the problem of loading correlated multivariate stochastic processes into a sequence of independent, pre-computable quantum state preparations. The method operates by modeling the underlying asset prices in terms of their logarithmic returns, which, under geometric Brownian motion, are distributed as independent multivariate normal random variables. This transformation decouples the temporal and cross-asset dependencies inherent in price-space formulations, enabling the decomposition of the full path distribution into a tensor product of identical, low-dimensional Gaussian distributions, each corresponding to a single asset at a single time step. These Gaussian distributions are prepared in parallel using a variational quantum circuit that has been pre-optimized offline to approximate the standard normal probability density function with high fidelity, using a Ry-CNOT ansatz trained via an energy-based objective derived from the quantum harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian, followed by a refinement step targeting the L∞ norm of the state approximation error. The resulting quantum state, representing a superposition of all possible log-return paths, is then subjected to an affine transformation that introduces the desired means, volatilities, and correlations among assets, using the Cholesky decomposition of the covariance matrix. This transformation is implemented via a sequence of quantum arithmetic operations, including addition, multiplication, and exponentiation, executed in parallel across all assets and time steps to minimize circuit depth. The transformed log-return state is then converted into the corresponding asset price path by applying the exponential function to each log-return, yielding a quantum state encoding the full price trajectory of the underlying basket. A payoff operator, tailored to the specific structure of the derivative being priced—such as an autocallable option or a target accrual redemption forward—is then applied to compute the discounted payoff associated with each path, storing the result as the amplitude of an ancilla qubit. This payoff computation involves conditional logic, comparator circuits, controlled rotations, and quantum arithmetic to evaluate complex trigger conditions, knock-out barriers, accrual caps, and binary payout structures, all while preserving quantum coherence. Finally, amplitude estimation is applied to extract the expected discounted payoff from the ancilla qubit’s state, achieving an overall estimation error that scales as O(1/N) for N quantum queries, in contrast to the O(1/√N) scaling of classical Monte Carlo. The entire algorithm is designed for fault-tolerant execution, with all quantum arithmetic operations performed in fixed-point representation to bound cumulative numerical errors, and all rotation angles synthesized using a Clifford+T gate decomposition with logarithmic depth scaling. Crucially, the re-parameterization method eliminates the exponential normalization factor that plagued prior Riemann summation approaches, ensuring that the quantum state remains well-conditioned and that the amplitude estimation procedure is not undermined by error amplification. The method further enables resource reuse: the variational Gaussian loaders, once trained, are problem-agnostic and can be deployed across any derivative pricing task involving Gaussian dynamics, thereby amortizing the training cost over multiple applications. The invention provides the first end-to-end resource estimates for pricing real-world derivatives such as basket autocallables and TARFs, demonstrating that with current and near-term fault-tolerant hardware assumptions—specifically, a logical T-gate rate of 10 MHz and a code distance supporting 10¹⁰ operations—a single derivative can be priced in approximately one second, with a total T-depth on the order of 10⁷ to 10⁸ and a logical qubit count below 20,000. These estimates represent a decisive improvement over prior methods, which either required unfeasibly large normalization factors or relied on impractical state preparation techniques such as Grover-Rudolph loading, which reintroduces the very Monte Carlo integration it seeks to replace. The invention thus establishes a concrete, scalable, and commercially viable pathway to quantum advantage in derivative pricing, providing financial institutions with a precise performance target for quantum hardware development and a structured framework for algorithmic implementation that can be extended to other stochastic financial models, including local volatility, stochastic volatility, and jump-diffusion processes.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention comprises a quantum algorithm for pricing financial derivatives that are path-dependent and computationally intensive under classical simulation, such as autocallable options and target accrual redemption forwards (TARFs). The algorithm operates by encoding the entire space of possible price trajectories of one or more underlying assets into a quantum superposition, computing the discounted payoff associated with each trajectory in parallel, and then extracting the expected value of these payoffs through quantum amplitude estimation. The critical innovation lies in the method by which the probability distribution over asset paths is prepared in quantum memory. Rather than attempting to load the joint distribution of asset prices directly—which, under geometric Brownian motion, is a highly correlated multivariate log-normal distribution—this invention transforms the problem into the space of logarithmic returns. In this return space, the dynamics of the underlying assets are governed by a multivariate normal distribution, whose structure permits a decomposition into a product of independent, identically distributed Gaussian components across time and assets, modulo a linear correlation structure that can be efficiently applied via affine transformation. This structural insight enables the separation of the state preparation task into two distinct phases: first, the preparation of a large number of identical, low-dimensional quantum states, each representing a standard normal distribution over a single log-return; and second, the application of a global affine transformation that introduces the desired means, volatilities, and correlations. The preparation of each standard normal state is performed using a variational quantum circuit that has been pre-trained offline to approximate the square root of the Gaussian probability density function with high fidelity. This circuit, composed of a sequence of single-qubit Y-rotations and entangling CNOT gates arranged in a linear ladder topology, is optimized using a two-stage training protocol. In the first stage, the circuit parameters are adjusted to minimize the expectation value of the quantum harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian, whose ground state is analytically known to be a Gaussian wavefunction. This physics-inspired objective provides a smooth, convex optimization landscape that efficiently guides the circuit toward a state that closely resembles the target distribution. In the second stage, the circuit is further refined by minimizing the L∞ norm of the difference between the measured probability distribution and the ideal Gaussian, ensuring that the worst-case approximation error is bounded and minimized. This two-step process ensures both global convergence and local precision, producing a reusable quantum subroutine that can be employed across any derivative pricing problem involving Gaussian dynamics, without requiring retraining for each new contract. Once the standard normal states are prepared in parallel across all d assets and T time steps, the algorithm applies a Cholesky decomposition of the d×d covariance matrix to introduce correlations among the assets. The Cholesky factor L is used to perform a linear transformation on the vector of independent log-returns, resulting in a new vector whose joint distribution matches the desired correlated multivariate normal distribution. This transformation is implemented using quantum arithmetic circuits that perform addition and multiplication operations on fixed-point encoded quantum registers. Each log-return is multiplied by the corresponding element of the Cholesky matrix and accumulated into a new register representing the transformed return. These operations are executed in parallel across all time steps and assets, with the use of ancilla qubits to manage intermediate results and ensure reversibility. The resulting correlated log-return state is then converted into the corresponding asset price path by applying the exponential function to each component, using a piecewise polynomial approximation implemented via quantum arithmetic. This step is again parallelized across all assets and time steps, with the precision of the exponential evaluation controlled by the choice of polynomial degree and number of subintervals, ensuring that the discretization error remains within the target tolerance. The final quantum state now encodes the entire set of possible price trajectories of the underlying assets, with each basis state corresponding to a unique path and its amplitude proportional to the square root of the probability of that path under the stochastic model. The next phase of the algorithm computes the discounted payoff associated with each path. This is accomplished by applying a payoff operator that is custom-designed for the specific derivative being priced. For an autocallable option, this operator evaluates a sequence of binary triggers at each payment date, checking whether the cumulative return of any underlying asset has exceeded a predefined strike level. If so, the contract is automatically called, and a fixed payout is triggered, while all future payoffs are nullified. This logic is implemented using a cascade of comparator circuits, AND and OR gates, and controlled rotations that apply the appropriate amplitude to the ancilla qubit only if the contract has not been previously called. Simultaneously, the operator evaluates whether a knock-in put condition has been satisfied, which requires tracking the minimum cumulative return over the life of the contract and comparing it to a barrier level. The payoff from this put option is computed using quantum arithmetic to evaluate a square root and arcsine function, which are then encoded into the ancilla amplitude via a controlled rotation. For a target accrual redemption forward (TARF), the payoff operator must handle a more complex structure involving multiple payment dates, a knock-out barrier, and an accrual cap. At each payment date, the operator first determines whether the underlying price has breached the knock-out threshold, in which case all remaining payoffs are extinguished. If not, it computes the partial payoff based on whether the price lies above or below a pair of strike levels, applying a multiplier to account for asymmetric payoffs. It then accumulates the total gain across all payment dates and checks whether the accrued amount exceeds the cap. If so, the payoff for that date is truncated to ensure the total gain does not surpass the cap, and the contract is terminated. This logic is implemented using a series of conditional comparisons, addition circuits to accumulate the running total, and multiplexers to select the appropriate payoff value based on the cap condition. All of these operations are performed in quantum parallel, such that the entire payoff function is evaluated simultaneously across all possible paths. The result is stored in the amplitude of a single ancilla qubit, whose state encodes the normalized expected discounted payoff. To extract this value, the algorithm employs iterative quantum amplitude estimation, a technique that iteratively refines an estimate of the probability that the ancilla qubit is measured in the |1 state. This method requires repeated applications of a unitary operator that alternates between the state preparation circuit and a reflection about the initial state and the target state, and it achieves an estimation error that scales as O(1/N) for N queries, offering a quadratic improvement over classical Monte Carlo. The total number of queries required to achieve a target accuracy ε is determined by empirical bounds derived from recent theoretical analyses, and the depth of the resulting circuit is dominated by the cost of the state preparation and payoff evaluation steps. The entire algorithm is designed for fault-tolerant execution on a quantum computer using a Clifford+T gate set. All quantum arithmetic operations—including addition, multiplication, square root, exponential, and arcsine—are implemented using fixed-point representations, with the number of qubits allocated to each register chosen to bound the cumulative arithmetic error within the target tolerance. The T-gate depth of each operation is calculated based on known gate synthesis techniques, and all parallelizable components are executed concurrently to minimize the overall circuit depth. The use of fixed-point arithmetic ensures that numerical errors do not grow unbounded, and the pre-training of the Gaussian state loaders ensures that the dominant source of error is not in state preparation but in the finite precision of the arithmetic operations and the amplitude estimation procedure. The invention further includes a comprehensive resource estimation framework that quantifies the total number of logical qubits and T-gate depth required to price specific derivatives to a given accuracy. For a basket autocallable option with three underlying assets, five payment dates, and a knock-in put, the algorithm requires approximately 8,000 logical qubits and a total T-depth of 9.5 × 10³ for the state preparation and payoff evaluation, with an additional 5.4 × 10⁷ T-gates required for amplitude estimation, yielding a total T-depth of approximately 5.4 × 10⁷. For a TARF with a single underlying asset and 26 payment dates, the resource requirements are approximately 11,500 logical qubits and a total T-depth of 8.2 × 10⁷. These estimates assume a target total error of 2 × 10⁻³, a confidence level of 68% matching classical Monte Carlo, and a logical T-gate rate sufficient to execute the circuit in under one second. Importantly, these resource requirements are orders of magnitude lower than those of prior methods that attempted to load the price-space distribution directly, which required normalization factors that grew exponentially with the number of time steps, rendering them infeasible even for modestly sized problems. The re-parameterization method introduced here eliminates this exponential scaling entirely, making quantum advantage not merely a theoretical possibility but a practical engineering target. The invention also enables future extensibility: the variational Gaussian loaders, once trained for a given register size and precision, can be reused across any derivative pricing problem that assumes Gaussian dynamics, including those with stochastic volatility, local volatility, or multi-factor models, provided the underlying returns remain approximately normal. The payoff operator can be modularly extended to accommodate new contract types, such as Asian options, lookback options, or cliquet structures, by composing additional conditional logic and arithmetic blocks. The algorithm’s architecture is inherently scalable: increasing the number of assets or time steps increases the number of parallel Gaussian loaders and arithmetic units proportionally, without introducing new sources of error or complexity. The method is also robust to hardware imperfections: because the most resource-intensive components—the Gaussian loaders—are pre-trained and optimized offline, the online execution of the algorithm depends only on the reliable execution of fixed, well-characterized quantum circuits, reducing the burden on real-time control systems. Furthermore, the use of amplitude estimation ensures that the algorithm’s accuracy is not compromised by the stochastic nature of quantum measurement; the final result is a deterministic estimate of the expected payoff, derived from a coherent superposition of all paths, not from sampling. This distinguishes the invention from classical Monte Carlo, where each run produces a different estimate due to random sampling, and from quantum algorithms that rely on repeated measurement of individual paths, which reintroduce the same statistical noise the algorithm seeks to eliminate. The invention thus represents a complete, self-contained quantum computational framework for derivative pricing that is both theoretically grounded and practically implementable, providing the financial industry with the first clear, quantified pathway to quantum advantage in one of its most computationally demanding applications.
