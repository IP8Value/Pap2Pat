## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of functional printing and additive manufacturing of electronic and magnetic materials. More specifically, it pertains to the formulation and deposition of nickel-based precursor inks capable of being thermally reduced to form pure, aligned, template-free nickel nanowires on a variety of substrates under the influence of a weak external magnetic field during the curing process. The invention further relates to the resulting anisotropic conductive and magnetic films that exhibit directionally enhanced electrical conductivity and magnetic anisotropy, making them particularly suitable for applications in printed electronics, magnetic sensors, data storage elements, optical devices, and flexible transducers. The technology disclosed herein operates within the intersection of materials chemistry, aerosol-based deposition systems, and magnetic field engineering, enabling the creation of complex micro- and nanostructured metallic architectures without the need for high-temperature sintering, vacuum environments, or post-processing purification steps.

## BACKGROUND

The fabrication of electrically conductive and magnetically responsive features on flexible, curved, or thermally sensitive substrates has long been a challenge in the field of printed electronics. Traditional methods for producing conductive traces rely on the use of metallic nanoparticle inks, typically composed of silver, copper, or gold, which are deposited via screen printing, inkjet, or gravure techniques and subsequently sintered to form continuous conductive pathways. While these approaches have achieved commercial success in applications such as RFID tags, flexible circuits, and touch sensors, they are fundamentally limited in their ability to generate structured magnetic materials. Ferromagnetic metals such as nickel, cobalt, and iron possess intrinsic properties that make them highly desirable for magnetic sensing, shielding, and data storage applications, yet their incorporation into printable systems has been severely constrained by chemical instability, oxidation susceptibility, and the lack of a viable pathway to form high-aspect-ratio nanostructures directly on surfaces.

Previous attempts to print magnetic materials have largely focused on the use of nickel nanoparticles suspended in organic carriers. These formulations suffer from significant drawbacks, including particle agglomeration during storage, sedimentation during printing, and clogging of fine nozzles due to the high solid loading required for adequate conductivity. Moreover, the sintering of such nanoparticle inks typically results in porous, isotropic films with limited control over microstructure and no inherent magnetic anisotropy. The formation of aligned magnetic nanostructures has historically required complex, multi-step processes involving electrospinning, template-assisted electrodeposition, or lithographic patterning followed by metal deposition and template removal. These methods are not only incompatible with large-area, roll-to-roll manufacturing but also introduce significant material waste, require expensive equipment, and often involve toxic chemicals or extreme environmental conditions such as high-pressure autoclaving or inert gas atmospheres.

Precursor inks based on metal-organic decomposition (MOD) chemistry have emerged as a promising alternative to nanoparticle inks due to their molecular homogeneity, low viscosity, and absence of particulate matter. Such inks are formulated by dissolving metal salts or complexes in organic solvents, allowing them to be deposited using precision printing techniques without the risk of nozzle clogging or particle settling. However, despite their advantages in printability, MOD inks have traditionally been limited to non-magnetic metals such as silver and copper, primarily because of the extreme sensitivity of ferromagnetic transition metals like nickel to oxidation during synthesis, storage, and thermal processing. Nickel, in particular, readily forms a passivating oxide layer upon exposure to ambient air, which inhibits its reduction to the metallic state and compromises the purity and functionality of the final product. Consequently, prior art has not demonstrated a viable method for producing pure, aligned nickel nanostructures from a solution-based precursor ink under ambient conditions using a simple thermal reduction process.

Furthermore, the concept of using an external magnetic field to guide the orientation of metallic nanostructures during thermal decomposition has never been disclosed or demonstrated in the literature. While magnetic fields have been employed in the alignment of pre-formed nanowires or magnetic colloids during deposition, such techniques require the prior synthesis of the nanostructures themselves, often under harsh conditions, followed by re-dispersion and re-deposition—a process that is neither scalable nor compatible with direct printing. The notion that a magnetic field applied during the chemical reduction of a metal complex in solution can directly induce the formation of aligned nanowires, rather than merely orienting pre-existing particles, represents a fundamental departure from established principles in materials science. The discovery that such alignment occurs spontaneously during the reduction phase, with the resulting nanowires exhibiting a “string of pearls” morphology indicative of in-situ sintering along field lines, has no precedent in the scientific or patent literature.

Existing methods for producing magnetic nanostructures are also fundamentally incapable of achieving the combination of high purity, structural alignment, and anisotropic property enhancement simultaneously. For instance, electrochemical deposition of nickel nanowires requires conductive templates such as anodized aluminum oxide or polycarbonate membranes, which must be removed after deposition, leaving behind fragmented or incomplete structures. Similarly, hydrothermal synthesis of nickel nanowires demands autoclaving at elevated temperatures and pressures for extended durations, followed by washing and drying under inert atmospheres to prevent oxidation. These processes are incompatible with the integration of magnetic features onto plastic, paper, or textile substrates, which are increasingly demanded in wearable electronics and biomedical devices. Moreover, none of these methods permit the patterning of complex geometries, such as grids or spirals, with directional control over magnetic anisotropy—something that is critical for applications in magnetoresistive sensors and tunable microwave devices.

The present invention overcomes all of these limitations by introducing a novel class of nickel precursor inks that are stable under ambient conditions, printable via non-contact aerosol deposition, and capable of undergoing a chemically clean reduction to pure metallic nickel when exposed to a mild thermal profile in the presence of a weak magnetic field. The resulting structures are not merely aligned particles but are true nanowires formed through a mechanism of field-guided nucleation and sintering, yielding anisotropic electrical and magnetic properties that surpass those of any previously reported printed magnetic material. This invention enables the direct, single-step fabrication of patterned magnetic nanostructures on virtually any substrate, without the need for templates, post-processing, or specialized environments, thereby opening new avenues for the mass production of next-generation electronic and magnetic devices.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method for the direct fabrication of aligned nickel nanowires on a substrate by printing a homogeneous precursor ink and thermally reducing it in the presence of a magnetic field. The method comprises the steps of formulating a nickel-containing precursor ink by dissolving a nickel salt complex in a solvent system comprising water and a chelating agent, printing the ink onto a substrate using a non-contact deposition technique, and subjecting the printed ink to a thermal treatment at a temperature sufficient to reduce the nickel complex to elemental nickel while simultaneously applying a magnetic field oriented in a predetermined direction. The resulting structure consists of pure, template-free nickel nanowires aligned along the direction of the applied magnetic field, exhibiting enhanced electrical conductivity and magnetic anisotropy along the alignment axis. The ink is formulated to contain a nickel source, a reducing agent, a stabilizing ligand, and optional additives to control rheology and surface tension, and is capable of being printed using a variety of deposition techniques including aerosol jet printing, inkjet printing, and microextrusion. The thermal reduction occurs at a temperature between 200 and 300 degrees Celsius, during which the organic components of the complex decompose into volatile byproducts, leaving behind a residue of substantially pure metallic nickel. The application of the magnetic field during reduction induces the formation of elongated, wire-like structures composed of fused nanoparticles that follow the magnetic field lines, resulting in a morphology characterized by a “string of pearls” arrangement. The alignment of these nanowires produces a significant anisotropy in both electrical and magnetic properties, with conductivity and remanent magnetization being substantially higher along the direction of alignment than perpendicular to it. The method further includes the capability to fabricate complex, multi-layered patterns by sequentially printing and reducing ink layers with alternating magnetic field orientations, thereby creating grid-like structures with isotropic magnetic response in multiple directions. The resulting films are free of residual oxides, carbonaceous contaminants, or template materials, and exhibit electrical resistivities as low as 560 µΩ·cm, which is comparable to bulk nickel when accounting for porosity. The invention further encompasses the resulting anisotropic nickel nanowire structures, which are suitable for use in magnetic sensors, magnetoresistive devices, flexible antennas, optical polarizers, and micro-transducers. The method is performed entirely under ambient atmospheric conditions, requires no inert gas environment, and is compatible with low-cost, high-throughput printing systems, making it uniquely suited for scalable manufacturing of functional magnetic electronics on flexible, curved, or temperature-sensitive substrates.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a comprehensive and integrated method for the fabrication of aligned nickel nanowires through the thermal reduction of a specially formulated precursor ink under the influence of a magnetic field during curing. The invention encompasses not only the chemical composition of the ink but also the precise sequence of printing and reduction steps that result in the spontaneous formation of high-aspect-ratio, directionally aligned nanostructures with unprecedented purity and anisotropic functionality. The method begins with the preparation of a homogeneous solution containing a nickel salt, a chelating ligand, and a solvent system designed to stabilize the complex in ambient conditions while enabling efficient thermal decomposition. The nickel salt employed is nickel formate, chosen for its low molecular weight, high solubility in aqueous media, and favorable decomposition profile that generates a reducing atmosphere upon heating. The chelating agent is ethylenediamine, a bidentate organic ligand that coordinates with the nickel ion to form a stable, water-soluble complex, preventing premature precipitation or oxidation. This complexation is critical to maintaining the ink’s stability during storage and printing, as free nickel ions are highly susceptible to oxidation in air, forming non-conductive nickel oxide layers that would compromise the final product’s conductivity and magnetic response. The solvent system is composed primarily of distilled water, which serves as the primary medium for dissolution, while also facilitating the formation of hydrogen gas during thermal decomposition—a byproduct that acts as an in-situ reducing agent, further suppressing oxidation and ensuring the complete reduction of nickel to its metallic state. The concentration of nickel formate in the ink is maintained at approximately 5.7 weight percent, a level sufficient to yield a conductive film after reduction while remaining low enough to preserve the ink’s fluidity and compatibility with precision printing systems. To further optimize the ink’s performance, minor additives are incorporated, including polyvinylpyrrolidone, which acts as a viscosity modifier and stabilizer to prevent droplet coalescence during aerosolization, and a surfactant such as BYK 333, which reduces surface tension to improve wetting on a variety of substrates, including those with low surface energy such as polyimide or polycarbonate. The ink is filtered through a 0.2-micrometer syringe filter to remove any particulate impurities that could interfere with nozzle operation, ensuring consistent and repeatable deposition.

The printing of the ink is accomplished using a non-contact aerosol deposition system, specifically an Aerosol JetTM platform, which atomizes the liquid ink into a fine mist of droplets using ultrasonic energy generated by a piezoelectric transducer operating at frequencies between one and two megahertz. The resulting aerosol, composed of droplets ranging from 0.5 to five micrometers in diameter, is transported by a carrier gas—typically air—through a series of aerodynamic focusing lenses that compress the aerosol stream into a narrow, collimated jet. This jet is directed toward the substrate with a standoff distance of approximately five millimeters, allowing for printing on both planar and non-planar surfaces without physical contact. The sheath gas, which surrounds the aerosol stream, prevents nozzle clogging and further refines the deposition pattern by confining the droplet distribution. The substrate, which may be composed of glass, silicon, polyethylene terephthalate, polyimide, paper, or textile materials, is optionally pre-treated with atmospheric plasma to enhance wettability and promote uniform ink spreading. The printing parameters—including the flow rate of the carrier and sheath gases, the translational speed of the deposition head, and the nozzle-to-substrate distance—are held constant throughout the process to ensure reproducibility. A solid square pattern of one centimeter by one centimeter is printed using a 25-gauge dispensing tip, resulting in a wet film thickness of approximately four micrometers per layer. The ink is deposited in single or multiple layers, with each layer being cured immediately after deposition to prevent interlayer mixing and to enable the controlled construction of three-dimensional structures.

The critical innovation of the invention lies in the thermal reduction step, which is performed in the presence of a magnetic field. After printing, the substrate is placed on a heated surface, such as a benchtop hot plate or an oven, and raised to a temperature of 240 degrees Celsius, a value determined through thermogravimetric and differential thermal analysis to be the minimum required for complete decomposition of the nickel formate-ethylenediamine complex. The heating is maintained for a duration of fifteen minutes, during which the organic ligands and counterions decompose into volatile species including carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, water vapor, and molecular hydrogen. Crucially, during this thermal treatment, a homogeneous magnetic field of 250 oersteds is applied perpendicular to the plane of the substrate, with the field direction aligned along a predetermined axis. The magnetic field is generated using a pair of permanent magnets or electromagnets positioned on either side of the substrate, ensuring uniform field strength across the entire printed area. The application of the magnetic field during reduction is not merely a passive influence but an active driver of structural formation. As the nickel complex undergoes thermal decomposition, nickel atoms are liberated in a nucleation-limited process. In the absence of a magnetic field, these atoms aggregate randomly, forming a porous, isotropic film composed of spherical nanoparticles ranging from 100 to 250 nanometers in diameter, with significant necking between adjacent particles but no preferred orientation. However, in the presence of the magnetic field, the nascent nickel nuclei experience a torque that aligns their magnetic moments with the field direction. This alignment induces a preferential growth axis, causing the nanoparticles to coalesce along the field lines rather than in random directions. The result is a chain-like morphology where individual nanoparticles are fused end-to-end, forming continuous, elongated structures that resemble a “string of pearls.” These structures are not merely aggregates of aligned particles but are true nanowires, with lengths extending up to several micrometers and diameters consistent with the original nanoparticle size. The alignment is not induced by external mechanical forces or template confinement but is an intrinsic consequence of the magnetic interaction during the reduction phase, a phenomenon that has never been previously observed or described.

The resulting nickel nanowires are substantially free of oxidation, as confirmed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, which reveals only the presence of nickel and trace amounts of carbon and oxygen, attributable to residual organic decomposition products or surface contamination. The absence of nickel oxide peaks in the X-ray diffraction pattern further corroborates the purity of the metallic phase. The crystallinity of the nanowires is confirmed by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy, which reveals lattice fringes corresponding to the face-centered cubic structure of elemental nickel. The alignment of the nanowires is quantitatively assessed using two-dimensional fast Fourier transform analysis of scanning electron microscope images. In samples reduced without a magnetic field, the FFT pattern exhibits a diffuse, isotropic distribution of intensity, indicative of random particle orientation. In contrast, samples reduced under a magnetic field display two distinct, high-intensity peaks at 90 and 270 degrees, corresponding to the axis of the applied field. When multiple layers are printed with alternating magnetic field orientations—first aligned along the x-axis, then the y-axis—the resulting grid structure exhibits peaks at 0, 90, 180, 270, and 360 degrees, demonstrating the ability to program complex, multi-directional alignment patterns. The intensity of the peaks in the top layer is greater than that of the bottom layer, reflecting the higher degree of alignment achieved in the most recently deposited and reduced layer, a feature that can be exploited to engineer graded anisotropy in multilayered devices.

The electrical properties of the resulting films are measured using a four-point probe technique, which eliminates contact resistance artifacts and provides an accurate assessment of the bulk conductivity of the printed structure. The resistivity of the nickel films reduced in the absence of a magnetic field is found to be approximately 1,200 µΩ·cm, a value consistent with the porosity and discontinuous nature of the nanoparticle network. In contrast, the resistivity of the aligned nanowire structures is reduced to 800 µΩ·cm along the direction of alignment, representing a 33% improvement in conductivity. Perpendicular to the alignment direction, the resistivity increases to approximately 1,400 µΩ·cm, confirming the anisotropic nature of the conduction pathway. When a grid structure is fabricated by alternating magnetic field orientations in successive layers, the resistivity becomes nearly isotropic, with values of approximately 560 µΩ·cm in both directions. This value is remarkable, as it is significantly lower than any previously reported resistivity for printed nickel structures and approaches the theoretical limit for porous nickel networks. The improvement in conductivity is attributed to the continuous, elongated pathways formed by the aligned nanowires, which provide fewer interparticle junctions and more direct conduction paths compared to the tortuous network of randomly oriented nanoparticles. The electrical anisotropy ratio, defined as the ratio of perpendicular to parallel resistivity, reaches values greater than 1.75, indicating a strong directional dependence of conductivity that can be leveraged in anisotropic sensors and directional antennas.

The magnetic properties of the films are evaluated using a vibrating sample magnetometer, which measures the hysteresis loops along different axes relative to the alignment direction. The saturation magnetization of all samples, regardless of alignment, is measured at approximately 30 emu/g, which is in close agreement with the bulk value for pure nickel, confirming the absence of significant oxide contamination. However, the remanent magnetization and coercivity exhibit dramatic differences depending on the presence and orientation of the magnetic field during reduction. In unaligned films, the remanent magnetization is 10 emu/g, and the coercive field is 120 oersteds, characteristic of a soft magnetic material with minimal shape anisotropy. In films reduced under a unidirectional magnetic field, the remanent magnetization increases to 20 emu/g along the alignment axis, while the perpendicular direction exhibits a remanence of only 7 emu/g. This corresponds to a magnetic anisotropy ratio of nearly 3:1, a value that is directly attributable to the shape anisotropy of the nanowires. The elongated morphology of the nanowires creates a strong demagnetizing field perpendicular to their axis, which stabilizes the magnetization along the length of the wire, resulting in higher remanence and lower switching fields in the aligned direction. The coercivity in the aligned direction also increases to 180 oersteds, indicating enhanced magnetic hardness due to the pinning of domain walls at the ends of the nanowires. In the grid structure, where nanowires are aligned in two perpendicular directions, the remanent magnetization is elevated in both directions, with values of 18 emu/g along each axis, demonstrating the ability to create isotropic magnetic response in two dimensions—a feature that is highly desirable for magnetic shielding applications and multi-axis sensors.

The method of the invention is uniquely scalable and compatible with industrial manufacturing processes. The precursor ink can be prepared in bulk quantities using standard laboratory equipment and stored in sealed containers for extended periods without degradation. The aerosol printing system is commercially available and can be integrated into roll-to-roll production lines, enabling high-speed, continuous printing on flexible substrates. The thermal reduction step can be performed using infrared heaters, convection ovens, or even laser-based systems, allowing for localized curing on temperature-sensitive materials. The magnetic field can be generated using arrays of permanent magnets embedded in the printing platform or by electromagnetic coils synchronized with the printing motion, enabling dynamic control of alignment direction during multi-layer printing. The entire process requires no vacuum, no inert gas, no high-pressure reactors, and no post-reduction washing or purification steps, making it significantly more cost-effective and environmentally sustainable than existing methods for producing magnetic nanostructures. Furthermore, the ink formulation can be readily adapted for other printing techniques, including inkjet, screen printing, and microextrusion, by adjusting the viscosity and surface tension through the addition of appropriate rheological modifiers. The resulting structures are not limited to planar geometries; the non-contact nature of the printing process allows for the fabrication of nanowire arrays on curved surfaces, such as cylindrical sensors or conformal antennas, opening new possibilities for wearable and implantable electronics.

The utility of the resulting aligned nickel nanowire structures extends across a broad spectrum of applications. In the field of magnetic sensing, the high remanence and shape anisotropy of the aligned nanowires make them ideal for use in magnetoresistive sensors, where the resistance of the structure changes in response to an external magnetic field. The anisotropic conductivity enables the design of directional sensors that respond selectively to fields oriented along the nanowire axis, improving signal-to-noise ratio and reducing cross-talk. In data storage, the high coercivity and stable remanence of the aligned structures permit the creation of high-density magnetic bit arrays on flexible substrates, potentially enabling novel forms of flexible memory devices. In optical applications, the aligned nanowires act as polarization-selective elements, transmitting light polarized parallel to their axis while blocking perpendicular polarization, thereby functioning as printed optical polarizers for use in displays and imaging systems. In biomedical applications, the biocompatibility of nickel and the ability to pattern complex geometries on flexible substrates make the structures suitable for use in “lab-on-a-chip” devices, where magnetic fields can be used to manipulate cells or biomolecules along predefined pathways. The grid structures, with their isotropic magnetic response, are particularly suited for use as skin-attachable loudspeakers and microphones, where the anisotropic mechanical response of the nanowire network can be exploited to convert electrical signals into sound and vice versa. The invention further enables the fabrication of tunable inductors and antennas, where the magnetic permeability and inductance can be controlled by the density and alignment of the nanowires, allowing for the creation of reconfigurable RF components on flexible substrates.

The method described herein represents a paradigm shift in the fabrication of magnetic functional materials. It eliminates the need for complex, multi-step synthesis and purification processes, replaces hazardous and expensive equipment with simple, ambient-condition operations, and introduces a novel mechanism of field-guided nanowire formation that is both scientifically unprecedented and industrially transformative. The ability to print pure, aligned nickel nanowires with programmable orientation, enhanced conductivity, and tunable magnetic properties in a single, scalable step constitutes a breakthrough that will enable the next generation of flexible, printed, and wearable electronic and magnetic devices. The invention is not merely an incremental improvement over existing technologies but a foundational innovation that redefines the boundaries of what is possible in functional printing.

### EXAMPLE 1

In a representative embodiment of the invention, a nickel precursor ink is formulated by dissolving 0.8 grams of nickel formate, with a molecular weight of 148.74 grams per mole, in a solution composed of 4 milliliters of distilled water and 0.72 milliliters of ethylenediamine. The ethylenediamine, which serves as a chelating agent to stabilize the nickel complex in aqueous solution, is added first and stirred for five minutes to ensure complete dissolution. The nickel formate is then introduced gradually under continuous magnetic stirring at room temperature, and the mixture is stirred for an additional fifteen minutes to achieve complete complexation. The resulting solution is a clear, pale green liquid with no visible particulates, indicating the formation of a homogeneous nickel-ethylenediamine complex. To enhance the printability of the ink, 1 percent by weight of polyvinylpyrrolidone with a molecular weight of 3,500 and a K-value of 12 is added to the solution, followed by the addition of 20 microliters of a 10 percent by volume solution of BYK 333 surfactant in distilled water. The polyvinylpyrrolidone functions as a viscosity modifier, preventing droplet coalescence during aerosolization, while the surfactant reduces the surface tension of the ink to approximately 44.01 millinewtons per meter, ensuring uniform wetting on both hydrophilic and hydrophobic substrates. The ink is then filtered through a 0.2-micrometer syringe filter to remove any potential particulate contaminants that could obstruct the nozzle of the printing system. The viscosity of the final ink is measured at 3.2 centipoise, a value well within the operational range of aerosol jet printing systems, which typically require viscosities between 1 and 10 centipoise for stable droplet formation.

The filtered ink is loaded into a reservoir of a NanoJet aerosol printer, which utilizes ultrasonic atomization to generate a polydisperse aerosol of droplets with diameters ranging from 0.5 to 5 micrometers. The printer is equipped with a 25-gauge dispensing tip and a carrier gas flow rate of 1.5 liters per minute, with a sheath gas flow rate of 2.0 liters per minute to maintain nozzle integrity. The substrate, a cleaned glass microscope slide, is mounted on a three-axis motorized stage capable of precise X-Y-Z positioning. Prior to printing, the substrate is treated with atmospheric plasma for thirty seconds to increase its surface energy and improve ink adhesion. The deposition head is positioned at a standoff distance of five millimeters above the substrate, and the stage is programmed to move at a constant speed of two millimeters per second in a linear path to deposit a solid square pattern measuring one centimeter by one centimeter. A single layer of ink is printed, resulting in a wet film thickness of approximately four micrometers, as measured by optical profilometry. The printed sample is then transferred to a benchtop hot plate, which is preheated to 240 degrees Celsius. A pair of neodymium permanent magnets, each with a magnetic field strength of 250 oersteds, are positioned on either side of the substrate such that the magnetic field lines are oriented perpendicular to the plane of the substrate and aligned along the x-axis. The sample is maintained at 240 degrees Celsius for fifteen minutes, during which time the organic components of the nickel complex decompose completely, releasing volatile byproducts including carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, water vapor, and molecular hydrogen. The hydrogen gas generated in situ acts as a reducing agent, ensuring that the nickel is reduced to its metallic state without the formation of oxides. Upon cooling to room temperature, the substrate is examined using scanning electron microscopy. The resulting film exhibits a continuous network of elongated structures, each approximately 150 nanometers in diameter and several micrometers in length, arranged in parallel alignment along the x-axis. The morphology of these structures is consistent with a “string of pearls” configuration, wherein individual nanoparticles of approximately 100 to 250 nanometers in diameter are fused end-to-end, forming a continuous conductive pathway. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy confirms the presence of nickel as the dominant elemental component, with only trace amounts of carbon and oxygen detected, attributable to residual organic decomposition products. The absence of any detectable peaks corresponding to nickel oxide in the X-ray diffraction pattern confirms the purity of the metallic phase.

To assess the electrical conductivity of the structure, a four-point probe measurement is performed using a Jandel RM3000 system. The resistivity along the direction of alignment is measured at 800 microohm-centimeters, while the resistivity perpendicular to the alignment direction is measured at 1,400 microohm-centimeters, yielding an anisotropy ratio of 1.75. The saturation magnetization of the film, as determined by vibrating sample magnetometry, is 30 emu per gram, matching the theoretical value for bulk nickel. The remanent magnetization along the alignment axis is 20 emu per gram, while the perpendicular direction exhibits a remanence of only 7 emu per gram, demonstrating a magnetic anisotropy ratio of nearly 3:1. The coercivity along the alignment axis is measured at 180 oersteds, significantly higher than the 120 oersteds observed in unaligned control samples, indicating enhanced magnetic hardness due to shape anisotropy. The alignment of the nanowires is quantified using two-dimensional fast Fourier transform analysis of the scanning electron microscope images. The FFT pattern displays two intense peaks at 90 and 270 degrees, corresponding to the direction of the applied magnetic field, with a full width at half maximum of less than 15 degrees, indicating a high degree of alignment. In a second iteration of the experiment, a second layer of ink is printed on top of the first, with the magnetic field now oriented along the y-axis. After a second thermal reduction cycle, the resulting grid structure exhibits a uniform distribution of nanowires in both the x- and y-directions. The four-point probe measurements reveal nearly isotropic resistivity, with values of 560 microohm-centimeters in both directions, while the magnetic hysteresis loops show elevated remanence in both axes, with values of 18 emu per gram. This demonstrates the ability to program multi-directional magnetic and electrical anisotropy through sequential printing and field-controlled reduction. The entire process, from ink formulation to final characterization, is completed under ambient atmospheric conditions, without the use of inert gas, vacuum, or post-processing purification steps. The resulting structures are mechanically robust, adhering strongly to the glass substrate, and exhibit no degradation upon exposure to ambient humidity or temperature cycling. This example illustrates the full scope of the invention, demonstrating the ability to fabricate pure, aligned, anisotropic nickel nanowire structures in a single, scalable, and industrially viable process.
