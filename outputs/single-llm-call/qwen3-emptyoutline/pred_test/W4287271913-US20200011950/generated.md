## BACKGROUND

The diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment planning for malignant brain tumors, particularly glioblastoma, have long relied on visual assessment of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans, supplemented by clinical history and molecular biomarkers. Despite advances in neuroimaging and computational analysis, the ability to accurately predict patient survival remains limited, largely due to the inherent heterogeneity of tumor biology and the limitations of conventional imaging biomarkers. Traditional approaches to tumor characterization focus primarily on the grossly visible tumor mass, including the contrast-enhancing region and the surrounding T2/FLAIR hyperintense zone, which are assumed to represent the extent of disease. However, these regions capture only a fraction of the biological activity associated with tumor progression. Emerging evidence suggests that the impact of glioblastoma extends far beyond the anatomical boundaries delineated by standard imaging protocols, infiltrating and deforming adjacent brain tissue in ways that are often imperceptible to the human eye but measurable through quantitative analysis.

The concept of a “tumor field effect” has gained increasing recognition in oncology, particularly in the context of solid tumors such as glioblastoma. This phenomenon refers to the systemic alterations in tissue structure, function, and molecular composition that occur in regions appearing normal on conventional imaging but are nonetheless influenced by the presence of the tumor. In glioblastoma, these alterations manifest as mechanical distortions of the surrounding parenchyma due to mass effect—pressure exerted by the expanding tumor mass that displaces, compresses, and stretches adjacent neural structures. These deformations are not merely passive consequences of tumor growth; they reflect active biomechanical interactions between proliferating tumor cells and the viscoelastic properties of brain tissue, potentially serving as a physical signature of tumor aggressiveness. The degree of tissue displacement, particularly in regions extending several millimeters beyond the visible tumor margin, has been correlated with clinical outcomes, including survival and neurological deterioration, yet these biomechanical cues have remained largely unexploited in predictive modeling.

Prior efforts to improve prognostic accuracy have focused on radiomic analysis—extracting large numbers of quantitative features from medical images to characterize texture, shape, intensity distribution, and spatial patterns. Radiomic features derived from the enhancing tumor core and peritumoral edema have demonstrated moderate success in predicting survival, with some models achieving concordance indices above 0.70 in retrospective cohorts. However, these models are often limited by their reliance on textural heterogeneity within regions that are themselves subject to partial volume effects, segmentation variability, and scanner-specific intensity variations. Moreover, the vast majority of radiomic studies have ignored the biomechanical context in which these textures exist, treating the tumor and its immediate surroundings as isolated entities rather than components of a dynamically deformed tissue system. Deep learning approaches, particularly convolutional neural networks trained on large image datasets, have shown promise in capturing complex, non-linear relationships in imaging data. However, these models frequently suffer from poor generalizability across multi-institutional datasets due to differences in imaging protocols, hardware, and acquisition parameters. Their performance often degrades significantly when applied to external validation cohorts, limiting their clinical utility.

Furthermore, clinical variables such as age, tumor volume, molecular markers (e.g., MGMT methylation, IDH mutation status), and extent of surgical resection, while informative, are not universally available across all clinical settings and often lack the sensitivity to capture subtle, early indicators of tumor aggressiveness. The absence of robust, image-based biomarkers that integrate both morphological and biomechanical dimensions of tumor behavior has created a critical gap in the ability to stratify patients accurately at the time of diagnosis. There is a clear and unmet need for a comprehensive imaging descriptor that transcends the limitations of conventional radiomics and deep learning by incorporating the physical consequences of tumor growth—namely, the deformation of normal-appearing brain tissue—as a quantifiable, prognostic signal.

The present invention addresses this gap by introducing a novel radiomic-deformation and textural heterogeneity descriptor, referred to as r-DepTH, which simultaneously captures two distinct but complementary aspects of tumor biology: the local textural heterogeneity within the tumor and peritumoral regions, and the global biomechanical deformation of the surrounding brain parenchyma induced by tumor mass effect. Unlike prior approaches that analyze tumor regions in isolation, r-DepTH explicitly models the tumor as a mechanical agent that distorts its environment, and it quantifies these distortions using diffeomorphic image registration between patient scans and a healthy brain atlas. The resulting deformation field is analyzed in concentric annular bands extending from the tumor margin outward, capturing the spatial gradient of tissue displacement. These biomechanical features are then integrated with established textural features derived from a three-dimensional gradient-based texture descriptor known as COLLAGE, which measures the alignment and co-occurrence of local intensity gradients within the tumor and peritumoral regions. By combining these two modalities—morphological texture and biomechanical deformation—the r-DepTH descriptor provides a more holistic representation of the tumor’s biological footprint, encompassing not only what the tumor looks like but also how it physically reshapes its surroundings.

This integrated approach is grounded in the hypothesis that more aggressive tumors exert greater mechanical stress on the surrounding brain tissue, resulting in more pronounced and spatially extensive deformations, which in turn correlate with worse clinical outcomes. The invention leverages advanced image registration techniques to isolate these deformations, ensuring that intensity variations within the tumor itself do not confound the measurement of tissue displacement. By applying a constrained, diffeomorphic transformation that excludes the tumor volume from the registration process, the method ensures that the resulting deformation field reflects only the structural alterations in the normal-appearing brain tissue. The resulting feature set, comprising over three hundred distinct quantitative measures, is then subjected to a supervised learning framework using LASSO-cox regression to identify the most predictive combination of features for survival stratification. The resulting risk score enables the classification of patients into high-risk and low-risk groups with statistically significant differences in overall survival, validated across independent multi-institutional cohorts.

The significance of this invention lies not merely in the addition of a new feature type to existing radiomic pipelines, but in the fundamental shift it represents in how tumor biology is interpreted through imaging. Rather than viewing the tumor as a discrete lesion to be segmented and characterized in isolation, r-DepTH treats the tumor as a dynamic, mechanical entity whose influence radiates outward, leaving a measurable signature in the surrounding tissue. This paradigm shift enables the extraction of prognostic information from regions previously considered irrelevant to tumor assessment, thereby unlocking a previously untapped source of biological insight. The invention is applicable to standard clinical MRI sequences—T1-weighted post-contrast, T2-weighted, and FLAIR—and requires no additional scanning protocols, making it readily translatable to routine clinical practice. Furthermore, its robustness to scanner variability, demonstrated through validation across multiple institutions, positions it as a viable tool for widespread adoption in both research and clinical settings. The invention thus represents a significant advancement in the field of neuro-oncologic imaging, offering a novel, non-invasive, and computationally rigorous method for predicting survival in glioblastoma patients with superior accuracy and generalizability compared to existing approaches.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention disclosed herein is a method and system for predicting patient survival in glioblastoma through the computation and application of a novel integrated radiomic-deformation and textural heterogeneity descriptor, referred to as r-DepTH. This descriptor is derived from routine clinical magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data and combines two distinct classes of quantitative features: biomechanical deformation features extracted from the normal-appearing brain tissue surrounding the tumor, and textural heterogeneity features extracted from the tumor and peritumoral regions. The method is implemented as a computational pipeline that processes multi-sequence MRI scans without requiring additional imaging protocols, specialized hardware, or invasive procedures. The resulting r-DepTH descriptor is employed within a supervised machine learning framework to generate a continuous survival risk score, which is then used to stratify patients into high-risk and low-risk groups based on their predicted overall survival. The invention is designed for use in clinical decision support systems, research databases, and prospective clinical trials, providing a standardized, reproducible, and objective method for prognostication that complements existing clinical and molecular assessments.

The method begins with the acquisition of pre-operative, multi-sequence MRI data from patients diagnosed with glioblastoma. The imaging protocol includes three standard sequences: gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted (Gd-T1w), T2-weighted (T2w), and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences. These sequences are obtained from a variety of clinical MRI scanners operating at 1.5 Tesla or higher, and are acquired as part of routine clinical care. The images are then preprocessed to ensure consistency across subjects and scanners. First, the Gd-T1w, T2w, and FLAIR images for each patient are co-registered to a standardized anatomical reference space, specifically the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI152) template, using a symmetric normalization (SyN) algorithm implemented in the Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs) software suite. This non-rigid registration aligns the patient’s brain anatomy with the atlas while preserving local tissue relationships. Simultaneously, skull stripping is performed during this registration process, removing non-brain tissues such as the scalp and skull to isolate the intracranial volume. Following co-registration, a non-parametric non-uniform intensity normalization technique is applied to correct for intensity inhomogeneities introduced by scanner-specific field inhomogeneities, ensuring that intensity values reflect true tissue properties rather than acquisition artifacts.

The next step involves the manual segmentation of tumor compartments by trained neuro-radiologists. Two distinct regions are delineated for each patient: the enhancing lesion, which corresponds to the region of blood-brain barrier disruption and active tumor proliferation, and the peritumoral hyperintense region, which corresponds to vasogenic edema and infiltrative tumor cells visible on T2w and FLAIR sequences. The enhancing lesion is segmented using the Gd-T1w sequence, while the peritumoral region is defined using the combined information from T2w and FLAIR sequences. Segmentation is performed manually using a validated annotation tool within the 3D Slicer software platform. To ensure accuracy and reproducibility, a tiered annotation protocol is employed, wherein two independent raters perform initial segmentations, and any discrepancies are resolved by a senior neuro-radiologist with over a decade of clinical experience. The resulting binary masks define the intra-tumoral region (I_T) and the peritumoral region (I_P). The remaining brain tissue, excluding these two regions, is designated as the brain around tumor (BAT) region (I_B), which constitutes the domain in which biomechanical deformation features are computed.

To quantify the biomechanical deformation of the BAT region, a diffeomorphic image registration is performed between the patient’s pre-processed MRI volume and a healthy, age-matched brain atlas (I_Atlas). The registration is performed using the SyN algorithm in ANTs, which employs a Lagrangian diffeomorphic framework to ensure that the transformation is smooth, invertible, and topologically consistent—properties essential for accurately modeling tissue displacement without introducing artificial folding or tearing. Crucially, the registration is constrained by a tumor-exclusive mask (I_mask), which excludes the enhancing lesion and peritumoral regions from the similarity metric calculation. This constraint ensures that the registration algorithm optimizes for structural alignment only in regions of normal-appearing brain tissue, thereby isolating the deformation caused by tumor mass effect rather than intensity differences within the tumor itself. The resulting forward transformation (T_r) maps the atlas space to the patient’s native space, while the inverse transformation (T_r^(-1)) maps the patient’s brain back to the atlas space, yielding a voxel-wise displacement vector field that quantifies the magnitude and direction of tissue deformation at every point in the BAT region.

From this inverse displacement field, a scalar deformation magnitude map is computed for each voxel in the BAT region. The magnitude at each voxel is determined by calculating the Euclidean norm of the three-dimensional displacement vector, resulting in a single scalar value that represents the total displacement experienced by that tissue element due to tumor-induced mass effect. To capture the spatial gradient of this deformation, the BAT region is partitioned into a series of concentric, uniformly sized annular sub-volumes (I_Bj), each extending radially outward from the tumor boundary. The annular bands are defined such that each has a thickness of five millimeters, beginning at five millimeters from the tumor margin and extending outward to a maximum distance of sixty millimeters, resulting in twelve distinct annular bands (j = 1 to 12). This spacing is chosen based on clinical guidelines that recommend a five-millimeter margin as a safe target volume for radiotherapy planning in glioblastoma, ensuring that the analysis captures the biologically relevant spatial extent of tumor influence. For each annular band, first-order statistical features are computed from the distribution of deformation magnitudes across all voxels within that band. These features include the mean, median, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis of the deformation magnitude values. The selection of these five statistics ensures a comprehensive characterization of the central tendency, dispersion, and shape of the deformation distribution, capturing both average displacement and the presence of extreme or asymmetric deformation patterns. The resulting feature set for the BAT region consists of sixty features (12 bands × 5 statistics), collectively denoted as F_B.

Simultaneously, textural heterogeneity features are extracted from the tumor and peritumoral regions using the three-dimensional Co-occurrence of Local Anisotropic Gradient Orientations (COLLAGE) descriptor. COLLAGE is a gradient-based texture analysis method that quantifies the spatial organization of intensity gradients within a local neighborhood, capturing subtle patterns of anisotropy and alignment that are not detectable through conventional intensity-based radiomic features. For each voxel within the enhancing lesion (I_T) and peritumoral region (I_P), the three-dimensional intensity gradient is computed in the x, y, and z directions using a Sobel filter. A 3D window centered on each voxel is then used to construct a gradient matrix F, from which two principal gradient orientations—θ(c) and φ(c)—are derived using eigenvalue decomposition. These orientations represent the dominant directions of intensity change in the local tissue microstructure. Two separate co-occurrence matrices, M_θ and M_φ, are then computed, each capturing the frequency with which pairs of gradient orientations occur at a specified spatial distance and direction within the local neighborhood. From each co-occurrence matrix, thirteen Haralick texture features are computed, including contrast, correlation, energy, homogeneity, entropy, and other higher-order statistical measures that describe the spatial arrangement of gradient orientations. These features are computed for every voxel within I_T and I_P, and then aggregated using first-order statistics—mean, median, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis—to produce a compact feature vector for each region. The resulting feature sets for the enhancing lesion and peritumoral region each consist of sixty-five features (13 Haralick statistics × 5 summary statistics), yielding a combined COLLAGE feature set of 130 features, denoted as F_T and F_P, respectively.

The r-DepTH descriptor is then constructed by concatenating the deformation feature set F_B, the enhancing lesion COLLAGE feature set F_T, and the peritumoral COLLAGE feature set F_P. The resulting descriptor contains a total of 320 features (60 + 130 + 130), each representing a quantifiable aspect of tumor biology—either the mechanical deformation of surrounding tissue or the textural heterogeneity within the tumor and its immediate vicinity. This integrated descriptor is designed to capture both the local, microstructural properties of the tumor and the global, macrostructural consequences of its growth. The descriptor is computed for every patient in the cohort and is stored as a high-dimensional numerical vector for subsequent analysis.

To derive a clinically actionable survival risk score, the r-DepTH descriptor is subjected to a supervised feature selection and modeling pipeline. The feature selection is performed using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) algorithm in conjunction with a Cox proportional hazards regression model. The LASSO method is chosen for its ability to simultaneously reduce model complexity by eliminating non-informative features and to mitigate overfitting by applying an L1 penalty to the regression coefficients. The Cox model is employed because it is specifically designed for survival analysis, allowing the estimation of hazard ratios while accounting for censored observations—patients who were lost to follow-up or who survived beyond the study period without experiencing the event of interest (death). The LASSO-Cox model is trained on a training cohort (S_t) of patients with known overall survival times, and it identifies the subset of r-DepTH features that are most strongly associated with survival outcomes. The selected features are then used to construct a linear risk score (Risc) for each patient, calculated as the weighted sum of the selected features, where the weights are the coefficients estimated by the LASSO-Cox model. The risk score is continuous and represents the relative risk of death for each patient, with higher scores indicating a greater predicted risk of early mortality.

To enable clinical stratification, the optimal threshold for dichotomizing patients into high-risk and low-risk groups is determined using X-tile software, which performs a grid search over all possible risk score thresholds to identify the value that maximizes the log-rank statistic between the two resulting groups. This threshold is applied to the training cohort to define the high-risk and low-risk groups, and the resulting survival curves are evaluated using Kaplan-Meier analysis and the log-rank test to confirm statistical significance. The performance of the risk model is quantified using the concordance index (C-index), which measures the proportion of all possible patient pairs for which the predicted risk score correctly ranks the order of survival events, and the hazard ratio (HR) with its 95% confidence interval (CI), which quantifies the relative risk of death between the high-risk and low-risk groups. The same model, including the selected features and the estimated coefficients, is then applied to an independent validation cohort (S_v) to assess generalizability. The risk scores for the validation cohort are computed using the identical formula derived from the training cohort, and survival differences between the high-risk and low-risk groups are evaluated using the same statistical methods.

The invention further includes a system for implementing the r-DepTH method in a clinical or research setting. The system comprises a computer-readable storage medium containing executable instructions that, when executed by one or more processors, cause the system to perform the steps of the method. The system includes a data acquisition module for receiving multi-sequence MRI data, a preprocessing module for co-registration, skull stripping, and intensity normalization, a segmentation module for defining the tumor and peritumoral regions, a deformation computation module for generating the displacement field and annular band statistics, a texture analysis module for computing COLLAGE features, a descriptor construction module for concatenating the feature sets, and a risk prediction module for applying the LASSO-Cox model to generate a survival risk score. The system may be integrated into a hospital’s picture archiving and communication system (PACS), a radiology workstation, or a cloud-based analytics platform, enabling automated computation of the r-DepTH descriptor and risk score immediately following image acquisition. The output of the system is a quantitative risk score and a binary classification (high-risk or low-risk) that can be displayed to clinicians alongside standard imaging reports, aiding in treatment planning, patient counseling, and enrollment in clinical trials.

The invention has been validated using a retrospective cohort of 207 glioblastoma patients from three independent institutions, with MRI data acquired on scanners from multiple vendors and at varying field strengths. The training cohort consisted of 145 patients, and the validation cohort consisted of 62 patients. The median overall survival in the training cohort was 14.2 months, and in the validation cohort, it was 13.8 months. The r-DepTH descriptor, when used in conjunction with the LASSO-Cox model, identified nine features that were most predictive of survival. These features included skewness and kurtosis of deformation magnitude in the fifth and tenth annular bands, as well as skewness of the sum variance and kurtosis of energy from the COLLAGE features in the enhancing lesion and peritumoral regions. The resulting risk score demonstrated a highly significant difference in survival between the high-risk and low-risk groups in the training cohort (p = 3.5 × 10^(-7)), with a C-index of 0.78 and a hazard ratio of 2.1 (95% CI: 1.6–2.8). In the independent validation cohort, the same model maintained strong predictive performance, with a p-value of 0.0024, a C-index of 0.75, and a hazard ratio of 1.94 (95% CI: 1.0–3.0). These results significantly outperformed models based on clinical variables alone, models using only COLLAGE features from the tumor and peritumoral regions, models using only deformation features from the BAT region, and state-of-the-art radiomic and deep learning approaches. The comparative radiomic model, which extracted over four thousand features using CapTk software, achieved a C-index of 0.75 in the training cohort but failed to generalize to the validation cohort (C-index = 0.60, p = 0.84). Similarly, a deep learning model based on a pre-trained convolutional neural network achieved a C-index of 0.80 in the training cohort but performed no better than random chance in the validation cohort (C-index = 0.50, p = 0.54). In contrast, the r-DepTH model demonstrated consistent performance across both cohorts, underscoring its robustness and generalizability.

The invention further includes a method for interpreting the biological significance of the selected features. For example, the high skewness of deformation magnitude in the fifth and tenth annular bands suggests that aggressive tumors induce asymmetric, non-uniform displacement of surrounding tissue, with a greater proportion of voxels experiencing extreme deformation in one direction. This may reflect localized areas of intense tumor infiltration or mechanical compression of critical white matter tracts. Similarly, the high kurtosis of COLLAGE energy features indicates the presence of rare, high-intensity gradient patterns that are spatially clustered, potentially representing microstructural disorganization associated with invasive tumor fronts. The fact that these features are derived from regions beyond the visible tumor margin supports the hypothesis that the biomechanical field effect is a quantifiable, prognostic signature of tumor aggressiveness.

The invention is not limited to glioblastoma and may be extended to other solid tumors that exhibit a field effect, including but not limited to breast cancer, lung cancer, prostate cancer, and hepatocellular carcinoma. The core methodology—using diffeomorphic registration to quantify tissue deformation in normal-appearing regions and integrating it with textural heterogeneity features—is broadly applicable to any organ system where tumors exert mechanical pressure on surrounding tissue. The method may also be adapted to incorporate additional modalities, such as diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) or magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS), to further enrich the feature set. Future iterations of the invention may include the incorporation of directional (phase) information from the displacement field, enabling the analysis of not only the magnitude but also the orientation of tissue deformation, which may provide additional insight into the directionality of tumor invasion.

The invention represents a paradigm shift in tumor imaging biomarker development by moving beyond the tumor as a discrete lesion to consider the tumor as a systemic mechanical agent whose influence extends into the surrounding tissue. By integrating biomechanical and textural features into a single, unified descriptor, the r-DepTH method captures a more complete picture of tumor biology than any existing approach. The invention is non-invasive, fully automated, compatible with standard clinical imaging protocols, and demonstrably superior in predictive accuracy and generalizability to existing methods. It provides clinicians with a powerful, quantitative tool for risk stratification that can inform treatment decisions, guide patient counseling, and improve the design of clinical trials. The invention is therefore a significant advancement in the field of neuro-oncology and medical imaging, offering a novel, robust, and clinically translatable method for predicting survival in patients with glioblastoma and potentially other solid tumors.
