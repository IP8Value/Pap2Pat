# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of biomedical research and translational medicine, specifically to the development of non-human animal models for the study of retinal pathologies characterized by disruption of glial homeostasis, blood-retinal barrier dysfunction, and neurovascular coupling failure. More particularly, the invention concerns a genetically modified non-human mammalian model that lacks functional expression of the dystrophin isoform Dp71, thereby exhibiting constitutive and reproducible alterations in Müller glial cell physiology, including mislocalization of potassium and water channel proteins, impaired osmotic regulation, and increased vascular permeability. This model serves as a robust and physiologically relevant platform for investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying retinal edema, diabetic retinopathy, retinal vein occlusion, and other ischemic or inflammatory retinal diseases. The invention further encompasses diagnostic methods for detecting early glial dysfunction prior to vascular pathology, as well as therapeutic methods and pharmaceutical compositions designed to restore glial channel localization, normalize water transport, reduce vascular leakage, and mitigate neuroinflammatory cascades in the retina. The utility of this model extends to high-throughput drug screening, preclinical validation of neuroprotective agents, and the development of biomarkers for early-stage retinal disease.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Retinal diseases such as diabetic retinopathy, retinal vein occlusion, and age-related macular degeneration represent leading causes of irreversible vision loss worldwide. Despite decades of research, therapeutic interventions remain largely reactive, targeting late-stage vascular abnormalities such as neovascularization and vascular leakage, while failing to address the underlying glial dysfunction that precedes and often drives these pathological changes. The retina, as a highly organized neural tissue, relies critically on the structural and functional integrity of Müller glial cells—the principal glial population that spans the entire thickness of the neural retina. These cells perform a multitude of essential roles, including the spatial buffering of extracellular potassium ions, the regulation of water homeostasis, the recycling of neurotransmitters, and the maintenance of the blood-retinal barrier (BRB), a specialized interface that restricts the passage of plasma components into the retinal parenchyma.

Central to these functions are two key membrane proteins: the inwardly rectifying potassium channel Kir4.1 and the aquaporin-4 water channel (AQP4). In healthy retinas, both Kir4.1 and AQP4 are highly polarized within Müller cell endfeet, where they cluster at the interface with retinal blood vessels and the inner limiting membrane. This precise subcellular localization is not accidental but is orchestrated by a macromolecular protein complex anchored to the cytoskeleton, of which the dystrophin isoform Dp71 is a critical component. Dp71, a product of the Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) gene, is expressed almost exclusively in the central nervous system and is enriched in Müller cell endfeet, where it interacts with a suite of dystrophin-associated proteins (DAPs), including β-dystroglycan and syntrophins, to form a scaffold that tethers Kir4.1 and AQP4 to specific membrane microdomains. Disruption of this scaffold leads to lateral diffusion and mislocalization of these channels, impairing their ability to mediate efficient potassium clearance and water efflux.

Prior studies in animal models have demonstrated that experimental retinal detachment, ischemia-reperfusion injury, or diabetic conditions induce reactive gliosis, characterized by upregulation of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), downregulation of AQP4, and redistribution of Kir4.1 from endfeet to the entire cell membrane. These changes correlate with reduced potassium conductance, impaired volume regulation, and increased vascular permeability—hallmarks of retinal edema. However, these models are inherently variable, transient, and often require invasive surgical procedures that introduce confounding inflammatory responses. Moreover, the temporal sequence of events—whether glial dysfunction is a cause or consequence of vascular damage—remains unresolved due to the lack of a genetically defined model that recapitulates the early glial pathology without secondary injury.

Recent evidence from studies on Dp71-null mice has revealed that the absence of Dp71 results in constitutive mislocalization of Kir4.1 and AQP4, even in the absence of external insult. These mice exhibit no overt retinal degeneration under baseline conditions, yet they display a strikingly increased permeability of the blood-retinal barrier, heightened susceptibility to osmotic stress, and altered electrophysiological properties of Müller cells that mirror those observed in diseased human retinas. Notably, retinal detachment in these animals fails to induce further alterations in channel localization, suggesting that Dp71 deficiency alone is sufficient to establish a pathological state equivalent to that induced by injury in wild-type animals. This observation implies that Dp71 is not merely a passive structural element but a master regulator of glial channel organization and function.

Despite these compelling findings, no prior patent or published model has exploited Dp71 deficiency as a stable, heritable, and clinically relevant platform for studying retinal edema or for screening therapeutics aimed at restoring glial homeostasis. Existing animal models of diabetic retinopathy rely on chemical induction (e.g., streptozotocin), genetic manipulation of metabolic pathways, or surgical trauma, all of which introduce systemic complications, delayed onset, or inconsistent phenotypes. Furthermore, no diagnostic method has been developed to detect glial channel mislocalization as an early biomarker of retinal disease prior to vascular leakage. Similarly, no therapeutic strategy has been designed to specifically target the Dp71-DAP-Kir4.1-AQP4 axis to restore glial function and prevent neurovascular uncoupling.

The present invention fills this critical gap by providing a genetically defined, non-human mammalian model that exhibits a consistent, reproducible, and physiologically accurate phenotype of glial dysfunction, vascular leakage, and osmotic instability—phenotypes that are directly translatable to human retinal diseases. The model is not merely a tool for observation but a platform for intervention, enabling the identification of molecular targets, the validation of pharmacological agents, and the development of diagnostic assays based on glial biomarkers. The invention further provides methods for diagnosing early-stage retinal pathology through detection of Dp71 pathway disruption and for treating such pathology by restoring channel localization, inhibiting inflammatory mediators, or enhancing glial resilience. This represents a paradigm shift in retinal disease modeling—from reactive vascular targeting to proactive glial restoration.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel non-human mammalian model characterized by the genetic deletion or functional inactivation of the dystrophin isoform Dp71, resulting in constitutive mislocalization of the inwardly rectifying potassium channel Kir4.1 and the water channel aquaporin-4 (AQP4) within Müller glial cells, impaired osmotic regulation of these cells, and increased permeability of the blood-retinal barrier. This model exhibits a stable, heritable, and reproducible phenotype that mirrors the early glial dysfunction observed in human retinal diseases such as diabetic retinopathy, retinal vein occlusion, and ischemic retinopathies, without requiring external injury or metabolic induction. The absence of Dp71 disrupts the dystrophin-associated protein complex that normally anchors Kir4.1 and AQP4 to the endfoot membrane domains of Müller cells, leading to their diffuse distribution along the entire glial process, reduced potassium conductance, and diminished capacity for water efflux. Consequently, Müller cells in this model display exaggerated swelling under hypotonic conditions, a phenomenon that is mediated by inflammatory pathways involving phospholipase A2 and cyclooxygenase, and which is not observed in wild-type animals under baseline conditions. Furthermore, the blood-retinal barrier in this model is significantly more permeable than in control animals, even in the absence of vascular pathology, indicating that glial dysfunction can precede and potentially drive vascular leakage.

The invention further encompasses diagnostic methods for identifying early-stage retinal disease by detecting the presence or absence of Dp71 expression, the subcellular localization pattern of Kir4.1 and AQP4, or the expression levels of downstream inflammatory mediators such as prostaglandins or arachidonic acid metabolites in retinal tissue or ocular fluids. These diagnostic methods may be applied to human patients through non-invasive imaging techniques, molecular assays of aqueous or vitreous humor, or genetic screening for mutations or epigenetic modifications affecting the DMD gene locus. The invention also provides therapeutic methods for treating retinal edema and neurovascular dysfunction by administering agents that restore the expression or localization of Dp71, stabilize the DAP complex, enhance Kir4.1 and AQP4 clustering, inhibit inflammatory pathways, or modulate osmotic balance in Müller cells. Such therapeutic agents include small molecules, peptides, gene therapy vectors, antisense oligonucleotides, or biologics that target the Dp71-DAP-Kir4.1-AQP4 axis or its downstream effectors. Additionally, the invention provides pharmaceutical compositions comprising one or more such therapeutic agents in pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, formulated for intravitreal, topical, or systemic delivery, with the purpose of preventing or reversing glial dysfunction and restoring blood-retinal barrier integrity.

The non-human model of the invention is particularly valuable for preclinical drug development, as it allows for the evaluation of therapeutic efficacy in a system where the primary pathology is glial, not vascular, thereby enabling the identification of compounds that act upstream of traditional anti-VEGF or anti-inflammatory therapies. The model further enables the discovery of novel biomarkers for early disease detection, the elucidation of pathogenic cascades linking glial dysfunction to vascular leakage, and the development of personalized therapeutic strategies based on the molecular profile of glial channel expression. Unlike existing models that require surgical induction or metabolic manipulation, this model is genetically stable, non-invasive, and exhibits a consistent phenotype across generations, making it ideal for longitudinal studies, high-throughput screening, and mechanistic investigations. The invention thus represents a transformative advance in the field of retinal neuroscience and ophthalmic therapeutics, providing a robust platform for the development of next-generation diagnostics and therapeutics aimed at preserving vision by targeting the glial root cause of retinal disease.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Non Human Model According to the Invention

The non-human model according to the invention is a genetically modified mammal, preferably a mouse, rat, or rabbit, in which the gene encoding the dystrophin isoform Dp71 has been disrupted, deleted, or otherwise rendered non-functional, resulting in the complete absence or severe reduction of Dp71 protein expression in Müller glial cells of the retina. This disruption may be achieved through homologous recombination, CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene editing, or other targeted genetic techniques that eliminate the specific exon or promoter region responsible for Dp71 transcription while preserving the expression of other dystrophin isoforms such as Dp427, Dp140, or Dp116. The genetic modification is stably inherited across generations, allowing for the establishment of a homogeneous, reproducible, and scalable animal colony. The model is characterized by the absence of Dp71 protein in the endfeet of Müller cells, as confirmed by immunohistochemical staining and Western blot analysis of retinal lysates, and this absence is not compensated by the upregulation of related cytoskeletal proteins such as utrophin, which may be present at elevated levels but does not restore the functional organization of Kir4.1 or AQP4.

In this model, the normal polarized expression of Kir4.1 and AQP4 is disrupted, with both proteins exhibiting diffuse localization along the entire length of Müller cell processes rather than being concentrated at the vitread endfeet and perivascular domains. This mislocalization is not accompanied by a reduction in total protein expression levels of Kir4.1, but is associated with a significant decrease in AQP4 protein abundance and a corresponding reduction in water permeability. Electrophysiological recordings from isolated Müller cells reveal a diminished inward rectifying potassium current density, despite normal total membrane capacitance, indicating that the number of functional channels per unit membrane area is reduced due to their dispersion away from the specialized membrane domains where they are most effective. The membrane potential of Müller cells remains relatively stable under baseline conditions, but the cells exhibit an exaggerated response to osmotic challenge, swelling to a significantly greater extent than cells from wild-type animals when exposed to hypotonic solutions. This swelling is not prevented by the application of potassium channel blockers such as barium chloride, suggesting that the underlying mechanism involves dysregulated water transport rather than ion imbalance alone.

The model further exhibits a constitutively increased permeability of the blood-retinal barrier, as demonstrated by the extravasation of Evans blue dye or fluorescently labeled albumin into the retinal parenchyma following intravenous administration, even in the absence of inflammation, hyperglycemia, or vascular injury. This increased permeability is not due to structural defects in endothelial tight junctions but is instead a consequence of glial dysfunction, as evidenced by the fact that transplantation of wild-type Müller cells into the retinas of Dp71-null animals restores barrier integrity, while transplantation of Dp71-null Müller cells into wild-type animals induces barrier leakage. The model also displays elevated levels of inflammatory mediators such as prostaglandin E2, arachidonic acid, and cyclooxygenase-2 metabolites in retinal tissue, which contribute to the osmotic instability of Müller cells. Inhibition of phospholipase A2 or cyclooxygenase with pharmacological agents such as 4-bromophenacyl bromide or indomethacin significantly reduces Müller cell swelling, confirming the involvement of these pathways in the pathological phenotype.

Importantly, the Dp71-null model does not exhibit spontaneous retinal degeneration, photoreceptor loss, or significant alterations in visual function under baseline conditions, distinguishing it from models of inherited retinal dystrophies. This feature is critical, as it allows for the study of glial dysfunction in isolation, without confounding neuronal loss or compensatory remodeling. The absence of overt degeneration also enables the model to be used for long-term studies of disease progression, therapeutic intervention, and recovery. The model may be further characterized by the presence of mild gliosis, as indicated by elevated levels of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) in Müller cells, but this is not as pronounced as in injury-induced models and does not correlate with neuronal damage. The model is therefore not a model of neurodegeneration but a model of glial dysregulation, making it uniquely suited for studying the early, pre-vascular phase of retinal diseases.

The non-human model may be generated using any mammalian species in which the DMD gene is sufficiently homologous to humans to permit conservation of Dp71 function and interaction with dystrophin-associated proteins. The preferred embodiment is the mouse, specifically the C57BL/6 background, due to its well-characterized genetics, availability of transgenic tools, and established protocols for retinal analysis. The genetic modification may be introduced via embryonic stem cell manipulation, pronuclear injection, or somatic cell nuclear transfer, and may be maintained as a homozygous knockout line to ensure complete absence of Dp71 expression. The model may also be engineered to carry reporter constructs such as GFP under the control of the Dp71 promoter to allow for real-time visualization of Dp71 expression dynamics in live tissue. The model may be crossed with other transgenic lines, such as those expressing fluorescent markers in endothelial cells, microglia, or neurons, to enable multi-cellular analysis of neurovascular interactions. The model may also be subjected to environmental stressors such as hyperglycemia, hypoxia, or elevated intraocular pressure to study the interaction between genetic predisposition and acquired risk factors in the development of retinal pathology.

### Uses of the Non Human Model According to the Invention

The non-human model according to the invention serves as a powerful and indispensable tool for a wide range of applications in biomedical research, drug discovery, and clinical translation. Its primary utility lies in its ability to recapitulate the earliest stages of retinal disease—those driven by glial dysfunction—before the onset of vascular leakage, inflammation, or neuronal damage. This makes the model uniquely suited for identifying novel therapeutic targets that act upstream of conventional anti-VEGF or anti-inflammatory strategies, which often fail to halt disease progression when administered at later stages. The model may be employed in high-throughput screening platforms to identify small molecules, peptides, or biologics that restore the localization of Kir4.1 and AQP4 to Müller cell endfeet, enhance the stability of the dystrophin-associated protein complex, or inhibit the inflammatory pathways that exacerbate osmotic instability. Compounds identified through such screens may then be validated in vivo for their ability to reduce vascular permeability, normalize Müller cell swelling, and preserve retinal function over time.

The model is particularly valuable for studying the pathogenic sequence of events linking glial channel mislocalization to blood-retinal barrier breakdown. By comparing the temporal progression of channel redistribution, osmotic swelling, inflammatory mediator release, and vascular leakage in the Dp71-null model, researchers can establish causal relationships between these events and identify critical time windows for therapeutic intervention. For example, the model demonstrates that vascular permeability increases prior to the onset of detectable retinal edema, suggesting that barrier dysfunction is not merely a consequence of fluid accumulation but a primary event that contributes to it. This insight enables the development of diagnostic criteria based on the detection of glial channel mislocalization rather than vascular leakage, allowing for earlier diagnosis and more effective treatment.

The model may also be used to investigate the role of systemic factors in retinal disease. For instance, the model may be subjected to diets high in fat or sugar, exposure to ambient hypoxia, or induction of systemic hypertension to determine whether these factors accelerate or exacerbate the glial phenotype. The model may be crossed with transgenic lines that overexpress or knock down specific inflammatory cytokines, growth factors, or signaling molecules to determine their contribution to the Dp71-null phenotype. The model may also be used to study the effects of aging on glial function, as the Dp71-null phenotype may become more pronounced with age, providing a platform for investigating age-related retinal vulnerability.

In addition to drug discovery, the model may be used to validate the efficacy of gene therapy approaches aimed at restoring Dp71 expression. Adeno-associated viral vectors encoding the Dp71 cDNA under the control of Müller cell-specific promoters may be delivered intravitreally to Dp71-null animals, and the restoration of Kir4.1 and AQP4 localization, reduction in vascular permeability, and normalization of osmotic responses may be quantified over time. This provides a direct preclinical test for gene therapy candidates before human trials. The model may also be used to evaluate the safety and biodistribution of novel delivery systems, such as nanoparticles or exosomes, designed to target Müller cells specifically.

The model may further be employed in the development of non-invasive diagnostic imaging techniques. For example, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with contrast agents that bind to AQP4 or Kir4.1 may be tested for their ability to detect glial channel mislocalization in vivo. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) may be used to assess subtle changes in retinal thickness or reflectivity that correlate with glial swelling. Fluorescence lifetime imaging (FLIM) or two-photon microscopy may be employed to visualize the spatial distribution of Dp71-associated proteins in live retinal tissue. These imaging modalities, once validated in the model, may be translated to human patients for early detection of retinal disease in asymptomatic individuals.

The model may also serve as a platform for studying the interaction between glial dysfunction and other neurological conditions. For example, the model may be crossed with models of Alzheimer’s disease, multiple sclerosis, or epilepsy to determine whether Dp71 deficiency exacerbates glial pathology in the central nervous system beyond the retina. The model may also be used to investigate the role of glial cells in neurovascular coupling, the process by which neuronal activity regulates local blood flow, which is known to be impaired in diabetic retinopathy and other neurodegenerative conditions.

Finally, the model may be used in educational and training settings to teach the principles of glial biology, neurovascular interactions, and retinal pathophysiology. Its consistent and reproducible phenotype makes it ideal for standardized laboratory instruction, and its genetic stability ensures that results are comparable across institutions and over time. The model thus represents a comprehensive and versatile platform for advancing both basic science and clinical applications in the field of retinal disease.

### Diagnostics Methods According to the Invention

The diagnostic methods according to the invention provide novel and highly specific means for detecting early-stage retinal disease by identifying molecular and functional biomarkers associated with Dp71-dependent glial dysfunction. These methods are designed to detect pathological changes in Müller cells before the onset of vascular leakage, retinal edema, or vision loss, thereby enabling intervention at a stage when therapeutic efficacy is greatest. The diagnostic approach is based on the observation that the absence or dysfunction of Dp71 leads to a characteristic cascade of molecular alterations, including the mislocalization of Kir4.1 and AQP4, the upregulation of inflammatory mediators, and the increased susceptibility of Müller cells to osmotic stress. These alterations can be detected in retinal tissue, ocular fluids, or even in circulating biomarkers derived from glial cells.

One diagnostic method involves the quantitative assessment of Dp71 protein expression in retinal biopsies or post-mortem tissue using immunohistochemistry, Western blotting, or mass spectrometry. In healthy individuals, Dp71 is abundantly expressed in the endfeet of Müller cells, forming a distinct banding pattern at the inner limiting membrane and around retinal capillaries. In individuals with early-stage retinal disease, this pattern is disrupted, with Dp71 either absent or diffusely distributed. The degree of Dp71 loss correlates with the severity of glial dysfunction and predicts the likelihood of subsequent vascular leakage. This method may be adapted for use in clinical settings through the development of antibody-based assays that detect Dp71 fragments in vitreous humor or aqueous humor obtained via minimally invasive paracentesis.

A second diagnostic method involves the spatial mapping of Kir4.1 and AQP4 localization in retinal tissue using high-resolution confocal microscopy or super-resolution imaging techniques. In healthy retinas, both proteins are concentrated in discrete puncta at the Müller cell endfeet. In diseased retinas, these puncta are dispersed along the entire glial process, and the ratio of endfoot-to-somatic fluorescence intensity is significantly reduced. This ratio may be quantified using automated image analysis software and used as a diagnostic index for glial channel mislocalization. The method may be extended to non-invasive imaging by developing contrast agents that bind specifically to Kir4.1 or AQP4 and are detectable by optical coherence tomography or fluorescence lifetime imaging, allowing for in vivo detection of channel redistribution without the need for tissue biopsy.

A third diagnostic method involves the detection of inflammatory mediators associated with Dp71 deficiency, such as prostaglandin E2, arachidonic acid, or cyclooxygenase-2 metabolites, in ocular fluids. These mediators are elevated in the vitreous humor of Dp71-null animals and are likely to be similarly elevated in human patients with diabetic retinopathy or retinal vein occlusion. Measurement of these metabolites by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry or enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay may serve as a biomarker panel for early disease detection. The combination of Dp71 expression levels, Kir4.1/AQP4 localization patterns, and inflammatory mediator concentrations may be integrated into a composite diagnostic score that predicts disease progression with high sensitivity and specificity.

A fourth diagnostic method involves the functional assessment of Müller cell osmotic responsiveness. This may be performed ex vivo using retinal slices obtained from vitrectomy specimens or post-mortem tissue. The slices are exposed to a hypotonic solution, and the change in cross-sectional area of Müller cell somata is measured using confocal microscopy and image analysis software. In healthy individuals, Müller cells exhibit minimal swelling, whereas in individuals with early glial dysfunction, swelling exceeds a predetermined threshold, indicating impaired volume regulation. This method may be adapted for clinical use by developing a microfluidic device that applies controlled osmotic stress to small volumes of vitreous-derived glial cells, enabling rapid, point-of-care assessment of glial health.

A fifth diagnostic method involves genetic screening for mutations, deletions, or epigenetic modifications in the DMD gene locus that affect the expression or splicing of the Dp71 isoform. Whole-exome sequencing, targeted next-generation sequencing panels, or methylation-specific PCR may be used to identify individuals with genetic predispositions to glial dysfunction. This method is particularly useful for identifying at-risk populations, such as carriers of Duchenne muscular dystrophy mutations who may develop retinal pathology even in the absence of skeletal muscle involvement.

The diagnostic methods of the invention may be combined into a multi-modal diagnostic platform that integrates molecular, structural, and functional assessments to provide a comprehensive profile of glial health. This platform may be implemented in clinical ophthalmology as a non-invasive or minimally invasive screening tool for patients with diabetes, hypertension, or a family history of retinal disease. Early detection through these methods enables timely intervention with glial-targeted therapies, potentially preventing the progression to irreversible vision loss. The diagnostic methods are also valuable for monitoring therapeutic response, as the restoration of Dp71 expression, Kir4.1/AQP4 localization, and osmotic stability may be used as surrogate endpoints in clinical trials, reducing the need for long-term follow-up and improving trial efficiency.

### Therapeutic Methods and Pharmaceutical Compositions According to the Invention

The therapeutic methods according to the invention are designed to restore the functional integrity of Müller glial cells by targeting the molecular pathways disrupted in the Dp71-deficient state. These methods aim to correct the mislocalization of Kir4.1 and AQP4, stabilize the dystrophin-associated protein complex, inhibit the inflammatory cascades that exacerbate osmotic instability, and ultimately restore the blood-retinal barrier. The therapeutic strategies are categorized into four principal approaches: molecular restoration of Dp71 function, pharmacological stabilization of glial channels, inhibition of inflammatory mediators, and modulation of osmotic homeostasis.

The first therapeutic approach involves the restoration of Dp71 expression through gene therapy. Recombinant adeno-associated viral vectors, particularly serotypes with high tropism for Müller cells such as AAV2, AAV8, or engineered variants like AAV7m8, are used to deliver a functional copy of the Dp71 cDNA under the control of a Müller cell-specific promoter, such as the glutamine synthetase promoter or the cellular retinaldehyde-binding protein promoter. The vector is administered via intravitreal injection, allowing for localized and sustained expression of Dp71 in the retina. Following delivery, Dp71 protein is expressed in the endfeet of Müller cells, where it reassembles the dystrophin-associated protein complex, leading to the re-clustering of Kir4.1 and AQP4 at their normal membrane domains. This results in the restoration of potassium buffering capacity, improved water efflux, reduced osmotic swelling, and normalization of blood-retinal barrier permeability. The therapeutic effect is long-lasting, with functional improvements observed for months after a single injection, making this approach highly suitable for chronic retinal diseases.

The second therapeutic approach involves the use of small molecules or peptides that stabilize the interaction between Dp71 and its associated proteins. These compounds are identified through high-throughput screening of chemical libraries using biophysical assays that measure binding affinity between Dp71 and β-dystroglycan or syntrophin. Lead compounds are optimized for blood-retinal barrier penetration, metabolic stability, and low toxicity. Once administered intravitreally or orally, these compounds act as molecular glue, enhancing the stability of the DAP complex and preventing the lateral diffusion of Kir4.1 and AQP4. In some embodiments, the compounds may mimic the structural domains of Dp71 that are responsible for channel anchoring, thereby acting as synthetic scaffolds in the absence of endogenous Dp71. These compounds may be formulated as sustained-release implants or nanoparticles for prolonged intraocular delivery.

The third therapeutic approach involves the inhibition of inflammatory pathways that contribute to Müller cell swelling. Pharmacological agents that inhibit phospholipase A2, such as 4-bromophenacyl bromide or its derivatives, or cyclooxygenase, such as indomethacin, celecoxib, or novel selective inhibitors, are administered to block the production of arachidonic acid and prostaglandins. These agents are delivered via intravitreal injection, topical eye drops, or oral formulations, and are shown to significantly reduce osmotic swelling in Dp71-null animals. In some embodiments, the inhibitors are combined with antioxidants such as N-acetylcysteine or resveratrol to further mitigate oxidative stress, which is known to exacerbate glial dysfunction. The therapeutic effect is not limited to the Dp71-null model but is also effective in wild-type animals subjected to ischemic or diabetic insults, indicating broad applicability.

The fourth therapeutic approach involves the modulation of osmotic homeostasis through the use of osmotic agents or ion channel modulators. Compounds such as bumetanide, which inhibits the Na-K-2Cl cotransporter, or acetazolamide, which reduces aqueous humor production, may be used to reduce retinal fluid accumulation. Alternatively, activators of potassium channels, such as retigabine or flupirtine, may be used to enhance Kir4.1 function independently of its localization. These agents may be combined with aquaporin modulators, such as TGN-020 or AQP4-blocking peptides, to fine-tune water transport. The combination of osmotic modulators with glial channel stabilizers provides a synergistic effect, restoring both ion and water balance in Müller cells.

Pharmaceutical compositions according to the invention comprise one or more of the above therapeutic agents in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier suitable for ocular delivery. The compositions may be formulated as sterile aqueous solutions, suspensions, emulsions, or sustained-release microspheres for intravitreal injection. Alternatively, the compositions may be formulated as topical eye drops, transscleral patches, or intraocular implants for prolonged release. The carrier may include penetration enhancers such as hyaluronic acid, chitosan, or cyclodextrins to facilitate transcorneal or transscleral delivery. The compositions may further include preservatives, tonicity agents, and buffers to ensure stability and biocompatibility. In some embodiments, the pharmaceutical composition is encapsulated in lipid nanoparticles or exosomes derived from Müller cells to enhance targeted delivery and reduce immune activation.

The therapeutic methods may be administered prophylactically in high-risk populations, such as diabetic patients with early signs of glial dysfunction, or therapeutically in patients with established retinal edema. The methods may be combined with existing therapies, such as anti-VEGF agents, corticosteroids, or laser photocoagulation, to provide a multi-pronged approach that targets both vascular and glial components of disease. The therapeutic efficacy of the methods is assessed by measuring reductions in retinal thickness via optical coherence tomography, decreases in vascular leakage via fluorescein angiography, normalization of Müller cell osmotic responses, and improvement in visual function via electroretinography or behavioral assays.

The invention thus provides a comprehensive set of therapeutic methods and pharmaceutical compositions that address the root cause of retinal edema—glial dysfunction—rather than its downstream consequences. By restoring the molecular architecture of Müller cells, these methods offer the potential for disease modification, not merely symptom control, representing a paradigm shift in the treatment of retinal vascular diseases.

## EXAMPLE

In a representative example, a cohort of adult male and female Dp71-null mice on a C57BL/6 background, aged between 8 and 12 weeks, was generated and maintained under standard laboratory conditions with free access to food and water. Control animals consisted of age- and sex-matched wild-type C57BL/6 mice. All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. Retinal tissue was harvested from both groups for molecular, histological, and functional analyses.

Immunohistochemical staining of retinal sections revealed that in wild-type mice, Dp71 protein was densely localized in the endfeet of Müller cells at the inner limiting membrane and surrounding retinal capillaries, forming a continuous, sharply defined banding pattern. In contrast, Dp71-null mice exhibited complete absence of Dp71 immunoreactivity in these regions, with no residual signal detectable above background levels. Kir4.1 and AQP4 proteins in wild-type mice were similarly concentrated at the endfeet, with minimal labeling along the glial shaft. In Dp71-null mice, Kir4.1 was diffusely distributed along the entire length of Müller cell processes, and AQP4 expression was reduced by approximately 40% as measured by Western blot densitometry. Co-localization analysis confirmed the loss of spatial correlation between Kir4.1 and AQP4 in the absence of Dp71.

Electrophysiological recordings from acutely isolated Müller cells demonstrated that the inward rectifying potassium current density, measured as the steady-state current amplitude at −140 mV divided by membrane capacitance, was reduced by 32% in Dp71-null mice compared to controls, despite no significant difference in total membrane capacitance. The resting membrane potential was unchanged, indicating that the cells remained viable but functionally impaired. When exposed to a hypotonic solution (60% osmolarity), Müller cells from Dp71-null mice swelled by 118% of their baseline area within four minutes, whereas cells from wild-type mice showed only a 5% increase. The swelling in Dp71-null mice was significantly attenuated by pre-treatment with the cyclooxygenase inhibitor indomethacin (10 µM), reducing swelling to 104% of baseline, and by the phospholipase A2 inhibitor 4-bromophenacyl bromide (300 µM), reducing swelling to 103%. The nucleoside adenosine (10 µM) also reduced swelling to 103%, suggesting a role for purinergic signaling in the osmotic response.

Vascular permeability was assessed using the Evans blue dye extravasation technique. Following intravenous injection of Evans blue (45 mg/kg), retinas from Dp71-null mice exhibited a 3.8-fold increase in dye concentration compared to wild-type controls, indicating a profound disruption of the blood-retinal barrier. Perfusion with citrate buffer confirmed the absence of residual dye in the vasculature, validating the specificity of the measurement. The increased permeability was not associated with changes in endothelial tight junction proteins such as ZO-1 or occludin, as confirmed by immunofluorescence and Western blot analysis, supporting the conclusion that barrier dysfunction originated from glial impairment.

To evaluate therapeutic efficacy, a subset of Dp71-null mice received a single intravitreal injection of an AAV8 vector encoding the full-length human Dp71 cDNA under the control of the human glutamine synthetase promoter. Six weeks after injection, retinal sections from treated animals showed robust expression of Dp71 protein in Müller cell endfeet, with a distribution pattern indistinguishable from that of wild-type controls. Kir4.1 and AQP4 were re-localized to the endfoot domain, with co-localization coefficients restored to 85% of wild-type levels. Potassium current density increased by 29% compared to untreated Dp71-null mice, and osmotic swelling was reduced to 106% of baseline. Evans blue extravasation was decreased by 72%, bringing permeability levels close to those of wild-type animals. No signs of retinal inflammation, gliosis, or immune cell infiltration were observed, confirming the safety and specificity of the gene therapy.

These results demonstrate that the Dp71-null mouse model faithfully recapitulates the key features of early-stage retinal disease and that targeted restoration of Dp71 function is sufficient to reverse the associated glial, vascular, and osmotic pathologies. The model and its associated diagnostic and therapeutic methods represent a transformative advance in the understanding and treatment of retinal edema and neurovascular dysfunction.
