# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a high-precision solar tracking system for concentrated photovoltaic applications, specifically a self-contained, image-based solar position sensor system incorporating a custom-designed reflecting Cassegrain telescope, a digital imaging device, and an embedded image processing algorithm capable of determining the precise center of the solar disk under diverse atmospheric conditions, including low irradiance, partial obscuration, and variable cloud cover. The system is engineered to provide sub-arcminute angular accuracy in solar alignment without reliance on direct photodiode-based feedback mechanisms, thereby overcoming the inherent limitations of conventional bar-shadow sensors and four-quadrant light detectors that suffer from signal degradation under diffuse lighting, photodiode mismatch, and spectral sensitivity drift. The invention integrates optical magnification, chromatic discrimination via HSL color space analysis, edge detection via Sobel filtering, and geometric centroid determination through three-point circular interpolation to deliver a robust, weather-resilient, and calibration-stable method for solar tracking that is uniquely suited for high-concentration photovoltaic systems requiring angular precision below 0.1 degrees. The system is designed for integration with dual-axis mechanical trackers in both terrestrial and mobile solar energy installations, enabling maximum energy capture across diurnal and seasonal variations in solar position, even under non-ideal sky conditions where traditional tracking systems fail to maintain operational fidelity.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE RELATED ART

Prior art solar tracking systems for concentrated photovoltaic applications have predominantly relied on passive photodetector arrays to infer the position of the sun by measuring differential irradiance across spatially separated sensing elements. Among these, the bar-shadow photosensor has been widely adopted due to its simplicity and low cost, comprising a central occluding bar and four photodiodes arranged at cardinal points around it. The system operates on the principle that when the sun is perfectly aligned with the sensor’s optical axis, the irradiance received by opposing photodiodes becomes balanced, and the difference between east-west and north-south pairs falls below a predetermined threshold, signaling correct alignment. However, this approach is critically vulnerable to environmental variability. Under cloudy or partially overcast conditions, the diffuse nature of sunlight causes uneven illumination across the photodiode array, leading to false imbalance signals and erroneous tracking corrections. Furthermore, manufacturing tolerances in photodiode sensitivity, spectral response, and temperature drift introduce systematic offsets that are difficult to compensate for without frequent recalibration, which is impractical in field-deployed systems. The resulting tracking errors, often exceeding one degree under non-clear-sky conditions, directly translate into significant power losses in high-concentration photovoltaic modules, which have acceptance angles as narrow as 0.5 degrees.

To mitigate these shortcomings, researchers have proposed the use of four-quadrant photodetectors coupled with pinhole apertures to improve spatial resolution and reduce the impact of non-uniform illumination. While this configuration enhances contrast and reduces the influence of photodiode mismatch, it remains fundamentally dependent on the intensity of incident light. In low-irradiance environments—such as dawn, dusk, or heavily overcast days—the signal-to-noise ratio deteriorates, causing the sensor to lose sensitivity and produce erratic or unstable outputs. Additionally, the pinhole mechanism introduces diffraction artifacts and limits the field of view, making it difficult to acquire the full solar disk and increasing susceptibility to false triggers from bright non-solar objects such as reflections or artificial light sources. These limitations render such systems unsuitable for continuous, all-weather operation required for commercial high-concentration photovoltaic installations.

More recent developments have explored the use of imaging-based approaches, leveraging commercial digital cameras or charge-coupled devices to capture the solar disk directly. These systems attempt to identify the sun by detecting its intensity profile or color signature within a captured image. Some implementations utilize multiple apertures and focal plane arrays to generate multiple solar spot images, enabling triangulation-based position estimation. Others employ polarized filters to suppress glare and enhance contrast. While these methods demonstrate improved immunity to diffuse light compared to photodiode-based sensors, they suffer from critical deficiencies in practical deployment. Many rely on fixed optical configurations that do not allow for adjustment of magnification or field of view, resulting in suboptimal resolution when the sun is near the horizon or during seasonal variations in solar altitude. Furthermore, the image processing algorithms employed in prior art often operate in the RGB color space, which is highly sensitive to variations in ambient illumination, camera gain, and exposure settings. As a result, these systems exhibit poor robustness under changing lighting conditions, frequently misidentifying bright clouds, reflective surfaces, or lens flare as the solar disk, leading to tracking drift and system instability.

Moreover, prior art systems frequently lack a mechanism for distinguishing the true solar disk from other bright circular features in the sky. Simple thresholding and blob detection techniques are prone to false positives, particularly when the sun is partially obscured by thin clouds or when the image contains bright, high-contrast regions such as aircraft contrails or specular reflections from water bodies. Even when the solar disk is correctly identified, the determination of its geometric center is often accomplished through rudimentary centroid calculations based on pixel intensity summation, which are highly susceptible to noise, uneven illumination across the disk, and edge distortion caused by atmospheric refraction or lens aberrations. These inaccuracies accumulate over time, resulting in progressive misalignment and reduced energy yield.

Additionally, existing systems typically do not account for the optical characteristics of the imaging path. Many implementations use standard lenses or simple magnifying elements that introduce spherical aberration, chromatic distortion, or vignetting, which degrade image quality and compromise the fidelity of the solar disk representation. The absence of a purpose-built optical system designed to optimize solar image clarity, contrast, and size for digital imaging has been a persistent gap in the prior art. Furthermore, prior systems rarely integrate mechanical stability, optical path length minimization, and compact form factor into a single unified design suitable for integration with commercial dual-axis trackers. As a result, many imaging-based solar trackers remain bulky, complex, and unsuitable for mass deployment in utility-scale or residential photovoltaic installations.

The present invention addresses these longstanding deficiencies by introducing a novel, integrated solar position sensing system that combines a custom-designed reflecting Cassegrain telescope optimized for solar image magnification and contrast enhancement, a high-resolution digital imaging sensor, and a multi-stage image processing algorithm that operates in the HSL color domain to isolate the solar disk with exceptional reliability under all sky conditions. Unlike prior art, the invention does not rely on intensity-based thresholds or photodiode differential measurements, but instead leverages the unique chromatic signature of the sun in the HSL space, coupled with geometric edge analysis and circular centroid determination, to achieve unprecedented tracking accuracy and stability. The system is engineered to be self-calibrating, immune to ambient light fluctuations, and capable of maintaining sub-0.05-degree precision even when the solar disk is partially obscured by up to 60% of its area. The integration of a compact, lightweight, and adjustable optical path—measuring no more than ten centimeters in total length—enables seamless mounting on standard dual-axis tracker platforms without requiring structural modifications. The invention thus represents a fundamental advancement in solar tracking technology, transitioning from intensity-dependent analog sensing to robust, vision-based digital positioning, thereby enabling reliable, high-yield operation of high-concentration photovoltaic systems in real-world environments previously considered unsuitable for such technologies.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to a solar tracking system that achieves unprecedented accuracy and stability in determining the position of the sun by employing a novel combination of an optimized optical imaging system and a sophisticated image processing algorithm, operating independently of traditional photodetector-based feedback mechanisms. The system comprises a reflecting Cassegrain telescope specifically configured to magnify and focus the image of the sun onto a digital image sensor, a high-resolution digital camera capable of capturing the focused solar disk with sufficient pixel density to resolve angular deviations below 0.05 degrees, and an embedded image processing controller that executes a multi-stage algorithm to isolate the solar disk from background noise, determine its geometric center with sub-pixel precision, and generate corrective signals for a dual-axis mechanical tracker. The telescope is constructed with two concave mirrors, a convex mirror, and a right-angle prism arranged to reflect and redirect the incoming solar radiation through an eyepiece, thereby achieving a compact optical path length of less than ten centimeters while maintaining high modulation transfer function values across the entire field of view, ensuring sharp, high-contrast solar images even under challenging atmospheric conditions. The system is designed to operate without external calibration under varying illumination levels, including overcast skies, partial cloud cover, and low solar elevation angles, by leveraging the unique spectral and luminance properties of the sun in the HSL (hue, saturation, lightness) color model, rather than relying on absolute intensity measurements.

The image processing algorithm begins with the acquisition of a digital image from the camera, which is then converted from the RGB color space into the HSL color space to decouple luminance variations from chromatic information. This transformation allows the system to distinguish the sun’s distinctive hue signature—characterized by a narrow band of orange-red tones—regardless of ambient brightness, camera exposure settings, or temporal fluctuations in irradiance. A dynamic lightness threshold is applied to binarize the image, converting it into a black-and-white representation where only pixels corresponding to the solar disk retain their intensity, while all other elements—including clouds, sky, and artificial glare—are suppressed. This binarization step is adaptive, adjusting its threshold based on the statistical distribution of lightness values within the image, thereby eliminating the need for fixed thresholds that are prone to failure under changing environmental conditions. Following binarization, the Sobel edge detection operator is applied to extract the boundary of the solar disk, producing a continuous, noise-resilient contour that delineates the sun’s perimeter with high fidelity, even when the disk is partially obscured or irregularly illuminated.

To determine the precise center of the solar disk, the system employs a three-point circular interpolation method, wherein three non-collinear points are selected along the detected edge contour, with the constraint that the arc length between the first and middle point is equal to that between the middle and third point. For each such triplet of points, the perpendicular bisectors of the connecting line segments are computed, and their intersection yields a candidate center coordinate. This process is repeated iteratively across hundreds of valid triplets extracted from the edge contour, and the final center is determined as the mode of all computed coordinates, effectively filtering out outliers caused by noise, atmospheric distortion, or local edge irregularities. This geometric approach ensures that the computed center corresponds to the true geometric centroid of the solar disk, rather than the intensity-weighted centroid, which can be biased by uneven illumination or limb darkening. The resulting center coordinates are compared against a reference point corresponding to the optical axis of the imaging system, and any deviation is translated into angular error signals for the dual-axis tracker, which then adjusts its orientation to minimize the positional offset.

The system further incorporates a configurable magnification mechanism, allowing the Cassegrain telescope to be adjusted between five and fifteen times magnification, enabling optimization of the pixel-to-degree ratio based on the resolution of the connected camera and the desired tracking precision. Experimental validation demonstrates that the combination of a 15× magnification telescope with a high-resolution camera (2304 × 1536 pixels) achieves a resolution of 0.0017 degrees per pixel, enabling tracking accuracy better than 0.04 degrees under all tested conditions, including simulated partial obscuration, low irradiance, and rapid cloud transients. The system maintains this level of precision even when up to 60% of the solar disk is shadowed, a condition under which conventional photodiode-based sensors fail catastrophically. The entire system is housed in a compact, lightweight enclosure designed for direct mounting on standard dual-axis tracker platforms, requiring no structural reinforcement or additional support mechanisms. The invention eliminates the need for periodic recalibration, is immune to photodiode mismatch and spectral drift, and operates reliably across a wide range of geographic latitudes and seasonal solar trajectories. By replacing analog, intensity-dependent sensing with digital, geometry-based image analysis, the invention provides a paradigm shift in solar tracking technology, enabling high-concentration photovoltaic systems to achieve maximum energy yield under all weather conditions previously considered prohibitive for such applications.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The preferred embodiment of the invention comprises a fully integrated solar tracking system designed for deployment in high-concentration photovoltaic installations, wherein the system’s operational integrity and precision are maintained under all environmental conditions, including clear skies, overcast conditions, partial cloud cover, and low solar elevation angles. The system is composed of three primary functional units: an optical imaging subsystem, a digital image acquisition subsystem, and a digital signal processing and control subsystem, all mechanically and electronically integrated into a single, compact, self-contained module. The optical imaging subsystem is centered around a custom-designed reflecting Cassegrain telescope, which is constructed from a sequence of precisely aligned optical elements: a primary concave mirror, a secondary convex mirror, a tertiary concave mirror, and a right-angle prism, arranged in a folded optical path to minimize the overall length of the system while maximizing image quality and magnification. The primary concave mirror, positioned at the base of the telescope assembly, receives incoming solar radiation and reflects it toward the secondary convex mirror, which is mounted at a focal point offset from the optical axis. The secondary mirror, in turn, reflects the converging rays toward the tertiary concave mirror, which further collimates and focuses the light. The light then strikes the right-angle prism, which redirects the optical path by ninety degrees, allowing the focused solar image to be projected vertically upward through an eyepiece lens and onto the sensor plane of a digital camera mounted directly above the prism. This folded configuration enables the entire optical path to be contained within a housing no longer than ten centimeters, making the system compact enough for direct mounting on standard dual-axis tracker platforms without requiring structural modifications or additional support brackets.

The optical design of the Cassegrain telescope was optimized using OSLO® optical simulation software to maximize the modulation transfer function (MTF) across the full field of view, ensuring that the solar disk is rendered with high contrast, minimal aberration, and uniform sharpness from center to edge. The MTF analysis confirmed that the system achieves modulation values exceeding 0.8 at 20 cycles per millimeter, indicating excellent resolving power and the ability to distinguish fine details in the solar disk’s edge, even under low-contrast conditions. The inclusion of the right-angle prism, rather than a flat mirror, was found to be critical in achieving balanced off-axis performance; alternative configurations using flat mirrors or relocating the prism between the convex and concave mirrors resulted in significant degradation of the MTF curve, particularly in the off-axis regions, leading to distorted or blurred solar images. The eyepiece lens, positioned immediately before the camera sensor, serves to fine-tune the final focus and magnification of the solar image, and is designed with a fixed focal length to ensure consistent image scale across all operating conditions. The telescope’s magnification can be adjusted between five and fifteen times by mechanically repositioning the tertiary mirror along a linear rail, allowing the system to be calibrated for different camera resolutions and tracking precision requirements. This adjustability ensures that the system can be optimized for either high-resolution cameras with small pixel sizes, where maximum pixel-to-degree ratio is desired, or for lower-resolution cameras where a broader field of view is preferred to accommodate greater mechanical tolerances.

The digital image acquisition subsystem consists of a high-resolution digital camera, preferably a CMOS-based sensor with a resolution of at least 1920 × 1080 pixels, capable of capturing the focused solar image with sufficient pixel density to resolve angular deviations smaller than 0.01 degrees per pixel. In the most preferred embodiment, a Logitech C920 camera with a resolution of 2304 × 1536 pixels is employed, which, when paired with the 15× magnification setting of the telescope, achieves a resolution of 0.0017 degrees per pixel. This level of resolution enables the system to detect minute positional shifts in the solar disk with extreme precision, translating into sub-arcsecond angular control capability. The camera is equipped with a fixed-focus lens and is mounted in a sealed, vibration-damped housing to prevent mechanical drift and ensure long-term stability. To protect the sensor from potential damage due to prolonged exposure to direct solar radiation, the system incorporates a neutral density filter (ND400) and an infrared cutoff filter (760 nm) positioned between the eyepiece and the camera sensor. The neutral density filter reduces the intensity of incoming light by a factor of 400, preventing sensor saturation and preserving dynamic range, while the infrared filter blocks wavelengths beyond 760 nanometers, eliminating heat-related sensor noise and reducing thermal drift in the image acquisition process. The filtered image is transmitted via a digital interface to the control subsystem, which processes the data in real time.

The digital signal processing and control subsystem is implemented using a microcontroller-based embedded system, programmed with a multi-stage image processing algorithm designed to isolate the solar disk from the background sky, determine its geometric center with sub-pixel accuracy, and generate corrective signals for the dual-axis tracker. The algorithm begins with the acquisition of a raw RGB image from the camera, which is immediately converted into the HSL color space using standard color transformation equations. In this space, the hue component represents the dominant wavelength of each pixel, the saturation component indicates the purity of the color, and the lightness component represents the perceived brightness. The sun’s spectral signature is characterized by a narrow band of hue values centered around 20 to 35 degrees on the color wheel, corresponding to the orange-red region of the spectrum, which is distinct from the blue and gray hues of the sky and the white or gray tones of clouds. By isolating pixels within this hue range and applying a dynamic lightness threshold derived from the statistical distribution of lightness values in the image, the algorithm effectively segments the solar disk from all other elements in the scene. This approach is fundamentally superior to intensity-based thresholding because it is insensitive to changes in ambient illumination, camera gain, exposure time, or atmospheric attenuation. The resulting binary image contains only the solar disk and any residual noise, which is then subjected to morphological filtering to remove isolated pixels and small artifacts.

Following binarization, the Sobel edge detection operator is applied to extract the boundary of the solar disk. The Sobel filter computes the gradient of intensity in both the horizontal and vertical directions, producing a high-contrast edge map that delineates the perimeter of the solar disk with high fidelity. Unlike simple thresholding or contour tracing methods, the Sobel operator is robust to noise and preserves the continuity of the edge even when the solar disk is partially obscured or unevenly illuminated. The resulting edge contour is then analyzed to identify valid triplets of non-collinear points along its perimeter. For each triplet, the algorithm selects three points such that the arc length between the first and middle point is equal to the arc length between the middle and third point, ensuring that the selected points are evenly distributed around the circumference of the disk. This constraint minimizes the influence of local edge distortions caused by atmospheric turbulence or lens aberrations. For each triplet, the perpendicular bisectors of the three line segments connecting the points are computed algebraically, and their intersection yields a candidate center coordinate. This process is repeated for hundreds of such triplets, generating a distribution of candidate center points. The final center is determined as the mode of this distribution—the coordinate that appears most frequently—thereby rejecting outliers caused by noise, cloud shadows, or transient artifacts. This geometric centroid determination method is fundamentally more accurate than intensity-weighted centroid calculations, which can be biased by limb darkening, uneven illumination, or sensor non-uniformity.

The computed center coordinates are then compared to a pre-determined reference point, which corresponds to the optical axis of the imaging system when the tracker is perfectly aligned with the sun. The difference between the computed center and the reference point is translated into angular error values in both azimuth and elevation axes, using a calibration matrix derived from the known pixel-to-degree ratio of the system. These angular error signals are fed into a proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controller, which generates motor control signals to drive the dual-axis tracker, adjusting its orientation to minimize the positional error. The system operates in a closed-loop fashion, continuously acquiring new images, processing them, and updating the tracker’s position at a rate of ten frames per second, ensuring real-time responsiveness to solar motion and transient atmospheric disturbances. A threshold value is applied to the angular error signal to prevent unnecessary micro-adjustments in response to noise or minor fluctuations. In the preferred embodiment, this threshold is set to five pixels, corresponding to an angular threshold of approximately 0.0085 degrees, which balances tracking precision with system stability. Experimental results demonstrate that when this threshold is set to five pixels, the system maintains a tracking error of less than 0.04 degrees in the azimuth axis and less than 0.03 degrees in the elevation axis, even under simulated partial obscuration of the solar disk by up to 60%. When the threshold is increased to ten pixels, the error remains below 0.07 degrees in azimuth and 0.05 degrees in elevation, confirming the system’s robustness across a range of operational parameters.

The system is further enhanced by the inclusion of a self-diagnostic and auto-calibration routine that runs during initial startup and at periodic intervals. This routine captures a series of images under known reference conditions—such as during early morning or late evening when the sun is near the horizon—and computes the average center position over multiple frames. This average is then stored as the reference point for subsequent tracking operations, compensating for any minor mechanical drift, thermal expansion, or optical misalignment that may occur over time. The system also includes a fail-safe mechanism that, if no valid solar disk is detected for a predetermined period (e.g., five minutes), enters a low-power standby mode and initiates a slow, systematic scan of the sky to reacquire the sun’s position, ensuring uninterrupted operation even after temporary obstructions such as passing birds or debris. The entire system is powered by a low-voltage DC supply, compatible with standard photovoltaic system power buses, and communicates with the tracker’s main controller via a standard serial or CAN bus interface, allowing seamless integration into existing solar energy architectures.

The mechanical housing of the system is constructed from lightweight, thermally stable materials such as anodized aluminum and carbon fiber composites, ensuring dimensional stability across a wide range of ambient temperatures. The optical elements are secured in precision-machined mounts with zero-backlash adjustment screws, allowing for fine-tuning of focus and alignment during installation. The camera and telescope are aligned along a common optical axis, and the entire assembly is sealed against moisture, dust, and condensation, making it suitable for outdoor deployment in harsh climatic conditions. The system requires no external calibration after initial installation and maintains its accuracy over extended periods without degradation, a critical advantage over photodiode-based systems that require frequent recalibration due to sensor drift. Field testing under real-world conditions, including locations with high humidity, dust, and seasonal temperature variations, has confirmed that the system retains its sub-0.05-degree tracking accuracy for over twelve months without maintenance. The invention thus represents a complete, self-contained solution for high-precision solar tracking that is reliable, scalable, and economically viable for deployment in utility-scale, commercial, and residential concentrated photovoltaic installations. By replacing fragile, intensity-dependent analog sensors with a robust, vision-based digital system, the invention enables solar energy systems to operate at peak efficiency regardless of weather conditions, thereby maximizing energy yield, reducing levelized cost of electricity, and accelerating the global transition to renewable energy.
