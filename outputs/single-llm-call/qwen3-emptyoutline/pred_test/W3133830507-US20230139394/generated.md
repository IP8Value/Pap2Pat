# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of speech recognition systems, particularly to methods and apparatuses for enhancing the accuracy and robustness of automatic speech recognition models when processing speech produced by individuals with speech disorders, including aphasia, apraxia, and dysarthria. More specifically, the invention pertains to a deep learning-based architecture that integrates non-invasively recorded electroencephalographic (EEG) signals with traditional acoustic features to improve the decoding performance of both isolated and continuous speech recognition systems. The invention is particularly suited for deployment in assistive communication technologies, speech therapy tools, and neuroprosthetic interfaces designed for individuals with neurological impairments affecting speech production. The system leverages the temporal dynamics of brain activity captured via scalp EEG to extract latent acoustic representations that complement and correct for distortions inherent in disordered speech, thereby enabling more accurate transcription of utterances that would otherwise be misrecognized by conventional acoustic-only models. The integration of neural signals with audio features represents a novel fusion of neurophysiological data and speech signal processing, offering a paradigm shift in the design of inclusive speech recognition systems capable of accommodating a broader spectrum of human vocal expression.

## BACKGROUND

Automatic speech recognition systems have achieved remarkable success in recognizing clear, normative speech produced by individuals without speech impairments. These systems, widely deployed in consumer voice assistants, smart home devices, and call center automation, rely primarily on acoustic features extracted from speech signals—such as Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs), spectrograms, or filter bank energies—to train deep neural networks that map audio inputs to corresponding textual outputs. However, these systems exhibit substantial performance degradation when confronted with speech produced by individuals suffering from neurological speech disorders, including aphasia, apraxia, and dysarthria. Aphasia, often resulting from stroke or traumatic brain injury, impairs the cognitive and linguistic mechanisms responsible for language formulation and comprehension, leading to fragmented, grammatically incorrect, or semantically disorganized utterances. Apraxia of speech involves a disruption in the motor planning necessary to coordinate the articulatory movements required for speech, resulting in inconsistent sound substitutions, distortions, and prolonged pauses. Dysarthria, typically caused by neuromuscular weakness or incoordination, produces slurred, slow, or hypokinetic speech with reduced intelligibility. Collectively, these conditions render conventional speech recognition systems ineffective, with word error rates exceeding 90% in severe cases, thereby excluding millions of individuals from accessing critical communication technologies.

Prior efforts to address this challenge have focused almost exclusively on expanding the training corpora to include speech samples from affected populations. While datasets such as AphasiaBank have enabled the training of acoustic models tailored to disordered speech, the fundamental limitations of relying solely on degraded audio inputs remain unaddressed. These models continue to struggle with the high variability, inconsistent phoneme realization, and temporal irregularities inherent in pathological speech, leading to persistent high phoneme and word error rates. Alternative approaches have explored the use of electromyographic (EMG) signals to infer articulatory intent, but these methods require invasive or semi-invasive electrode placement near the facial musculature, limiting their practicality for long-term, everyday use. Furthermore, while some studies have demonstrated that EEG signals contain information related to speech perception and production in neurotypical individuals, no prior work has successfully harnessed non-invasive EEG recordings to enhance the performance of speech recognition systems specifically for individuals with aphasia, apraxia, or dysarthria.

Recent neuroscientific research has established that EEG signals, despite their low spatial resolution and susceptibility to noise, possess sufficient temporal fidelity to capture neural correlates of speech articulation at the millisecond scale, consistent with the natural rate of human speech production. Studies have shown that specific frequency bands and spatial patterns in EEG activity correlate with phonological encoding, articulatory motor planning, and auditory feedback processing—all of which are disrupted in speech disorders. However, these findings have not been translated into functional speech recognition systems. Existing attempts to combine EEG with speech recognition have been limited to small vocabularies, isolated words or vowels, and have not been validated on continuous speech or on populations with clinically diagnosed speech impairments. Moreover, no prior method has demonstrated how to extract and integrate latent acoustic representations from EEG signals as a complementary modality to traditional audio features in a trainable deep learning framework that simultaneously optimizes for both feature alignment and recognition accuracy.

The absence of a systematic, end-to-end methodology for fusing EEG-derived features with acoustic features in a manner that enhances speech recognition performance for disordered speech constitutes a critical gap in the field. There exists a pressing need for a non-invasive, scalable, and clinically viable solution that leverages the rich, time-locked neural activity associated with speech production to compensate for the acoustic deficiencies of pathological speech. Such a system would not only improve recognition accuracy but also provide a foundational architecture for next-generation speech prosthetics and real-time therapy aids that adapt to the evolving speech patterns of recovering patients. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel deep learning architecture that trains a regression model to map EEG signals to acoustic feature representations, which are then concatenated with traditional MFCC features to enhance the input to a speech recognition network. This approach enables the system to infer the intended acoustic output from neural activity even when the actual speech signal is distorted, incomplete, or temporally irregular, thereby achieving a substantial and statistically significant improvement in recognition performance across both isolated and continuous speech tasks.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a novel method and system for enhancing the accuracy of automatic speech recognition for individuals with speech disorders, including aphasia, apraxia, and dysarthria, by integrating non-invasively recorded electroencephalographic (EEG) signals with conventional acoustic features within a unified deep learning framework. The invention comprises a multi-stage architecture in which EEG signals, recorded synchronously with speech production, are first preprocessed to remove artifacts and extract a set of time-domain and spectral features. These features are then subjected to a non-linear dimensionality reduction technique to yield a compact, informative representation that preserves the discriminative structure of the neural activity related to speech. A regression model, trained independently on paired EEG and acoustic feature data, learns to predict acoustic representations—specifically Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients—from the reduced EEG feature space. These predicted acoustic representations, which encode the brain’s intended speech output, are then concatenated with the actual acoustic features extracted from the recorded speech signal and jointly fed into a downstream speech recognition model. The speech recognition model, implemented as a gated recurrent unit-based architecture, is trained to map this fused input to the corresponding textual output, whether in the form of isolated sentences or continuous speech sequences. The system is designed to operate in a manner that is agnostic to the specific type of speech disorder, enabling broad applicability across diverse patient populations without requiring disorder-specific tuning. The invention further includes a data collection protocol and preprocessing pipeline tailored to the unique characteristics of disordered speech, including the handling of pauses, phoneme distortions, and variable utterance lengths. The resulting system achieves a performance improvement exceeding fifty percent in word-level accuracy for isolated speech recognition tasks and demonstrates statistically significant reductions in word error rate for continuous speech recognition, surpassing all prior approaches that rely solely on acoustic inputs. Importantly, the invention does not require invasive neural recordings, real-time calibration, or subject-specific retraining, making it suitable for clinical deployment and long-term home use. The system is capable of operating with a reduced set of EEG sensors, specifically those over the frontal and temporal lobes, thereby minimizing hardware complexity and improving user comfort. Furthermore, the invention demonstrates that EEG-derived acoustic representations provide a more robust enhancement than those derived from electromyographic signals, confirming the unique value of neural activity over muscular activity in capturing speech intent. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in speech recognition technology by introducing the first method to effectively utilize non-invasive brain signals to augment and correct the acoustic signal in real-time, enabling accurate speech-to-text transcription for individuals whose speech has been rendered unintelligible by neurological impairment.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive system and method for improving the performance of automatic speech recognition systems when processing speech produced by individuals with neurological speech disorders, including aphasia, apraxia, and dysarthria. The system operates by integrating two distinct but complementary modalities: non-invasively recorded electroencephalographic (EEG) signals and conventional acoustic features extracted from the speech waveform. The core innovation lies in the use of a trained regression model to derive latent acoustic representations from EEG signals, which are then fused with the actual acoustic features of the speech signal to form a richer, more informative input to a downstream speech recognition network. This fusion enables the system to compensate for the distortions, omissions, and temporal irregularities inherent in disordered speech by leveraging the brain’s internal representation of intended speech, as captured by EEG activity. The entire pipeline is implemented using deep learning techniques and is trained end-to-end in a supervised manner using paired EEG-speech data collected from individuals with diagnosed speech disorders. The system is designed to be modular, allowing integration with any existing speech recognition architecture, whether for isolated word or continuous speech recognition, and is optimized for real-world deployment with minimal hardware requirements.

The system begins with the acquisition of synchronized EEG and speech signals from a subject during speech production. Electroencephalographic data is collected using a standard 10-20 electrode placement system, with twenty-nine scalp electrodes positioned to capture neural activity across the entire cortex. The EEG signals are sampled at a rate of one thousand hertz and are subjected to a series of preprocessing steps to enhance signal quality and remove artifacts. A fourth-order infinite impulse response bandpass filter is applied to retain neural activity within the physiologically relevant range of 0.1 hertz to seventy hertz, eliminating low-frequency drift and high-frequency noise. A notch filter centered at sixty hertz is employed to suppress power line interference, a common source of contamination in clinical and home environments. Following filtering, electromyographic (EMG) artifacts, which arise from facial muscle movements during articulation, are removed using a linear regression technique that models the EMG signals as linear predictors of their influence on the EEG channels. The regression coefficients are computed using the ordinary least squares method, and the resulting residual EEG signals are free from the dominant muscular noise that would otherwise obscure the underlying neural correlates of speech. The cleaned EEG signals are then downsampled to one hundred hertz to match the temporal resolution of the acoustic features and to reduce computational load.

From the preprocessed EEG signals, five distinct features are extracted per channel at each time step. These features include the root mean square, which captures the overall amplitude of the signal; the zero-crossing rate, which reflects the frequency of sign changes and is sensitive to spectral content; the moving window average, which provides a smoothed estimate of signal energy over time; the kurtosis, which quantifies the peakedness or tail heaviness of the signal distribution and is indicative of non-Gaussian neural activity; and the power spectral entropy, which measures the unpredictability of the power distribution across frequency bands and has been shown to correlate with cognitive load and speech processing. These features, originally proposed in prior research for speech perception studies, are selected for their ability to encode both temporal dynamics and spectral characteristics of neural activity related to speech production. The extraction of these five features across all twenty-nine EEG channels results in an initial feature vector of dimension one hundred forty-five at each time step. This high-dimensional representation, while rich in information, contains significant redundancy and non-linear correlations that hinder effective learning by downstream models. To address this, a non-linear dimensionality reduction technique is applied using kernel principal component analysis with a polynomial kernel of degree three. This method maps the original feature space into a lower-dimensional latent space while preserving the intrinsic geometric structure of the data, which is essential for capturing the complex, non-linear relationships between neural activity and speech intent. The optimal number of components is determined by analyzing the cumulative explained variance, and the dimensionality is reduced to ten, resulting in a compact yet highly informative representation of the EEG signal that retains the most discriminative information for speech recognition.

Simultaneously with EEG processing, the speech signal is recorded using a mono-channel microphone at a sampling rate of sixteen kilohertz. The speech waveform is preprocessed to remove background noise and normalize amplitude, and Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients are extracted as the primary acoustic features. These coefficients are computed using a filter bank tuned to the human auditory system, followed by a discrete cosine transform to decorrelate the spectral components. Thirteen MFCCs are extracted at a rate of one hundred hertz, matching the temporal resolution of the EEG features to ensure precise temporal alignment. The MFCCs represent the spectral envelope of the speech signal and are the standard input for most modern speech recognition systems. However, in the context of disordered speech, these features are often corrupted by articulatory distortions, incomplete phoneme realization, and irregular timing, leading to poor recognition performance. To mitigate this, the invention introduces a regression model that learns to predict the MFCCs from the reduced EEG feature representation. This regression model is implemented as a single-layer gated recurrent unit with one hundred twenty-eight hidden units, followed by a time-distributed dense layer with thirteen output units and a linear activation function. The GRU is chosen for its ability to model temporal dependencies in sequential data, which is essential for capturing the dynamic evolution of neural activity during speech production. The model is trained using mean squared error as the loss function and the Adam optimizer, with early stopping employed to prevent overfitting. The training data consists of paired samples of the reduced EEG features and the corresponding ground truth MFCCs, collected from the same subjects during speech production. The regression model learns to map the brain’s internal neural representation of speech to the expected acoustic output, effectively learning what the subject intended to say, even when the actual speech signal deviates significantly from that intention.

Once the regression model is trained, it is deployed as a feature generator during inference. For any new speech utterance, the EEG signal is preprocessed and reduced to the ten-dimensional latent representation, which is then fed into the trained regression model to produce a predicted set of thirteen MFCCs. These predicted MFCCs, referred to as the EEG-derived acoustic representation, are concatenated with the actual MFCCs extracted from the speech signal, resulting in a fused feature vector of dimension twenty-six at each time step. This fused representation now contains both the observed acoustic signal and the brain’s intended acoustic signal, effectively doubling the information available to the speech recognition model while providing a corrective signal that compensates for distortions in the actual speech. The fused features are then fed into a downstream speech recognition model, which may be configured for either isolated sentence recognition or continuous speech recognition, depending on the application.

For isolated sentence recognition, the recognition model is implemented as a single-layer GRU with five hundred twelve hidden units, followed by a dropout layer with a drop rate of twenty percent to regularize training and prevent overfitting. The output of the GRU at the final time step is passed through a dense layer with fifty-seven units, corresponding to the number of unique sentences in the vocabulary, and a linear activation function. The logits from this layer are then passed through a softmax function to produce a probability distribution over the possible sentence labels. The model is trained using categorical cross-entropy as the loss function, with the Adam optimizer and early stopping applied to ensure convergence. The training labels are encoded as one-hot vectors, and the batch size is set to fifty. The model is trained for ten epochs, and the training and validation loss curves demonstrate stable convergence without signs of overfitting, indicating that the fused input provides sufficient discriminative power to generalize effectively to unseen utterances.

For continuous speech recognition, the architecture is adapted to handle variable-length sequences and to predict character-level outputs rather than discrete sentence labels. The system employs an encoder-decoder structure, with the encoder consisting of a single-layer GRU with five hundred twelve hidden units that processes the fused feature sequence. The decoder consists of a dense layer with a linear activation function followed by a softmax layer that outputs a probability distribution over the character set at each time step. The model is trained using connectionist temporal classification loss, which enables alignment-free training of sequences with variable durations. The CTC loss function is optimized using the Adam optimizer over one hundred epochs with a batch size of fifty. During inference, a beam search decoder with a four-gram external language model is employed to refine the character-level predictions into coherent word sequences. This configuration allows the system to handle natural speech with pauses, repetitions, and incomplete utterances, which are common in disordered speech. The system achieves a statistically significant reduction in word error rate compared to baseline models that use only acoustic features, with a p-value of less than twenty-one millionths, confirming the robustness and reliability of the improvement.

The invention further demonstrates that the performance enhancement is not dependent on the use of all twenty-nine EEG sensors. Experiments conducted using only the sensors located over the frontal and temporal lobes—regions known to be involved in speech production and perception—yielded recognition accuracy comparable to that achieved with the full sensor array. This finding is critical for practical deployment, as it allows for the design of lightweight, wearable EEG headsets with fewer electrodes, improving user comfort, reducing setup time, and lowering system cost. Additionally, the system was tested with truncated input sequences, simulating the frequent pauses and halting speech patterns observed in aphasic and apraxic speech. Even when only half of the full speech sequence was provided as input, the system maintained superior performance compared to the baseline, indicating that the EEG-derived acoustic representation provides sufficient contextual information to compensate for incomplete acoustic input. This capability is particularly valuable in real-world applications where users may produce fragmented or interrupted utterances.

The invention also includes a comparative analysis of the effectiveness of EEG-derived features versus EMG-derived features. A parallel set of experiments was conducted in which EMG signals, recorded from facial muscles during articulation, were processed using the same feature extraction and dimensionality reduction pipeline and concatenated with MFCCs. The results showed that while EMG features provided a marginal improvement over the baseline, the enhancement was substantially smaller than that achieved with EEG-derived features. This outcome confirms that the neural signals captured by EEG carry more direct and informative representations of speech intent than the muscular activity captured by EMG, which reflects only the final motor output and is itself subject to the distortions of the disorder. The superior performance of EEG-based features underscores the unique value of the invention in leveraging brain activity as a source of corrective information.

The system is trained and validated using a large-scale dataset comprising eight thousand eight hundred fifty-four synchronized EEG-speech samples collected from nine individuals with diagnosed aphasia, apraxia, or dysarthria. The vocabulary consists of fifty-seven common daily-use English sentences, selected for their relevance to everyday communication. The data was collected in a clinical speech therapy setting using standardized protocols, with subjects performing two tasks: reading sentences displayed on a screen and repeating sentences they had just heard. This dual-task design ensures that both production and perception components of speech are captured, enhancing the model’s ability to generalize across different speech contexts. The dataset was split into seventy percent training, ten percent validation, and twenty percent testing, with no overlap between the sets to ensure unbiased evaluation. All preprocessing, feature extraction, and model training were performed using open-source machine learning libraries, ensuring reproducibility and facilitating future research.

The invention’s performance is evaluated using standard metrics appropriate for each task. For isolated sentence recognition, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score are computed, with epsilon added to denominators to prevent numerical instability. For continuous speech recognition, word error rate is the primary metric, with confidence intervals calculated to assess statistical significance. The results demonstrate that the proposed system achieves a performance improvement of over fifty percent in isolated speech recognition compared to baseline acoustic-only models, and a statistically significant reduction in word error rate for continuous speech recognition, with a confidence interval for the proposed method ranging from 44.25% to 47.13%, compared to 48.58% to 51.10% for the baseline. These improvements are consistent across all subjects, regardless of the severity or type of speech disorder, indicating the system’s robustness and generalizability.

The system is designed to be deployable in real-world settings, including clinical environments, home-based therapy systems, and assistive communication devices. While the current implementation operates in an offline mode for research validation, the architecture is inherently suitable for real-time operation. The latency introduced by the regression model is minimal and scales linearly with input sequence length, making it feasible for deployment on embedded hardware with sufficient computational resources. Future iterations of the system will incorporate on-device inference optimization, model quantization, and streaming feature extraction to enable real-time performance. The system does not require subject-specific calibration or retraining, as the regression model learns generalizable mappings from neural activity to acoustic intent across diverse individuals, making it a scalable solution for widespread use.

The invention further enables the development of closed-loop speech therapy systems, in which the recognition output is used to provide immediate feedback to the user, reinforcing correct articulation and encouraging linguistic recovery. The ability of the system to interpret incomplete or distorted speech with high accuracy allows it to function effectively even during the early stages of rehabilitation, when speech is most impaired. This capability transforms the system from a passive transcription tool into an active therapeutic aid, supporting the neurological reorganization that underlies speech recovery.

In summary, the invention presents a novel, non-invasive, and highly effective method for enhancing speech recognition for individuals with speech disorders by integrating brain signals with acoustic inputs. The system’s ability to extract and utilize latent acoustic representations from EEG signals represents a fundamental advancement in the field, overcoming the limitations of traditional acoustic-only models and providing a scalable, clinically viable solution for a population long excluded from modern communication technologies. The invention is not limited to the specific architectures or feature sets described herein but encompasses any system that employs the core principle of deriving acoustic representations from neural activity to augment speech recognition, making it broadly applicable to future innovations in neuroprosthetics, assistive technologies, and human-computer interaction.
