# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a novel computational method and system for evaluating the therapeutic efficacy of botanical extracts, pharmaceutical compounds, or other bioactive agents on human disease states through the quantitative analysis of intracellular signaling pathway dynamics. Specifically, the invention provides a method for calculating a Cannabis Drug Efficiency Index (CDEI), a standardized, mathematically derived metric that quantifies the capacity of a given therapeutic agent to restore transcriptomic profiles of diseased tissue toward those observed in healthy, non-diseased tissue. The method integrates high-throughput gene expression data with topologically structured signaling pathway models to compute pathway-level perturbations, applies weighted statistical normalization based on sample consistency, and derives a single, interpretable efficiency score that reflects the degree to which a treatment reverses disease-associated molecular signatures. The invention is particularly useful in the fields of personalized medicine, pharmacogenomics, drug discovery, and precision therapeutics, especially where complex, multi-component interventions such as cannabis-derived extracts are evaluated for their ability to modulate inflammatory, neoplastic, neurodegenerative, or autoimmune conditions. The system is designed to operate independently of the specific source of the bioactive agent, making it broadly applicable to synthetic drugs, natural products, combination therapies, and nutraceuticals, provided that transcriptomic or equivalent omics data can be obtained from treated and untreated disease models relative to healthy controls.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The development of effective therapeutic interventions for chronic diseases has long been hindered by the heterogeneity of human biological responses, the complexity of molecular signaling networks, and the limitations of traditional pharmacological evaluation methods. Conventional approaches to drug efficacy assessment rely heavily on phenotypic outcomes—such as symptom reduction, biomarker concentration changes, or survival rates—which often fail to capture the underlying molecular mechanisms driving disease progression or therapeutic reversal. While high-throughput technologies such as RNA sequencing and microarray analysis have enabled the generation of comprehensive gene expression profiles from clinical and experimental samples, the translation of these data into actionable therapeutic insights has proven challenging. Many existing bioinformatic tools analyze gene expression data at the individual gene level, identifying differentially expressed genes without accounting for the functional context in which these genes operate. This gene-centric perspective neglects the fact that biological function emerges from the coordinated activity of interconnected molecular components within signaling, metabolic, and regulatory pathways.

Several computational methods have been developed to bridge the gap between gene expression data and pathway-level biological activity. These include Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), Pathway-Express, Topology-Based scoring methods, and OncoFinder, among others. While these approaches offer improvements over simple gene list enrichment, they suffer from critical limitations. Many do not incorporate the directional nature of molecular interactions—such as activation, inhibition, or feedback regulation—thereby treating pathways as unordered gene sets. Others require extensive prior knowledge of kinetic parameters, protein concentrations, or interaction strengths, which are unavailable for the vast majority of human signaling components. Furthermore, most existing methods are designed to identify pathways that are statistically enriched in disease states relative to healthy controls, but they lack the capacity to evaluate the ability of a therapeutic intervention to normalize those perturbations. In other words, while these methods can tell us which pathways are dysregulated in disease, they cannot reliably determine whether a given drug restores those pathways toward a healthy configuration.

In the context of cannabinoid-based therapeutics, this limitation is particularly acute. Cannabis extracts contain hundreds of chemically distinct compounds, including cannabinoids, terpenoids, flavonoids, and fatty acid derivatives, each of which may interact with multiple cellular targets through complex, often non-linear mechanisms. Traditional pharmacological models that attempt to correlate single compounds—such as tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) or cannabidiol (CBD)—with clinical outcomes have yielded inconsistent results, largely because they ignore the synergistic, antagonistic, or modulatory effects of the full phytochemical spectrum. Moreover, the biological effects of cannabis extracts vary significantly across tissue types, disease etiologies, and individual genetic backgrounds, further complicating the identification of universally effective formulations. There exists no established method to rank cannabis extracts—or any complex botanical mixture—according to their capacity to reverse disease-specific molecular signatures in a quantitative, reproducible, and mechanistically grounded manner.

The need for such a method is urgent. Chronic inflammatory conditions, including dermatitis, oral mucositis, inflammatory bowel disease, and neuroinflammation, affect hundreds of millions of people worldwide. Cannabis-derived products are increasingly used as alternative or adjunctive therapies for these conditions, yet their selection remains largely empirical, guided by anecdotal reports, crude cannabinoid content measurements, or non-specific anti-inflammatory assays. Clinicians lack a reliable tool to determine which extract, among dozens of available formulations, is most likely to restore normal cellular function in a specific patient or tissue type. Similarly, pharmaceutical developers lack a computational framework to prioritize candidates for costly clinical trials. The absence of a standardized, pathway-based, drug-efficiency metric has resulted in a landscape of inconsistent outcomes, variable product quality, and limited regulatory acceptance.

Previous attempts to quantify drug efficacy using transcriptomic data have largely focused on machine learning models trained on historical datasets. While such models can achieve high predictive accuracy, they are inherently opaque, dependent on the quality and bias of their training data, and non-generalizable across different diseases or tissue types. They also do not provide insight into the biological mechanisms underlying the predicted efficacy. Other approaches rely on proprietary algorithms or require access to proprietary databases, limiting their accessibility and reproducibility. There is a clear and unmet need for a transparent, mathematically rigorous, and biologically grounded method that can compute a single, interpretable score reflecting the ability of a therapeutic agent to normalize disease-associated signaling perturbations across multiple pathways, using only publicly available pathway topology data and standard transcriptomic inputs.

The present invention addresses these shortcomings by introducing a novel computational framework that combines the signaling pathway impact analysis (SPIA) algorithm with a statistically weighted, three-condition comparison model—healthy control, untreated disease, and drug-treated disease—to derive a Cannabis Drug Efficiency Index (CDEI). This index is not merely a correlation or enrichment score; it is a normalized, directional, and statistically validated measure of therapeutic reversal. It accounts for the topology of signaling networks, the directionality of molecular interactions, the consistency of pathway response across biological replicates, and the statistical significance of the shift from diseased to normalized states. The method does not require prior knowledge of drug targets, kinetic constants, or protein concentrations. It operates entirely on gene expression data and known pathway architectures, making it widely applicable, reproducible, and scalable. The invention further includes a software system that automates the entire analytical pipeline, from raw sequencing data processing to CDEI score generation, enabling its use by clinicians, researchers, and pharmaceutical developers without specialized bioinformatics expertise.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is a method and system for calculating a Cannabis Drug Efficiency Index (CDEI) that quantifies the capacity of a therapeutic agent to restore the molecular state of diseased tissue to that of healthy tissue, based on the integrated analysis of transcriptomic data and signaling pathway topology. The method operates by first obtaining high-throughput gene expression profiles from three distinct biological conditions: a healthy control state, an untreated disease state, and a disease state treated with a therapeutic agent. These profiles are then processed to calculate the signed log-fold change in gene expression for each gene relative to the average expression observed in the healthy control group. Using a pre-defined topology of intracellular signaling pathways, which includes information on activating and inhibiting interactions between gene products, the method computes a perturbation factor for each gene that accounts not only for its own expression change but also for the propagated effects of upstream regulators within the pathway. From these perturbation factors, an accuracy vector is derived that isolates the influence of network propagation from direct gene expression changes, allowing for a more accurate estimation of pathway-level activation or suppression. The sum of these accuracy values across all genes in a pathway yields a pathway perturbation score, known as the Signaling Pathway Impact Analysis (SPIA) score.

To ensure robustness and biological relevance, the method then calculates a pathway weight factor for each signaling pathway based on the proportion of samples within the treated disease group that exhibit a consistent direction of pathway perturbation—either all positive or all negative—relative to the mean perturbation of the group. This weight factor adjusts the mean SPIA score for each pathway to reflect the consistency of response across biological replicates, thereby reducing noise from outlier samples and enhancing the reliability of downstream statistical comparisons. The adjusted pathway scores are then subjected to a one-sample Student’s t-test to determine whether the mean pathway perturbation in the treated disease state is significantly different from zero, which represents the baseline state of the healthy control. Simultaneously, the same statistical comparison is performed for the untreated disease state to quantify the magnitude of disease-induced pathway disruption. The absolute values of the resulting t-statistics for both the untreated and treated conditions are then used to compute the Cannabis Drug Efficiency Index according to a precisely defined formula: CDEI = 2 × (|tU| / (|tT| + |tU|) − 0.5), where |tU| represents the absolute t-value for the untreated disease versus control comparison, and |tT| represents the absolute t-value for the treated disease versus control comparison. This formula ensures that the CDEI score ranges between −1 and 1, with a value of 0 indicating no therapeutic effect, a value of 1 indicating complete restoration of the healthy molecular state, and a value less than 0 indicating that the treatment exacerbates the disease signature.

The invention further includes a computer-implemented system that automates the entire analytical workflow, including data preprocessing, normalization, pathway mapping, SPIA calculation, weight factor determination, statistical testing, and CDEI score generation. The system accepts raw sequencing reads or normalized gene expression matrices as input and outputs a ranked list of therapeutic agents according to their CDEI scores for a given disease and tissue type. The system is designed to be platform-independent, operating on both Windows and Linux operating systems, and includes a graphical user interface that guides users through each step of the process, validates input data integrity, and generates comprehensive reports including pathway-level heatmaps, statistical summaries, and drug efficacy rankings. The method is not limited to cannabis extracts; it is equally applicable to any bioactive compound or mixture for which transcriptomic data can be obtained under the three-condition paradigm. The invention enables the objective, reproducible, and mechanism-based selection of therapeutic agents for personalized treatment strategies, the prioritization of candidates for clinical development, and the stratification of patient populations based on predicted molecular response to therapy.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE EMBODIMENTS

### Overview of Signaling Pathway Impact Analysis (SPIA) Method

The Signaling Pathway Impact Analysis (SPIA) method, as employed in the present invention, constitutes the foundational computational engine that transforms raw gene expression data into biologically meaningful pathway-level perturbation scores. Unlike traditional gene set enrichment methods that treat pathways as static lists of genes, SPIA explicitly models the dynamic, directional nature of intracellular signaling networks, accounting for the causal relationships between upstream regulators and downstream effectors. The method begins with the construction of a directed graph representation of each signaling pathway, where each node corresponds to a gene or its encoded protein product, and each edge represents a known molecular interaction—either activation or inhibition—between two components. These interactions are derived from curated biological databases such as QIAGEN SABiosciences, Reactome, KEGG, and WikiPathways, ensuring that the topology reflects current scientific understanding of human cellular signaling. The adjacency matrix of this graph is defined such that each element a_ij equals 1 if there exists a direct interaction from gene g_i to gene g_j, including self-loops to account for autoregulatory feedback, and 0 otherwise. This matrix serves as the structural scaffold upon which the perturbation propagation is computed.

For each gene in a given pathway, the method calculates a perturbation factor that quantifies the net influence of both direct expression changes and indirect, network-mediated effects. The perturbation factor for gene g is defined as the sum of its own signed log-fold change in expression relative to the healthy control population, plus a weighted contribution from all upstream genes that regulate it. The weight assigned to each upstream gene γ is determined by the type of interaction (activation or inhibition) and the number of downstream targets that γ influences, ensuring that genes with broad regulatory influence contribute proportionally more to the perturbation of their targets. Specifically, the perturbation factor PF(g) is computed as the sum of ΔE(g), the direct expression change of gene g, and the summation over all upstream genes γ of the product of β_γg, the interaction coefficient, and PF(γ) divided by n_down(γ), the number of downstream genes regulated by γ. The interaction coefficient β_γg is assigned a value of +1 when γ activates g and −1 when γ inhibits g, thereby preserving the directional nature of the signaling cascade. This recursive formulation allows the perturbation to propagate through the network, capturing the amplification, attenuation, or inversion of signals as they traverse multiple layers of regulation.

To isolate the contribution of network propagation from direct gene expression changes, the method computes an accuracy vector Acc(g), defined as the difference between the perturbation factor PF(g) and the direct expression change ΔE(g). This vector represents the extent to which the observed expression of gene g is influenced by its position within the signaling network rather than by autonomous transcriptional regulation. The mathematical derivation of this vector leads to a compact matrix representation, where the entire accuracy vector can be expressed as the product of a transformation matrix B, the inverse of the identity matrix minus B, and the vector of direct expression changes ΔE. The matrix B is constructed such that each row corresponds to a gene and each column to a potential upstream regulator, with entries equal to β_γg divided by n_down(γ). This formulation enables efficient computation of pathway perturbations even for large, complex networks containing hundreds of interacting components. The overall SPIA score for a given pathway is then obtained by summing the accuracy values across all genes in that pathway, yielding a single scalar value that reflects the net direction and magnitude of pathway activation or suppression in the diseased state relative to the healthy control.

This approach offers several critical advantages over alternative methods. First, it does not require arbitrary thresholds for defining differentially expressed genes, thereby avoiding the loss of information inherent in binary classification schemes. Second, it incorporates the directionality of interactions, allowing the method to distinguish between pathways that are activated versus those that are suppressed, which is essential for predicting the therapeutic outcome of interventions that may target specific nodes in the network. Third, by propagating perturbations through the network topology, SPIA captures the systemic consequences of molecular changes that would be invisible to gene-centric analyses. For example, a modest upregulation of a key upstream kinase may lead to a substantial downstream activation of multiple effector genes, a phenomenon that SPIA quantifies as a single, coherent pathway-level signal. This systemic perspective is particularly valuable in the context of complex botanical extracts, where multiple compounds may act on different nodes of the same pathway, producing a combined effect that cannot be predicted by examining individual gene responses. The SPIA method, therefore, provides a biologically realistic and mathematically rigorous framework for translating high-dimensional transcriptomic data into interpretable, pathway-level insights that form the basis for the subsequent calculation of the Cannabis Drug Efficiency Index.

### Calculation of Cannabis Drug Efficiency Index (CDEI)

The calculation of the Cannabis Drug Efficiency Index (CDEI) represents the core innovation of the present invention, integrating the pathway-level perturbation scores generated by the SPIA method with a statistically rigorous, three-condition comparison framework to produce a single, interpretable metric of therapeutic efficacy. The CDEI is not a simple correlation or enrichment score; it is a normalized, directional, and statistically validated measure of the ability of a therapeutic agent to reverse the molecular signature of disease and restore it to a state resembling that of healthy tissue. The method begins with the acquisition of transcriptomic data from three distinct biological conditions: a healthy control group, an untreated disease group, and a disease group treated with the therapeutic agent under evaluation. Each condition is represented by multiple biological replicates to ensure statistical robustness and to account for inter-individual variability. For each pathway in the reference database, the SPIA score is computed for every sample in each condition, yielding a distribution of pathway perturbation values for the control, untreated, and treated states.

The next step involves the computation of a pathway weight factor, which adjusts the mean SPIA score of each pathway to reflect the consistency of response across biological replicates within the treated disease group. This is a critical innovation that distinguishes the present invention from prior methods, which often treat all samples equally regardless of their agreement in pathway directionality. For pathways whose mean SPIA score in the treated disease group is positive, the weight factor is defined as the proportion of samples in that group exhibiting a positive SPIA score. For pathways whose mean SPIA score is negative, the weight factor is defined as the proportion of samples exhibiting a negative SPIA score. This ensures that pathways with high internal consistency—where nearly all treated samples show the same direction of perturbation—are given greater weight in the final analysis, while pathways with inconsistent or divergent responses are downweighted, reducing the influence of noise or biological heterogeneity. The adjusted mean SPIA score for each pathway, denoted SPIAμ, is then obtained by multiplying the unweighted mean SPIA score by its corresponding weight factor. This adjustment ensures that the statistical comparison that follows is based not merely on the average perturbation, but on the perturbation that is consistently observed across the treated population.

With the adjusted pathway scores in hand, the method proceeds to perform a one-sample Student’s t-test for each pathway in the treated disease group, testing the null hypothesis that the mean SPIAμ value is equal to zero. The same test is performed for the untreated disease group to quantify the magnitude of disease-induced pathway disruption. The resulting t-statistics, which reflect the strength and direction of the deviation from the healthy baseline, are converted to their absolute values to eliminate directional bias, as the goal is to measure the degree of restoration regardless of whether the pathway was initially overactive or underactive. The absolute t-values for the untreated and treated conditions are denoted |tU| and |tT|, respectively. These values serve as the primary inputs for the CDEI formula, which is defined as CDEI = 2 × (|tU| / (|tT| + |tU|) − 0.5). This formula is mathematically elegant in its design: when |tT| is equal to |tU|, the ratio becomes 0.5, and the CDEI evaluates to zero, indicating no therapeutic effect. When |tT| approaches zero, meaning the treated state has been restored to the healthy baseline, the ratio approaches 1, and the CDEI reaches its maximum value of 1, indicating perfect therapeutic efficiency. Conversely, if |tT| exceeds |tU|, the ratio falls below 0.5, resulting in a negative CDEI value, which indicates that the treatment has exacerbated the disease signature rather than ameliorated it. The CDEI score thus provides a continuous, interpretable scale ranging from −1 to 1, where each value corresponds to a quantifiable degree of therapeutic success or failure.

This formulation has several key advantages. First, it is scale-invariant, meaning that it does not depend on the absolute magnitude of gene expression changes but rather on their relative consistency and statistical significance in the context of the disease and treatment conditions. Second, it is inherently comparative, requiring the presence of all three conditions—control, untreated, and treated—to compute a meaningful score, thereby preventing false positives that might arise from comparing only diseased and treated states without a healthy baseline. Third, it is statistically grounded, relying on the well-established principles of the Student’s t-test to ensure that only perturbations that are significantly different from zero contribute to the final score. Fourth, it is agnostic to the nature of the therapeutic agent, whether it is a single compound, a botanical extract, a combination therapy, or a synthetic drug, as long as transcriptomic data can be obtained under the required conditions. The method does not require knowledge of drug targets, pharmacokinetics, or biochemical mechanisms; it operates purely on the observable molecular outcome of treatment. This makes it uniquely suited for evaluating complex, multi-component interventions such as cannabis extracts, where the active constituents and their mechanisms of action are often unknown or poorly characterized. The CDEI score, therefore, serves as a direct, quantitative measure of therapeutic efficacy that is both biologically meaningful and clinically actionable.

### Example of CDEI Calculations

To illustrate the practical application of the Cannabis Drug Efficiency Index (CDEI) calculation, consider a hypothetical dataset derived from three experimental conditions involving human 3D EpiOral tissues exposed to tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα) to induce inflammation, followed by treatment with a cannabis extract. The dataset includes six biological replicates for each condition: a healthy control group (C), an untreated inflamed group (U), and a treated inflamed group (T). Gene expression profiles were obtained via RNA sequencing, and the data were processed using standard bioinformatics pipelines to generate normalized gene counts. These counts were then mapped to 241 inflammation-related signaling pathways from the QIAGEN SABiosciences database, and the SPIA algorithm was applied to compute pathway perturbation scores for each sample. For the purpose of this example, we focus on the five most relevant pathways: NF-κB signaling, JAK-STAT signaling, MAPK signaling, TNF signaling, and IL-17 signaling.

For the NF-κB pathway, the mean SPIA score in the untreated group (U) was +3.2, indicating strong pathway activation, while the mean SPIA score in the treated group (T) was +0.4, suggesting partial restoration. The weight factor for this pathway was calculated as 0.83, since five out of six treated samples exhibited a positive SPIA score, consistent with the direction of the mean. The adjusted mean SPIA score (SPIAμ) was therefore 3.2 × 0.83 = 2.66. The one-sample t-test comparing this adjusted mean to zero yielded a t-statistic of 4.12, so |tU| = 4.12. For the treated group, the mean SPIAμ was 0.4 × 0.83 = 0.33, and the t-test yielded a t-statistic of 0.89, so |tT| = 0.89. Applying the CDEI formula: CDEI = 2 × (4.12 / (0.89 + 4.12) − 0.5) = 2 × (4.12 / 5.01 − 0.5) = 2 × (0.822 − 0.5) = 2 × 0.322 = 0.644. This result indicates a moderate to high efficiency of the cannabis extract in restoring NF-κB pathway activity toward the healthy baseline.

For the JAK-STAT pathway, the untreated group exhibited a mean SPIA score of −2.8, indicating suppression, and the treated group showed a mean SPIA score of −0.1. The weight factor was 0.83, as five out of six treated samples showed negative perturbation. The adjusted mean SPIAμ for the untreated group was −2.8 × 0.83 = −2.32, yielding |tU| = 3.98. For the treated group, the adjusted mean SPIAμ was −0.1 × 0.83 = −0.083, yielding |tT| = 0.72. The CDEI calculation is then: CDEI = 2 × (3.98 / (0.72 + 3.98) − 0.5) = 2 × (3.98 / 4.70 − 0.5) = 2 × (0.847 − 0.5) = 2 × 0.347 = 0.694. This indicates a similarly high efficiency in restoring JAK-STAT pathway suppression to the healthy state.

In contrast, for the MAPK pathway, the untreated group had a mean SPIA score of +1.5, and the treated group had a mean SPIA score of +2.1. The weight factor remained 0.83, yielding adjusted means of 1.25 and 1.74, respectively. The t-statistics were |tU| = 2.56 and |tT| = 3.10. The CDEI calculation yields: CDEI = 2 × (2.56 / (3.10 + 2.56) − 0.5) = 2 × (2.56 / 5.66 − 0.5) = 2 × (0.452 − 0.5) = 2 × (−0.048) = −0.096. This negative value indicates that the cannabis extract, rather than restoring the pathway, exacerbated its activation beyond the disease baseline, suggesting a potentially harmful effect in this context.

The final CDEI score for the cannabis extract across all pathways is computed as the weighted average of the individual pathway CDEI scores, with weights proportional to the number of genes in each pathway and the statistical significance of the pathway perturbation. In this example, the overall CDEI for the extract is calculated as 0.72, placing it among the most effective candidates for anti-inflammatory therapy in this tissue type. The system then ranks this extract against others tested under identical conditions, producing a prioritized list of candidates based on their ability to restore molecular homeostasis. This example demonstrates that the CDEI method does not merely identify which pathways are affected by treatment, but quantifies the degree to which treatment successfully reverses disease-associated perturbations, even when those perturbations are subtle, inconsistent, or bidirectional. It further reveals that a treatment may be highly effective for some pathways while being detrimental for others, a critical insight that would be lost in aggregate measures such as overall gene expression changes or cytokine levels. The CDEI, therefore, provides a nuanced, pathway-specific, and statistically robust evaluation of therapeutic efficacy that is uniquely suited to the complexity of modern biological interventions.
