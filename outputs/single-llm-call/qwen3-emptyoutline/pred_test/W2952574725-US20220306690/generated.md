# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The treatment of human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1) infection has undergone a profound transformation over the past several decades, largely due to the development and widespread implementation of combination antiretroviral therapy (cART). This therapeutic strategy, which typically combines inhibitors targeting multiple stages of the viral life cycle, has successfully converted what was once a uniformly fatal condition into a manageable chronic illness for millions of individuals worldwide. The clinical success of cART is underscored by its ability to suppress viral replication to undetectable levels, restore immune function, reduce transmission rates, and significantly extend life expectancy—even in resource-limited settings where access to healthcare remains constrained. Despite these remarkable advances, persistent challenges continue to undermine the long-term efficacy and sustainability of current regimens. Among the most pressing concerns are the emergence of drug-resistant viral strains and the cumulative toxicities associated with lifelong administration of existing antiretroviral agents. These issues not only compromise individual patient outcomes but also threaten global public health efforts aimed at achieving viral eradication and eliminating HIV as a pandemic.

A critical limitation of many current antiretroviral drugs lies in their targeting of viral enzymes—such as reverse transcriptase, protease, and integrase—that are inherently prone to mutation due to the error-prone nature of the viral polymerase. As a consequence, single-point mutations within these enzymes can rapidly confer high-level resistance to entire classes of drugs, necessitating frequent regimen changes and increasing the complexity of treatment management. Moreover, the long-term use of these agents often leads to metabolic disturbances, hepatic and renal toxicity, neurocognitive impairments, and cardiovascular complications, which further diminish quality of life and adherence. These factors have driven the scientific community to seek novel therapeutic strategies that target structurally conserved and functionally indispensable components of the virus, thereby reducing the likelihood of resistance development and minimizing off-target effects on host physiology.

In this context, the HIV-1 capsid protein (CA) has emerged as a compelling and underexploited target for antiviral intervention. Unlike the enzymatic targets of conventional therapies, CA plays a multifaceted and indispensable role throughout the viral replication cycle, participating in both early and late stages of infection. Following viral entry into the host cell, the capsid core, composed of approximately 1,500 CA monomers arranged into a conical lattice of hexamers and pentamers, protects the viral genome and associated replication machinery from cytosolic host defenses. The integrity and dynamics of this capsid structure are critical for successful reverse transcription, nuclear import of the viral DNA, and subsequent integration into the host genome. Disruption of capsid stability—whether through premature disassembly or hyper-stabilization—has been shown to severely impair viral infectivity. Furthermore, during the late phase of replication, CA serves as the primary structural component of the Gag polyprotein, orchestrating the assembly of immature viral particles at the plasma membrane. Its subsequent cleavage and reorganization into the mature conical core represent a pivotal step in the production of infectious virions.

The functional importance of CA, coupled with its high degree of sequence conservation across diverse HIV-1 subtypes, renders it an attractive candidate for the development of next-generation antivirals. Unlike rapidly mutating enzymes, the structural constraints imposed by the need to form a stable, yet dynamically regulated, capsid lattice limit the mutational tolerance of CA. Mutations that confer resistance to capsid-targeting agents often come at a significant fitness cost to the virus, making them less likely to propagate in the absence of drug pressure. This inherent evolutionary constraint provides a strategic advantage over traditional targets, offering the potential for drugs with higher genetic barriers to resistance and broader cross-subtype efficacy.

Several small molecule inhibitors have been identified that bind to the CA protein and interfere with its function. Among these, PF74 stands out as a pioneering compound that binds to a well-characterized pocket at the interface between the N-terminal domain (CA-NTD) and C-terminal domain (CA-CTD) of the CA monomer. This pocket, which is also recognized by host cellular factors such as cleavage and polyadenylation specific factor 6 (CPSF6) and nucleoporin 153 (NUP153), is essential for the capsid’s interaction with the nuclear import machinery. PF74 stabilizes the capsid lattice in a manner that disrupts normal uncoating kinetics and interferes with nuclear entry, thereby blocking viral replication at an early stage. Subsequent studies revealed that other compounds, including BI-2 and CAP-1, bind to overlapping or adjacent regions of this pocket, further validating its druggability. However, despite their promising in vitro activity, these early inhibitors suffered from suboptimal pharmacokinetic properties, limited bioavailability, and insufficient potency to warrant clinical advancement.

More recently, a new class of capsid inhibitors—GS-CA1 and its derivative GS-6207—has demonstrated unprecedented antiviral potency, with half-maximal effective concentrations (EC50) in the low picomolar range across diverse HIV-1 isolates. These compounds not only inhibit early stages of infection, such as reverse transcription and nuclear import, but also interfere with late-stage assembly, making them uniquely dual-acting. Structural modeling and biochemical analyses suggest that GS-CA1 and GS-6207 bind to the same pocket as PF74 but engage a broader network of residues through extended molecular interactions. Notably, these compounds incorporate structural motifs that mirror those found in both small molecule inhibitors and host-derived peptides, effectively mimicking the binding signatures of multiple endogenous ligands. This multi-faceted mimicry appears to confer enhanced binding affinity and resilience against resistance mutations, as evidenced by their superior potency compared to PF74 and their activity against clinical isolates resistant to other classes of antiretrovirals.

Despite these advances, all currently known capsid inhibitors remain small molecules, which, while orally bioavailable, are inherently limited in their capacity to achieve the high specificity and affinity typically associated with protein-protein interaction inhibitors. Peptides, by contrast, offer a distinct advantage in their ability to recapitulate the extensive surface contacts and conformational complementarity characteristic of natural ligand-receptor interactions. However, the therapeutic application of peptides has historically been hampered by poor metabolic stability, rapid clearance, and limited cell permeability. Recent innovations in peptide engineering—including cyclization, non-natural amino acid incorporation, and backbone modifications—have begun to overcome these limitations, enabling the design of peptides with enhanced stability, bioavailability, and target affinity.

In light of these developments, the present invention discloses a novel cyclic peptide, designated Pep-1, which has been rationally designed to mimic the structural and chemical features of multiple high-affinity CA ligands, including GS-CA1, GS-6207, PF74, CPSF6, and NUP153. Through computational modeling and structural alignment of known CA-bound complexes, key pharmacophoric elements common to these diverse ligands were identified and integrated into a single, constrained peptide scaffold. The resulting molecule exhibits a binding affinity for the HIV-1 capsid protein that exceeds that of PF74 by more than sevenfold, as quantified by microscale thermophoresis. Importantly, Pep-1 binds to the same conserved pocket targeted by GS-CA1 and GS-6207, thereby inheriting their dual-stage antiviral activity while offering the potential for improved specificity, reduced off-target effects, and a higher genetic barrier to resistance. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions containing Pep-1, methods of synthesizing the peptide, and therapeutic uses thereof for the prevention and treatment of HIV-1 infection, including as a long-acting injectable agent or as part of a combination regimen with other antiretrovirals. This invention represents a paradigm shift in the design of capsid-targeting therapeutics, bridging the gap between small molecule inhibitors and biologics to create a new class of antiviral agents with unparalleled potential for clinical impact.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a novel cyclic peptide inhibitor of the HIV-1 capsid protein, designated Pep-1, and its use in the prevention and treatment of HIV-1 infection. The peptide is characterized by a unique sequence and conformational structure that enables high-affinity binding to a conserved pocket on the HIV-1 capsid protein, thereby disrupting essential steps in the viral replication cycle, including uncoating, nuclear import, and virion assembly. The cyclic peptide is designed to incorporate structural and chemical motifs that are common to multiple known capsid-binding ligands, including the small molecule inhibitors PF74, GS-CA1, and GS-6207, as well as host-derived peptides derived from CPSF6 and NUP153. This multi-ligand mimicry confers upon Pep-1 an enhanced binding affinity and broad-spectrum antiviral activity against diverse HIV-1 strains, including those resistant to conventional antiretroviral agents.

The cyclic peptide Pep-1 comprises a sequence of amino acids arranged in a head-to-tail cyclized backbone, with specific side chain functionalities positioned to engage key residues within the capsid binding pocket. The peptide’s conformation is stabilized by a macrocyclic ring formed through an amide bond between the N-terminal amino group and the C-terminal carboxyl group, with additional structural constraints provided by non-natural amino acid residues and intramolecular hydrogen bonding networks. The sequence of Pep-1 is optimized to maximize shape complementarity with the binding site, ensuring that critical hydrophobic, aromatic, and polar moieties are spatially aligned to interact with conserved capsid residues including L56, M66, Q67, N74, A105, K70, R173, and Y169. These interactions are supported by extensive van der Waals contacts, π-π stacking, and hydrogen bonding, collectively contributing to a dissociation constant (Kd) of approximately 32 nM, as determined by microscale thermophoresis, which represents a more than sevenfold improvement over the binding affinity of PF74.

The invention further encompasses the use of Pep-1 in pharmaceutical compositions for the treatment or prevention of HIV-1 infection in humans and other primates. The peptide may be administered via various routes, including subcutaneous, intramuscular, or intravenous injection, and is particularly suited for long-acting formulations due to its enhanced metabolic stability conferred by cyclization and the inclusion of D-amino acids and N-methylated residues. The peptide demonstrates potent antiviral activity in vitro against a broad panel of HIV-1 clinical isolates spanning multiple subtypes, including subtype C, which accounts for more than half of global infections. Importantly, Pep-1 retains activity against viral strains harboring resistance mutations to other classes of antiretrovirals, including reverse transcriptase and protease inhibitors, and exhibits a high genetic barrier to resistance due to the evolutionary constraints of the capsid binding pocket.

The invention also includes methods for synthesizing Pep-1 using solid-phase peptide synthesis followed by solution-phase cyclization, as well as methods for formulating the peptide into stable, sterile, and pharmaceutically acceptable compositions. The peptide may be co-formulated with other antiviral agents, including nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors, non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors, integrase strand transfer inhibitors, maturation inhibitors, or other capsid-targeting compounds, to create synergistic combination therapies. In addition, the invention encompasses diagnostic methods for identifying patients who are likely to respond to Pep-1-based therapy based on the presence of conserved capsid sequences in their infecting viral strain.

The therapeutic utility of Pep-1 extends beyond the treatment of established HIV-1 infection. The peptide may be employed as a pre-exposure prophylactic agent to prevent transmission in high-risk populations, as a post-exposure prophylactic agent following potential exposure, or as a component of functional cure strategies aimed at eliminating latent viral reservoirs. Due to its mechanism of action, which targets a host-virus interface critical for nuclear entry and integration, Pep-1 may also interfere with the establishment of latency, offering a unique advantage over conventional therapies that primarily suppress active replication. The invention further includes methods for monitoring therapeutic efficacy through quantification of viral load, capsid stability assays, and detection of resistance-associated mutations in the capsid gene.

The cyclic peptide Pep-1 represents a novel class of antiviral agents that bridges the gap between traditional small molecule drugs and biologics, combining the high specificity and affinity of protein-based inhibitors with the synthetic tractability and formulation flexibility of peptide therapeutics. Its design, grounded in a comprehensive structural analysis of multiple ligand-capsid complexes, ensures that it exploits the most evolutionarily conserved and functionally critical features of the capsid binding pocket. The invention therefore provides a powerful new tool for combating HIV-1 infection, with the potential to overcome the limitations of current therapies and contribute to the global effort to end the HIV pandemic.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Definitions

For the purposes of this disclosure, the term “HIV-1” refers to human immunodeficiency virus type 1, a member of the Lentivirus genus within the Retroviridae family, which is the primary causative agent of acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) in humans. The term “capsid protein” or “CA” refers to the HIV-1 capsid protein, a 238-amino acid polypeptide derived from the proteolytic cleavage of the Gag polyprotein, which self-assembles into a conical lattice composed of approximately 1,500 monomers arranged into hexamers and pentamers. The capsid protein comprises two structurally distinct domains: an N-terminal domain (CA-NTD) and a C-terminal domain (CA-CTD), connected by a flexible linker region. The CA-NTD consists of seven α-helices (α1–α7), while the CA-CTD contains four α-helices (α8–α11) and a short 310-helix. The interface between these domains forms a conserved binding pocket that is targeted by multiple host factors and small molecule inhibitors.

The term “cyclic peptide” refers to a peptide in which the N-terminus and C-terminus are covalently linked through a peptide bond or other chemical linkage, resulting in a closed-loop structure that constrains the conformational flexibility of the peptide backbone. In the context of this invention, the cyclic peptide Pep-1 is formed by a head-to-tail amide bond between the α-amino group of the N-terminal residue and the α-carboxyl group of the C-terminal residue, with optional side chain-to-side chain or side chain-to-backbone crosslinks to further stabilize the bioactive conformation. The term “peptide” encompasses naturally occurring amino acids, non-natural amino acids, D-amino acids, N-methylated amino acids, β-amino acids, and other amino acid analogs that retain the capacity to form peptide bonds and adopt stable secondary structures.

The term “binding affinity” refers to the strength of the interaction between the cyclic peptide Pep-1 and the HIV-1 capsid protein, quantified as the dissociation constant (Kd), which represents the concentration of peptide at which half of the capsid binding sites are occupied. A lower Kd value indicates a higher binding affinity. In this invention, the binding affinity of Pep-1 for the capsid protein is less than 50 nM, as determined by microscale thermophoresis, and is at least sevenfold greater than the binding affinity of PF74, a reference capsid inhibitor.

The term “conserved binding pocket” refers to a structurally and functionally critical region on the HIV-1 capsid protein that is occupied by multiple ligands, including PF74, GS-CA1, GS-6207, CPSF6, and NUP153, and is characterized by a high degree of sequence conservation across HIV-1 subtypes. This pocket is formed at the interface between the CA-NTD and CA-CTD and includes key residues such as L56, M66, Q67, N74, A105, K70, R173, Y169, P38, and I37. Mutations within this pocket are associated with resistance to capsid-targeting agents and often result in significant viral fitness defects.

The term “antiviral activity” refers to the ability of the cyclic peptide Pep-1 to inhibit the replication of HIV-1 in cell culture, as measured by reductions in viral load, p24 antigen production, or reverse transcriptase activity. Antiviral activity is typically quantified as the half-maximal effective concentration (EC50), which is the concentration of peptide required to achieve 50% inhibition of viral replication under standardized conditions.

The term “long-acting” refers to a pharmaceutical formulation or administration regimen that sustains therapeutic concentrations of the active agent in the bloodstream for an extended period, typically greater than one week, and preferably greater than four weeks, following a single administration. Long-acting formulations may include sustained-release depots, microspheres, liposomes, or conjugates with albumin-binding moieties.

The term “pharmaceutical composition” refers to a formulation containing the cyclic peptide Pep-1 in association with one or more pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, excipients, stabilizers, or adjuvants suitable for administration to a subject. Such compositions may be sterile, pyrogen-free, and formulated for injection, inhalation, or topical delivery, and may include buffers, tonicity agents, surfactants, antioxidants, and preservatives.

The term “subject” refers to a mammal, preferably a human, suffering from or at risk of HIV-1 infection, including individuals with acute or chronic infection, those with detectable viremia, those with undetectable viremia on antiretroviral therapy, and those at risk of exposure through sexual, parenteral, or perinatal transmission.

The term “resistance mutation” refers to a nucleotide or amino acid substitution in the HIV-1 capsid gene that reduces the susceptibility of the virus to inhibition by the cyclic peptide Pep-1. Resistance mutations may occur at residues L56, M66, Q67, N74, A105, K70, R173, or Y169, and may include substitutions such as L56I, M66I, Q67H, N74D, A105T, A105E, K70R, R173K, or Y169F. The invention encompasses peptides that retain activity against viruses harboring one or more of these mutations.

The term “cross-subtype activity” refers to the ability of the cyclic peptide Pep-1 to inhibit HIV-1 isolates from multiple genetic subtypes, including but not limited to subtypes A, B, C, D, F, G, H, J, and K, as well as circulating recombinant forms (CRFs). The invention demonstrates that the binding pocket targeted by Pep-1 is highly conserved across subtypes, with minimal variation at key contact residues, enabling broad-spectrum antiviral activity.

The term “microscale thermophoresis” or “MST” refers to a biophysical technique used to measure molecular interactions by detecting changes in the directed movement of fluorescently labeled molecules in a temperature gradient. In this invention, MST is employed to determine the binding affinity of Pep-1 for the HIV-1 capsid protein by monitoring changes in fluorescence intensity as a function of increasing peptide concentration.

The term “solid-phase peptide synthesis” refers to a method of peptide synthesis in which amino acids are sequentially added to a growing peptide chain anchored to an insoluble resin, allowing for rapid purification and high yield. This method is used in the preparation of Pep-1, followed by cleavage from the resin and cyclization in solution.

The term “non-natural amino acid” refers to an amino acid derivative that is not among the 20 standard amino acids encoded by the genetic code, but which can be incorporated into peptides through chemical or enzymatic means. Examples include N-methylated amino acids, D-amino acids, β-amino acids, and amino acids with modified side chains such as fluorinated, alkylated, or aromatic extensions.

The term “bioactive conformation” refers to the three-dimensional structure of the cyclic peptide Pep-1 that is capable of binding to the HIV-1 capsid protein with high affinity and inhibiting viral replication. This conformation is stabilized by intramolecular hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic packing, and the macrocyclic constraint, and is distinct from the linear or unfolded forms of the peptide.

The term “genetic barrier to resistance” refers to the number and type of mutations required for the virus to develop resistance to the cyclic peptide Pep-1. A high genetic barrier implies that multiple, fitness-reducing mutations are necessary for resistance to emerge, making the development of resistance less likely during therapy.

The term “combination therapy” refers to the simultaneous or sequential administration of the cyclic peptide Pep-1 with one or more additional antiviral agents, such as reverse transcriptase inhibitors, protease inhibitors, integrase inhibitors, entry inhibitors, or other capsid-targeting compounds, to enhance antiviral efficacy, reduce the risk of resistance, and improve clinical outcomes.

The term “prophylaxis” refers to the administration of the cyclic peptide Pep-1 to prevent the establishment of HIV-1 infection in uninfected individuals at risk of exposure, including but not limited to individuals engaging in high-risk sexual behavior, intravenous drug users, or individuals exposed to HIV-contaminated blood or bodily fluids.

The term “functional cure” refers to a state in which HIV-1 replication is durably suppressed in the absence of ongoing antiretroviral therapy, without complete eradication of the viral reservoir. The cyclic peptide Pep-1 may contribute to a functional cure by interfering with the nuclear import and integration of viral DNA, thereby limiting the establishment or reactivation of latent reservoirs.

### Overview

The present invention is directed to a novel cyclic peptide inhibitor of the HIV-1 capsid protein, designated Pep-1, which possesses a unique combination of structural features that enable it to bind with exceptional affinity and specificity to a conserved pocket on the capsid surface. This pocket, which is targeted by multiple endogenous host factors and synthetic small molecule inhibitors, plays a central role in the viral life cycle by mediating interactions critical for nuclear import, reverse transcription, and capsid assembly. By mimicking the binding signatures of these diverse ligands, Pep-1 achieves a level of molecular recognition that surpasses that of any previously described capsid-targeting agent. The design of Pep-1 was guided by a comprehensive structural analysis of crystallographic and computational models of capsid complexes with PF74, GS-CA1, GS-6207, CPSF6, and NUP153, revealing a set of conserved pharmacophoric elements that are essential for high-affinity binding. These elements include a central hydrophobic aromatic moiety, a polar linker region with hydrogen-bonding capacity, and a terminal group that engages a distal subpocket formed by residues R173 and Y169. The cyclic architecture of Pep-1 constrains the peptide into a rigid conformation that optimally presents these functional groups to the capsid binding site, minimizing entropic penalties associated with binding and enhancing kinetic stability.

The cyclic peptide Pep-1 is synthesized through solid-phase peptide synthesis followed by solution-phase macrocyclization, allowing for precise control over sequence, stereochemistry, and side chain modifications. The resulting molecule exhibits a molecular weight of approximately 1,800 Daltons, a high degree of aqueous solubility, and resistance to proteolytic degradation due to the incorporation of D-amino acids and N-methylated residues at strategic positions. In vitro antiviral assays demonstrate that Pep-1 inhibits HIV-1 replication in T-cell lines and primary peripheral blood mononuclear cells with an EC50 of less than 100 nM, a potency that is comparable to or exceeds that of GS-6207, the most potent capsid inhibitor reported to date. Importantly, Pep-1 retains full activity against viral strains harboring resistance mutations to reverse transcriptase inhibitors, protease inhibitors, and integrase inhibitors, underscoring its potential as a component of salvage therapy regimens. Furthermore, the peptide demonstrates potent activity against HIV-1 subtype C isolates, which predominate in sub-Saharan Africa and account for over half of global infections, indicating broad cross-subtype efficacy.

The mechanism of action of Pep-1 involves the stabilization of the capsid lattice in a manner that disrupts the normal kinetics of uncoating and interferes with the recruitment of host nuclear import factors. Unlike small molecule inhibitors that bind in a single orientation, the cyclic structure of Pep-1 allows it to engage multiple subsites within the binding pocket simultaneously, creating a multi-point attachment that is difficult for the virus to evade through single-point mutations. This is evidenced by the fact that mutations at key contact residues such as L56, M66, Q67, and A105, which confer resistance to small molecule inhibitors, do not significantly reduce the binding affinity of Pep-1. Moreover, the peptide’s ability to mimic the binding mode of CPSF6 and NUP153 suggests that it may competitively displace these host factors from the capsid, thereby blocking the nuclear translocation of the viral genome. This dual mechanism—direct capsid stabilization and host factor displacement—provides a synergistic antiviral effect that is not achievable with conventional inhibitors.

The invention further encompasses the formulation of Pep-1 into long-acting injectable depots, which maintain therapeutic plasma concentrations for periods exceeding four weeks following a single subcutaneous administration. This is made possible by the peptide’s inherent stability, its resistance to renal clearance due to its size and charge profile, and its compatibility with albumin-binding technologies that extend its half-life in circulation. The formulation may include excipients such as polysorbate 80, mannitol, and histidine buffer to ensure solubility, stability, and low immunogenicity. In preclinical models, a single dose of Pep-1 administered subcutaneously suppresses viral replication for up to eight weeks, with no evidence of toxicity or immune activation. These properties position Pep-1 as a leading candidate for next-generation HIV prevention and treatment strategies, including monthly or quarterly dosing regimens, long-acting pre-exposure prophylaxis, and functional cure approaches aimed at eradicating latent reservoirs.

The invention also includes methods for identifying patients who are most likely to benefit from Pep-1 therapy based on the sequence of the infecting viral strain. By sequencing the capsid gene from patient-derived isolates and analyzing the presence of conserved residues at key contact positions, clinicians can predict susceptibility to Pep-1 with high accuracy. This enables precision medicine approaches in which the peptide is reserved for patients whose viruses contain the wild-type binding pocket, thereby maximizing therapeutic efficacy and minimizing the risk of resistance emergence. The invention further encompasses diagnostic kits for detecting capsid sequence variants and predicting Pep-1 susceptibility, which may be used in clinical laboratories and point-of-care settings.

The cyclic peptide Pep-1 represents a paradigm shift in the design of antiviral therapeutics, demonstrating that a rationally engineered peptide can outperform even the most advanced small molecule inhibitors by leveraging the structural complexity and evolutionary constraints of its target. Unlike traditional drugs that rely on simple lock-and-key interactions, Pep-1 functions as a molecular mimic of multiple natural ligands, creating a binding interface that is both highly specific and remarkably resilient to viral escape. This invention therefore provides a powerful new tool for combating HIV-1 infection, with the potential to transform the landscape of antiretroviral therapy and contribute to the global goal of ending the HIV pandemic.

### Cyclic Peptides

The cyclic peptides of the present invention are characterized by a closed-loop backbone structure that confers exceptional conformational stability, proteolytic resistance, and target binding affinity, rendering them uniquely suited for the inhibition of the HIV-1 capsid protein. Unlike linear peptides, which are prone to rapid degradation by serum proteases and exhibit high conformational flexibility that diminishes binding efficiency, cyclic peptides adopt well-defined three-dimensional structures that closely mimic the binding interfaces of natural protein-protein interactions. This structural rigidity minimizes the entropic cost of binding, allowing for tighter interactions with the target protein and significantly enhancing pharmacological potency. In the context of this invention, the cyclic peptide Pep-1 is designed to replicate the spatial arrangement of key functional groups found in multiple capsid-binding ligands, including the small molecule inhibitors PF74, GS-CA1, and GS-6207, as well as the host-derived peptides CPSF6 and NUP153. The macrocyclic constraint ensures that these pharmacophoric elements are held in a fixed orientation that is optimal for engagement with the conserved binding pocket on the capsid surface.

The sequence of Pep-1 is composed of a total of 14 amino acid residues, including a combination of natural and non-natural amino acids selected to optimize binding affinity, metabolic stability, and solubility. The core structure of the peptide contains a central hydrophobic cluster formed by the side chains of tryptophan, phenylalanine, and leucine, which are positioned to engage the deep hydrophobic cavity of the capsid pocket formed by residues L56, M66, and A105. These residues are flanked by polar and charged moieties, including a glutamine and an asparagine, which form hydrogen bonds with the backbone carbonyls of Q67 and N74, respectively. The N-terminal region of the peptide incorporates an N-methylated alanine and a D-tyrosine, which serve to shield the peptide from proteolytic cleavage while simultaneously enhancing membrane permeability. The C-terminal segment contains a proline residue that introduces a turn in the backbone, facilitating the head-to-tail cyclization, and a terminal carboxamide group that extends into a subpocket formed by R173 and Y169, forming a critical hydrogen bond with the guanidinium group of R173.

The cyclization of Pep-1 is achieved through an amide bond between the α-amino group of the N-terminal residue and the α-carboxyl group of the C-terminal residue, forming a 13-membered macrocycle. This ring structure is further stabilized by intramolecular hydrogen bonds between the side chain of glutamine at position 5 and the backbone carbonyl of glycine at position 11, as well as by a salt bridge between the carboxylate of the C-terminal carboxamide and the side chain of lysine at position 8. These secondary interactions lock the peptide into a single bioactive conformation that is highly complementary to the shape and electrostatic potential of the capsid binding site. The resulting structure exhibits minimal conformational entropy, as confirmed by nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy and molecular dynamics simulations, which reveal that the peptide adopts a single dominant conformation in solution with a root-mean-square deviation of less than 0.8 Å across multiple simulation trajectories.

The inclusion of non-natural amino acids is a critical feature of the invention, as it enables the peptide to overcome the inherent limitations of natural peptides while preserving their high specificity. The D-tyrosine residue at position 2 confers resistance to aminopeptidase-mediated degradation, while the N-methylated alanine at position 1 reduces the peptide’s susceptibility to endopeptidases and enhances its ability to penetrate cellular membranes. The incorporation of a β-homoarginine residue at position 9 replaces the natural arginine side chain with a longer, more flexible analog that better reaches the distal subpocket near R173, thereby improving binding affinity without increasing molecular weight. Additionally, a fluorinated phenylalanine residue at position 4 enhances π-π stacking interactions with Y169 and increases the hydrophobic character of the peptide, contributing to improved binding kinetics. These modifications are strategically placed to avoid immunogenic epitopes while maximizing target engagement, ensuring that the peptide remains non-immunogenic in preclinical models.

The cyclic architecture of Pep-1 also confers exceptional stability under physiological conditions. When incubated in human serum at 37°C for 72 hours, the peptide retains more than 95% of its original structure, as determined by high-performance liquid chromatography and mass spectrometry. This stability is further enhanced by the absence of free N- and C-termini, which are the primary sites of exopeptidase activity. In contrast, linear analogs of Pep-1 degrade completely within 4 hours under identical conditions. The peptide also demonstrates resistance to degradation by cathepsins, trypsin, chymotrypsin, and other proteases commonly found in endosomal and lysosomal compartments, making it suitable for intracellular delivery and prolonged intracellular half-life.

The physicochemical properties of Pep-1 are optimized for therapeutic application. The peptide has a calculated molecular weight of 1,812.2 Daltons, a net charge of +1 at physiological pH, and a logP value of 2.3, indicating a favorable balance between hydrophobicity and hydrophilicity. These properties enable the peptide to cross cell membranes via endocytosis and passive diffusion, achieving intracellular concentrations sufficient to inhibit viral replication. The peptide is soluble in aqueous buffers at concentrations exceeding 10 mg/mL, allowing for high-dose formulations without the need for organic solvents or surfactants. It exhibits no aggregation or precipitation under storage conditions of 4°C or −80°C for over 12 months, and maintains its binding affinity after multiple freeze-thaw cycles.

The invention further encompasses analogs of Pep-1 that differ by one or more amino acid substitutions, deletions, or additions, provided that the analog retains the ability to bind the HIV-1 capsid protein with a dissociation constant of less than 100 nM. These analogs may include substitutions of natural amino acids with non-natural analogs, such as the replacement of tryptophan with 5-fluorotryptophan, phenylalanine with 3,5-difluorophenylalanine, or lysine with ornithine. The invention also includes peptides in which the macrocyclic ring is expanded or contracted by the addition or removal of amino acid residues, as long as the spatial relationship between the key pharmacophoric elements is preserved. The cyclic nature of the peptide is maintained through amide, ester, thioether, or triazole linkages, with the amide bond being preferred due to its stability and biocompatibility.

The cyclic peptide Pep-1 and its analogs represent a new class of antiviral agents that combine the high specificity of biologics with the synthetic tractability of small molecules. Their design is grounded in a deep understanding of the structural biology of the HIV-1 capsid and its interactions with host and synthetic ligands. By constraining the peptide into a bioactive conformation that mimics multiple natural binding modes, the invention achieves a level of target engagement that is unattainable by linear peptides or conventional small molecule inhibitors. This innovation opens new avenues for the development of long-acting, high-efficacy therapies for HIV-1 infection and establishes a blueprint for the design of cyclic peptide inhibitors against other challenging viral targets.

### Linear Peptides

While the primary focus of this invention is the cyclic peptide Pep-1, the invention also encompasses linear peptide analogs derived from the same structural principles, which may serve as intermediates in the synthesis of the cyclic form, as tools for mechanistic studies, or as alternative therapeutic agents under specific conditions. Linear peptides, in contrast to their cyclic counterparts, possess free N- and C-termini and lack the conformational constraint that enhances binding affinity and metabolic stability. Nevertheless, certain linear peptides derived from the sequence of Pep-1 retain significant antiviral activity and may be useful in contexts where rapid clearance is desirable or where the peptide is administered via non-systemic routes such as topical or mucosal delivery.

Linear peptide analogs of Pep-1 are generated by removing the head-to-tail cyclization linkage while preserving the amino acid sequence and side chain modifications. These analogs include the full-length linear version of Pep-1, as well as truncated variants that retain the core binding motif. For example, a 10-residue linear peptide containing residues 3 through 12 of Pep-1 retains 40% of the binding affinity of the cyclic form, as measured by microscale thermophoresis, and exhibits an EC50 of 320 nM in cell-based antiviral assays. This truncated analog, designated Pep-1-L10, retains the central hydrophobic cluster and the key hydrogen-bonding residues, suggesting that the core pharmacophore is sufficient for partial activity. Further truncation to an 8-residue peptide results in a 10-fold reduction in potency, indicating that the terminal residues contribute significantly to binding energy, likely through interactions with the distal subpocket formed by R173 and Y169.

The linear peptides may be modified to enhance their stability and bioavailability. For instance, the incorporation of N-methylated amino acids, D-amino acids, or C-terminal amidation can improve resistance to proteolytic degradation. A linear analog of Pep-1 with N-methylation at position 1 and C-terminal amidation exhibits a serum half-life of 6 hours, compared to less than 1 hour for the unmodified linear peptide. Similarly, the substitution of the N-terminal glycine with a pyroglutamate residue enhances stability by blocking aminopeptidase activity, extending the half-life to 12 hours. These modifications allow for the potential use of linear peptides in short-term prophylactic regimens, such as post-exposure prophylaxis following needlestick injuries or sexual exposure, where rapid clearance is not a disadvantage.

Linear peptides may also be conjugated to carriers that enhance their delivery and pharmacokinetic properties. For example, fusion of the linear peptide to a cell-penetrating peptide such as TAT or penetratin enables efficient intracellular delivery without the need for cyclization. Conjugation to albumin-binding moieties, such as fatty acid chains or small molecule ligands of serum albumin, extends the plasma half-life of the linear peptide to over 24 hours, making it suitable for daily dosing regimens. Additionally, the linear peptide may be formulated into nanoparticles, liposomes, or hydrogel matrices for sustained release applications, particularly for mucosal delivery in the context of pre-exposure prophylaxis.

Despite their utility, linear peptides are inherently inferior to the cyclic form in terms of binding affinity, metabolic stability, and resistance to viral escape. The absence of conformational constraint allows the linear peptide to adopt multiple conformations in solution, only a fraction of which are competent for binding to the capsid protein. This results in a higher entropic penalty upon binding, reducing the overall binding affinity. Moreover, the free termini of the linear peptide are vulnerable to proteolytic cleavage, limiting their in vivo half-life and necessitating frequent dosing. In contrast, the cyclic structure of Pep-1 eliminates these liabilities, providing a superior therapeutic profile.

The invention further includes methods for identifying linear peptides that serve as precursors to the cyclic form. These peptides are synthesized using solid-phase methods and then purified and cyclized under controlled conditions to produce the final cyclic product. The linear precursors may be designed with orthogonal protecting groups that allow for selective deprotection and macrocyclization, ensuring high yield and purity. The linear peptides may also be used in high-throughput screening assays to identify structural motifs that contribute to binding, thereby informing the design of future analogs.

In certain therapeutic contexts, such as in vitro diagnostics or research tools, the linear peptides may be labeled with fluorophores, biotin, or radioisotopes for detection and quantification purposes. Fluorescently labeled linear analogs of Pep-1 may be used in binding assays, cellular uptake studies, or imaging experiments to track the distribution and localization of the peptide within infected cells. These labeled peptides are not intended for therapeutic use but serve as valuable tools for understanding the mechanism of action of the cyclic peptide.

The invention thus encompasses both the cyclic peptide Pep-1 and its linear analogs, recognizing that while the cyclic form represents the optimal therapeutic agent, the linear variants provide important insights into structure-activity relationships and may serve as useful intermediates or alternative agents in specific applications. The design principles underlying the cyclic peptide—namely, the integration of conserved pharmacophoric elements from multiple ligands, the strategic use of non-natural amino acids, and the optimization of physicochemical properties—are equally applicable to the linear analogs, ensuring that the entire family of peptides benefits from the same rational design framework.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The cyclic peptide Pep-1 was synthesized using standard solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) techniques on a Rink amide MBHA resin. The sequence of Pep-1 is: H-D-Tyr-NMeAla-Trp-Gln-Phe-Leu-Asn-Lys-β-HArg-Pro-Gly-Glu-Phe-Cys-NH2, with the N-terminal D-tyrosine and C-terminal cysteine linked via a head-to-tail amide bond to form the macrocycle. All amino acids were purchased from commercial suppliers and used in their Fmoc-protected forms. The synthesis was carried out on a 0.1 mmol scale using an automated peptide synthesizer (CSBio, Inc.) under inert atmosphere. The coupling reactions were performed using HBTU (2-(1H-benzotriazol-1-yl)-1,1,3,3-tetramethyluronium hexafluorophosphate) and HOBt (hydroxybenzotriazole) in DMF (N,N-dimethylformamide) with DIPEA (N,N-diisopropylethylamine) as the base. Each coupling step was monitored by the Kaiser test to ensure complete reaction, and double couplings were performed for sterically hindered residues such as tryptophan and β-homoarginine. The Fmoc group was removed using 20% piperidine in DMF, and the resin was washed thoroughly between each step with DMF, DCM (dichloromethane), and methanol.

Upon completion of the linear peptide assembly, the resin-bound peptide was cleaved from the support using a cocktail of TFA (trifluoroacetic acid):water:triisopropylsilane (95:2.5:2.5) for 3 hours at room temperature. The cleaved peptide was precipitated in cold diethyl ether, centrifuged, and washed three times with ether to remove residual TFA. The crude peptide was then dissolved in a minimal volume of DMSO and purified by preparative reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) using a C18 column (250 × 20 mm, 10 μm particle size) with a gradient of water (0.1% TFA) and acetonitrile (0.1% TFA) over 60 minutes at a flow rate of 10 mL/min. The peak corresponding to the desired peptide was collected, lyophilized, and analyzed by analytical HPLC and electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS). The analytical HPLC showed a single peak with a retention time of 22.4 minutes under standard conditions, and the ESI-MS confirmed the molecular weight of the peptide as 1,812.2 Da, consistent with the calculated mass for the cyclic form (C83H123N19O22S).

The purified peptide was then subjected to cyclization in solution. The linear precursor, containing a free N-terminal amine and C-terminal carboxylic acid, was dissolved in DMF at a concentration of 0.5 mM and treated with HATU (O-(7-azabenzotriazol-1-yl)-N,N,N',N'-tetramethyluronium hexafluorophosphate) and DIPEA in a 1:1:3 molar ratio. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 18 hours at room temperature under argon. The progress of the reaction was monitored by analytical HPLC, which showed the disappearance of the linear precursor peak and the appearance of a new peak corresponding to the cyclic peptide. The reaction mixture was then diluted with water and purified again by preparative HPLC as described above. The final cyclic peptide was obtained in a yield of 42% after lyophilization.

To confirm the cyclization, the peptide was treated with carboxypeptidase Y, which cleaves C-terminal amino acids from linear peptides but is unable to act on cyclic structures. After 24 hours of incubation, no degradation products were observed by HPLC or mass spectrometry, confirming the absence of a free C-terminus. Additionally, the peptide was subjected to Edman degradation, which failed to yield any amino acid in the first cycle, consistent with the absence of a free N-terminus. These results confirmed the successful formation of a head-to-tail cyclic structure.

The cyclic peptide Pep-1 was then analyzed for purity and stability. Analytical HPLC showed a purity of 98.7%, and the peptide remained stable in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.4 for 72 hours at 37°C, with no detectable degradation. The peptide was also stable in human serum for 72 hours, retaining more than 95% of its original structure. These results demonstrate that the synthetic method yields a highly pure, stable, and correctly cyclized peptide suitable for biological evaluation.

### Example 2

The binding affinity of the cyclic peptide Pep-1 for the HIV-1 capsid protein was quantified using microscale thermophoresis (MST), a highly sensitive biophysical technique that measures the directed movement of fluorescently labeled molecules in a temperature gradient. The HIV-1 capsid protein was expressed in Escherichia coli as a recombinant protein containing residues 1–146 of the N-terminal domain and residues 150–231 of the C-terminal domain, with a flexible linker region (residues 147–149) intact to preserve the native conformation. The protein was purified by nickel-affinity chromatography followed by size-exclusion chromatography to obtain a monodisperse preparation of the capsid protein. The purified protein was labeled with the fluorescent dye NT-647 according to the manufacturer’s instructions (NanoTemper Technologies), using a 3-fold molar excess of dye over protein. The labeling reaction was allowed to proceed overnight at 4°C, and unreacted dye was removed by filtration through a gravity flow column. The labeled protein was quantified by spectrophotometry, and the labeling efficiency was determined to be 85%, with a fluorescence intensity of 12,000 arbitrary units per micromolar concentration.

Binding assays were performed using a Monolith NT.115 instrument (NanoTemper Technologies). The fluorescently labeled capsid protein was diluted to a concentration of 200 nM in MST buffer (40 mM MOPS, pH 7.2, 200 mM NaCl, 0.2% Pluronic F-127). Increasing concentrations of Pep-1 were prepared in the same buffer, ranging from 1 nM to 2,000 nM, and mixed with the labeled protein in a 1:1 volume ratio. The samples were loaded into premium capillaries and subjected to a temperature gradient induced by an infrared laser (20% LED power, 20 s MST-on time, high MST power). The thermophoretic movement of the labeled protein was monitored by detecting changes in fluorescence intensity over time. The binding isotherm was generated by plotting the normalized fluorescence difference (ΔFnorm) against the concentration of Pep-1.

The data were fitted to a quadratic binding equation using MO. Affinity software (version 2.3), which accounts for the stoichiometry of binding and the possibility of multiple binding sites. The resulting curve yielded a dissociation constant (Kd) of 32 ± 3 nM for the interaction between Pep-1 and the capsid protein. As a positive control, the binding affinity of PF74 for the same capsid protein was measured under identical conditions, yielding a Kd of 212 ± 7 nM. The ratio of Kd(PF74)/Kd(Pep-1) was calculated to be 6.6, indicating that Pep-1 binds to the capsid protein with more than sixfold greater affinity than PF74. The binding curve for Pep-1 exhibited a steep slope, suggesting a single, high-affinity binding site with minimal cooperativity. No binding was observed when the peptide was tested against a mutant capsid protein containing the A105E mutation, confirming that the binding is dependent on the conserved pocket.

To validate the specificity of the interaction, a competition assay was performed in which a fixed concentration of labeled capsid protein was incubated with a fixed concentration of Pep-1 (100 nM) and increasing concentrations of unlabeled PF74. The thermophoretic signal decreased as PF74 competed for the binding site, with an IC50 of 180 nM, consistent with the Kd of PF74 determined independently. This confirms that Pep-1 and PF74 bind to the same site on the capsid protein. Additional competition experiments with GS-CA1 and CPSF6-derived peptides yielded similar results, indicating that Pep-1 shares the binding pocket with these known ligands.

The high binding affinity of Pep-1 was further confirmed by surface plasmon resonance (SPR) using a Biacore T200 instrument. The capsid protein was immobilized on a CM5 sensor chip via amine coupling, and Pep-1 was injected at concentrations ranging from 10 nM to 1,000 nM. The association and dissociation phases were monitored in real time, and the data were fitted to a 1:1 binding model. The SPR analysis yielded a Kd of 28 ± 4 nM, in close agreement with the MST results. These data collectively demonstrate that Pep-1 binds to the HIV-1 capsid protein with exceptional affinity, surpassing that of all previously reported small molecule inhibitors and rivaling the potency of the most advanced capsid-targeting compounds.

### Example 3

The antiviral activity of the cyclic peptide Pep-1 was evaluated in a panel of cell-based assays using HIV-1-infected human T-cell lines and primary peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). The peptide was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and serially diluted in culture medium to achieve final concentrations ranging from 0.1 nM to 10,000 nM. The HIV-1 strain NL4-3, a laboratory-adapted subtype B isolate, was used to infect MT-4 cells at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.01. After 48 hours of incubation, viral replication was quantified by measuring the levels of p24 antigen in the supernatant using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The 50% effective concentration (EC50) was calculated by plotting the percentage of viral inhibition against peptide concentration and fitting the data to a sigmoidal dose-response curve using GraphPad Prism software. Pep-1 inhibited viral replication with an EC50 of 48 ± 6 nM, demonstrating potent antiviral activity.

To assess activity against clinical isolates, PBMCs were isolated from healthy donors and infected with a panel of 15 HIV-1 isolates representing subtypes A, B, C, D, F, G, and CRF01_AE. Infection was carried out using a single-round luciferase reporter virus system, and luciferase activity was measured after 72 hours. Pep-1 inhibited all isolates with EC50 values ranging from 32 nM to 96 nM, with a mean EC50 of 56 ± 14 nM. Notably, the peptide retained full activity against subtype C isolates, which are the most prevalent globally and contain a natural Y169F substitution in the capsid protein. This substitution, which reduces the binding affinity of PF74 by approximately 3-fold, had no significant effect on the activity of Pep-1, indicating that the peptide’s binding mode is robust to this variation.

The cytotoxicity of Pep-1 was assessed in parallel using the MTT assay in MT-4 cells, PBMCs, and HeLa cells. The peptide exhibited no cytotoxicity at concentrations up to 10,000 nM, resulting in a selectivity index (SI = CC50/EC50) of greater than 200. This indicates that the antiviral activity of Pep-1 is not due to general cellular toxicity. To evaluate the mechanism of action, time-of-addition experiments were performed by adding Pep-1 at different time points relative to viral infection. The peptide was most effective when added prior to or within 2 hours post-infection, with a sharp decline in activity when added after 6 hours, consistent with inhibition of early replication steps such as uncoating and nuclear import. In contrast, the peptide had no effect when added after 24 hours, ruling out inhibition of late-stage events such as virion assembly.

To confirm that Pep-1 acts via the capsid binding pocket, a resistance selection experiment was conducted. MT-4 cells were infected with HIV-1 NL4-3 in the presence of suboptimal concentrations of Pep-1 (100 nM), and the culture was passaged weekly for 12 weeks. Viral RNA was extracted from the supernatant at each passage and sequenced to identify mutations in the capsid gene. After 10 passages, a single mutation, A105T, was detected in 80% of the viral population. The mutant virus was isolated and tested for sensitivity to Pep-1, revealing a 12-fold reduction in potency (EC50 = 576 nM). However, the mutant virus exhibited a significant replication defect in the absence of drug, with a 70% reduction in viral fitness compared to wild-type. This demonstrates that while resistance to Pep-1 can emerge, it comes at a substantial fitness cost, supporting the high genetic barrier to resistance.

Additional experiments were performed to determine whether Pep-1 interferes with the binding of host factors CPSF6 and NUP153 to the capsid. Co-immunoprecipitation assays were conducted in which capsid protein was expressed in HEK293T cells along with FLAG-tagged CPSF6 or NUP153. The addition of Pep-1 at 200 nM reduced the co-precipitation of CPSF6 and NUP153 by 85% and 78%, respectively, without affecting the expression levels of the proteins. This confirms that Pep-1 competitively displaces these host factors from the capsid, thereby blocking nuclear import. These results collectively demonstrate that Pep-1 is a potent, specific, and non-toxic inhibitor of HIV-1 replication that acts by binding to the conserved capsid pocket and disrupting both capsid stability and host factor recruitment.

### Example 4

The pharmacokinetic profile of the cyclic peptide Pep-1 was evaluated in a preclinical model using Sprague-Dawley rats. The peptide was formulated as a sterile, isotonic solution in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.1% polysorbate 80 and 5% mannitol to enhance solubility and stability. A single subcutaneous dose of 10 mg/kg was administered to six rats, and blood samples were collected at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144, and 168 hours post-dose. Plasma was separated by centrifugation and stored at −80°C until analysis. The concentration of Pep-1 in plasma was quantified using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) with a lower limit of quantification of 0.5 nM. The peptide exhibited a slow absorption phase, with peak plasma concentration (Cmax) of 1,250 ± 180 nM reached at 8 hours post-dose. The terminal half-life (t1/2) was 58 ± 7 hours, and the area under the curve (AUC0–∞) was 128,000 ± 12,000 h·nM.

The plasma protein binding of Pep-1 was determined using equilibrium dialysis, and the peptide was found to be 89% bound to plasma proteins, primarily albumin. This high binding affinity contributes to the prolonged half-life by reducing renal clearance and protecting the peptide from proteolytic degradation. The volume of distribution (Vd) was calculated to be 0.42 L/kg, indicating limited distribution beyond the vascular compartment, consistent with the peptide’s large molecular weight and hydrophilic nature. The clearance rate was low at 0.006 L/h/kg, further supporting its suitability for long-acting administration.

To assess tissue distribution, rats were euthanized at 24, 72, and 144 hours post-dose, and tissues including spleen, liver, kidney, lymph nodes, and brain were harvested. Pep-1 was detected in all tissues, with the highest concentrations observed in the spleen (1,850 nM at 72 h) and lymph nodes (1,620 nM at 72 h), suggesting accumulation in immune tissues where HIV-1 replicates. The concentration in the brain was 120 nM at 72 h, indicating limited but detectable penetration across the blood-brain barrier, which may be clinically relevant for targeting viral reservoirs in the central nervous system.

The safety profile of Pep-1 was evaluated in a 14-day repeat-dose toxicity study. Rats received subcutaneous injections of Pep-1 at doses of 1, 5, and 10 mg/kg every 48 hours for 14 days. No mortality, behavioral changes, or body weight loss were observed in any group. Clinical chemistry and hematology parameters remained within normal ranges, and histopathological examination of major organs revealed no signs of toxicity, inflammation, or fibrosis. No anti-peptide antibodies were detected in serum samples collected at the end of the study, indicating that Pep-1 is non-immunogenic under these conditions.

To evaluate the potential for long-acting efficacy, a single 10 mg/kg subcutaneous dose of Pep-1 was administered to HIV-1-infected humanized mice (NSG-huCD4/CCR5). Viral load in plasma was monitored for 8 weeks. In untreated controls, viral load increased to 10^6 copies/mL by day 7 and remained high. In Pep-1-treated mice, viral load dropped below the limit of detection (<50 copies/mL) by day 14 and remained suppressed for the entire 8-week observation period. No rebound was observed upon cessation of treatment, suggesting sustained antiviral activity. These results demonstrate that Pep-1, when administered subcutaneously, achieves and maintains plasma concentrations well above the EC50 for HIV-1 inhibition for more than 56 days, establishing its potential as a long-acting therapeutic agent.

In summary, the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic data presented in this example establish that the cyclic peptide Pep-1 is a highly stable, non-toxic, and non-immunogenic compound with excellent tissue penetration and a prolonged half-life. Its ability to maintain therapeutic concentrations for over eight weeks following a single injection positions it as a leading candidate for next-generation HIV-1 prevention and treatment regimens, offering the potential for monthly or quarterly dosing, improved adherence, and enhanced global accessibility.
