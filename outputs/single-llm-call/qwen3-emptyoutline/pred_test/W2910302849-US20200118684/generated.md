# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a computer-implemented system and method for predicting post-transplant survival outcomes in organ recipients, with particular application to kidney transplantation, and more broadly to other solid organ transplants including liver, heart, and lung. The invention employs a dynamic, cohort-specific ensemble modeling framework that adaptively selects and applies distinct predictive algorithms to subpopulations of transplant candidates based on clinically relevant stratification criteria, most notably recipient age. The system integrates high-dimensional clinical, demographic, and donor-specific variables into a unified computational architecture that optimizes predictive accuracy through model heterogeneity across patient subgroups, thereby overcoming the limitations of uniform, population-averaged models currently in clinical use. The invention is designed for integration into national organ allocation systems, hospital transplant evaluation platforms, and real-time donor-recipient matching algorithms, enabling more precise prioritization of candidates on waiting lists and improved allocation efficiency. The method leverages machine learning techniques including conditional inference-based random survival forests and regularized Cox proportional hazards modeling, calibrated through rigorous cross-validation and performance metrics such as Harrell’s concordance index and integrated Brier score, to deliver statistically superior survival predictions that are robust to missing data, heterogeneous covariate effects, and non-linear interactions among predictors. The invention is not limited to kidney transplantation but is structurally generalizable to any clinical domain where survival prediction from heterogeneous patient populations is required, including oncology, cardiology, and critical care, where stratified modeling can enhance prognostic precision and resource allocation.

## BACKGROUND

The clinical management of organ transplantation, particularly kidney transplantation, has long been constrained by the inadequacy of existing predictive models to accurately estimate post-transplant survival probabilities across diverse patient populations. For decades, the standard approach to predicting transplant outcomes has relied on parametric survival models, most notably the Cox proportional hazards model, which assumes a constant hazard ratio across all patients and linear relationships between covariates and survival time. While mathematically elegant and interpretable, such models fail to capture the complex, non-linear, and interaction-driven nature of biological systems that govern transplant success. The introduction of the Estimated Post Transplant Survival (EPTS) score in 2013 by the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network marked a significant advancement in standardizing kidney allocation, but its foundation remains rooted in a simplified Cox model using only four recipient variables—age, dialysis duration, prior transplant history, and diabetes status—while explicitly excluding donor characteristics and many clinically significant recipient factors such as comorbidities, socioeconomic indicators, and immunological risk profiles. As a result, the EPTS model exhibits a concordance index of approximately 0.69, meaning it correctly ranks only slightly more than two-thirds of randomly selected patient pairs in terms of their relative post-transplant survival likelihood, leaving substantial room for improvement in allocation accuracy.

Further complicating the issue is the heterogeneity of the transplant population. Patients under the age of 50 exhibit markedly different survival trajectories, risk factor dependencies, and response patterns to donor organ quality compared to those over 50. For instance, younger recipients are more likely to benefit from high-quality donor organs, are more resilient to surgical stress, and have longer expected lifespans that amplify the impact of early graft failure. In contrast, older recipients are more susceptible to non-graft-related mortality due to cardiovascular disease, infection, and frailty, rendering donor quality less predictive of overall survival. Yet, existing models treat all recipients as a single homogeneous cohort, forcing a single set of coefficients and variable weights to apply universally. This one-size-fits-all paradigm leads to systematic misclassification, particularly among elderly patients for whom donor variables are disproportionately influential and among younger patients for whom non-traditional risk factors such as geographic region, socioeconomic status, and prior hospitalization patterns play a greater role than previously acknowledged. The consequence is a suboptimal allocation system that may assign high-quality organs to recipients with lower expected survival while overlooking candidates who, despite advanced age or comorbidities, have a high probability of long-term survival if matched with an appropriately compatible donor.

Moreover, the clinical data used to train and validate these models are inherently noisy, incomplete, and high-dimensional, with hundreds of potential variables collected across decades of transplant records. Missing data are pervasive, particularly for variables related to pre-transplant hospitalizations, laboratory values, and psychosocial assessments, which are often inconsistently recorded across transplant centers. Traditional approaches to handling missing data, such as listwise deletion or mean imputation, introduce bias and reduce statistical power, while more sophisticated methods like multiple imputation are computationally intensive and often incompatible with the rigid structure of legacy allocation algorithms. Furthermore, many variables exhibit non-linear relationships with survival, such as the U-shaped risk curve associated with recipient body mass index or the threshold effects of dialysis duration beyond which survival declines precipitously. These phenomena are poorly captured by linear models and require flexible, non-parametric techniques capable of adaptive partitioning and interaction detection.

Recent attempts to improve upon the EPTS model have included the Life Years from Transplant (LYFT) score and flexible parametric models incorporating splines and time-varying effects, but these innovations remain constrained by their reliance on a single modeling framework applied uniformly across the population. Even advanced machine learning approaches, such as standard random survival forests or gradient boosting machines, have been applied to transplant data without stratification, resulting in marginal improvements at best. These models, while capable of capturing non-linearities and interactions, suffer from overfitting in subpopulations where data are sparse and from a failure to recognize that the most predictive variables differ fundamentally between age groups. For example, donor-related variables such as kidney donor risk index (KDRI) or cold ischemia time are highly predictive in younger recipients but contribute little to survival estimation in older recipients, where competing causes of mortality dominate. Conversely, variables such as recipient education level or distance to transplant center, which may reflect access to care and adherence to immunosuppressive regimens, are more predictive in younger cohorts but irrelevant in older ones. The failure to account for these cohort-specific variable importances results in a model that is neither optimized for any subgroup nor robust across the entire population.

Additionally, the clinical infrastructure supporting organ allocation lacks the computational flexibility to incorporate complex, data-driven models into real-time decision-making pipelines. Current systems are built on legacy databases and rule-based algorithms that cannot handle the computational demands of ensemble modeling, variable selection, or dynamic model switching. There exists, therefore, a critical gap between the availability of rich, longitudinal transplant data and the inability of existing systems to extract and deploy the full predictive potential of that data. The present invention addresses this gap by introducing a novel, scalable, and clinically deployable architecture that dynamically partitions the recipient population into homogeneous subcohorts, selects the optimal predictive algorithm for each cohort based on empirical performance, and integrates donor and recipient variables in a unified framework that adapts to the unique risk profile of each candidate. The system is designed to operate within existing clinical workflows, requiring only standard electronic health record inputs and producing a continuous survival probability estimate that can be directly integrated into waiting list ranking algorithms. Unlike prior models, it does not require manual feature engineering, assumes no parametric form for survival distributions, and automatically adjusts to changes in data quality, variable availability, and population demographics over time. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift from static, population-averaged prediction to dynamic, individualized, and context-sensitive survival modeling, with the potential to significantly improve transplant outcomes, reduce organ waste, and enhance equity in organ allocation.

## SUMMARY

The present invention is a computer-implemented system and method for predicting post-transplant survival in organ recipients by employing a stratified ensemble modeling approach that dynamically selects and applies distinct predictive algorithms to clinically homogeneous subpopulations defined by key demographic and clinical variables, most notably recipient age. The invention operates by first partitioning a cohort of transplant candidates into two or more subgroups based on a predetermined stratification criterion, such as a threshold age of fifty years, which has been empirically determined to correspond to a significant divergence in survival patterns, risk factor importance, and model performance. For each subgroup, the system performs an independent variable selection process using a combination of permutation importance derived from random survival forests and L1-regularized Cox regression to identify the most predictive variables specific to that subgroup. The system then selects the optimal predictive model for each subgroup by comparing the performance of multiple candidate algorithms—including conditional inference-based random survival forests, Cox proportional hazards models, and other non-parametric survival learners—using cross-validated metrics such as Harrell’s concordance index and integrated Brier score. In the preferred embodiment, a random survival forest with conditional inference trees is selected for younger recipients, while a regularized Cox proportional hazards model is selected for older recipients, due to superior performance in each respective subgroup. The selected models are trained independently on their respective subgroups using high-dimensional clinical data encompassing recipient demographics, medical history, dialysis characteristics, immunological markers, donor attributes, and procedural variables, with missing data handled through a combination of imputation and exclusion strategies that preserve statistical integrity without introducing bias. The system outputs a continuous, time-dependent probability of survival for each candidate, calibrated to a specified time horizon such as five years post-transplant, and is capable of generating rankings of candidates on a waiting list based on predicted survival likelihood. The invention further includes a validation framework that tests model performance across multiple resampled datasets, evaluates robustness to missing data, and compares predictive accuracy against existing clinical models such as the EPTS score. The system is designed for integration into national organ allocation networks, hospital transplant evaluation platforms, and real-time donor-recipient matching engines, enabling dynamic, data-driven prioritization of candidates that maximizes the utility of available organs and improves overall transplant outcomes. The invention is not limited to kidney transplantation and may be extended to other solid organ transplants by redefining the stratification criteria and adapting the variable set to the organ-specific risk profile, thereby providing a generalizable framework for precision survival prediction in transplantation medicine.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive, computer-implemented system and method for predicting post-transplant survival outcomes in organ recipients through a novel, stratified ensemble modeling architecture that fundamentally departs from the conventional paradigm of uniform, population-wide survival prediction. The system is designed to operate on large-scale, multi-center, longitudinal clinical datasets containing hundreds of variables related to recipient and donor characteristics, medical history, procedural details, and post-operative follow-up. The core innovation lies in the recognition that transplant survival is not governed by a single, universal set of risk factors and relationships, but rather by distinct, cohort-specific patterns that vary systematically with key demographic and clinical parameters. By partitioning the recipient population into homogeneous subgroups and applying tailored predictive models to each, the invention achieves a level of predictive accuracy that is unattainable by any single-model approach, regardless of its complexity or sophistication. The system is implemented as a software module that may be integrated into existing organ allocation databases, electronic health record systems, or real-time matching platforms, and is capable of processing new candidate entries in real time to generate individualized survival probability estimates that inform clinical decision-making and organ allocation prioritization.

The process begins with the ingestion of a comprehensive dataset comprising records of transplant recipients who have undergone solid organ transplantation, with the preferred embodiment focused on kidney transplantation. The dataset includes both living and deceased donor transplants, pediatric and adult recipients, and contains censored observations where survival duration is unknown beyond the last follow-up date. The data encompasses a wide array of variables, including recipient age, sex, race, body mass index, blood type, diabetes status, history of prior transplants, duration of dialysis, cause of end-stage renal disease, immunological sensitization levels, pre-transplant hospitalizations, socioeconomic indicators such as zip code and insurance type, donor age, donor sex, donor race, donor body mass index, donor history of hypertension or diabetes, kidney donor risk index, cold ischemia time, human leukocyte antigen mismatch levels, transplant center location, and numerous laboratory values collected prior to and after transplantation. The initial dataset may contain over four hundred potential variables, many of which are subject to missing data due to inconsistent recording practices across transplant centers. The first step in the system’s operation is data preprocessing, which involves the systematic cleaning and structuring of the input data to ensure compatibility with the modeling pipeline. Variables that are present in fewer than ninety-five percent of the observations are removed unless they have been previously established in the literature as clinically critical, in which case they are retained if present in at least eighty percent of observations. Variables that are recorded twice in duplicate form or that are known only after the transplant procedure—such as early graft function or acute rejection episodes—are excluded to prevent data leakage and ensure that the model relies solely on pre-transplant information, which is necessary for its use in pre-transplant candidate ranking. The resulting dataset, after this filtering process, contains seventy-three variables that are deemed clinically relevant and sufficiently complete for modeling purposes.

A critical component of the preprocessing phase is the handling of missing data. The system employs a dual strategy to address missing values: for categorical variables, missing entries are labeled as “unknown” and retained in the dataset, allowing the model to learn the survival implications of missingness itself, which may reflect systemic disparities in care access or documentation. For continuous, non-categorical variables, observations with missing values are excluded from the training and validation sets, a method that, while reducing sample size, preserves the integrity of the data distribution and avoids the introduction of bias that can occur with imputation methods such as mean substitution or regression imputation. This exclusion strategy is validated through cross-validation using both the exclusion-only approach and predictive mean matching imputation, ensuring that the final model’s performance is robust to different data handling protocols. The system also includes a mechanism for grouping high-cardinality categorical variables to reduce model complexity and prevent overfitting. For example, the variable representing kidney diagnosis, which originally contained seventy-five distinct categories, is systematically aggregated into eight clinically coherent groups based on their similar effects on the hazard function, as determined through a statistical clustering procedure that controls for confounding variables such as age, sex, and comorbidities. This grouping is performed using a hierarchical clustering algorithm that minimizes within-group heterogeneity while maximizing between-group separation in terms of survival outcomes, ensuring that the resulting categories are both statistically meaningful and clinically interpretable.

Following data preprocessing, the system performs cohort stratification to divide the recipient population into subgroups that exhibit distinct survival patterns and variable importance profiles. The stratification criterion is determined through an empirical analysis of one hundred survival decision trees, each constructed using recipient age as the sole splitting variable and optimized for maximum separation of survival curves according to the methodology of Strobl et al. The average split point across these trees is calculated to be forty-eight point seven years, which is rounded to fifty years for clinical practicality and interpretability. This threshold is chosen not arbitrarily, but based on the observed divergence in survival trajectories: recipients aged fifty and younger exhibit an average five-year survival probability of ninety-three percent, whereas those aged fifty-one and older exhibit an average five-year survival probability of eighty percent, indicating a clinically significant difference in baseline risk. The stratification into two cohorts—cohort one for recipients aged fifty and younger, and cohort two for recipients aged fifty-one and older—enables the system to apply different modeling strategies to each group, recognizing that the determinants of survival differ fundamentally between these populations. For instance, in the younger cohort, donor-related variables such as kidney donor risk index, cold ischemia time, and human leukocyte antigen mismatch are among the most predictive features, reflecting the importance of graft quality and compatibility in long-term survival. In contrast, in the older cohort, recipient-specific variables such as cardiovascular history, diabetes complications, and frailty indicators dominate the predictive landscape, while donor variables contribute minimally to survival estimation due to the overwhelming influence of non-graft-related mortality.

The next phase of the system involves variable selection within each cohort, a process that identifies the most informative predictors for survival prediction in each subgroup. This is accomplished through a two-step procedure that combines the strengths of non-parametric and parametric approaches. First, permutation importance is calculated for each variable using a random survival forest model trained on the respective cohort. In this method, the predictive accuracy of the model is measured before and after randomly shuffling the values of each variable; the magnitude of the resulting decrease in accuracy reflects the variable’s contribution to prediction. Variables with the highest permutation importance scores are retained for further analysis. Second, a Cox proportional hazards model with L1 regularization (Lasso) is fitted to each cohort, and the optimal penalty parameter is determined through ten-fold cross-validation using partial likelihood deviance as the objective function. The Lasso regularization promotes sparsity by driving the coefficients of less important variables to zero, thereby selecting a subset of variables that maximizes predictive performance while minimizing model complexity. The system identifies the optimal penalty value, denoted L0, which minimizes the partial likelihood deviance, and also identifies the largest penalty value, denoted Lσ, that yields a deviance within one standard deviation of the minimum. The Lσ penalty is selected for final variable selection because it produces a more parsimonious model with fewer variables, reducing the risk of overfitting and improving generalizability, while maintaining performance close to the theoretical optimum. The final set of variables for each cohort consists of those that are both among the top twenty variables by permutation importance and retain non-zero coefficients in the Lasso model. This dual criterion ensures that only variables that are both highly predictive and statistically stable are included in the final model. The resulting variable sets for the two cohorts are distinct: cohort one includes eighteen variables, including donor age, donor risk index, cold ischemia time, recipient body mass index, diabetes status, dialysis duration, and geographic region, while cohort two includes twelve variables, dominated by recipient comorbidities such as ischemic heart disease, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, and prior hospitalizations, with donor variables largely excluded due to their minimal predictive contribution.

With the variable sets defined for each cohort, the system proceeds to select the optimal predictive model for each subgroup by comparing the performance of multiple candidate algorithms using cross-validation. For cohort one, a random survival forest composed of conditional inference trees is trained with eight hundred trees and four variables randomly sampled at each split, as recommended by Strobl et al. A key innovation in this implementation is the modification of the tree-splitting criterion: rather than allowing all statistically significant splits to proceed, the system imposes a threshold on the splitting test statistic, requiring it to exceed 0.3 before a split is permitted. This constraint reduces tree depth, prevents overfitting to noise in the data, and results in shallower, more interpretable trees without compromising predictive accuracy, as confirmed by cross-validated Harrell’s concordance index. For cohort two, a Cox proportional hazards model with Lasso regularization is trained using the selected variables, and its performance is compared against the random survival forest. The Cox model demonstrates superior concordance index performance in this cohort (0.664 versus 0.655), likely due to the more linear and additive nature of the risk factors in older recipients, where the proportional hazards assumption holds more closely and the number of relevant variables is smaller. The system therefore selects the random survival forest for cohort one and the Cox model for cohort two, creating a hybrid ensemble that leverages the strengths of both non-parametric and parametric approaches in their respective domains.

The final predictive model is trained on the full dataset for each cohort using the selected algorithm and variable set. The training process is performed independently for each subgroup, ensuring that the models are not influenced by the characteristics of the other cohort. The system then evaluates the overall performance of the combined model using ten independent cross-validation samples, each consisting of eighty thousand training observations and twenty thousand out-of-sample validation observations. The primary performance metric is Harrell’s concordance index, which measures the proportion of all possible pairs of recipients for which the model correctly ranks the one with the longer predicted survival time. The secondary metric is the integrated Brier score, which quantifies the average squared difference between predicted survival probabilities and observed outcomes across the entire time horizon, providing a measure of calibration accuracy. The proposed model achieves a five-year concordance index of 0.724, significantly higher than the 0.690 reported for the EPTS model and 0.697 observed when the EPTS model is retrained on the same dataset. Even when donor variables are excluded from the proposed model—thereby enabling direct comparison with the EPTS model, which does not incorporate donor data—the concordance index remains at 0.717, still superior to the EPTS score. The integrated Brier score for the proposed model is 0.081, compared to 0.092 for the EPTS model, indicating superior calibration and predictive precision. These results are consistent across multiple resampling iterations and are robust to the use of predictive mean matching imputation for missing data, confirming that the model’s performance is not an artifact of the exclusion strategy.

The system is further validated through a series of sensitivity analyses. First, the model is tested on subsets of the data excluding pediatric recipients and living donor transplants, which are often treated separately in clinical guidelines. The performance remains stable, indicating that the model generalizes well across different transplant types. Second, the model is evaluated on its ability to predict survival at varying time intervals—six months, one year, three years, and five years—and demonstrates consistent superiority over the EPTS model at all time points, with the greatest advantage observed at the five-year horizon. Third, the model is stratified by recipient race, socioeconomic status, and transplant center volume, and no significant degradation in performance is observed, suggesting that the model is equitable and not biased by demographic or geographic factors. Fourth, the model is tested on a separate cohort of patients not included in the original dataset, and its predictive accuracy remains high, confirming external validity. The system also provides interpretability features, including variable importance rankings for each cohort, partial dependence plots that illustrate the non-linear relationship between key predictors and survival probability, and survival curves for prototypical patient profiles, allowing clinicians to understand how changes in specific variables—such as increasing donor age or extending dialysis duration—affect predicted outcomes.

The output of the system is a continuous probability estimate of five-year post-transplant survival for each candidate, computed in real time based on the input variables. This probability is used to rank candidates on the transplant waiting list, with higher probabilities corresponding to higher priority. The system can be configured to operate in two modes: a donor-inclusive mode, which incorporates donor characteristics and is used when a potential donor organ is available and a matching recipient must be selected, and a donor-excluded mode, which uses only recipient characteristics and is used for pre-emptive ranking of candidates on the waiting list before donor organs become available. In the donor-excluded mode, the system still achieves a concordance index of 0.717, demonstrating that it can provide accurate prioritization even in the absence of donor information, a critical capability for managing large waiting lists where donor availability is uncertain. The system is designed to be modular and scalable, capable of processing thousands of candidate profiles per hour on standard computing infrastructure, and can be updated periodically with new data to retrain the models and adapt to evolving population characteristics. The software architecture is built on open-source machine learning libraries, including the R packages “partykit” for conditional inference trees and “randomForestSRC” for random survival forests, with a Python-based interface for integration into clinical databases. The system includes automated quality control checks to detect data anomalies, missing variable patterns, and model drift over time, and provides alerts to system administrators when retraining is recommended.

The invention is not limited to kidney transplantation and may be extended to other solid organ transplants by modifying the stratification criterion and variable set. For liver transplantation, for example, stratification might be based on Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score thresholds or etiology of liver disease, while for heart transplantation, stratification could be based on functional status or the presence of pulmonary hypertension. The underlying architecture—partitioning, cohort-specific variable selection, algorithm selection, and ensemble prediction—remains unchanged, making the invention a generalizable framework for precision survival modeling in transplantation medicine. The system’s ability to adapt to different organ types, patient populations, and clinical contexts renders it a transformative tool for improving the efficiency, equity, and effectiveness of organ allocation worldwide. By replacing static, one-size-fits-all models with dynamic, context-sensitive ensembles, the invention enables a paradigm shift in transplant medicine, where each candidate is evaluated not according to a population average, but according to their unique biological and clinical profile, ensuring that the most suitable organs are matched to the recipients most likely to benefit from them. This not only improves individual patient outcomes but also maximizes the societal value of every donated organ, reducing waste, minimizing re-transplantation, and ultimately saving more lives.
