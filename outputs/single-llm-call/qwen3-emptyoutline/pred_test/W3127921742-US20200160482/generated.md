# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present disclosure relates generally to integrated imaging and vision systems, and more specifically to dynamic thermal management techniques for three-dimensional stacked sensor architectures that integrate image sensing, vision processing, and memory within a single package. The invention provides novel runtime mechanisms that orchestrate temperature regulation in near-sensor processing systems by dynamically adjusting the operational mode of the vision processing unit in response to real-time imaging fidelity requirements and ambient environmental conditions. These mechanisms enable substantial reductions in system power consumption while preserving the quality of captured images necessary for accurate computer vision inference and human perception. The disclosed system is particularly applicable to wearable life-logging devices, automotive dashcams, augmented reality headsets, and other mobile vision systems where continuous object detection and occasional high-fidelity image capture must coexist under strict power and thermal constraints. By exploiting the rapid thermal transient response of the sensor die—specifically, the ability to reduce junction temperature by more than ten degrees Celsius within milliseconds upon cessation of near-sensor computational activity—the invention introduces a paradigm shift in how thermal management is conceived for imaging systems, moving beyond traditional processor-centric thermal design power limits to a fidelity-aware, application-driven thermal control framework. The invention is implemented as a runtime controller that operates independently of the host system on the near-sensor processor, enabling seamless integration into existing mobile and embedded vision platforms without requiring modifications to the underlying hardware architecture or sensor fabrication process.

## BACKGROUND

Modern imaging systems have evolved from passive capture devices into active computational platforms capable of real-time scene understanding through embedded neural networks. The proliferation of vision-enabled devices—from smartphones and surveillance cameras to autonomous vehicles and wearable personal recorders—has created an urgent demand for energy-efficient architectures that can sustain high-resolution, high-frame-rate visual processing without excessive power draw. Traditional vision systems rely on a distributed architecture in which the image sensor, typically fabricated on a separate silicon die, transmits raw pixel data over high-bandwidth serial interfaces such as CSI or MIPI to a remote system-on-chip (SoC) where computational tasks such as object detection, classification, and tracking are executed. This separation, while mechanically and thermally convenient, incurs prohibitive energy costs due to the repeated transfer of massive volumes of pixel data across off-chip interconnects. For instance, a 4K resolution sensor operating at 30 frames per second generates data rates exceeding two gigabits per second, and the associated energy expenditure for transmitting this data can exceed three nanojoules per pixel, dwarfing the energy required for sensing or even computation. The cumulative power consumption of such systems, often reaching several watts, renders them unsuitable for battery-powered applications requiring prolonged operation.

To address this inefficiency, recent advances in three-dimensional integrated circuit stacking have enabled the co-location of the image sensor, a dedicated vision processing unit, and on-chip memory within a single package. This near-sensor processing architecture dramatically reduces the volume of data that must exit the sensor die, replacing multi-megabyte frame transfers with only a few bytes of high-level features or inference results transmitted over low-power interfaces such as I²C. As a result, system-level power consumption can be reduced by more than fifty percent compared to conventional architectures. However, this architectural advantage comes with a critical and previously underappreciated drawback: the close physical proximity of the vision processing unit to the image sensor creates a thermal coupling that elevates the temperature of the sensor die during sustained computational activity. Unlike traditional processors, where thermal management is primarily concerned with preventing junction temperatures from exceeding a fixed thermal design power threshold to avoid reliability degradation, image sensors are uniquely sensitive to temperature fluctuations because they directly influence the quality of the captured visual data.

The fundamental physics of image sensor operation dictate that elevated temperatures increase the rate of dark current leakage in photodiodes and amplify the thermal noise generated by readout amplifiers and analog circuitry. This manifests as increased pixel variance, elevated baseline intensity, and visible graininess in captured images—degradations that cannot be fully corrected by digital post-processing without introducing artifacts such as blurring, color shifts, or unnatural texture suppression. These artifacts are particularly detrimental in applications requiring fiducial accuracy, such as forensic documentation, medical imaging, or automated insurance claims processing, where the integrity of the visual record is paramount. Moreover, in low-light conditions, where sensors are already operating at high analog gain and extended exposure times, even modest temperature increases can push noise levels beyond acceptable thresholds, rendering the captured data unusable for downstream vision algorithms. Conventional dynamic thermal management techniques, developed for general-purpose CPUs and GPUs, are ill-suited for this context. These techniques typically rely on coarse-grained throttling, clock gating, or workload migration based on aggregate power consumption or die-wide temperature thresholds, ignoring the fact that image fidelity is not a static requirement but a dynamic, application-specific, and context-dependent constraint. A system running continuous object detection may tolerate noisy, low-fidelity frames for hours, but must be capable of instantaneously producing a high-fidelity image the moment a significant event is detected—such as a pedestrian stepping into the road or a face entering the frame. The temporal mismatch between the millisecond-scale thermal response of the sensor die and the second-scale decision latency of traditional thermal controllers renders existing approaches ineffective for this class of applications.

Furthermore, prior attempts to mitigate thermal noise in stacked sensors have focused on static design-time solutions, such as partitioning neural network layers between the sensor and the host, or incorporating fixed thermal shielding and heat-spreading layers during fabrication. These methods, while beneficial, are inflexible and cannot adapt to changing lighting conditions, ambient temperatures, or application demands. They also fail to exploit the key insight that the sensor’s junction temperature does not decay gradually over seconds, as previously assumed, but instead exhibits a rapid, nearly exponential drop within tens of milliseconds when computational power is removed. This transient behavior, which has been empirically validated across multiple commercial sensor platforms, presents a unique opportunity: the ability to intentionally elevate sensor temperature during continuous vision processing to save energy, then trigger an immediate, controlled thermal reset to achieve high-fidelity capture when needed. No prior system has leveraged this phenomenon to create a feedback loop between imaging fidelity requirements and thermal state, nor has any prior work proposed a runtime controller capable of orchestrating this behavior in real time. The absence of such a mechanism represents a fundamental gap in the evolution of embedded vision systems, limiting their deployment in real-world, power-constrained, fidelity-sensitive environments. This disclosure bridges that gap by introducing a novel runtime architecture that dynamically modulates the operational mode of the near-sensor processing unit in response to both environmental context and application-driven fidelity requests, thereby achieving unprecedented levels of energy efficiency without compromising the integrity of critical image captures.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a runtime thermal management system for three-dimensional stacked image sensors that dynamically regulates sensor temperature to simultaneously optimize energy efficiency and image fidelity. The system comprises a novel runtime controller, termed Stagioni, which operates on the near-sensor processing unit and governs the transition between two distinct operational modes: near-sensor processing mode, in which the vision processing unit actively executes computational tasks such as object detection and classification, and capture mode, in which the vision processing unit is deactivated to allow the sensor die to cool and capture high-fidelity images. The controller employs two complementary temperature-aware scheduling policies—stop-capture-go and seasonal migration—to adaptively manage sensor temperature based on real-time environmental conditions, application-specific fidelity requirements, and dynamic power constraints. The stop-capture-go policy temporarily halts near-sensor processing to permit rapid thermal decay of the sensor junction, enabling on-demand high-fidelity image capture while minimizing system power through duty cycling. The seasonal migration policy, in contrast, shifts computational workload to a thermally isolated far-sensor processing unit located on the host system, allowing continuous vision processing while the sensor cools, thereby preserving application performance. Both policies are governed by situational thermal boundaries that are not fixed but are dynamically adjusted in response to ambient temperature and lighting conditions, ensuring that sensor temperature remains below thresholds necessary for acceptable image fidelity under varying operational contexts. The invention further incorporates a validated thermal model that accurately predicts the transient junction temperature response of the sensor die, enabling precise timing of mode transitions to achieve target temperature drops within milliseconds. By exploiting the rapid thermal decay characteristic of the sensor die—wherein a reduction in near-sensor power dissipation of one watt results in a temperature drop of approximately five and a half degrees Celsius within twenty milliseconds—the system achieves substantial reductions in average system power, ranging from twenty-two to fifty-three percent, depending on the power profile and fidelity demands of the application. Crucially, the invention does not require modifications to the underlying sensor hardware, fabrication process, or communication interfaces, making it fully compatible with existing commercial stacked sensor architectures. The runtime controller is implemented as a lightweight operating system service that resides on the near-sensor processor, enabling seamless integration into mobile and embedded vision platforms without introducing significant latency or computational overhead. The system’s ability to autonomously adapt thermal boundaries to ambient lighting and temperature conditions ensures robust performance across diverse real-world scenarios, from bright outdoor daylight to dimly lit indoor environments. By aligning thermal management with the intrinsic requirements of imaging fidelity rather than the arbitrary constraints of processor thermal design power, the invention enables a new class of energy-efficient, high-fidelity vision systems that can operate continuously in power-constrained environments while preserving the integrity of critical visual events.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention comprises a comprehensive runtime thermal management system for three-dimensional stacked image sensors that dynamically balances the competing demands of energy efficiency and image fidelity through intelligent, context-aware modulation of the sensor’s operational mode. At its core, the system is built upon the empirical observation that the junction temperature of a stacked image sensor, when subjected to near-sensor processing activity, rises predictably over time due to thermal coupling with the underlying vision processing unit, but decays rapidly—on the order of milliseconds—when that processing activity is suspended. This transient behavior, which contrasts sharply with the slower thermal time constants of traditional processors, creates a unique opportunity to decouple continuous vision processing from high-fidelity image capture. The invention leverages this insight by introducing a runtime controller, Stagioni, that operates independently on the near-sensor processor and governs transitions between two distinct operational states: near-sensor processing mode and capture mode. In near-sensor processing mode, the vision processing unit is fully active, executing vision algorithms such as object detection, scene classification, or motion estimation on low-fidelity frames, while the sensor operates at an elevated temperature that maximizes energy efficiency by minimizing off-chip data movement. In capture mode, the vision processing unit is deactivated, allowing the sensor die to cool rapidly through natural conduction and radiation, thereby reducing thermal noise and restoring image fidelity to levels suitable for high-quality image capture. The controller determines when to transition between these modes based on a combination of pre-defined fidelity thresholds, real-time environmental measurements, and application-initiated requests for high-fidelity capture. These transitions are not governed by fixed temperature limits but by dynamic thermal boundaries that adapt to ambient conditions such as lighting intensity and surrounding temperature, ensuring that the sensor operates at the minimal temperature necessary to meet the current fidelity requirement.

The system’s first operational policy, stop-capture-go, is designed for architectures where computational resources are constrained and duplication of the vision processing unit is not feasible. In this mode, the controller periodically suspends near-sensor processing by gating the clock or power to the vision processing unit and associated memory, thereby placing the sensor into capture mode. During this suspension, the sensor is allowed to cool, and any incoming frames are either discarded or buffered without processing. The duration of this suspension is carefully calibrated to achieve a sufficient temperature drop—typically sufficient to reduce the junction temperature by at least ten degrees Celsius—within a time window that does not exceed the latency constraints of the application. For example, in a life-logging application that requires high-fidelity capture upon detection of a person entering the frame, the controller may trigger a stop-capture-go event immediately after an object detection event is confirmed, entering capture mode for a duration of thirty-three milliseconds, which corresponds to the time required to capture a single frame. During this interval, the sensor temperature may drop from eighty-seven degrees Celsius to seventy-four degrees Celsius, a reduction sufficient to restore image quality to a signal-to-noise ratio of thirty-five decibels, which is considered excellent under low-light conditions. Upon completion of the capture, the controller resumes near-sensor processing, allowing the system to return to its energy-efficient state. The frequency and duration of these stop intervals are determined by the system’s duty cycle, which is optimized to minimize average power consumption while ensuring that the sensor does not exceed the situational temperature threshold required for acceptable fidelity. The duty cycle is calculated based on the power dissipation profile of the vision processing unit, the thermal resistance and capacitance characteristics of the sensor stack, and the desired fidelity level. For instance, in a system operating under bright daylight conditions with low noise sensitivity, the thermal boundary may be set at a higher temperature, allowing for a longer duty cycle and fewer interruptions, whereas in dim lighting, the boundary is lowered, resulting in more frequent but shorter stop intervals. This policy achieves the lowest system power among all evaluated approaches, as it eliminates the need for redundant computational hardware and relies solely on the existing near-sensor infrastructure. However, it incurs a performance cost in the form of dropped frames, which may be acceptable in applications where temporal continuity is less critical than energy efficiency, such as long-duration surveillance or personal memory logging.

The second operational policy, seasonal migration, is designed for systems that require continuous vision processing without interruption, such as real-time augmented reality tracking or autonomous navigation. In this mode, the system is equipped with a second vision processing unit located on the host system, thermally isolated from the sensor die by virtue of being mounted on a separate printed circuit board and connected via a standard camera interface. When the sensor temperature approaches an upper thermal boundary, the controller initiates a migration event, transferring the computational workload from the near-sensor unit to the far-sensor unit. This transition is executed with minimal latency through the use of pre-copy techniques, wherein the state of the neural network model, including weights, activation buffers, and intermediate feature maps, is preemptively synchronized to the target processor before the actual migration occurs. The migration itself requires only a brief handshake between the two processing units, resulting in a switching overhead of approximately one hundred microseconds, which is negligible compared to the typical frame capture time of thirty-three milliseconds. Once the migration is complete, the near-sensor unit is deactivated, allowing the sensor to cool, while the far-sensor unit continues processing the incoming video stream without interruption. When the sensor temperature falls below a lower thermal boundary, the controller initiates a reverse migration, returning the workload to the near-sensor unit to resume energy-efficient operation. The upper and lower thermal boundaries are dynamically adjusted based on ambient lighting and temperature conditions, ensuring that the system operates within the fidelity constraints of the current environment. For example, in a high-ambient-temperature scenario, the upper boundary is lowered to prevent the sensor from reaching a temperature where noise becomes visually apparent, while in a low-light environment, the boundary is raised to allow for longer periods of near-sensor processing before a migration is triggered. The result is a system that maintains continuous vision performance while achieving power savings comparable to stop-capture-go, but without the penalty of frame drops. The trade-off is the requirement for duplicated computational hardware, which increases system cost and area, but this overhead is justified in applications where performance continuity is non-negotiable.

The invention further incorporates a validated thermal model that accurately predicts the transient temperature response of the sensor die under varying power conditions. This model is based on a resistance-capacitance (RC) network representation of the stacked sensor package, where each layer—sensor, memory, and vision processing unit—is modeled as a thermal capacitance connected by thermal resistances corresponding to the conductive pathways between them. The model accounts for static power leakage, which increases exponentially with temperature, and dynamic power dissipation, which is proportional to the computational workload. The model parameters are derived from industry-standard fabrication guidelines, commercial sensor datasheets, and empirical measurements obtained from a modified OnSemi AR0330 image sensor instrumented with an on-chip thermal sensor. Validation against real-world measurements demonstrates that the model predicts steady-state temperatures with an error margin of less than one-tenth of a percent and accurately captures the millisecond-scale transient decay following power removal, with a predicted junction time constant of twenty milliseconds closely matching the observed thirty-millisecond decay in hardware. This high-fidelity model enables the runtime controller to precisely calculate the duration of stop intervals or migration triggers required to achieve a target temperature drop, eliminating the need for heuristic or trial-and-error tuning. For example, if the application requires a temperature drop of twelve degrees Celsius to achieve a signal-to-noise ratio of thirty-five decibels, and the model determines that each watt of power reduction yields a five-point-five degree Celsius drop, the controller can calculate that a power reduction of two and one-quarter watts is required. Given the known thermal time constant, the controller can then determine that a stop interval of approximately twenty milliseconds will achieve ninety-eight percent of the desired temperature drop, allowing for precise, deterministic control over image fidelity.

The system’s situational awareness is another critical innovation. Unlike traditional thermal management systems that operate under fixed constraints, Stagioni continuously monitors ambient lighting conditions and environmental temperature through integrated sensors and adjusts its thermal boundaries accordingly. In bright daylight conditions, where the sensor operates at low ISO and short exposure times, the noise sensitivity is low, and the upper thermal boundary may be set as high as eighty degrees Celsius, permitting extended near-sensor processing. In dimly lit environments, where the sensor must operate at high ISO and long exposure times, the same temperature may produce unacceptable noise levels, forcing the boundary to be lowered to sixty degrees Celsius. The controller adapts to these changes in real time, as demonstrated in experimental evaluations where the lighting conditions were varied randomly between outdoor, indoor, and low-light scenarios. The resulting temperature trace shows a smooth, continuous adjustment of the upper and lower boundaries in response to the changing environment, with the sensor temperature oscillating within the dynamically adjusted limits without overshoot or instability. This adaptability ensures that the system performs optimally across a wide range of real-world conditions without requiring manual calibration or user intervention.

The system’s implementation is designed for seamless integration into existing mobile and embedded platforms. The runtime controller is implemented as a lightweight operating system service that runs on the near-sensor processor, communicating with the host system only when necessary for migration events or high-fidelity capture requests. It does not require modifications to the sensor fabrication process, the camera interface, or the host operating system, making it compatible with commercial stacked sensors such as those used in recent smartphone and automotive vision systems. The controller interfaces with the application layer through a simple API that allows vision applications to request high-fidelity captures at any time, triggering an immediate transition to capture mode regardless of the current thermal state. This ensures that critical events, such as the detection of a face or a moving object, are always captured with maximum fidelity, even if the sensor is currently operating at a high temperature. The controller also incorporates a power-aware scheduling algorithm that minimizes the number of mode transitions by optimizing the duty cycle based on the trade-off between energy savings and switching overhead. Each transition between modes incurs a small but non-zero energy cost due to the activation and deactivation of power domains and the synchronization of state between processing units. The controller balances this cost against the energy saved by operating in the more efficient mode, ensuring that the net power consumption is minimized over time. In systems with minimal switching overhead, such as those employing pre-copy migration, higher duty cycles are favored, while in systems with higher overhead, the controller favors longer intervals between transitions to reduce the frequency of switching.

The invention has been evaluated using an FPGA-based emulation platform that replicates the energy, thermal, and noise characteristics of a commercial 3D stacked sensor architecture. The platform emulates an AR0330 image sensor, a 4 Gb LPDDR4 memory unit, and a Myriad2 vision co-processor, all connected to a host SoC with a duplicate vision processing unit via a 1 Gbps Ethernet link simulating a CSI interface. The evaluation was conducted across a range of ambient temperatures from twenty to forty degrees Celsius and three lighting conditions: outdoor daylight, indoor office lighting, and dimly lit office lighting. The system was tested with two vision workloads: image classification using MobileNet and object detection using a lightweight variant of YOLO. The results demonstrate that both stop-capture-go and seasonal migration reduce average system power by between twenty-two and fifty-three percent compared to the baseline of full far-sensor processing, with the magnitude of savings directly correlated to the power profile of the vision algorithm and the fidelity requirements of the application. Stop-capture-go achieved the lowest power consumption, as it eliminated the need for duplicated hardware, but incurred frame drops that reduced effective frame rate in high-duty-cycle scenarios. Seasonal migration preserved full frame rate but required additional computational resources. Both policies demonstrated robust adaptability to changing environmental conditions, with thermal boundaries adjusting automatically to maintain image fidelity across all test scenarios. The system’s ability to respond to on-demand capture requests within twenty milliseconds was validated across all conditions, with no measurable latency introduced by the controller’s decision-making process.

The implications of this invention extend far beyond the specific implementation described. By decoupling thermal management from processor-centric constraints and aligning it with the intrinsic requirements of imaging fidelity, the invention enables a new generation of vision systems that can operate continuously in power-constrained environments without sacrificing the quality of critical visual data. This capability unlocks applications that were previously infeasible, such as wearable life loggers that can record a person’s entire day with high-fidelity snapshots triggered by meaningful events, automotive dashcams that preserve high-quality evidence during collisions while maintaining low power consumption during routine driving, and augmented reality headsets that provide seamless tracking while capturing crisp images for social sharing. The invention also establishes a new paradigm for system design, where thermal behavior is not treated as a constraint to be tolerated but as a controllable variable to be exploited. Future systems may integrate multiple thermal zones, each governed by independent fidelity requirements, or may incorporate predictive models that anticipate future capture events based on contextual cues such as user gaze or motion patterns. The runtime controller described herein serves as the foundational element for this new class of imaging-aware systems, providing a scalable, adaptable, and energy-efficient mechanism for managing the delicate balance between computational performance and visual fidelity.
