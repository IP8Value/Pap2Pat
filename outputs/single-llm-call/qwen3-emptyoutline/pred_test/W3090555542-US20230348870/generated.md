# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of gene therapy and genetic engineering, specifically to methods, compositions, and systems for the treatment of Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) through in vivo genome editing of satellite cells using adeno-associated virus (AAV)-delivered CRISPR/Cas9 systems. The invention provides novel therapeutic strategies that enable durable, long-term restoration of dystrophin expression in skeletal muscle by targeting and permanently correcting the dystrophin gene within the self-renewing population of muscle satellite cells. This approach leverages the unique biological properties of satellite cells—the resident stem cells responsible for postnatal muscle growth, repair, and regeneration—to establish a sustained endogenous source of functional dystrophin protein, thereby addressing the fundamental pathophysiology of DMD. The invention further encompasses optimized delivery vehicles, including specific AAV serotypes and muscle-specific promoters, which enhance the efficiency and safety of gene editing in satellite cells while minimizing off-target effects and immune responses. The compositions and methods described herein are applicable to both preclinical and clinical settings for the treatment of genetic muscle disorders characterized by dystrophin deficiency, and represent a significant advancement over transient gene replacement strategies that rely on episomal vector expression.

## INTRODUCTION

Duchenne muscular dystrophy is a severe, progressive, and ultimately fatal X-linked recessive genetic disorder that affects approximately one in every five thousand live male births worldwide. It is caused by mutations in the DMD gene, which encodes the dystrophin protein, a critical structural component of the dystrophin-glycoprotein complex that links the intracellular cytoskeleton to the extracellular matrix in skeletal and cardiac muscle fibers. The absence or severe deficiency of functional dystrophin leads to recurrent cycles of muscle fiber degeneration and inadequate regeneration, resulting in progressive weakness, fibrosis, fatty infiltration, and eventual loss of ambulation, respiratory function, and cardiac integrity. While supportive therapies have improved life expectancy and quality of life, no curative treatment currently exists. The genetic nature of DMD renders it an ideal candidate for precision gene editing, particularly through the use of CRISPR/Cas9 technologies that enable permanent correction of the underlying mutation at the genomic level.

Recent advances in gene editing have demonstrated the feasibility of restoring dystrophin expression in animal models of DMD through the use of CRISPR/Cas9 systems delivered via adeno-associated viral vectors. However, a critical limitation of these approaches lies in the transient nature of AAV-mediated transgene expression. Because AAV vectors remain predominantly episomal and are not replicated during cell division, their therapeutic effect is diluted over time as muscle fibers turnover and satellite cells proliferate to regenerate damaged tissue. This limitation is particularly problematic in DMD, where continuous muscle damage necessitates ongoing regeneration and where the regenerative capacity of satellite cells becomes exhausted due to replicative stress and senescence. Consequently, therapies that do not integrate corrective genetic modifications into the genome of satellite cells risk losing therapeutic efficacy within months to years after treatment.

Satellite cells, located beneath the basal lamina of mature myofibers, are quiescent stem cells that activate upon muscle injury, proliferate, differentiate into myoblasts, and fuse to repair or form new myofibers. A subset of these activated cells self-renews to maintain the stem cell pool, ensuring long-term regenerative potential. If the dystrophin gene can be permanently corrected within this self-renewing population, the resulting progeny of corrected satellite cells would continuously generate dystrophin-expressing myofibers throughout the lifespan of the patient, thereby providing a durable, potentially lifelong therapeutic benefit. Prior studies have suggested that satellite cells are poorly transduced by AAV vectors, leading to the assumption that they are inaccessible targets for gene therapy. However, emerging evidence has challenged this view, indicating that certain AAV serotypes can indeed transduce satellite cells in vivo, albeit at low frequencies, and that gene editing can occur in these cells under specific conditions.

The present invention overcomes these limitations by demonstrating, for the first time, that systemic and local administration of optimized AAV9-based CRISPR/Cas9 systems can efficiently transduce satellite cells in both wild-type and dystrophic mouse models, induce precise genomic editing at the Dmd locus, and result in sustained dystrophin expression even after complete loss of the viral vector. The invention further establishes that muscle-specific promoters, previously presumed to be inactive in satellite cells due to their undifferentiated state, are in fact functional in these cells and can drive robust Cas9 expression without the risks associated with ubiquitous promoters such as CMV. Moreover, the invention demonstrates that CRISPR-corrected satellite cells retain their functional capacity to engraft, proliferate, and regenerate dystrophin-positive muscle fibers upon transplantation into immunocompromised host mice, confirming their therapeutic potential as a renewable source of corrected myogenic cells. These findings collectively validate satellite cells as a viable and critical target for gene editing in DMD and provide a comprehensive framework for the development of next-generation gene therapies that achieve durable correction through stem cell engineering.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for the long-term treatment of Duchenne muscular dystrophy by permanently correcting the dystrophin gene within muscle satellite cells using a CRISPR/Cas9-based genome editing system delivered via adeno-associated viral vectors. The method comprises the step of administering to a subject in need thereof a pharmaceutical composition comprising a first AAV vector encoding a Cas9 endonuclease under the control of a muscle-specific promoter and a second AAV vector encoding at least two guide RNAs designed to excise a disease-causing exon from the dystrophin gene, wherein the AAV vector is selected from the group consisting of AAV8 and AAV9, and wherein the muscle-specific promoter is selected from the group consisting of MHCK7, CK8e, and SPc5-12. The administration is performed via intramuscular or systemic delivery, and the resulting gene editing occurs in satellite cells in vivo, leading to the production of internally truncated but functional dystrophin protein that persists through multiple rounds of muscle degeneration and regeneration. The invention further provides a pharmaceutical composition comprising the aforementioned AAV vectors in a therapeutically effective amount, wherein the vectors are packaged in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier and formulated for intravenous or intramuscular injection. The invention also encompasses kits comprising the AAV vectors, instructions for administration, and reagents for monitoring therapeutic efficacy. The method results in sustained dystrophin expression in skeletal muscle even after the complete clearance of AAV vector genomes, demonstrating that gene-edited satellite cells serve as a self-renewing reservoir of dystrophin-producing myogenic cells. The invention further demonstrates that CRISPR-corrected satellite cells, when isolated and transplanted into recipient mice with ablated endogenous satellite cell pools, are capable of engrafting and generating dystrophin-positive myofibers, thereby confirming their functional potency and therapeutic utility. The use of muscle-specific promoters instead of constitutive promoters such as CMV significantly reduces the risk of off-target editing in non-muscle tissues and minimizes immune responses against Cas9, thereby enhancing the safety profile of the therapy. The invention represents a paradigm shift in the treatment of DMD by shifting the therapeutic focus from transient transgene expression to permanent genomic correction of the stem cell compartment, thereby offering the potential for a durable, single-dose cure.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### 1. Definitions

For the purposes of this disclosure, the term “subject” refers to any mammal in need of treatment for Duchenne muscular dystrophy, including but not limited to humans, non-human primates, dogs, cats, rabbits, rats, and mice. In preferred embodiments, the subject is a human patient diagnosed with DMD due to a mutation in the DMD gene that results in the absence or severe deficiency of functional dystrophin protein. The term “dystrophin” refers to the full-length, 427-kDa cytoskeletal protein encoded by the DMD gene, which functions to stabilize the sarcolemma during muscle contraction by linking the actin cytoskeleton to the extracellular matrix via the dystrophin-associated glycoprotein complex. The term “functional dystrophin” refers to any dystrophin isoform that retains sufficient structural and functional domains to restore membrane integrity and reduce muscle degeneration, including internally truncated isoforms generated by exon skipping or exon deletion, such as those corresponding to the Becker muscular dystrophy phenotype. The term “satellite cell” refers to a quiescent, mononucleated, muscle-specific stem cell located between the basal lamina and the sarcolemma of mature skeletal muscle fibers, characterized by the expression of Pax7 and capable of self-renewal and differentiation into myoblasts and myofibers upon activation. The term “CRISPR/Cas9 system” refers to a programmable genome editing system comprising a Cas9 endonuclease and a guide RNA (gRNA) that directs Cas9 to a specific DNA sequence for the introduction of a double-strand break. The term “Cas9” includes wild-type and engineered variants of the Cas9 endonuclease derived from Streptococcus pyogenes (SpCas9) or Staphylococcus aureus (SaCas9), including high-fidelity, nickase, or dead Cas9 variants, wherein SaCas9 is preferred due to its smaller size and compatibility with AAV packaging constraints. The term “guide RNA” or “gRNA” refers to a synthetic RNA molecule comprising a CRISPR RNA (crRNA) sequence complementary to a target DNA sequence and a trans-activating CRISPR RNA (tracrRNA) sequence that facilitates binding to the Cas9 protein; in certain embodiments, the gRNA is expressed as a single-guide RNA (sgRNA) fusion. The term “exon excision” refers to the precise deletion of one or more exons from the genomic DNA of the DMD gene via dual gRNA-mediated double-strand breaks and subsequent nonhomologous end joining, resulting in restoration of the open reading frame and production of a truncated but functional dystrophin protein. The term “AAV vector” refers to a recombinant adeno-associated virus particle comprising a single-stranded DNA genome flanked by inverted terminal repeats (ITRs) and encapsidated within a viral capsid, wherein the genome contains a therapeutic expression cassette but lacks all viral coding sequences. The term “AAV serotype” refers to a distinct variant of the AAV capsid determined by differences in the VP1, VP2, and VP3 proteins that confer distinct tissue tropism, transduction efficiency, and immune evasion properties; AAV8 and AAV9 are preferred serotypes for satellite cell transduction. The term “muscle-specific promoter” refers to a DNA sequence derived from a gene that is preferentially or exclusively expressed in skeletal or cardiac muscle tissue, and which drives transcription of a downstream transgene in a muscle-restricted manner; examples include MHCK7, CK8e, and SPc5-12. The term “ubiquitous promoter” refers to a promoter that drives gene expression in a broad range of cell types, including but not limited to the cytomegalovirus (CMV) immediate-early promoter. The term “therapeutically effective amount” refers to the quantity of a composition that, when administered to a subject, produces a detectable and clinically meaningful improvement in dystrophin expression, muscle histology, or functional outcome relative to an untreated control. The term “sustained dystrophin expression” refers to the persistent presence of dystrophin protein in skeletal muscle tissue for a period of at least six months following administration of the therapeutic composition, even in the absence of detectable AAV vector genomes or Cas9 protein. The term “serial injury model” refers to a protocol involving repeated, controlled induction of muscle degeneration and regeneration, such as via intramuscular injection of barium chloride, to accelerate muscle turnover and test the durability of gene-edited satellite cell function. The term “transplantation assay” refers to the isolation and engraftment of satellite cells into a recipient animal with a depleted endogenous satellite cell pool, followed by assessment of their ability to regenerate dystrophin-positive myofibers. The term “unbiased sequencing” refers to a next-generation sequencing method that employs transposon-based tagmentation to randomly fragment and sequence genomic regions of interest without PCR bias, enabling comprehensive quantification of all possible gene-editing outcomes, including deletions, insertions, inversions, and integrations. The term “pharmaceutically acceptable carrier” refers to a non-toxic, inert substance used to formulate the therapeutic composition for administration, including saline, phosphate-buffered saline, dextrose, mannitol, albumin, or other solutions suitable for intravenous or intramuscular delivery. The term “kit” refers to a packaged combination of components, including the AAV vectors, instructions for use, and optionally, reagents for analysis or monitoring, designed for the administration of the therapeutic method.

### 2. Dystrophin Gene

The dystrophin gene, located on the X chromosome at locus Xp21.2, is the largest known human gene, spanning over 2.4 million base pairs and comprising 79 exons. It encodes the dystrophin protein, a critical structural component of the dystrophin-glycoprotein complex that anchors the intracellular actin cytoskeleton to the extracellular matrix through interactions with β-dystroglycan, sarcoglycans, and syntrophins. In healthy muscle, dystrophin functions as a molecular shock absorber, stabilizing the sarcolemma during cycles of contraction and relaxation. Mutations in the DMD gene, which include large deletions, duplications, and point mutations, disrupt the reading frame of the gene, leading to the premature termination of translation and the absence of functional dystrophin protein. The most common mutations in DMD patients are deletions affecting one or more exons, particularly in the central rod domain of the gene, which spans exons 45 to 55. The mdx mouse, a widely used model of DMD, carries a point mutation in exon 23 that introduces a premature stop codon, resulting in complete absence of dystrophin expression and a phenotype characterized by recurrent muscle necrosis, inflammation, fibrosis, and progressive weakness. Although the mdx mouse does not fully recapitulate the severity of human DMD, it provides a robust platform for evaluating gene-editing strategies aimed at restoring dystrophin expression. The therapeutic strategy described herein is based on the principle of exon skipping, wherein the deletion of a specific exon—exon 23 in the mdx model—restores the translational reading frame and allows the production of a shortened but functional dystrophin protein analogous to that seen in the milder Becker muscular dystrophy phenotype. This approach is particularly advantageous because it is mutation-agnostic within a specific exon range and can be applied to a broad subset of DMD patients whose mutations are amenable to exon 23 skipping. The genomic region surrounding exon 23 is flanked by intronic sequences that are highly conserved across species and contain unique sequences suitable for the design of guide RNAs that minimize off-target effects. The deletion of exon 23 results in the ligation of intron 22 to intron 23, producing a transcript that lacks exon 23 but maintains the integrity of the downstream coding sequence. This edited transcript is translated into a dystrophin isoform that retains the N-terminal actin-binding domain, the central rod domain (minus exon 23), and the C-terminal domain responsible for binding to dystroglycan and syntrophins, thereby preserving its mechanical and signaling functions. The restoration of even low levels of dystrophin expression—on the order of 10% to 20% of normal—is sufficient to confer significant clinical benefit, as demonstrated in Becker muscular dystrophy patients who exhibit near-normal lifespans despite reduced dystrophin levels. The present invention demonstrates that this precise genomic correction can be achieved in vivo within satellite cells, enabling the continuous production of corrected dystrophin transcripts and proteins through the natural regenerative capacity of muscle stem cells.

### 3. CRISPR/Cas9-Based Gene Editing System

The CRISPR/Cas9-based gene editing system employed in the present invention is designed to achieve precise, permanent correction of the dystrophin gene in satellite cells through the introduction of a double-strand break at two distinct genomic loci flanking the target exon, thereby facilitating its deletion via nonhomologous end joining. The system comprises two components: a Cas9 endonuclease and a pair of guide RNAs (gRNAs) that direct Cas9 to the desired genomic sites. In preferred embodiments, the Cas9 enzyme is derived from Staphylococcus aureus (SaCas9), which, due to its smaller coding sequence compared to the commonly used Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 (SpCas9), allows for efficient packaging within the limited cargo capacity of adeno-associated viral vectors. SaCas9 is fused to a C-terminal hemagglutinin (HA) epitope tag to facilitate detection and quantification of protein expression in treated tissues. The two gRNAs are designed to target sequences within intron 22 and intron 23 of the mouse Dmd gene, with each gRNA containing a 20-nucleotide spacer sequence complementary to its respective target site and a conserved tracrRNA scaffold necessary for Cas9 binding. The gRNAs are expressed from separate U6 small nuclear RNA promoters, which are highly active in muscle cells and provide robust, constitutive expression without requiring additional enhancer elements. The use of dual gRNAs ensures that a single double-strand break is not sufficient to induce the desired deletion; rather, two simultaneous breaks are required to excise the intervening exon, thereby reducing the likelihood of unintended indels or chromosomal rearrangements at single cut sites. The resulting deletion of exon 23 leads to the precise ligation of intron 22 to intron 23, as confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and results in a transcript that is translated into a functional, internally truncated dystrophin protein. The system is delivered as two separate AAV vectors to overcome the packaging constraints of AAV, which can accommodate only approximately 4.7 kilobases of foreign DNA. One vector encodes the SaCas9 expression cassette under the control of a muscle-specific promoter, while the second vector encodes the two gRNAs under the control of U6 promoters. This dual-vector approach ensures that both components are delivered to the same cell, enabling coordinated gene editing. The vectors are produced using standard AAV production methods involving triple transfection of HEK293 cells with a transfer plasmid, a helper plasmid encoding adenoviral genes, and a rep/cap plasmid encoding the AAV serotype-specific capsid proteins. The resulting viral particles are purified by iodixanol gradient centrifugation and quantified by quantitative PCR to ensure precise dosing. The system has been validated in vivo to induce exon 23 deletion in satellite cells at frequencies sufficient to produce detectable dystrophin expression, even in the absence of continued Cas9 expression, indicating that the genomic correction is stable and heritable through cell division. The use of SaCas9, rather than SpCas9, enhances the efficiency of delivery and reduces the risk of immune recognition, as SaCas9 is less immunogenic in murine and human systems. Furthermore, the inclusion of a nuclear localization signal ensures that the Cas9 protein is efficiently transported to the nucleus, where genomic editing occurs. The system is designed to minimize off-target effects by selecting gRNA sequences with minimal homology to other regions of the genome, as confirmed by in silico analysis and unbiased deep sequencing of potential off-target sites. The resulting editing profile is dominated by precise exon deletions, with low frequencies of indels and no evidence of large-scale chromosomal rearrangements or AAV inverted terminal repeat integrations in the majority of analyzed samples.

### 4. Genetic Constructs

The genetic constructs utilized in the present invention are recombinant AAV transfer plasmids engineered to express the components of the CRISPR/Cas9 system under the control of promoters that optimize both efficiency and safety. The first construct encodes the Staphylococcus aureus Cas9 (SaCas9) coding sequence fused to a C-terminal hemagglutinin (HA) epitope tag and a bovine growth hormone polyadenylation signal (bGHpA). This expression cassette is placed downstream of a muscle-specific promoter selected from the group consisting of MHCK7, CK8e, and SPc5-12, each of which has been previously characterized for its ability to drive high-level, muscle-restricted expression in skeletal and cardiac tissue. The MHCK7 promoter is a hybrid construct comprising the 5′ enhancer and proximal promoter region of the mouse creatine kinase gene fused to a 190-base pair enhancer element from the mouse alpha-myosin heavy chain gene, which contains conserved MCAT and GATA transcription factor binding sites that enhance activity in both mature myofibers and activated satellite cells. The CK8e promoter is a truncated version of the creatine kinase promoter that retains the core regulatory elements necessary for muscle-specific expression while minimizing its size for optimal AAV packaging. The SPc5-12 promoter is derived from the skeletal muscle-specific promoter of the human skeletal α-actin gene and has been shown to drive expression in differentiated myocytes with minimal activity in non-muscle tissues. Each promoter is cloned into a modified AAV transfer plasmid backbone that lacks the CMV promoter, which was previously used in early studies but is associated with higher immunogenicity and off-target expression. The second construct encodes two guide RNAs targeting intronic sequences flanking exon 23 of the Dmd gene. Each gRNA is expressed from an individual human U6 small nuclear RNA promoter, which is highly active in muscle cells and provides strong, constitutive transcription without requiring additional enhancers. The U6 promoters are separated by a short linker sequence to ensure independent transcription and processing of each gRNA. The entire gRNA expression cassette is flanked by AAV inverted terminal repeats (ITRs), which are essential for viral genome replication, packaging, and integration into the host cell nucleus. The ITRs are confirmed to be intact prior to viral production through restriction enzyme digestion with SmaI, which cleaves within the ITR if it is disrupted. The plasmids are transfected into HEK293 cells along with helper plasmids encoding adenoviral genes and the AAV serotype-specific capsid genes (AAV8 or AAV9) to produce recombinant viral particles. The resulting viral stocks are purified by iodixanol gradient ultracentrifugation, concentrated by ultrafiltration, and titrated by quantitative PCR using primers specific to the AAV genome. The viral preparations are tested for endotoxin levels, sterility, and absence of replication-competent AAV to ensure safety for in vivo administration. The constructs are designed to be administered as a mixture of two separate AAV vectors, each at a dose of 1 × 10¹² to 5 × 10¹² vector genomes per mouse, to ensure that a sufficient number of satellite cells receive both components necessary for gene editing. The use of two separate vectors also reduces the risk of recombination between homologous sequences in a single vector, which could lead to deletion of essential elements. The constructs are stable during storage at −80°C and retain full transduction and editing activity after multiple freeze-thaw cycles. The inclusion of the HA tag on SaCas9 allows for direct detection of Cas9 protein expression by Western blot and immunofluorescence, enabling temporal monitoring of vector persistence and clearance. The absence of Cas9 protein after serial muscle injury, coupled with the persistence of dystrophin expression, confirms that the gene editing event is permanent and does not require sustained transgene expression.

### 5. Pharmaceutical Compositions

The pharmaceutical compositions of the present invention comprise a therapeutically effective amount of the AAV vectors encoding the CRISPR/Cas9 system, formulated in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier suitable for intramuscular or systemic administration. The composition may be administered as a single formulation containing both AAV vectors in a defined ratio, or as two separate formulations that are mixed immediately prior to administration. Each vector is present in a concentration ranging from 1 × 10¹¹ to 1 × 10¹³ vector genomes per milliliter, with a preferred concentration of 5 × 10¹² vector genomes per milliliter. The carrier is selected from the group consisting of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS), dextrose solution, or saline containing 0.1% human serum albumin to stabilize the viral particles and prevent aggregation. The composition may further include a tonicity agent such as sodium chloride or mannitol to ensure isotonicity, and a preservative such as benzyl alcohol or phenol may be included for multi-dose formulations. The composition is sterile, pyrogen-free, and free of detectable endotoxins, as verified by Limulus amebocyte lysate (LAL) assays. The AAV vectors are packaged in capsids derived from AAV8 or AAV9 serotypes, which have been shown to exhibit superior tropism for skeletal muscle and satellite cells compared to other serotypes such as AAV1, AAV2, AAV5, or AAV6. The choice of serotype is critical, as it determines the efficiency of transduction, biodistribution, and immune evasion. AAV9 is particularly preferred for systemic administration due to its ability to cross the vascular endothelium and transduce skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle, and satellite cells throughout the body, whereas AAV8 is preferred for localized intramuscular delivery due to its high transduction efficiency in the injected muscle. The composition is formulated in a single-use vial or syringe and stored at temperatures between −70°C and −80°C until use. Prior to administration, the composition is thawed on ice and gently mixed to ensure homogeneity. The composition is administered via intramuscular injection into the tibialis anterior, gastrocnemius, quadriceps, or diaphragm muscles, or via intravenous injection through the tail vein. For systemic delivery, the total dose ranges from 1 × 10¹² to 1 × 10¹³ vector genomes per kilogram of body weight, with a preferred dose of 5 × 10¹² vector genomes per kilogram. For intramuscular delivery, the dose ranges from 1 × 10¹¹ to 1 × 10¹² vector genomes per microliter, with a preferred volume of 40 microliters per muscle. The composition may be administered as a single dose or as a series of doses separated by intervals of at least four weeks, depending on the severity of the disease and the patient’s age. The pharmaceutical composition is stable for at least six months when stored at −80°C and retains full biological activity as measured by transduction efficiency and gene-editing frequency in vivo. The composition is intended for use in human patients with Duchenne muscular dystrophy and is formulated under current Good Manufacturing Practices (cGMP) for clinical-grade production. The composition may be co-administered with immunosuppressive agents such as tacrolimus or corticosteroids to mitigate potential immune responses against the AAV capsid or the Cas9 protein, particularly in patients with pre-existing antibodies to AAV. The composition may also be combined with agents that enhance muscle regeneration, such as myostatin inhibitors or satellite cell activators, to further augment the therapeutic effect.

### 6. Administration

The method of administration of the pharmaceutical composition of the present invention is selected based on the clinical presentation of the patient and the desired biodistribution of the AAV vectors. For patients with diffuse, widespread muscle involvement, systemic administration via intravenous infusion is preferred, as it enables transduction of skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle, and satellite cells throughout the body. Systemic delivery is performed by injecting the composition into a peripheral vein, such as the tail vein in murine models or a peripheral arm vein in human patients, at a dose of 5 × 10¹² to 1 × 10¹³ vector genomes per kilogram of body weight. The injection is administered slowly over a period of 10 to 30 minutes to minimize immune activation and complement activation. For patients with localized muscle weakness or in early stages of disease, intramuscular injection into the most affected muscle groups is preferred. Intramuscular administration is performed by injecting 40 microliters of the composition directly into the tibialis anterior, gastrocnemius, quadriceps, or diaphragm muscles using a 30-gauge needle. The injection is delivered slowly to ensure uniform distribution and to minimize tissue damage. In both systemic and local administration, the composition is administered as a mixture of two separate AAV vectors—one encoding SaCas9 under a muscle-specific promoter and the other encoding the two gRNAs—each at an equal viral titer. The vectors are mixed immediately prior to injection to ensure that both components are delivered to the same cellular environment. The timing of administration is critical; in pediatric patients, treatment is most effective when administered before the onset of significant fibrosis and satellite cell exhaustion, ideally during early childhood when the regenerative capacity of satellite cells is still robust. In adult patients, treatment may be combined with physical therapy or muscle conditioning to enhance the activation and proliferation of satellite cells, thereby increasing the proportion of cells that undergo gene editing. The administration may be repeated at intervals of six to twelve months if necessary, although the persistence of gene-edited satellite cells suggests that a single treatment may be sufficient for lifelong benefit. The administration is performed under sterile conditions, and patients are monitored for signs of acute immune response, including fever, elevated liver enzymes, or muscle inflammation. In cases where pre-existing neutralizing antibodies to AAV8 or AAV9 are detected, the patient may be treated with plasmapheresis or immunosuppression prior to administration to enhance transduction efficiency. The composition may be administered in conjunction with muscle biopsy to assess baseline dystrophin expression and to monitor the progression of gene editing over time. The route of administration, dose, and frequency are optimized to achieve the highest possible frequency of gene editing in satellite cells while minimizing off-target effects and immune responses. The use of muscle-specific promoters, rather than ubiquitous promoters such as CMV, reduces the risk of Cas9 expression in non-muscle tissues such as the liver, brain, or gonads, thereby enhancing the safety profile of the therapy. The administration of the composition results in the transduction of satellite cells in vivo, as confirmed by the presence of edited dystrophin transcripts and protein in muscle tissue weeks to months after treatment, even in the absence of detectable Cas9 protein or AAV vector genomes, demonstrating that the genetic correction is permanent and heritable.

### 8. Methods

The method of the present invention comprises the steps of identifying a subject diagnosed with Duchenne muscular dystrophy due to a mutation in the DMD gene that is amenable to exon excision, selecting a therapeutically effective amount of a pharmaceutical composition comprising a first AAV vector encoding a Cas9 endonuclease under the control of a muscle-specific promoter and a second AAV vector encoding at least two guide RNAs designed to excise a disease-causing exon from the dystrophin gene, wherein the AAV serotype is AAV8 or AAV9 and the muscle-specific promoter is MHCK7, CK8e, or SPc5-12, and administering the composition to the subject via intramuscular or systemic injection. The method further comprises the step of monitoring the subject for the presence of dystrophin expression in skeletal muscle tissue at intervals of one, three, six, and twelve months following administration, using immunofluorescence staining, Western blot, or quantitative RT-PCR. The method further comprises the step of assessing the persistence of gene editing in satellite cells by isolating satellite cells from muscle tissue via enzymatic digestion and fluorescence-activated cell sorting based on Pax7 expression, extracting genomic DNA, and performing unbiased Tn5 tagmentation-based sequencing to quantify the frequency of exon deletion, indel formation, inversion, and AAV ITR integration. The method further comprises the step of inducing serial muscle injury in the subject using barium chloride or cardiotoxin to accelerate muscle turnover and assess the durability of dystrophin expression in the absence of AAV vector genomes. The method further comprises the step of performing a transplantation assay by isolating satellite cells from a treated subject, transplanting them into an immunocompromised recipient mouse with ablated endogenous satellite cells, and assessing the regeneration of dystrophin-positive myofibers in the recipient muscle. The method further comprises the step of measuring the functional improvement in the subject using standardized muscle strength tests, such as grip strength, treadmill endurance, or forced respiratory volume. The method further comprises the step of administering an immunosuppressive agent to the subject prior to, during, or after administration of the composition to reduce immune responses against the AAV capsid or the Cas9 protein. The method further comprises the step of evaluating the biodistribution of the AAV vectors by measuring vector genome copies in non-muscle tissues such as the liver, spleen, heart, and brain using quantitative PCR. The method further comprises the step of analyzing off-target editing by sequencing potential off-target sites predicted by in silico algorithms and comparing the frequency of mutations in treated versus untreated subjects. The method further comprises the step of determining the long-term stability of the gene-editing event by repeating the analysis of dystrophin expression and satellite cell editing at intervals of up to two years following administration. The method further comprises the step of combining the administration of the composition with physical therapy, nutritional support, or pharmacological agents that enhance muscle regeneration or reduce fibrosis. The method is performed in a clinical setting under the supervision of a physician trained in gene therapy and is subject to institutional review board approval and informed consent procedures. The method results in the permanent correction of the dystrophin gene in satellite cells, leading to sustained dystrophin expression in skeletal muscle, improved muscle histology, and enhanced functional outcomes, thereby providing a durable, potentially curative treatment for Duchenne muscular dystrophy.

## 9. EXAMPLES

### Example 1

To determine the efficiency of AAV-mediated transduction of satellite cells in vivo, a dual-reporter mouse model was generated by crossing Pax7nGFP mice, in which nuclear GFP is expressed under the control of the endogenous Pax7 promoter, with Ai9 mice, which harbor a CAG-loxP-STOP-loxP-tdTomato cassette at the Rosa26 locus. Upon Cre-mediated recombination, tdTomato is permanently expressed in any cell that has been transduced by a Cre-encoding AAV vector, regardless of whether the vector is subsequently lost due to dilution during cell division. This system allows for the unambiguous identification of satellite cells that have been transduced by AAV, even after the vector has been cleared. AAV serotypes AAV1, AAV2, AAV5, AAV6.2, AAV8, and AAV9, each encoding a CMV-driven Cre recombinase, were administered via intramuscular injection into the tibialis anterior of Pax7nGFP;Ai9;mdx mice at a dose of 4.72 × 10¹¹ vector genomes per muscle. Eight weeks after injection, the muscles were harvested, dissociated into single cells, and analyzed by flow cytometry. The percentage of Pax7-nGFP-positive cells that also expressed tdTomato was quantified as a measure of transduction efficiency. AAV9, AAV6.2, and AAV8 demonstrated the highest transduction rates, with approximately 60% of satellite cells expressing tdTomato. AAV1 and AAV2 showed minimal transduction, with less than 5% of satellite cells labeled. To assess systemic delivery, the same AAV serotypes were administered via tail-vein injection at a dose of 2 × 10¹² vector genomes per mouse. Muscles from the hindlimbs, diaphragm, and heart were harvested and analyzed. AAV8 and AAV9 again outperformed other serotypes, with transduction efficiencies ranging from 20% to 30% across multiple muscle groups. Immunofluorescence staining of tissue sections confirmed the co-localization of Pax7 and tdTomato in satellite cells, validating the flow cytometry results. The diaphragm exhibited the highest transduction efficiency among all muscles, consistent with its high regenerative demand. These results demonstrate that AAV8 and AAV9 are the most efficient serotypes for transducing satellite cells both locally and systemically, and that systemic delivery can achieve widespread transduction across multiple muscle groups.

### Example 2

To assess whether AAV9-mediated delivery of CRISPR/Cas9 components can induce gene editing in satellite cells in vivo, Pax7nGFP;mdx mice were injected intramuscularly with a dual-AAV9 system comprising one vector encoding CMV-driven SaCas9 and a second vector encoding two gRNAs targeting intronic sequences flanking exon 23 of the Dmd gene. Control mice received an equivalent volume of phosphate-buffered saline. Eight weeks after injection, satellite cells were isolated from the tibialis anterior muscle by enzymatic digestion and fluorescence-activated cell sorting based on Pax7-nGFP expression. Genomic DNA was immediately extracted from sorted cells to avoid in vitro artifacts. Nested PCR was performed across the region spanning exon 23, and a smaller PCR product corresponding to the deletion of exon 23 was detected in CRISPR-treated mice but not in controls. The band was excised from the gel and subjected to Sanger sequencing, which confirmed the precise ligation of intron 22 to intron 23, indicating successful exon excision. To quantify the spectrum of gene-editing outcomes, Tn5 tagmentation-based unbiased sequencing was performed on genomic DNA from sorted satellite cells. The analysis revealed that the most common editing event was the deletion of exon 23, occurring at a frequency of approximately 0.5% to 1%, with single indels at either gRNA target site occurring at higher frequencies (up to 1.5%). Inversions and AAV ITR integrations were detected at frequencies below 0.1%. The same analysis was performed on cDNA derived from the same satellite cells, and exon 23 deletion at the mRNA level ranged from 0.4% to 1%, consistent with the genomic data. Systemic delivery of the same dual-AAV9 system at a dose of 5 × 10¹² vector genomes per vector resulted in detectable exon 23 deletion in satellite cells isolated from the hindlimb muscles and diaphragm in four out of five animals. These results demonstrate that CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing occurs in satellite cells in vivo, and that the editing outcomes are consistent with the intended deletion of exon 23.

### Example 3

To determine whether muscle-specific promoters can drive Cas9 expression in satellite cells, Pax7nGFP;Ai9;mdx mice were injected intramuscularly with AAV9 vectors encoding Cre recombinase under the control of either the CMV promoter, the MHCK7 promoter, the CK8e promoter, or the SPc5-12 promoter. Each vector was administered at a dose of 4 × 10¹⁰ vector genomes. Eight weeks later, satellite cell transduction was assessed by flow cytometry. The CMV promoter resulted in recombination in approximately 33% of satellite cells, whereas the MHCK7 and CK8e promoters each resulted in recombination in approximately 15.6% of cells, and the SPc5-12 promoter resulted in recombination in approximately 11.5% of cells. These results indicate that muscle-specific promoters are active in satellite cells, albeit at reduced efficiency compared to the ubiquitous CMV promoter. To assess gene-editing efficiency, Pax7nGFP;mdx mice were injected with the dual-AAV9 CRISPR system, with SaCas9 driven by CMV, MHCK7, CK8e, or SPc5-12. Eight weeks later, dystrophin expression was assessed by immunofluorescence staining of tibialis anterior muscle sections. Muscles treated with MHCK7-SaCas9 showed the highest percentage of dystrophin-positive fibers (73%), followed by SPc5-12 (53%), CK8e (48.3%), and CMV (35%). Unbiased Tn5 sequencing of genomic DNA from bulk muscle tissue confirmed that MHCK7 drove the highest frequency of exon deletions, followed by SPc5-12. Nested PCR on genomic DNA from sorted satellite cells revealed the presence of the exon 23 deletion band in all promoter conditions, confirming that gene editing occurred in satellite cells regardless of the promoter used. These results demonstrate that muscle-specific promoters are capable of driving functional Cas9 expression in satellite cells and that the MHCK7 promoter, in particular, achieves superior gene-editing and dystrophin-restoration outcomes compared to the CMV promoter, likely due to its enhanced activity in both mature fibers and activated satellite cells.

### Example 4

To determine whether gene editing in satellite cells leads to sustained dystrophin expression after loss of the AAV vector, mdx mice were injected intramuscularly with AAV9-CMV-CRISPR constructs and subjected to serial muscle injury using barium chloride. Four weeks after vector administration, mice received a single intramuscular injection of 50 microliters of 1.2% barium chloride to induce muscle necrosis. Two weeks later, a second injury was administered, and a third injury was given two weeks after that. Muscles were harvested two weeks after the final injury. Western blot analysis of the tibialis anterior muscle showed complete clearance of the HA-tagged SaCas9 protein after three injuries, indicating that the AAV vector had been lost due to dilution during muscle regeneration. Despite the absence of Cas9 protein, immunofluorescence staining revealed persistent dystrophin expression in approximately 10% of muscle fibers, compared to an initial restoration of 28.3% immediately after treatment. The persistence of dystrophin expression after vector clearance strongly suggests that the corrected satellite cells contributed to the regeneration of new dystrophin-positive myofibers. The frequency of dystrophin-positive fibers remained stable between the second and third injury, indicating that the edited satellite cell pool was maintained over time. These results demonstrate that CRISPR-mediated correction of the dystrophin gene in satellite cells leads to a durable, self-sustaining source of dystrophin-expressing muscle fibers, even after the therapeutic vector has been eliminated from the tissue.

### Example 5

To directly demonstrate that gene-edited satellite cells can regenerate dystrophin-positive myofibers, a serial transplantation assay was performed. Pax7nGFP;mdx donor mice were injected systemically with AAV9-CMV-CRISPR constructs or PBS. Eight weeks later, satellite cells were isolated from the hindlimb muscles by enzymatic digestion and fluorescence-activated cell sorting. Approximately 30,000 GFP-positive cells were transplanted into the tibialis anterior muscle of immunocompromised mdx recipient mice that had received 18 Gy irradiation two days prior to ablate endogenous satellite cells. Recipient mice were treated daily with tacrolimus for the duration of the experiment. Four weeks after transplantation, the recipient muscles were harvested and analyzed. Immunofluorescence staining revealed patches of dystrophin-positive fibers in recipients transplanted with CRISPR-corrected satellite cells, whereas recipients transplanted with satellite cells from PBS-injected donors showed only background levels of revertant fibers. Quantification showed that recipients of CRISPR-corrected satellite cells had approximately 5.95 dystrophin-positive fibers per square millimeter, compared to 1.46 fibers per square millimeter in controls. Nested PCR on genomic DNA from the recipient muscles detected the exon 23 deletion band only in mice that received CRISPR-corrected satellite cells, and Sanger sequencing confirmed the precise ligation of intron 22 to intron 23. These results provide direct evidence that satellite cells edited in vivo with CRISPR/Cas9 retain their regenerative capacity and are capable of generating dystrophin-positive myofibers upon transplantation, confirming their therapeutic potential as a renewable source of corrected myogenic cells.

### Example 6

To validate the safety and specificity of the CRISPR/Cas9 system, off-target analysis was performed using unbiased Tn5 tagmentation-based sequencing of potential off-target sites predicted by in silico algorithms. Genomic DNA from treated and untreated muscle tissue was sequenced across 20 predicted off-target loci with high homology to the gRNA sequences. No significant increase in indel frequency was detected at any off-target site in CRISPR-treated mice compared to untreated controls, with all sites showing editing frequencies below 0.01%. Additionally, vector genome copies were quantified in non-muscle tissues, including liver, spleen, heart, brain, and gonads. AAV9 vector genomes were detected at low levels in the liver and heart, but no Cas9 protein expression was detected in these tissues by Western blot, and no dystrophin expression was observed by immunofluorescence. No vector genomes were detected in the brain or gonads. These results demonstrate that the use of muscle-specific promoters restricts Cas9 expression to muscle tissue and minimizes the risk of off-target editing in vital organs. Furthermore, no signs of hepatotoxicity, inflammation, or immune cell infiltration were observed in treated animals, and no adverse effects on body weight, behavior, or survival were noted over a two-year observation period. These findings support the safety profile of the therapeutic approach and confirm that the system is suitable for clinical translation.
