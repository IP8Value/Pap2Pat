# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of pharmaceutical compositions and methods for the treatment of muscular dystrophy, particularly Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), through the pharmacological upregulation of utrophin expression. More specifically, the invention concerns the use of nabumetone or a pharmaceutically acceptable salt, ester, prodrug, or metabolite thereof as a therapeutic agent for increasing the expression of utrophin A in skeletal and cardiac muscle tissue, thereby compensating for the absence of dystrophin and ameliorating the pathological manifestations of DMD. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical formulations containing nabumetone, methods of administering such formulations to patients, and kits for the treatment of neuromuscular degenerative disorders characterized by dystrophin deficiency. The compositions and methods disclosed herein are particularly suited for long-term systemic administration in pediatric and adult populations affected by DMD, offering a non-genetic, small-molecule-based therapeutic strategy that circumvents the limitations associated with gene therapy, protein replacement, or mutation-specific exon-skipping approaches.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Duchenne muscular dystrophy is a severe, progressive, and ultimately fatal genetic disorder affecting approximately one in every 3,500 to 5,000 male births worldwide. It is caused by mutations in the dystrophin gene located on the X chromosome, resulting in the complete or near-complete absence of the dystrophin protein from the sarcolemma of skeletal and cardiac muscle fibers. Dystrophin serves as a critical structural link between the intracellular actin cytoskeleton and the extracellular matrix, forming the core of the dystrophin-associated protein complex (DAPC). Without dystrophin, the DAPC disassembles, leading to membrane instability, uncontrolled calcium influx, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and recurrent cycles of muscle fiber necrosis and attempted regeneration. Over time, regenerative capacity is exhausted, and muscle tissue is replaced by fibrotic and adipose tissue, resulting in progressive weakness, loss of ambulation, respiratory insufficiency, cardiomyopathy, and premature death, typically by the third decade of life.

Despite decades of intensive research and numerous clinical trials, no cure for DMD currently exists, and the standard of care remains largely supportive, consisting of corticosteroids to slow disease progression, physical therapy, respiratory support, and cardiac management. While emerging therapies such as antisense oligonucleotide-mediated exon skipping, stop codon readthrough agents, and viral vector-mediated microdystrophin delivery have shown promise in select patient subpopulations, they are limited by narrow applicability—often restricted to specific mutation types—and face significant challenges related to delivery efficiency, immunogenicity, durability of effect, and cost. Moreover, the large size of the dystrophin cDNA (approximately 14 kilobases) renders conventional viral vectors inadequate for full-length gene replacement, necessitating the use of truncated, partially functional variants whose therapeutic efficacy remains suboptimal.

In this context, alternative therapeutic strategies that bypass the need to replace dystrophin altogether have gained considerable attention. One such strategy involves the upregulation of utrophin, an autosomal paralog of dystrophin that shares approximately 74% amino acid sequence homology and possesses a nearly identical domain architecture. Utrophin is naturally expressed during fetal myogenesis and is capable of functionally substituting for dystrophin by binding to the same set of proteins within the DAPC, thereby restoring membrane stability and mechanotransduction. In adult skeletal muscle, utrophin expression is restricted to the neuromuscular and myotendinous junctions, but its ectopic overexpression throughout the muscle fiber—achieved via transgenic overexpression or viral delivery in murine models of DMD—has been shown to fully rescue the dystrophic phenotype, including improvements in muscle strength, reduction in fibrosis, normalization of serum creatine kinase levels, and extended lifespan. Importantly, because utrophin is expressed in fetal muscle and is constitutively present in multiple non-muscle tissues—including the liver, kidney, and lung—in both healthy individuals and DMD patients, its therapeutic upregulation is not expected to trigger immune recognition as a foreign antigen, thereby circumventing a major safety concern inherent in dystrophin replacement therapies.

Numerous preclinical approaches have been explored to enhance utrophin expression, including the administration of heregulin, L-arginine, and histone deacetylase inhibitors such as trichostatin A, as well as the delivery of engineered transcription factors targeting the utrophin A promoter. While these strategies have demonstrated efficacy in animal models, their translation to the clinic has been hindered by practical limitations: heregulin and other protein-based agents suffer from poor bioavailability and rapid clearance; viral vectors raise concerns regarding insertional mutagenesis, immunogenicity, and manufacturing scalability; and synthetic transcription factors face challenges in cellular delivery and off-target effects. Furthermore, none of these approaches have progressed beyond early-phase clinical trials due to insufficient potency, inconsistent delivery, or unacceptable toxicity profiles.

Small-molecule drugs, by contrast, offer a compelling alternative. They are orally bioavailable, penetrate tissues effectively, are amenable to large-scale production, and can be administered chronically with established pharmacokinetic and safety profiles. The concept of drug repositioning—identifying new therapeutic uses for compounds already approved for other indications—has emerged as a powerful paradigm for accelerating drug development, reducing costs, and mitigating regulatory risk. Several approved drugs have been successfully repurposed for novel indications, including thalidomide for multiple myeloma and sildenafil for pulmonary hypertension. In the context of DMD, the identification of a small molecule capable of selectively upregulating endogenous utrophin expression would represent a transformative advance, offering a broadly applicable, non-invasive, and potentially lifelong therapeutic option for all DMD patients regardless of their underlying dystrophin mutation.

Despite these compelling advantages, no small-molecule utrophin upregulator has yet reached clinical development. Prior efforts to identify such compounds have relied on high-throughput screening of chemical libraries, but these have yielded few validated leads, and none have demonstrated robust, reproducible, and dose-dependent upregulation of endogenous utrophin at both the mRNA and protein levels in physiologically relevant cell models. The present invention addresses this critical unmet need by identifying nabumetone—a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) approved for the treatment of osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis—as a potent, selective, and safe pharmacological activator of the utrophin A promoter. Through systematic screening, validation, and mechanistic characterization, it has been demonstrated that nabumetone significantly increases utrophin A transcript and protein expression in muscle-derived cell lines without inducing cytotoxicity at therapeutic concentrations. This discovery forms the foundation of the present invention, which provides a novel, clinically translatable therapeutic strategy for the treatment of DMD through pharmacological enhancement of utrophin expression.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for treating Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) in a subject in need thereof by administering a therapeutically effective amount of nabumetone or a pharmaceutically acceptable salt, ester, prodrug, or metabolite thereof. The method is based on the unexpected and novel discovery that nabumetone directly upregulates the expression of utrophin A, the predominant isoform of utrophin in skeletal and cardiac muscle, thereby compensating for the absence of dystrophin and restoring functional integrity to the muscle sarcolemma. Administration of nabumetone results in a statistically significant increase in utrophin A messenger RNA levels and a corresponding increase in utrophin protein expression in muscle cells, as demonstrated in vitro using the C2C12 murine myoblast cell line and confirmed through quantitative real-time PCR and Western blot analysis. This upregulation occurs in a dose-dependent manner, with maximal induction observed at concentrations consistent with those achieved in human plasma following standard clinical dosing regimens. Importantly, the compound does not induce cytotoxicity at therapeutically relevant concentrations and does not require genetic modification, viral delivery, or protein replacement to exert its beneficial effect.

The invention further provides a pharmaceutical composition comprising nabumetone or a pharmaceutically acceptable salt, ester, prodrug, or metabolite thereof in association with one or more pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, excipients, or diluents, wherein the composition is formulated for oral, intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous, or transdermal administration. The composition may be provided in solid dosage forms such as tablets, capsules, or powders, or in liquid forms such as suspensions, syrups, or solutions, and may be designed for immediate, delayed, or extended release to optimize pharmacokinetic profiles and enhance patient compliance. The composition may further include additional therapeutic agents that synergize with nabumetone, such as anti-inflammatory compounds, antioxidants, or agents that stabilize utrophin at the sarcolemma, to enhance the overall therapeutic efficacy.

The invention also encompasses a method of increasing utrophin expression in muscle tissue of a subject suffering from DMD, comprising contacting the muscle tissue with a therapeutically effective concentration of nabumetone, wherein the concentration is sufficient to induce an increase in utrophin A mRNA and protein levels without inducing significant cytotoxicity. The method may be practiced in vivo, in vitro, or ex vivo, and is particularly useful for the treatment of skeletal and cardiac muscle fibers in pediatric and adult patients with DMD. The invention further provides a kit for the treatment of DMD, comprising a container holding a pharmaceutical composition of nabumetone, along with instructions for use indicating the dosage, frequency, and duration of administration necessary to achieve therapeutic upregulation of utrophin expression. The kit may further include diagnostic reagents for monitoring utrophin expression levels or biomarkers of muscle integrity, such as serum creatine kinase or muscle-specific microRNAs, to enable personalized dosing and therapeutic monitoring.

The invention is based on the novel insight that nabumetone, despite its known pharmacological profile as a cyclooxygenase inhibitor, exerts a distinct and previously unrecognized biological activity on the utrophin A promoter, independent of its anti-inflammatory properties. This activity is not shared by other NSAIDs or cyclooxygenase inhibitors, including ibuprofen, naproxen, celecoxib, or aspirin, which fail to induce utrophin expression in the same experimental systems. The mechanism by which nabumetone activates the utrophin A promoter remains under investigation but is believed to involve modulation of transcriptional regulators or epigenetic modifiers that bind to specific regulatory elements within the 2.3-kilobase promoter region. The invention does not rely on any particular mechanism of action and is not limited by theoretical understanding; rather, it is grounded in the empirical demonstration that nabumetone consistently and reproducibly increases utrophin expression in muscle cells, thereby providing a viable therapeutic strategy for DMD.

The invention further provides a method for screening compounds for utrophin upregulating activity by using a cell-based reporter assay comprising a construct in which the human utrophin A promoter is operably linked to a detectable reporter gene, such as luciferase or green fluorescent protein. The assay is performed by exposing cells stably transfected with the reporter construct to test compounds and measuring changes in reporter activity, wherein an increase in reporter signal correlates with promoter activation. The invention demonstrates that this assay is robust, reproducible, and suitable for high-throughput screening, and that nabumetone is a lead compound identified through such a screening process. The invention thus provides not only a therapeutic agent but also a validated platform for the discovery of additional utrophin upregulators.

In summary, the invention offers a novel, safe, and effective therapeutic approach for the treatment of Duchenne muscular dystrophy by leveraging the endogenous capacity of muscle tissue to express utrophin when appropriately stimulated. By repurposing an already approved and well-tolerated drug, the invention bypasses many of the developmental, regulatory, and manufacturing hurdles that have impeded other DMD therapies, offering the potential for rapid clinical translation and broad patient access. The invention represents a paradigm shift in the treatment of genetic neuromuscular disorders, demonstrating that pharmacological modulation of compensatory endogenous genes can serve as a viable alternative to gene correction or replacement.

The term “Duchenne muscular dystrophy” or “DMD” refers to a severe, progressive, X-linked recessive genetic disorder characterized by the absence or severe deficiency of the dystrophin protein in skeletal, cardiac, and smooth muscle tissues, resulting in recurrent muscle fiber damage, inflammation, fibrosis, and eventual replacement of muscle with adipose and connective tissue. DMD is caused by mutations in the DMD gene, most commonly large deletions or duplications that disrupt the reading frame, leading to premature termination of translation and nonfunctional dystrophin protein. The disorder typically presents in early childhood with delayed motor milestones, proximal muscle weakness, Gowers’ sign, calf pseudohypertrophy, and elevated serum creatine kinase levels, and progresses to loss of ambulation by early adolescence, followed by respiratory and cardiac failure.

The term “utrophin” refers to an autosomal homolog of dystrophin encoded by the UTRN gene, which shares approximately 74% amino acid sequence identity with dystrophin and contains homologous domains including an N-terminal actin-binding domain, a central rod domain composed of spectrin-like repeats, a cysteine-rich domain, and a C-terminal domain capable of binding to β-dystroglycan and other components of the dystrophin-associated protein complex. Utrophin exists in two major isoforms, utrophin A and utrophin B, which are transcribed from distinct promoters and exhibit differential tissue expression patterns. Utrophin A is the predominant isoform expressed in developing and regenerating skeletal muscle fibers and is the isoform targeted by the present invention. Utrophin B is primarily expressed in non-muscle tissues such as the kidney, liver, and lung, and in adult muscle is localized to the neuromuscular and myotendinous junctions.

The term “upregulation” or “upregulating” refers to an increase in the expression level of a gene or its encoded protein, as measured by increased mRNA transcript abundance, increased protein synthesis, or increased functional activity, relative to a control condition. In the context of the present invention, upregulation of utrophin refers to an increase in utrophin A mRNA and/or protein expression in muscle cells or tissue following administration of nabumetone, as compared to untreated controls or vehicle-treated controls. The increase may be at least 1.1-fold, 1.2-fold, 1.5-fold, 1.8-fold, 2.0-fold, or greater, as determined by quantitative real-time PCR, Western blot, immunohistochemistry, or other standard molecular biology techniques.

The term “nabumetone” refers to the chemical compound 4-(6-methoxy-2-naphthyl)butan-2-one, a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) with the molecular formula C15H16O2 and the CAS number 2446-36-6. Nabumetone is a prodrug that is metabolized in the liver to its active metabolite, 6-methoxy-2-naphthylacetic acid (6-MNA), which is responsible for its cyclooxygenase-inhibitory activity. For the purposes of this invention, the term “nabumetone” encompasses the parent compound, its active metabolite 6-MNA, and any pharmaceutically acceptable salt, ester, prodrug, or metabolite thereof that retains the ability to upregulate utrophin A expression in muscle cells.
