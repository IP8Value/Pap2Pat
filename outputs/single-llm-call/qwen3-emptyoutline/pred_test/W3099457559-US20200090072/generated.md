# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to quantum computing systems and methods for solving discrete optimization problems using quantum walks derived from classical Markov chains, particularly those governed by the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm and Glauber dynamics. More specifically, the invention provides a novel, fully circuit-based implementation of Szegedy’s quantum walk that circumvents the need for an oracle-based transition operator, thereby enabling efficient, scalable, and physically realizable quantum algorithms for sampling from Boltzmann distributions and minimizing energy functions over discrete configuration spaces. The invention is particularly applicable to quantum hardware architectures designed for fault-tolerant computation using gate-based models, including those based on superconducting qubits, trapped ions, or topological qubits, where the cost of arbitrary single-qubit rotations and multi-controlled gates must be minimized. The invention further extends to heuristic quantum algorithms for combinatorial optimization, including but not limited to finding ground states of Ising models, solving constraint satisfaction problems, optimizing machine learning loss functions, and simulating statistical mechanical systems at finite temperature. The disclosed methods achieve performance advantages over classical Monte Carlo methods by leveraging quantum interference, phase estimation, and adiabatic state preparation techniques, while avoiding the exponential overhead typically associated with oracle implementations of classical transition matrices. The invention is distinguished by its explicit, low-depth quantum circuit design that integrates move preparation, spin-flip operations, reflection operators, and Boltzmann coin rotations into a unified, modular structure, enabling direct implementation on near-term and future quantum devices without reliance on black-box or unphysical oracles.

## SUMMARY

The invention discloses a quantum algorithm for preparing a coherent representation of the stationary distribution of a classical reversible Markov chain, specifically one satisfying detailed balance, and for using this state to solve discrete optimization problems through heuristic quantum evolution. Unlike prior approaches that assume the existence of a unitary oracle implementing the classical transition matrix, the invention provides a complete, explicit quantum circuit implementation of the entire Szegedy quantum walk operator without requiring such an oracle. This is achieved by decomposing the walk into four distinct, physically realizable components: a move preparation unitary that encodes a sparse proposal distribution in unary qubit representation; a conditional spin-flip operator that applies local updates to the system register based on the selected move; a reflection operator that inverts the phase of the all-zero state in the move and coin registers; and a Boltzmann coin operator that applies a rotation to a single coin qubit conditioned on the current system state and proposed move, such that the rotation angle encodes the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance probability. These components are arranged in a fixed sequence to form a unitary operator that is isometric to the full Szegedy walk operator, yet avoids the costly uncomputation of auxiliary registers and the arithmetic operations required to compute transition probabilities on-the-fly. The invention further introduces two novel heuristic quantum algorithms for optimization: a Zeno-based adiabatic protocol with rewind, which successively projects the system onto the stationary states of a sequence of gradually annealed Markov chains, and a unitary-only protocol that sequentially applies the quantum walk operators without intermediate measurement, followed by a final computational basis measurement. Both protocols are implemented using the disclosed circuit architecture and demonstrate a polynomial quantum speedup over classical simulated annealing on Ising models, with empirical scaling exponents ranging from 0.39 to 0.75 depending on problem structure. The invention further includes a parallelized classical walk that matches the gate complexity of the quantum implementation, enabling fair comparison and suggesting that the quantum advantage arises not merely from reduced query complexity but from intrinsic quantum parallelism and interference. The circuit design minimizes depth through binary-tree parallelization of multi-qubit operations, uses unary encoding for move selection to avoid costly arithmetic, and employs quantum signal processing techniques as optional alternatives for high-precision Boltzmann coin rotations. The invention enables practical quantum advantage on problems where classical methods suffer from exponential mixing times, such as spin glasses and frustrated systems, and provides a blueprint for integrating quantum walks into hybrid quantum-classical optimization pipelines. The disclosed methods are robust to moderate errors in rotation synthesis and do not require error correction during the heuristic phase, making them suitable for execution on noisy intermediate-scale quantum devices.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### I. Introduction

The field of quantum algorithms for optimization has long been guided by the promise of quadratic speedups over classical Markov chain Monte Carlo methods, particularly through the quantization of reversible classical walks as formalized by Szegedy. This theoretical advantage, rooted in the ability of quantum walks to coherently explore state spaces with interference effects and to measure eigenvalues of the walk operator with quadratically fewer queries than classical mixing times, has inspired numerous proposals for quantum sampling and optimization. However, these proposals have consistently encountered a fundamental practical barrier: the assumption that a unitary oracle implementing the classical transition matrix can be efficiently realized. In reality, constructing such an oracle requires the evaluation of acceptance probabilities conditioned on system states, the uncomputation of auxiliary registers used in intermediate computations, and the precise implementation of arithmetic operations that scale with the size of the configuration space. These requirements render prior proposals infeasible on physical quantum hardware, where gate counts, circuit depth, and the cost of arbitrary rotations dominate the overall complexity and error budget. The present invention overcomes this barrier by entirely eliminating the need for such an oracle. Instead, it provides a direct, modular, and low-depth quantum circuit that realizes the full Szegedy quantum walk operator through a sequence of elementary quantum gates acting on a system register, a move register, and a coin register. This circuit is constructed from four distinct components, each designed to mirror a physical step in the classical Metropolis-Hastings algorithm while leveraging quantum parallelism and conditional dynamics. The system register encodes the current configuration of the discrete optimization problem, such as the spin state of an Ising model; the move register encodes a proposed local update in unary representation to avoid binary arithmetic; the coin register controls the acceptance of the proposed move through a rotation whose angle is determined by the energy difference induced by the move. The entire sequence of operations—preparing a uniform superposition of moves, applying the move conditionally, reflecting about the all-zero state, and rotating the coin according to the Boltzmann weight—is arranged to produce a unitary operator whose spectral properties are identical to those of the quantized classical walk, thereby preserving the theoretical guarantees of the Szegedy framework while making it physically implementable. This innovation transforms the quantum walk from a theoretical construct reliant on unphysical oracles into a concrete algorithmic primitive suitable for experimental realization. The invention further extends beyond state preparation to introduce two novel heuristic protocols for optimization: one based on the quantum Zeno effect with a rewind mechanism to maintain coherence across an annealing schedule, and another that dispenses with measurement entirely and relies on unitary evolution alone. Both protocols are shown through numerical simulations to outperform classical simulated annealing on benchmark problems, achieving superquadratic and subquadratic speedups depending on the structure of the energy landscape. The invention thus bridges the gap between abstract quantum algorithmic theory and practical quantum computation, providing a complete, self-contained, and scalable solution to a class of problems that have resisted efficient classical solutions for decades.

### II. General Considerations

The foundation of the invention lies in the quantization of reversible classical Markov chains, a framework established by Szegedy that maps a classical transition matrix to a unitary operator acting on a doubled Hilbert space. The classical walk is defined by a transition probability matrix W, where W_yx denotes the probability of transitioning from state x to state y. For such a walk to be reversible, it must satisfy the detailed balance condition: π_x W_yx = π_y W_xy, where π is the stationary distribution. This condition ensures that the Markov chain is ergodic and converges to a unique equilibrium distribution, which is often the Boltzmann distribution e^{-βE(x)} / Z for a given energy function E(x) and inverse temperature β. The quantum walk operator U_W is constructed by embedding the classical walk into a larger space, typically C^d ⊗ C^d, where d is the number of states. The operator W acts as |x⟩ ⊗ |0⟩ → |x⟩ ⊗ |w_x⟩, where |w_x⟩ = ∑_y √W_yx |y⟩ is the quantum state encoding the transition probabilities from x. A reflection operator R = 2Π_0 - I, where Π_0 projects onto the subspace spanned by {|x⟩ ⊗ |0⟩}, and a swap operator Λ that exchanges the two registers, are then combined to form U_W = ΛW R W† ΛW. The eigenvalues of U_W are related to the eigenvalues λ_k of W via cos θ_k = λ_k, and the eigenstate corresponding to θ_k = 0 encodes the stationary distribution as |π⟩ ⊗ |0⟩. Measuring this state in the computational basis yields samples from π. The key insight underlying the invention is that the operator W, while mathematically well-defined, is not a primitive operation in quantum hardware. Its implementation requires the evaluation of W_yx for arbitrary x and y, which in turn requires computing the acceptance probability A_yx = min(1, e^{-β(E(y)-E(x))}) and uncomputing auxiliary registers used in this computation. This uncomputation is particularly costly when the move is rejected, as the state must be restored to its original form without leaving traces in ancillary qubits. The invention circumvents this issue by recognizing that the entire operator U_W can be realized without ever constructing W explicitly. Instead, the components of U_W are implemented directly using quantum gates that simulate the physical steps of the classical algorithm: selecting a move, applying it conditionally, deciding whether to accept it via a phase rotation, and reflecting to enforce coherence. This approach eliminates the need for any arithmetic computation of transition probabilities during runtime and avoids the exponential overhead of uncomputation. The use of unary encoding for the move register is a critical design choice that enables this elimination. Rather than encoding the index of a proposed move in binary, which would require log d qubits and complex arithmetic to decode, the invention uses N qubits to represent N possible moves, with exactly one qubit in state |1⟩ and the rest in |0⟩. This allows the conditional application of a move to be implemented via a simple set of controlled-NOT and Toffoli gates, where each move corresponds to a fixed pattern of spin flips. The coin register, a single qubit, is rotated by an angle θ_xj = arccos(√A_xj) conditioned on the system state and the active move, thereby encoding the acceptance probability as a quantum phase. The reflection operator, which inverts the phase of the all-zero state in the move and coin registers, is implemented using a binary-tree structure of Toffoli gates and ancillary qubits, achieving logarithmic depth rather than linear. This design ensures that the entire walk operator can be executed in a depth that scales polynomially with the system size and the sparsity of the interaction graph, rather than exponentially as would be required by an oracle-based implementation. The invention further acknowledges that the precision of the Boltzmann coin rotation is critical to maintaining detailed balance and ensuring the correctness of the stationary distribution. However, unlike prior approaches that demand high-precision rotations for every application, the invention demonstrates through numerical simulations that moderate precision, on the order of 10^{-3} to 10^{-4} radians, is sufficient to preserve the qualitative behavior of the algorithm. This tolerance for error significantly reduces the number of T gates required for rotation synthesis, making the algorithm more feasible on hardware with limited gate fidelity. The invention thus redefines the paradigm of quantum walk implementation by replacing abstract oracles with concrete, decomposable quantum circuits that respect the physical constraints of real quantum devices.

### III. Example Embodiments

An exemplary embodiment of the invention is implemented for a (k,d)-local Ising model, where the system consists of n spins taking values in {+1, -1}, and the energy function is given by E(x) = -∑_{Ω} J_Ω ∏_{i∈Ω} x_i, where each subset Ω contains at most k spins and each spin interacts with at most d others. The proposal distribution is chosen to be uniform over a set M of N sparse moves, where each move z_j ∈ M flips a constant number of spins, and each spin is involved in a constant number of moves. For instance, in the case of single-spin flips, N = n and each move flips exactly one spin. The system register contains n qubits, each initialized in a superposition state such as |+⟩^⊗n to represent a uniform prior over all configurations. The move register contains N qubits encoded in unary, initialized to |00...010...0⟩ with the single 1 at position j corresponding to the j-th move. The coin register is a single qubit initialized to |0⟩. The first component of the walk, move preparation, transforms the initial state into an equal superposition over all moves: (1/√N) ∑_{j=1}^N |j⟩_M. This is achieved by applying a sequence of √SWAP gates arranged in a binary tree, which efficiently converts the single |1⟩ in the unary register into a uniform superposition. When N is not a power of two, the distribution is padded with trivial moves (those that leave the system unchanged), increasing the number of moves to 2^⌈log_2 N⌉, which slows the walk by at most a factor of √2—a negligible cost compared to the savings in gate complexity. The second component, spin flip, applies a conditional bit-flip to the system register based on the active move. For each move j, a set of Toffoli gates is applied, with the j-th qubit of the move register and the coin qubit as controls, and the target qubits being those spins that are flipped by move j. For single-spin flips, this requires exactly one Toffoli gate per move, applied in sequence or in parallel using additional scratchpad qubits to copy the coin state and enable simultaneous application. The third component, reflection, implements the operator R = 2Π_0 - I, where Π_0 projects onto the state |0⟩_M ⊗ |0⟩_C. This is achieved by using a binary-tree structure of Toffoli gates and ancillary qubits to compute the logical AND of all N+1 qubits (N in the move register and one in the coin register). The result is stored in an ancilla, which is then used to apply a Z gate to a fresh ancillary qubit, producing the desired phase flip. The ancillas are then uncomputed in reverse order, yielding a depth of 2 log_2 N in terms of Toffoli gates. The fourth and most critical component, the Boltzmann coin, applies a rotation to the coin qubit conditioned on both the system state and the active move. For each move j, the rotation angle θ_j(x) is determined by the energy difference Δ_j(x) = E(x) - E(x • z_j), where x • z_j denotes the configuration obtained by flipping the spins in move j. For the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm, the rotation angle is θ_j(x) = arccos(√min(1, e^{-βΔ_j(x)})). This rotation is implemented as a sequence of controlled single-qubit rotations, where each rotation is conditioned on the specific configuration of the spins in the set N_j, the subset of system qubits that determine Δ_j(x). Because the model is (k,d)-local, each N_j has size at most k·d, and thus each rotation can be synthesized using a constant number of T gates. The total number of such rotations is N, and they must be applied sequentially due to their shared target qubit. To reduce depth, the coin qubit can be copied into the conjugate basis of σ_y, transforming the sequence of rotations into a tensor product of independent rotations, which can then be applied in parallel if the sets N_j are non-overlapping. Alternatively, quantum signal processing techniques can be employed to synthesize the entire Boltzmann coin as a single unitary by encoding the energy differences as phases in a controlled Hamiltonian evolution, followed by a Fourier transform to extract the desired function. This approach trades qubit overhead for gate depth and may be advantageous for models with dense interactions. The complete walk operator is formed by applying these four components in sequence: move preparation, spin flip, reflection, and Boltzmann coin. The resulting unitary is isometric to the Szegedy walk operator and preserves its spectral properties, including the eigenvalue θ=0 corresponding to the stationary distribution. This embodiment demonstrates that the entire quantum walk can be implemented without any arithmetic computation, without any uncomputation of intermediate states, and without any reliance on an oracle. The circuit depth scales as O(N log N) for the reflection and move preparation, O(N) for the spin flip, and O(N log(1/ε)) for the Boltzmann coin, where ε is the precision of the rotation synthesis. The total number of qubits required is n + N + 1 + O(N) ancillae, which is polynomial in the system size and acceptable for near-term devices.

### IV. Example Computing Environments

The invention is designed for implementation on a variety of quantum computing architectures, each with distinct strengths and limitations in qubit connectivity, gate fidelity, and coherence time. In a superconducting qubit platform, such as those developed by IBM or Google, the system register is encoded in a linear or two-dimensional array of transmon qubits, with each qubit representing a spin in the Ising model. The move register is implemented using a separate set of transmons, arranged in a linear chain to facilitate the binary-tree structure of the √SWAP and Toffoli gates. The coin qubit is a dedicated transmon with high coherence time, optimized for high-fidelity single-qubit rotations. The Toffoli gates required for the spin flip and reflection components are implemented using native three-qubit gates or decomposed into a sequence of CNOTs and single-qubit rotations, with error mitigation techniques such as dynamical decoupling applied to reduce decoherence. The √SWAP gates, which are in the third level of the Clifford hierarchy, are implemented using calibrated microwave pulses that directly realize the desired unitary, avoiding the need for costly gate synthesis. In a trapped ion system, the system and move registers are encoded in the internal states of ions in a linear trap, with all-to-all connectivity enabling direct implementation of multi-controlled gates. The reflection operator, which requires an (N+1)-fold controlled-Z gate, can be implemented in a single step using a global entangling pulse, significantly reducing depth compared to gate-based decompositions. The Boltzmann coin rotations are performed using precisely timed laser pulses that induce state-dependent phase shifts, with the angle determined by the measured energy difference computed classically and fed back to the control system. In a photonic quantum computer, the system and move registers are encoded in the presence or absence of photons in different spatial modes, and the operations are implemented using linear optical elements, beam splitters, and phase shifters. The reflection operator is realized using a network of interferometers that detect the all-zero state and apply a phase flip via interference. The coin rotation is implemented using a variable phase shifter controlled by the state of the system register, with the rotation angle determined by a classical precomputation of the energy differences. In all cases, the invention is compatible with error correction schemes, though such schemes are not required for the heuristic protocols described herein, as the algorithm is robust to moderate noise levels. The invention is also designed to be executed on hybrid quantum-classical systems, where a classical co-processor precomputes the energy differences Δ_j(x) for all moves and all configurations, storing them in a lookup table or computing them on-the-fly using a sparse representation. The quantum processor then retrieves the appropriate rotation angle via a classical control signal, enabling the use of large systems where the full energy landscape cannot be stored. The invention further supports offline compilation of rotation angles, where the required rotations are precomputed and stored as a sequence of T gates, which are then teleported into the computation using magic state distillation. This approach increases the qubit overhead but relaxes the timing constraints on the quantum processor, making the algorithm feasible on devices with slower gate speeds. The invention is thus adaptable to a wide range of hardware platforms, from noisy intermediate-scale quantum devices to future fault-tolerant machines, and is optimized for minimal circuit depth, low qubit count, and compatibility with existing gate sets.

### V. Further Example Embodiments

A further embodiment of the invention extends the core circuit architecture to include adaptive annealing schedules and dynamic move sets. In the standard embodiment, the inverse temperature β is held fixed during the execution of the quantum walk, and the stationary distribution corresponds to a Boltzmann distribution at that temperature. However, in optimization problems, the goal is to sample from the ground state, which corresponds to β → ∞. To achieve this, the invention introduces a sequence of quantum walks U_{W_j}, each corresponding to a different value of β_j, where β_j increases from 0 to a final value β_final over L steps. The first embodiment, termed the Zeno protocol with rewind, begins with the system initialized in the uniform distribution |π_0⟩ = |+⟩^⊗n, which is the stationary state at β=0. A binary measurement is performed to project the state onto the stationary subspace of U_{W_1}, using quantum phase estimation to resolve the eigenvalue θ=0. If the measurement succeeds, the state collapses to |π_1⟩, the stationary state at β_1. If it fails, the algorithm does not restart from β=0 but instead applies a “rewind” operation: it reverses the previous walk operator U_{W_1}^†, reverts to the previous state |π_0⟩, and attempts the projection again. This process is repeated until the projection succeeds, after which the system proceeds to β_2, and so on. The rewind mechanism dramatically reduces the number of failed attempts compared to a naive Zeno protocol, which requires a full restart after each failure. The total time to solution is computed as the sum of the number of applications of U_{W_j} required to achieve a successful projection at each step, multiplied by the number of repetitions needed to boost the final success probability to a desired level. A second embodiment, termed the unitary-only protocol, dispenses with measurement entirely. Starting from |π_0⟩, the algorithm applies the sequence of unitaries U_{W_L} U_{W_{L-1}} ... U_{W_1} in sequence, without any intermediate measurement. The final state |ψ(L)⟩ is then measured in the computational basis, and the outcome is accepted as a candidate solution. This protocol is heuristic in nature, lacking a formal proof of convergence, but numerical simulations demonstrate that it outperforms even the Zeno protocol with rewind in many cases. The reason for this unexpected performance is believed to lie in the phase randomization induced by the sequential application of non-commuting unitaries, which mimics the effect of measurement without collapsing the state. A third embodiment introduces a parallelized classical walk as a baseline for comparison and as a potential target for quantization. In this variant, instead of proposing a single spin flip per step, the algorithm proposes a simultaneous update to all spins, where each spin is flipped with probability q and accepted with a probability that depends only on the initial configuration, not on prior accepted flips. This parallelization increases the effective step size of the classical walk and reduces the number of steps required to reach equilibrium, but it breaks detailed balance. The invention demonstrates that the quantum version of this parallel walk can be implemented using the same circuit architecture, suggesting that the framework may be extended to irreversible walks in future work. A fourth embodiment incorporates quantum signal processing to synthesize the Boltzmann coin as a single unitary transformation. Instead of applying N separate rotations, the energy differences Δ_j(x) are encoded as phases in a controlled Hamiltonian evolution, and a quantum signal processing algorithm is used to approximate the function f(e^{iλΔ}) = e^{i2θ}, where θ is the desired rotation angle. This approach reduces the number of required rotations from O(N) to O(polylog(N, 1/ε)), at the cost of increased circuit depth and ancilla qubits. This embodiment is particularly advantageous for models with dense interactions, where N is large and the sets N_j overlap significantly. A fifth embodiment integrates the invention into a variational quantum algorithm, where the parameters of the move set M, the annealing schedule β_j, and the rotation precision ε are optimized using a classical optimizer to maximize the probability of measuring the ground state. The quantum processor executes the circuit for a given set of parameters, and the measurement outcomes are fed back to the optimizer, which adjusts the parameters to improve performance. This hybrid approach enables the algorithm to adapt to the specific structure of the energy landscape, potentially unlocking further speedups beyond those predicted by theoretical bounds. These embodiments collectively demonstrate the versatility and extensibility of the invention, showing that the core circuit architecture can be adapted to a wide range of optimization problems, hardware constraints, and algorithmic strategies.

### VI. Concluding Remarks

The invention represents a fundamental advancement in the practical realization of quantum algorithms for optimization by eliminating the reliance on unphysical oracles and providing a complete, circuit-based implementation of Szegedy’s quantum walk. By decomposing the walk into four modular components—move preparation, spin flip, reflection, and Boltzmann coin—the invention enables the direct implementation of quantum walks on real quantum hardware, without the exponential overhead of arithmetic computation or uncomputation. The use of unary encoding, binary-tree parallelization, and tolerance for moderate rotation errors ensures that the circuit depth and qubit count remain polynomial in the system size, making the invention feasible on near-term quantum devices. The introduction of two novel heuristic protocols—the Zeno algorithm with rewind and the unitary-only evolution—demonstrates that quantum advantage can be achieved not only through theoretical speedups but through the coherent manipulation of quantum states over time, even in the absence of measurement. Numerical simulations on Ising models reveal that these protocols achieve polynomial speedups over classical simulated annealing, with exponents ranging from 0.39 to 0.75, surpassing the expected quadratic advantage and suggesting that quantum interference effects provide more than just a square-root improvement in mixing time. The invention further establishes a fair comparison with classical algorithms by introducing a parallelized classical walk that matches the gate complexity of the quantum implementation, thereby isolating the quantum advantage to the intrinsic properties of quantum evolution. The adaptability of the circuit architecture to different hardware platforms, from superconducting qubits to trapped ions and photonic systems, ensures broad applicability across the quantum computing landscape. The invention also opens new directions for future research, including the quantization of irreversible Markov chains, the integration of machine learning for adaptive annealing, and the use of quantum signal processing to compress the Boltzmann coin into a single unitary. Importantly, the invention does not merely provide a faster algorithm—it provides a new paradigm for quantum optimization, one grounded in physical realizability rather than theoretical abstraction. The path to practical quantum advantage in optimization is no longer dependent on the existence of perfect oracles or fault-tolerant architectures; it is now within reach through the careful design of quantum circuits that respect the constraints of real hardware. The invention thus transforms quantum walks from a theoretical curiosity into a practical tool for solving some of the most challenging problems in science and engineering, from materials design to machine learning, and paves the way for a new generation of quantum algorithms that are not only theoretically sound but physically executable.
