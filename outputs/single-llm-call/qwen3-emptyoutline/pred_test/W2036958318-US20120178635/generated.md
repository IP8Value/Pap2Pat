# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Chromosomal translocations represent a fundamental class of genomic aberrations that underlie the pathogenesis of numerous hematologic malignancies and solid tumors. These structural rearrangements, which involve the exchange of genetic material between non-homologous chromosomes, frequently result in the formation of novel fusion genes that drive oncogenic signaling through constitutive activation of kinases, transcription factors, or other regulatory proteins. The Philadelphia chromosome, a hallmark translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22, exemplifies the clinical significance of such events. This balanced reciprocal translocation, designated t(9;22)(q34;q11), generates the BCR-ABL1 fusion gene, which encodes a chimeric tyrosine kinase with unregulated activity that is both necessary and sufficient to initiate chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) and a subset of B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemias. The breakpoint in the ABL1 gene typically occurs within a large intronic region upstream of the tyrosine kinase domain, while the corresponding breakpoint in the BCR gene is localized to a well-defined 5.8-kilobase region known as the major breakpoint cluster region (M-bcr). In over 90% of CML cases, this specific fusion event is the sole molecular driver of disease, making its detection not only diagnostic but also essential for monitoring therapeutic response and predicting relapse.

Current diagnostic paradigms for detecting such translocations rely heavily on cytogenetic techniques, including conventional karyotyping and fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH). Karyotyping, although historically the gold standard, requires actively dividing cells and necessitates a multi-day in vitro culture period to obtain sufficient metaphase spreads for analysis. This limitation renders karyotyping unsuitable for samples with low proliferative capacity, such as those derived from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues or peripheral blood samples with minimal blast counts. Furthermore, its resolution is constrained to approximately 5 megabases, precluding the identification of cryptic or micro-rearrangements that may be clinically relevant. FISH, while applicable to interphase nuclei and thus circumventing the need for cell culture, still lacks the precision to define the exact nucleotide position of the translocation breakpoint. Although FISH can detect the juxtaposition of BCR and ABL1 signals, it cannot distinguish between different fusion variants or provide the sequence-level detail required for designing patient-specific molecular assays. As a result, neither technique yields a durable, sequence-defined biomarker that can be reliably amplified and quantified in subsequent clinical follow-up.

Real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) has emerged as the most sensitive method for detecting BCR-ABL1 transcripts, capable of identifying as few as one leukemic cell among 10⁴ to 10⁶ normal cells. However, this method is fundamentally dependent on the integrity and abundance of RNA, which is inherently unstable due to the presence of ribonucleases and the chemical lability of the 2′-hydroxyl group in ribose. RNA degradation during sample collection, transport, or storage—particularly in clinical settings where ideal conditions cannot always be maintained—can lead to false-negative results. Moreover, transcriptional silencing of the fusion gene, epigenetic regulation, or alternative splicing can further obscure the presence of the chimeric transcript, even when the underlying DNA rearrangement persists. Studies have demonstrated that patients with undetectable BCR-ABL1 transcripts by RT-PCR may still harbor residual leukemic DNA, raising critical questions regarding the true extent of disease eradication and the feasibility of treatment discontinuation. Consequently, a DNA-based detection method that is independent of transcriptional activity and resistant to degradation would represent a transformative advancement in the management of translocation-driven diseases.

The advent of high-throughput sequencing technologies has opened new avenues for the detection of structural variants, including translocations, through the analysis of paired-end tags (PETs). This approach involves fragmenting genomic DNA, ligating adapters with known sequences to both ends of each fragment, and sequencing both termini of each fragment in a paired manner. When a translocation occurs, the two ends of a single DNA fragment will map to distinct chromosomal loci, generating a discordant pair that signals the presence of a structural rearrangement. While genome-wide PET sequencing has proven effective in identifying novel fusion events in research settings, its application to clinical diagnostics has been hindered by prohibitive costs and excessive data complexity. Sequencing an entire human genome to detect a single, known translocation is neither efficient nor economically viable. A targeted approach is therefore required—one that selectively enriches for genomic regions harboring known translocation breakpoints while preserving the ability to identify the precise junctional sequence at base-pair resolution.

The present invention addresses these limitations through the development of Anchored ChromPET, a novel, integrated methodology that combines targeted genomic capture, high-throughput paired-end sequencing, and sample multiplexing to enable the sensitive, specific, and cost-effective detection of chromosomal translocations. The method employs a biotinylated RNA bait designed to hybridize to a region of interest—such as the M-bcr in BCR-ABL1-positive leukemias—thereby enriching for DNA fragments that span the translocation junction. The enriched fragments are then subjected to paired-end sequencing using barcoded adapters, allowing multiple patient samples to be processed simultaneously in a single sequencing lane. Bioinformatic analysis of the resulting paired-end reads identifies junctional chromPETs—those in which one end maps to the target region and the other to the partner gene—and employs a statistical voting algorithm to predict the most probable breakpoint location with remarkable precision. This predictive capability enables the design of patient-specific PCR primers that amplify the exact junctional sequence, yielding a stable, DNA-based biomarker that can be used for diagnosis, minimal residual disease monitoring, and longitudinal tracking of disease evolution. Unlike RNA-based assays, this biomarker is unaffected by transcriptional silencing, is resistant to degradation during sample handling, and remains detectable even in archived FFPE tissues and cell-free circulating nucleic acids. The method’s ability to detect both reciprocal translocation products—BCR-ABL1 and ABL1-BCR—further enhances its diagnostic robustness and provides insight into the molecular mechanisms of chromosomal breakage and repair. By eliminating the need for cell culture, achieving base-pair resolution of breakpoints, and enabling multiplexed, high-throughput analysis at a fraction of the cost of whole-genome sequencing, Anchored ChromPET represents a paradigm shift in the molecular diagnosis of translocation-associated diseases.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and highly effective method for detecting and characterizing chromosomal translocations in biological samples through the use of targeted enrichment, paired-end sequencing, and bioinformatic breakpoint prediction. The method, termed Anchored ChromPET, enables the identification of precise DNA junction sequences resulting from chromosomal rearrangements, thereby generating a stable, patient-specific molecular biomarker that can be used for diagnosis, prognosis, and longitudinal monitoring of disease. The invention is particularly useful for detecting translocations such as the BCR-ABL1 fusion associated with chronic myeloid leukemia and Philadelphia chromosome-positive acute lymphoblastic leukemia, but is broadly applicable to any translocation for which a genomic region of interest can be defined.

The method comprises the following key steps: first, genomic DNA is extracted from a biological sample and fragmented into discrete pieces of defined size. These fragments are then ligated to Y-shaped adapters that contain a unique molecular barcode sequence, enabling the multiplexing of multiple samples in a single sequencing run. The resulting library of DNA fragments is hybridized to a biotinylated RNA bait that is complementary to a targeted genomic region known to harbor translocation breakpoints, such as the major breakpoint cluster region of the BCR gene. The RNA-DNA hybrids are captured using streptavidin-coated magnetic beads, thereby enriching the library for fragments that contain sequences from the region of interest. Non-hybridized fragments are washed away, and the captured DNA is eluted and converted into double-stranded form through PCR amplification using primers complementary to the adapter sequences.

The enriched library is then subjected to high-throughput paired-end sequencing, yielding millions of short sequence reads, each consisting of two ends derived from the same original DNA fragment. The sequencing data is processed through a bioinformatic pipeline that assigns each paired-end tag to its respective sample based on the embedded barcode, maps the two ends of each tag to a reference genome, and classifies the tags as either normal (both ends mapping within the same gene or region) or junctional (each end mapping to a different chromosomal locus). Junctional tags are further analyzed using a statistical algorithm that predicts the most likely location of the translocation breakpoint by aggregating positional information from multiple tags. Each junctional tag contributes a weighted vote to a series of intervals along the target region and its partner gene, with the weight determined by the expected fragment size distribution derived from normal chromosomal tags. The interval with the highest cumulative vote is identified as the predicted breakpoint location.

Once the breakpoint is predicted, patient-specific primers are designed to flank the predicted junctional region, enabling the amplification of the exact translocation sequence by conventional PCR. The amplified product can then be sequenced to confirm the breakpoint at the nucleotide level, or quantified using real-time PCR to monitor disease burden over time. Because the method relies on DNA rather than RNA, the resulting biomarker is stable under suboptimal storage conditions, including formalin fixation, prolonged ambient temperature exposure, and long-term archival in paraffin blocks. Furthermore, the method is capable of detecting translocations in samples with very low tumor cell content, as few as one leukemic cell among 10,000 normal cells, and can identify both reciprocal fusion products, providing a comprehensive view of the rearrangement.

The invention also encompasses a system for performing the method, including a kit comprising the necessary reagents for library preparation, bait synthesis, hybridization, enrichment, and sequencing, as well as software for data analysis and breakpoint prediction. The system is scalable and adaptable to any translocation of interest by simply redesigning the RNA bait to target a different genomic region. The method does not require cell culture, is compatible with peripheral blood, bone marrow aspirates, FFPE tissues, and cell-free circulating DNA, and can be performed in a single laboratory workflow with minimal hands-on time. The resulting DNA-based biomarker offers superior stability, sensitivity, and specificity compared to existing RNA-based methods, and provides a durable, sequence-defined target for molecular diagnostics and personalized medicine.

The term false positive refers to a test result that incorrectly indicates the presence of a condition. The term false negative refers to a test result that incorrectly indicates the absence of a condition. The term sensitivity refers to the proportion of true positives correctly identified by the test. The term specificity refers to the proportion of true negatives correctly identified by the test. The term positive predictive value refers to the probability that a positive test result is a true positive. The term negative predictive value refers to the probability that a negative test result is a true negative. The term likelihood ratio refers to the ratio of the probability of a given test result in patients with the disease to the probability of the same result in patients without the disease. The term diagnostic accuracy refers to the overall ability of the test to correctly classify patients as having or not having the condition. The term clinical utility refers to the impact of the test on patient management and outcomes. The term patient stratification refers to the grouping of patients based on molecular characteristics to guide treatment selection. The term personalized medicine refers to the tailoring of medical treatment to the individual characteristics of each patient. The term molecular diagnostics refers to the analysis of nucleic acids to detect disease-associated alterations. The term next-generation sequencing refers to high-throughput sequencing technologies that enable parallel sequencing of millions of DNA fragments. The term Illumina sequencing refers to sequencing by synthesis technology developed by Illumina, Inc. The term Solexa sequencing refers to the original sequencing technology developed by Solexa, now part of Illumina. The term barcoded adapter refers to an adapter containing a unique nucleotide sequence that identifies the sample of origin. The term multiplexing refers to the simultaneous processing of multiple samples in a single sequencing run. The term sequencing depth refers to the average number of times a nucleotide is sequenced in a given sample. The term coverage refers to the proportion of a target region that is sequenced at least once. The term variant calling refers to the identification of differences between the sequenced DNA and the reference genome. The term alignment refers to the process of matching sequencing reads to a reference genome. The term mapping refers to the assignment of sequencing reads to specific genomic locations. The term reference genome refers to a representative sequence of a species’ genome used as a template for comparison. The term human reference genome refers to the assembled sequence of the human genome, such as GRCh38. The term genomic locus refers to the specific physical location of a gene or DNA sequence on a chromosome. The term chromosomal arm refers to the long (q) or short (p) segment of a chromosome extending from the centromere. The term cytogenetic band refers to a region of a chromosome that stains with a particular pattern during karyotyping. The term breakpoint cluster region refers to a genomic region where translocation breakpoints occur with high frequency. The term fusion gene refers to a gene created by the joining of parts of two previously separate genes. The term chimeric transcript refers to an RNA molecule derived from a fusion gene. The term oncogene refers to a gene that has the potential to cause cancer when mutated or expressed at high levels. The term tumor suppressor gene refers to a gene that protects a cell from one step on the path to cancer. The term kinase refers to an enzyme that transfers phosphate groups from high-energy donor molecules to specific substrates. The term tyrosine kinase refers to a kinase that phosphorylates tyrosine residues on proteins. The term constitutive activation refers to the persistent, unregulated activity of a protein, often due to fusion or mutation. The term apoptosis refers to programmed cell death. The term cell cycle arrest refers to the halting of cell division at a specific phase. The term proliferation refers to the multiplication of cells. The term differentiation refers to the process by which a less specialized cell becomes a more specialized cell type. The term clonality refers to the origin of a population of cells from a single progenitor. The term monoclonal refers to a population of cells derived from a single ancestor. The term polyclonal refers to a population of cells derived from multiple ancestors. The term heterogeneity refers to the presence of genetic or phenotypic diversity within a population of cells. The term genetic heterogeneity refers to the presence of different mutations or rearrangements among patients with the same disease. The term molecular heterogeneity refers to differences in the genetic alterations underlying a disease. The term diagnostic biomarker refers to a measurable indicator of the presence or absence of a disease. The term predictive biomarker refers to a measurable indicator of the likely response to a specific therapy. The term therapeutic biomarker refers to a measurable indicator of the biological effect of a therapy. The term pharmacodynamic biomarker refers to a biomarker that reflects the biological effect of a drug on its target. The term pharmacokinetic biomarker refers to a biomarker that reflects the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion of a drug. The term companion diagnostic refers to a diagnostic test used to identify patients who are most likely to benefit from a specific therapeutic agent. The term precision oncology refers to the use of molecular profiling to guide cancer treatment. The term liquid biopsy refers to the analysis of circulating biomarkers in body fluids, such as blood or urine, as a non-invasive alternative to tissue biopsy. The term circulating tumor DNA refers to fragments of DNA released into the bloodstream by dying tumor cells. The term tumor burden refers to the total amount of cancer present in the body. The term disease-free survival refers to the length of time after treatment during which no signs of disease are detected. The term overall survival refers to the length of time from diagnosis or treatment until death from any cause. The term relapse refers to the return of disease after a period of remission. The term remission refers to the absence of detectable disease following treatment. The term minimal residual disease refers to the presence of a small number of cancer cells that remain after treatment and are undetectable by conventional methods. The term molecular remission refers to the absence of detectable disease at the molecular level, as confirmed by highly sensitive assays. The term treatment response refers to the degree to which a therapy reduces disease burden. The term prognostic marker refers to a biomarker that predicts the likely course or outcome of a disease. The term predictive marker refers to a biomarker that indicates the likelihood of response to a specific therapy. The term pharmacogenomics refers to the study of how genetic variation influences individual responses to drugs. The term drug resistance mutation refers to a genetic alteration that reduces the effectiveness of a therapeutic agent. The term targeted therapy refers to a treatment that specifically inhibits a molecular driver of disease, such as a fusion kinase. The term tyrosine kinase inhibitor refers to a class of drugs that block the activity of tyrosine kinases, such as imatinib, dasatinib, and nilotinib. The term molecular monitoring refers to the repeated measurement of molecular biomarkers to assess disease status over time. The term diagnostic algorithm refers to a stepwise decision-making process for interpreting test results. The term decision support system refers to a computerized tool that assists clinicians in interpreting complex diagnostic data. The term integration into clinical workflow refers to the incorporation of the method into routine diagnostic procedures without disrupting existing processes. The term cost-effectiveness analysis refers to the evaluation of the economic value of a diagnostic test relative to its benefits. The term health economics refers to the study of the allocation of resources in healthcare systems. The term scalability refers to the ability of the method to be applied to larger numbers of samples or broader genomic regions without a proportional increase in cost or complexity. The term throughput refers to the number of samples that can be processed per unit of time. The term turnaround time refers to the interval between sample receipt and result delivery. The term automation refers to the use of instruments or software to reduce manual intervention and increase throughput and consistency. The term standardization refers to the establishment of uniform protocols and criteria across laboratories. The term inter-laboratory reproducibility refers to the consistency of results obtained when the same method is performed in different laboratories. The term intra-laboratory reproducibility refers to the consistency of results obtained when the same method is performed repeatedly within the same laboratory. The term reference material refers to a sample with a known composition used to calibrate or validate a test. The term certified reference material refers to a reference material that has been validated by an authoritative body. The term quality assurance refers to the systematic activities implemented to ensure that a product or service meets specified requirements. The term quality control refers to the operational techniques and activities used to fulfill requirements for quality. The term audit refers to a systematic examination of procedures, records, and results to ensure compliance with standards. The term accreditation refers to formal recognition by an external body that a laboratory meets defined standards of competence. The term ISO 15189 refers to an international standard for medical laboratories. The term CLIA refers to the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments, a U.S. regulatory framework for laboratory testing.
