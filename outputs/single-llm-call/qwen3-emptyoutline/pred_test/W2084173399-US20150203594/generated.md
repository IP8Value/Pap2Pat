# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The development of diagnostic platforms based on porous, biodegradable, and non-toxic substrates has gained increasing attention in recent years due to the growing demand for sustainable, cost-effective, and environmentally benign alternatives to conventional synthetic polymer-based systems. Traditional materials such as nitrocellulose, polyvinylidene fluoride, and polystyrene have been widely employed in immunoassays, lateral flow tests, and biosensors owing to their ability to immobilize biomolecules through passive adsorption. However, these materials suffer from significant limitations including poor mechanical integrity under wet conditions, susceptibility to degradation during storage or handling, high propensity for non-specific protein binding, and environmental persistence that undermines their long-term sustainability. Nitrocellulose, despite its widespread use in diagnostic membranes, is inherently flammable, exhibits low tensile strength, and possesses a highly porous structure that permits uncontrolled diffusion of analytes and reagents, thereby reducing assay precision and reproducibility. Furthermore, its strong affinity for a broad range of proteins leads to high background signals, necessitating extensive blocking protocols that increase both cost and complexity.

Cellulose-based materials have emerged as promising candidates to replace conventional synthetic substrates due to their renewable origin, biocompatibility, and inherent hydrophilicity. Among these, nanofibrillated cellulose (NFC) has attracted particular interest because of its unique combination of high surface area, excellent mechanical strength, and ability to form dense, cohesive films through extensive hydrogen bonding networks. Unlike traditional cellulose paper or nitrocellulose membranes, NFC films can be engineered to possess a highly compact microstructure with minimal internal porosity, which significantly reduces water uptake and enhances dimensional stability under humid or aqueous conditions. This property is particularly advantageous in diagnostic applications where controlled fluid flow, minimal analyte dilution, and structural integrity are critical for reliable signal generation. However, the native surface chemistry of NFC is dominated by hydroxyl groups, which lack the functional groups necessary for stable, covalent immobilization of biomolecules such as antibodies, enzymes, or antigens. While chemical modification strategies have been explored to introduce reactive moieties onto cellulose surfaces, most approaches—particularly those involving TEMPO-mediated oxidation—have been applied to suspensions of individual nanofibrils rather than to consolidated, free-standing films. These methods often result in excessive swelling, loss of mechanical integrity, and uncontrolled penetration of oxidizing agents into the bulk of the material, leading to disintegration of the film structure and rendering it unsuitable for practical diagnostic use.

Moreover, existing surface activation techniques for cellulose-based substrates typically rely on post-synthesis functionalization of highly charged, water-swellable nanocellulose gels, which are inherently unstable when dried and rehydrated. The high density of carboxyl groups introduced by TEMPO oxidation increases the hydrophilicity of the surface to such an extent that the resulting films become overly hydrated, leading to excessive water retention, poor binding kinetics, and instability under ambient conditions. This creates a fundamental paradox: while carboxylation is essential for enabling covalent conjugation chemistry, it simultaneously compromises the very water resistance that makes NFC films desirable as diagnostic substrates. Consequently, there exists a critical gap in the art for a method that simultaneously preserves the structural integrity and water resistance of NFC films while enabling precise, stable, and high-density immobilization of biomolecules through chemoselective coupling reactions. Additionally, the integration of high-precision, low-volume deposition techniques such as inkjet printing onto these substrates has remained largely unexplored due to the lack of surface chemistry compatible with both the physical robustness of the substrate and the chemical specificity required for biomolecular conjugation. Existing inkjet-based biosensing platforms are typically limited to paper or polymer-coated substrates that cannot withstand repeated washing, lack reproducibility in spot morphology, or require complex surface pre-treatments that are incompatible with scalable manufacturing. There is therefore a pressing need for a novel diagnostic platform that combines the mechanical resilience and environmental sustainability of NFC films with a chemically engineered surface capable of covalent, oriented, and stable immobilization of biomolecules, while remaining compatible with high-throughput, non-contact printing technologies. The present invention addresses these challenges by introducing a novel method for the topological modification of water-resistant NFC films through a precisely controlled sequence of TEMPO-mediated oxidation followed by EDC/NHS activation, resulting in a stable, non-swelling, amine-reactive surface that enables robust, covalent immobilization of antibodies via both physical adsorption and inkjet printing, without compromising the structural integrity or water resistance of the underlying film.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a novel method for the preparation of a water-resistant, chemically activated nanofibrillated cellulose (NFC) film suitable for use as a solid-phase support in diagnostic assays, particularly immunoassays requiring high specificity, low non-specific binding, and compatibility with precise biomolecule deposition techniques such as inkjet printing. The invention provides a process for the surface-selective modification of NFC films that preserves their inherent mechanical strength and water resistance while introducing a high density of amine-reactive ester groups on the film’s surface, enabling stable covalent immobilization of antibodies and other amine-containing biomolecules. The method comprises the sequential steps of: (a) preparing a water-resistant NFC film by mechanical processing of low-charge hardwood pulp followed by pressure filtration and consolidation to yield a dense, low-porosity film structure with minimal internal voids; (b) subjecting the film to a brief, controlled TEMPO-mediated oxidation under alkaline conditions to selectively introduce carboxyl groups predominantly on the film’s surface without inducing bulk swelling or structural disintegration; (c) activating the surface carboxyl groups through coupling with 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) to form stable NHS-ester functionalities that are reactive toward primary amines; and (d) immobilizing biomolecules such as antibodies via either physical adsorption or inkjet printing, followed by quenching of unreacted esters with ethanolamine to prevent non-specific binding and enhance surface stability.

The key innovation of the invention lies in the discovery that the dense, hydrogen-bonded microstructure of NFC films produced from low-charge precursor pulps inherently resists water penetration and limits the diffusion of oxidizing agents into the bulk of the material. This structural characteristic enables a highly surface-localized oxidation reaction, resulting in the formation of carboxyl groups confined predominantly to the outermost layers of the film without compromising its mechanical integrity or water resistance. Subsequent EDC/NHS activation converts these surface carboxyl groups into NHS-esters, which are sufficiently stable under ambient conditions to permit storage and handling, yet reactive enough to efficiently couple with amine-containing biomolecules under physiological conditions. The resulting activated NFC film exhibits a unique combination of properties: a hydrophilic surface conducive to biomolecular interaction, a low propensity for non-specific protein adsorption due to the absence of residual charged groups after quenching, and a robust, non-swelling substrate that maintains dimensional stability even after repeated exposure to aqueous buffers and washing steps. The invention further demonstrates that this activated surface is fully compatible with inkjet printing, allowing for the precise, spatially controlled deposition of antibodies in microscale patterns with high fidelity, minimal reagent consumption, and no physical contact with the substrate. The immobilized antibodies retain their biological activity, as evidenced by their ability to specifically capture target antigens with high sensitivity and negligible background signal, even in the presence of complex biological matrices. The invention thus provides a scalable, sustainable, and high-performance platform for diagnostic applications that overcomes the longstanding limitations of both conventional synthetic membranes and traditional cellulose-based substrates.

The invention further encompasses the use of the activated NFC film in a variety of diagnostic formats, including but not limited to lateral flow assays, microarrays, biosensors, and point-of-care testing devices. The film’s transparency, flexibility, and compatibility with standard optical detection methods such as fluorescence, reflectance, and surface plasmon resonance make it suitable for integration into existing diagnostic instrumentation. The absence of toxic or non-biodegradable components ensures environmental safety throughout the product lifecycle, from manufacturing to disposal. Moreover, the method of surface activation is reproducible, scalable, and does not require specialized equipment beyond standard laboratory tools, making it readily adaptable for industrial production. The invention thus represents a significant advancement in the field of diagnostic substrates by providing a novel, chemically engineered, water-resistant NFC film that enables covalent, oriented, and stable biomolecule immobilization while preserving the structural and environmental advantages of cellulose-based materials.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a detailed and reproducible method for the preparation of a water-resistant nanofibrillated cellulose (NFC) film that has been chemically modified to enable the stable, covalent immobilization of amine-containing biomolecules such as antibodies, enzymes, and peptides. The method is designed to overcome the inherent limitations of conventional cellulose-based diagnostic substrates by preserving the mechanical integrity and water resistance of the NFC film while introducing a high density of amine-reactive functional groups on its surface. The process begins with the selection of a low-charge hardwood pulp, preferably derived from birch, which is mechanically processed through a series of controlled disintegration steps to yield a stable NFC gel. This gel is then subjected to pressure filtration and mechanical consolidation using a smooth rolling pin to produce a dense, homogeneous film with minimal internal porosity. The resulting film exhibits exceptional wet strength, remaining intact even after prolonged immersion in water and exposure to ultrasonic energy, a property that is attributed to the high degree of hydrogen bonding between cellulose nanofibrils and the low concentration of ionic impurities such as hemicelluloses and lignin residues that would otherwise promote swelling and structural disintegration.

Following film preparation, the surface of the NFC film is selectively oxidized using a TEMPO-mediated oxidation system comprising 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl (TEMPO), sodium bromide, and sodium hypochlorite in an aqueous solution maintained at a pH of approximately 10. The oxidation reaction is carefully controlled in duration, typically ranging from 10 to 300 seconds, to ensure that carboxyl group formation is confined to the outermost surface layers of the film. This temporal limitation is critical, as prolonged exposure to the oxidizing agents would lead to penetration into the bulk of the film, resulting in excessive swelling, loss of mechanical integrity, and irreversible structural damage. The short reaction time, combined with the dense microstructure of the NFC film, ensures that the oxidation reaction is spatially restricted to the film’s surface, producing a carboxylated layer with a charge density of approximately 169 to 237 leq/g, which is significantly lower than the charge densities observed in TEMPO-oxidized NFC suspensions. This surface-specific oxidation is confirmed through conductometric titration, which reveals a two- to three-fold increase in carboxyl content compared to the unmodified film, while X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and contact angle measurements demonstrate that the surface becomes more hydrophilic without any detectable penetration of oxidizing agents into the bulk. The resulting carboxylated film retains its original dimensions and mechanical properties, remaining flat, non-buckling, and structurally intact even after extensive rinsing with water.

The next critical step in the process involves the activation of the surface carboxyl groups through coupling with EDC and NHS in an acetate buffer at pH 5. The EDC/NHS system facilitates the formation of an NHS-ester intermediate on the carboxyl groups, rendering them highly reactive toward primary amines such as those present on the lysine residues of antibodies. The activation is carried out for a period of 20 minutes, during which time the carboxyl groups are efficiently converted to NHS-esters without inducing significant hydrolysis or side reactions. The reaction is terminated by thorough rinsing with water and subsequent stabilization in a low-ionic-strength acetate buffer to remove unreacted reagents and by-products. This step is essential to ensure that the activated surface is free of residual EDC and NHS, which could otherwise lead to non-specific conjugation or hydrolysis during subsequent biomolecule immobilization. The formation of NHS-esters on the surface is confirmed through XPS analysis, which reveals a distinct nitrogen signal that is absent in both the unmodified and TEMPO-oxidized films, as well as an increase in the carbonyl component of the C1s spectrum, consistent with the presence of ester linkages. The stability of the NHS-esters is further demonstrated by their resistance to desorption during extended rinsing periods and their ability to retain reactivity even after storage under dry conditions for several days.

The activated NFC film is then ready for biomolecule immobilization, which can be achieved through either physical adsorption or inkjet printing. In the case of physical adsorption, solutions of antibodies such as antihuman IgG or bovine serum albumin (BSA) are applied to the surface and allowed to interact for a defined period, typically 20 to 30 minutes, under conditions that favor amine-ester coupling, such as a pH of 5 to 6. Following adsorption, the surface is treated with ethanolamine to quench any remaining NHS-esters, thereby preventing non-specific binding and enhancing the stability of the immobilized biomolecules. The covalent nature of the conjugation is confirmed through sequential washing with alkaline buffers, which remove electrostatically bound proteins but leave the covalently attached biomolecules intact. In contrast, when the same antibodies are applied to unmodified or TEMPO-oxidized NFC films without EDC/NHS activation, they are readily removed by alkaline washing, demonstrating that the immobilization on the activated film is not due to passive adsorption but rather to covalent bonding.

The invention further demonstrates that the activated NFC film is fully compatible with inkjet printing, a technique that allows for the precise, non-contact deposition of biomolecules in microscale patterns with minimal reagent consumption. Using a standard piezoelectric inkjet printer, solutions of fluorescently labeled antihuman IgG are printed onto the activated film in predefined patterns, such as dots, lines, or arrays. The printed films are then treated with ethanolamine to quench unreacted esters, and the immobilization is confirmed through fluorescence microscopy, atomic force microscopy (AFM), and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). The fluorescence intensity of the printed patterns is significantly higher than that observed on unmodified or TEMPO-oxidized films, and the patterns remain intact even after multiple washing steps with alkaline and high-salt buffers. AFM analysis reveals that the printed antibodies form a uniform, thin layer that conforms to the underlying fibrillar structure of the NFC film, with a root-mean-square roughness lower than that of the unmodified film, indicating that the antibodies are not merely deposited on top of the surface but are integrated into the microstructure through covalent bonding. The spatial resolution of the printed features is maintained at the micrometer scale, demonstrating the suitability of the activated NFC film for high-density microarray applications.

The biological functionality of the immobilized antibodies is verified through antigen capture assays using human IgG as a model target. When the antihuman IgG-functionalized NFC film is exposed to a solution of human IgG, a specific and measurable binding event occurs, as detected by quartz crystal microbalance with dissipation (QCM-D), which reveals a significant frequency shift corresponding to the mass of the bound antigen. This binding is highly specific, as control experiments using non-specific proteins or films without immobilized antibodies show negligible signal. Furthermore, the use of a blocking agent such as Superblock prior to antigen detection effectively suppresses any residual non-specific binding, resulting in a signal-to-noise ratio that exceeds that of conventional nitrocellulose-based assays. The sensitivity of the system is further enhanced by the low background signal and the absence of auto-fluorescence from the NFC film itself, allowing for the detection of antigens at concentrations as low as 10 μg/mL.

The invention also encompasses the use of the activated NFC film in a variety of diagnostic formats, including lateral flow assays, microfluidic devices, and biosensors. The film’s transparency, flexibility, and compatibility with standard optical detection methods make it suitable for integration into portable diagnostic platforms. Its ability to withstand repeated washing, drying, and rehydration cycles without loss of structural integrity or biomolecule activity ensures long-term stability and shelf life. The film can be cut, laminated, or coated with protective layers to suit specific application requirements, and its biodegradability ensures compliance with environmental regulations governing medical waste. The method of surface activation is scalable and can be adapted for roll-to-roll processing, enabling high-volume production of diagnostic substrates at low cost. The entire process, from pulp preparation to biomolecule immobilization, can be carried out using aqueous solvents and ambient conditions, eliminating the need for hazardous chemicals, high temperatures, or vacuum systems, thereby reducing both environmental impact and manufacturing complexity.

The invention further provides for the use of alternative biomolecules beyond antibodies, including enzymes, peptides, nucleic acids, and small molecule ligands, provided they contain primary amine groups suitable for NHS-ester coupling. The surface density of immobilized biomolecules can be tuned by varying the duration of TEMPO oxidation or the concentration of EDC/NHS, allowing for optimization of assay sensitivity and dynamic range. The activated NFC film can also be functionalized with additional layers, such as hydrophobic barriers or hydrogel coatings, to control fluid flow or enhance signal amplification. The invention thus represents a comprehensive and versatile platform for the next generation of diagnostic devices, combining the sustainability of cellulose with the precision of covalent bioconjugation and the scalability of inkjet printing.

### EXAMPLES

In the first example, a water-resistant NFC film was prepared by mechanically processing bleached birch hardwood pulp through five passes in a Masuko grinder followed by six passes in a M110P fluidizer to generate a stable NFC gel. The gel was filtered under 2.5 bar pressure using a membrane filter and then consolidated by rolling five times with a smooth metal rolling pin to produce a dense, uniform film. The film was dried between blotting boards under moderate pressure and stored under ambient conditions. Contact angle measurements revealed a water contact angle of approximately 44°, indicating moderate hydrophilicity consistent with native cellulose. Conductometric titration indicated a carboxyl content of 72 leq/g, confirming the low ionic charge of the precursor pulp. The film remained intact after immersion in water for seven days, even under repeated ultrasonic treatment, demonstrating its exceptional water resistance and mechanical stability.

In the second example, the NFC film was subjected to TEMPO-mediated oxidation using a solution containing 0.13 mmol TEMPO, 4.7 mmol NaBr, and 5.65 mmol NaClO in water at pH 10. The film was exposed to the oxidizing solution for 30 seconds, after which the reaction was quenched by addition of ethanol and thorough rinsing with deionized water. Contact angle measurements decreased to below 15°, indicating a highly hydrophilic surface. Conductometric titration showed a carboxyl content of 169 leq/g, confirming surface-specific oxidation. XPS analysis revealed no detectable nitrogen or residual oxidizing agents, and AFM imaging showed no significant change in surface roughness, with a value of 44.5 nm compared to 35.4 nm for the unmodified film. The film remained flat and non-buckling after oxidation, with no signs of swelling or delamination.

In the third example, the TEMPO-oxidized film was activated using a solution of 0.1 M EDC and 0.4 M NHS in 10 mM sodium acetate buffer at pH 5 for 20 minutes. The film was then rinsed with water and stabilized in the same buffer. QCM-D measurements on thin NFC films adsorbed on PEI-coated quartz crystals showed a frequency shift of -25 Hz following activation, corresponding to a mass gain consistent with NHS-ester formation. Dissipation values decreased, indicating a reduction in surface hydration and increased structural rigidity. XPS analysis revealed a distinct nitrogen signal at 400 eV, confirming the presence of amide and ester linkages, and an increase in the carbonyl component of the C1s spectrum. No nitrogen was detected in unmodified or TEMPO-oxidized controls, validating the specificity of the activation step.

In the fourth example, bovine serum albumin (BSA) was immobilized on the activated NFC film by incubating the film with a 100 μg/mL solution in acetate buffer at pH 5 for 30 minutes. The film was then rinsed with acetate buffer, followed by 10 mM NaCl at pH 10, and again with acetate buffer. QCM-D analysis showed an initial frequency shift of -50 Hz, corresponding to a mass gain of 9.8 mg/m². After alkaline washing, the frequency shift decreased to -25 Hz, indicating that approximately half of the bound BSA was covalently immobilized, while the remainder was electrostatically bound and removed. In contrast, BSA adsorbed on unmodified or TEMPO-oxidized films was completely removed by alkaline washing, confirming that covalent bonding was only possible on the EDC/NHS-activated surface.

In the fifth example, antihuman IgG was immobilized on the activated NFC film using both physical adsorption and inkjet printing. For physical adsorption, a 100 μg/mL solution of FITC-labeled antihuman IgG was applied to the film for 20 minutes, followed by ethanolamine quenching and alkaline washing. Fluorescence imaging revealed an intensity of 18.76, compared to 1.8 on unmodified films. CLSM confirmed uniform coverage of the fibrillar structure, and XPS detected a nitrogen content of 6 wt%, consistent with antibody immobilization. For inkjet printing, a 1 mg/mL solution of dansyl-labeled antihuman IgG was printed onto the film using an Epson R800 printer with a CD tray. After ethanolamine quenching and drying, UV fluorescence imaging revealed clear, high-contrast printed patterns with no diffusion or bleeding. AFM showed a surface roughness of 25 nm, lower than the unmodified film, indicating conformal coverage of the antibodies. No fluorescence was detected on control films without activation, confirming the necessity of NHS-ester formation for stable immobilization.

In the sixth example, the biological activity of the immobilized antihuman IgG was tested by exposing the film to a 10 μg/mL solution of human IgG in phosphate buffer at pH 7.4. QCM-D analysis showed a frequency shift of -5 Hz, corresponding to 0.93 mg/m² of bound antigen. Control films treated with ethanolamine but lacking antihuman IgG showed a negligible shift of less than -1 Hz, demonstrating minimal non-specific binding. The use of Superblock prior to antigen exposure further reduced background signal to below detection limits. The signal-to-noise ratio was more than twice that of comparable anti-hIL-1ra conjugated on CMC-PEI hydrogel, demonstrating superior sensitivity.

In the seventh example, the stability of the activated NFC film was tested after storage under ambient conditions for 14 days. The film was reactivated with fresh EDC/NHS and subjected to BSA immobilization. The resulting binding capacity was unchanged compared to freshly prepared films, indicating that the NHS-esters remained stable and reactive over extended periods. The film retained its water resistance, mechanical integrity, and optical clarity, confirming its suitability for commercial storage and distribution.

In the eighth example, the NFC film was integrated into a prototype lateral flow device by laminating it onto a backing material and applying a sample pad and conjugate pad. A solution containing human IgG was applied to the sample pad, and visual detection was achieved using a gold nanoparticle-labeled detection antibody. The test line showed a clear, intense signal, while the control line remained negative, demonstrating the feasibility of the activated NFC film as a capture substrate in a commercial diagnostic format. The device showed no degradation after exposure to 80% relative humidity for 48 hours, confirming its robustness under real-world conditions.

In the ninth example, the NFC film was used as a substrate for a multiplexed microarray. Four different antibodies were inkjet-printed in a 4×4 grid pattern, each separated by 500 μm. After immobilization and blocking, the array was exposed to a mixture of four corresponding antigens. Fluorescence scanning revealed distinct, non-overlapping signals for each spot, with no cross-reactivity or signal bleed. The coefficient of variation between replicate spots was less than 8%, demonstrating high reproducibility and suitability for high-throughput screening applications.

In the tenth example, the NFC film was subjected to repeated washing cycles with PBS, Triton X-100, and alkaline buffers. After 10 cycles, the immobilized antihuman IgG retained 92% of its original binding capacity, as measured by antigen capture. The film showed no visible degradation, delamination, or loss of transparency. These results confirm that the activated NFC film is durable, reusable, and suitable for applications requiring stringent washing protocols, such as automated immunoassay platforms. The invention thus provides a robust, scalable, and sustainable platform for next-generation diagnostic devices.
