# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of semiconductor nanocrystals, specifically to heavily doped semiconductor quantum dots with precisely controlled electronic and optical properties through the incorporation of transition metal impurities. More particularly, the invention concerns a method for the synthesis of indium arsenide nanocrystals doped with copper, silver, or gold atoms in controlled stoichiometric ratios, resulting in tunable bandgap energies, modified photoluminescence characteristics, and altered charge carrier dynamics without the formation of secondary phases or surface metal clusters. The invention further encompasses the structural, electronic, and optical characterization of such doped nanocrystals, demonstrating that the nature of the dopant—its ionic radius, preferred lattice site, valence state, and diffusion kinetics—dictates whether the resulting material exhibits n-type, p-type, or isovalent behavior, thereby enabling the rational design of quantum dots with tailored optoelectronic responses for use in photodetectors, light-emitting diodes, solar cells, and quantum information devices.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Semiconductor nanocrystals, or quantum dots, have long been recognized for their size-tunable optical properties arising from quantum confinement effects. These materials have found widespread application in displays, biological imaging, photovoltaics, and single-photon sources due to their high quantum yields, narrow emission linewidths, and solution processability. However, the ability to control the intrinsic electronic structure of these nanocrystals beyond mere size and shape has remained a significant challenge. Traditional approaches to modifying the electronic properties of quantum dots have relied on alloying with other semiconductors, surface ligand engineering, or core-shell architectures. While effective in certain contexts, these methods often introduce interfacial strain, compositional inhomogeneity, or parasitic recombination pathways that degrade performance.

Doping of semiconductor nanocrystals with foreign atoms has been proposed as a more direct route to modulate carrier concentration and Fermi level position. However, the feasibility of intentional doping in nanoscale systems has been widely debated due to the high surface-to-volume ratio, limited lattice volume, and strong Coulomb interactions that dominate at the nanoscale. In bulk semiconductors, doping is a well-established technique, where substitutional or interstitial impurities introduce free carriers by donating or accepting electrons. In nanocrystals, however, the same dopants may behave unpredictably: they may segregate to the surface, form metallic clusters, remain inert due to kinetic trapping, or induce lattice distortions that alter optical transitions without changing carrier density.

Previous attempts to dope III-V quantum dots with transition metals have yielded inconsistent results. Some reports have claimed successful n-type doping with elements such as zinc or manganese, but these often suffer from poor reproducibility, low dopant incorporation efficiency, or the formation of secondary phases that obscure the true electronic effects. Other studies have observed spectral shifts attributed to doping, but without definitive evidence of charge transfer or carrier modulation, leaving open the possibility that observed changes arise from surface oxidation, ligand exchange, or structural disorder rather than true electronic doping. Moreover, the lack of a unified theoretical framework capable of explaining the behavior of multiple dopants confined within a nanocrystal has hindered the development of predictive design rules.

In particular, the doping of indium arsenide nanocrystals with copper, silver, or gold has not been previously demonstrated in a controlled, reproducible, and well-characterized manner. While these metals are known dopants in bulk III-V materials, their behavior in nanocrystalline form remains largely unexplored. Copper, for instance, is known in bulk InAs to act as an interstitial donor, while silver and gold are typically substitutional acceptors or isovalent impurities. However, whether these roles persist in nanocrystals of 2–5 nm diameter, where quantum confinement and surface effects dominate, has not been established. Furthermore, the question of whether dopant atoms can penetrate the nanocrystal lattice before surface passivation occurs, whether they occupy substitutional or interstitial sites, and how their presence affects the density of states near the band edges, has remained unanswered.

Prior art has also failed to provide a method for quantifying the number of dopant atoms per nanocrystal with sufficient precision to correlate doping level with optical response. Techniques such as inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy have been used in bulk materials but are rarely applied to ensembles of nanocrystals due to the low mass of material and the difficulty of complete dissolution without altering the chemical state of the dopants. Similarly, while transmission electron microscopy and X-ray diffraction have been employed to assess structural integrity, they lack the sensitivity to detect atomic-scale distortions or dopant location, especially when dopant concentrations are below the detection limit of conventional imaging.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy has been used to probe surface chemistry but has not been systematically applied to determine the oxidation state and local electronic environment of dopants embedded within the nanocrystal lattice. Scanning tunneling microscopy and spectroscopy, though capable of resolving individual nanocrystals and their electronic states, have not been combined with controlled doping protocols to establish a direct link between dopant identity, concentration, and energy level shifts in a statistically significant manner.

Moreover, theoretical models of doping in quantum dots have been largely confined to single-impurity hydrogenic approximations, which fail to capture the complex many-body interactions that arise when multiple dopants are present within a nanocrystal. These models predict only red shifts in absorption and emission spectra due to the introduction of donor or acceptor levels within the bandgap. However, experimental observations in some systems have shown blue shifts, which cannot be explained by such simple models. No prior theoretical framework has accounted for the interplay between dopant concentration, spatial distribution, Coulomb repulsion, and quantum confinement to predict both red and blue shifts in the same material system under varying doping conditions.

There is therefore a critical need in the art for a method of doping semiconductor nanocrystals that enables precise control over dopant type, concentration, and location; a method that ensures dopant incorporation into the lattice without phase segregation; a method that allows quantitative measurement of dopant content per nanocrystal; and a theoretical model that explains the counterintuitive optical shifts observed upon doping. The present invention fulfills these needs by introducing a novel, reproducible, and scalable synthetic protocol for the doping of indium arsenide nanocrystals with copper, silver, and gold, coupled with comprehensive characterization techniques that unambiguously demonstrate the nature and consequences of doping at the single-nanocrystal level.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for the synthesis of heavily doped indium arsenide nanocrystals with controlled incorporation of copper, silver, or gold atoms into the crystal lattice, resulting in tunable electronic and optical properties that depend critically on the identity and concentration of the dopant. The method comprises the preparation of indium arsenide nanocrystals via the thermal decomposition of tris(trimethylsilyl)arsine and indium chloride in trioctylphosphine at elevated temperatures, followed by the controlled addition of a solution containing a transition metal salt, a cationic surfactant, and a long-chain amine to the nanocrystal suspension under inert conditions. The transition metal salt is selected from the group consisting of copper(II) chloride, silver nitrate, silver chloride, or gold(III) chloride, and is added in a molar ratio relative to the nanocrystals that allows for the incorporation of between 0.1 and 15 metal atoms per nanocrystal without the formation of secondary phases or surface precipitates.

The resulting doped nanocrystals exhibit a shift in the first exciton absorption peak that is dependent on the identity of the dopant: copper doping induces a blue shift of up to 150 meV, silver doping induces a red shift of up to 120 meV, and gold doping results in negligible shift, preserving the intrinsic bandgap of the undoped nanocrystal. Photoluminescence measurements reveal that all dopants cause partial quenching of emission, with complete quenching occurring upon precipitation, indicating that the dopants introduce non-radiative recombination pathways without forming emissive defect states. The absence of metal-rich domains in transmission electron microscopy and the preservation of the zinc blende crystal structure in X-ray diffraction confirm that the dopants are incorporated into the lattice rather than segregated at the surface.

Quantitative analysis by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy demonstrates that the number of dopant atoms per nanocrystal is directly proportional to the molar ratio of dopant precursor to nanocrystals in solution, with a linear relationship observed over a wide range of concentrations. The dopant concentration is further corroborated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, which identifies the presence of copper, silver, and gold in the core of the nanocrystals through the appearance of distinct 2p, 3d, and 4f core-level peaks, respectively, with binding energies consistent with metallic or +1 oxidation states. The absence of higher oxidation states such as Cu²⁺ or Ag⁺ in the majority of the signal suggests that the dopants are in a reduced state, consistent with electron transfer from the nanocrystal lattice.

Theoretical modeling based on a pseudo-crystal approach, in which dopant atoms are treated as deep, spatially distributed impurities with hydrogenic wavefunctions and strong local interactions, successfully reproduces the experimentally observed blue shift for copper doping and red shift for silver doping. This model accounts for the formation of impurity bands that hybridize with the conduction or valence band depending on dopant concentration and spatial distribution, and predicts that at low concentrations, donor-like behavior leads to red shifts, while at higher concentrations, filling of the ground state leads to blue shifts in absorption, a phenomenon not explainable by single-impurity models. The model further predicts that gold, due to its isovalent nature and similar ionic radius to indium, introduces no net charge and causes no significant lattice distortion, consistent with the observed spectral invariance.

Scanning tunneling spectroscopy of individual doped nanocrystals confirms that the conduction band edge shifts to lower energies upon copper doping, to higher energies upon silver doping, and remains unchanged upon gold doping, with shifts that are significantly larger than the inherent variations between individual nanocrystals of the same nominal size. The magnitude of these shifts correlates with the number of dopant atoms per nanocrystal as determined by ICP-AES, establishing a direct, quantitative link between dopant content and electronic structure. The lattice parameter remains essentially unchanged for all dopants, as confirmed by X-ray diffraction line broadening analysis and the absence of peak shifts, indicating that the observed optical shifts are not due to strain-induced bandgap changes but to electronic doping effects.

The invention further provides that the dopant atoms diffuse into the nanocrystal lattice within minutes of exposure, as predicted by calculated diffusion coefficients that exceed the nanocrystal dimensions by orders of magnitude, enabling rapid and uniform doping even at room temperature. The use of didodecyldimethylammonium bromide and dodecylamine as surfactants ensures solubility of the metal precursors in toluene and facilitates their interaction with the nanocrystal surface without inducing aggregation or etching. The entire doping process is conducted under inert atmosphere to prevent oxidation, and precipitation is carried out using methanol for copper and gold-doped nanocrystals and acetone for silver-doped nanocrystals to preserve the integrity of the dopant-lattice interaction.

The resulting doped nanocrystals are stable in solution for extended periods, exhibit no evidence of dopant leaching, and retain their optical properties upon drying and redispersion. The invention enables the fabrication of quantum dots with precisely engineered Fermi levels, carrier concentrations, and optical transitions, opening new avenues for the design of quantum dot-based devices with tailored charge transport, light emission, and sensing properties. The method is scalable, reproducible, and compatible with solution-based processing, making it suitable for integration into industrial optoelectronic manufacturing processes.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

The present invention is directed to a method for the synthesis of heavily doped indium arsenide nanocrystals with controlled incorporation of transition metal dopants, specifically copper, silver, or gold, resulting in predictable and tunable modifications to the electronic structure and optical properties of the nanocrystals. The method comprises three distinct but interrelated stages: the synthesis of undoped indium arsenide nanocrystals, the controlled doping of these nanocrystals with transition metal atoms, and the comprehensive characterization of the resulting doped nanocrystals to confirm dopant incorporation, quantify dopant concentration, and correlate doping with optical and electronic behavior.

The synthesis of the undoped indium arsenide nanocrystals is initiated under an inert atmosphere using standard Schlenk-line techniques to exclude moisture and oxygen, which could lead to oxidation of the precursors or degradation of the nanocrystal surface. A solution of indium(III) chloride is prepared in trioctylphosphine at a concentration of 1.4 molar, yielding a clear, colorless solution. Tris(trimethylsilyl)arsine, a highly reactive arsenic precursor, is then added in a stoichiometric ratio corresponding to a 2:1 molar ratio of indium to arsenic, ensuring complete conversion to indium arsenide without excess of either component. The mixture is then heated to 300 degrees Celsius under vigorous stirring, at which point a rapid nucleation event occurs, resulting in the formation of small, monodisperse nuclei. The temperature is then reduced to 260 degrees Celsius, and additional precursor solution is injected in controlled aliquots to promote growth rather than new nucleation. The growth process is monitored in real time by withdrawing small aliquots of the reaction mixture and measuring their absorption spectra using a UV-visible-near infrared spectrometer. The first exciton peak, corresponding to the fundamental electron-hole transition, is tracked as a function of time, and growth is halted when the peak reaches the desired energy, which corresponds to a nanocrystal diameter between 2.5 and 4.5 nanometers. The reaction is then quenched by cooling to room temperature, and the nanocrystals are transferred into a glovebox maintained at less than 1 part per million of oxygen and moisture.

To improve the size distribution and remove small, unstable nuclei, a size-selective precipitation procedure is employed. Anhydrous toluene is added to the reaction mixture to dilute the solvent, followed by the slow addition of anhydrous methanol, which acts as a non-solvent. The nanocrystals precipitate out of solution, while smaller, less stable particles remain dissolved. The precipitate is collected by centrifugation, redispersed in toluene, and the precipitation step is repeated to achieve a size distribution with a full width at half maximum of less than 10 percent. The resulting nanocrystals are characterized by transmission electron microscopy, which confirms their spherical morphology and narrow size distribution, and by X-ray diffraction, which verifies the zinc blende crystal structure with no evidence of secondary phases.

The doping procedure is carried out immediately after the size selection step, while the nanocrystals are still in solution and before any surface passivation or ligand exchange can occur. A metal precursor solution is prepared by dissolving a transition metal salt—copper(II) chloride, silver nitrate, silver chloride, or gold(III) chloride—in toluene along with didodecyldimethylammonium bromide and dodecylamine. The surfactants serve a dual purpose: they solubilize the metal salt in the non-polar toluene medium and facilitate the interaction between the metal ions and the nanocrystal surface by partially displacing the native trioctylphosphine ligands without inducing aggregation. The concentration of the metal salt is varied to achieve a range of metal-to-nanocrystal ratios from 0.01 to 2.0, corresponding to an estimated 0.1 to 15 metal atoms per nanocrystal. The solution is prepared fresh immediately prior to use and is added dropwise to a stirred solution of the indium arsenide nanocrystals in toluene. The addition is performed slowly over a period of several minutes to ensure homogeneous mixing and to avoid local supersaturation that could lead to heterogeneous nucleation.

Upon addition of the metal solution, the nanocrystal suspension undergoes a visible color change, indicating an interaction between the metal ions and the nanocrystal surface. The mixture is then stirred for a fixed period of 15 minutes at room temperature, during which time the metal ions diffuse into the nanocrystal lattice. This diffusion is rapid, occurring on a timescale of seconds to minutes, as predicted by calculated diffusion coefficients that exceed the nanocrystal diameter by more than two orders of magnitude. The diffusion is driven by the high chemical potential of the metal ions at the surface and the thermodynamic favorability of incorporation into substitutional or interstitial sites within the lattice, as determined by the ionic radius and valence state of the dopant.

Following the doping period, the nanocrystals are precipitated by the addition of either methanol or acetone, depending on the dopant. For copper and gold-doped nanocrystals, methanol is used as the anti-solvent, while for silver-doped nanocrystals, acetone is employed. This differential precipitation protocol is critical to preserving the integrity of the dopant-lattice interaction, as acetone is less polar and less likely to induce surface oxidation or ligand displacement in silver-doped samples. The precipitated nanocrystals are collected by centrifugation, washed multiple times with toluene and the respective anti-solvent, and then redispersed in toluene for characterization.

The resulting doped nanocrystals are subjected to a comprehensive suite of analytical techniques to confirm dopant incorporation, quantify concentration, and determine the nature of the electronic modifications. UV-visible-near infrared spectroscopy reveals that copper doping induces a systematic blue shift in the first exciton absorption peak, with the magnitude of the shift increasing with the metal-to-nanocrystal ratio. For nanocrystals with a diameter of approximately 3.6 nanometers, a blue shift of up to 150 millielectronvolts is observed at the highest doping levels. In contrast, silver doping results in a red shift of up to 120 millielectronvolts, with the magnitude again increasing with dopant concentration. Gold doping, however, produces no measurable shift in the absorption edge, indicating that gold atoms do not alter the bandgap energy of the nanocrystal. Photoluminescence measurements, performed using a 633-nanometer helium-neon laser as the excitation source, show that all dopants cause a progressive quenching of the emission intensity, with complete quenching occurring at the highest doping levels. This quenching is attributed to the introduction of non-radiative recombination centers, likely arising from the localized states associated with the dopant ions, rather than to the formation of emissive defect complexes.

Transmission electron microscopy, performed at 120 kilovolts, shows no evidence of metal-rich domains, secondary phases, or surface precipitates in any of the doped samples. The nanocrystals retain their spherical morphology and uniform size distribution, confirming that the dopants are incorporated into the lattice rather than aggregating on the surface. Powder X-ray diffraction patterns, obtained using copper K-alpha radiation, show that the main reflections corresponding to the zinc blende structure of indium arsenide are preserved in all doped samples. The peak positions remain unchanged within the resolution of the instrument, indicating that the lattice parameter is not significantly altered by the presence of the dopants. However, a slight broadening of the higher-angle peaks is observed in the copper-doped samples, consistent with the introduction of local strain or disorder, while the silver-doped samples exhibit a broader tailing around 2theta equal to 30 degrees, suggesting a more extended distortion of the lattice. The absence of any new diffraction peaks confirms that no crystalline metal phases such as Cu, Ag, or Au are formed.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy is employed to determine the chemical state and location of the dopant atoms within the nanocrystals. Survey spectra of the doped samples show the presence of In 3s, 3p, 3d, and MNN transitions, As 3d and 3p transitions, and, critically, the appearance of distinct peaks corresponding to the 2p orbitals of copper, the 3d orbitals of silver, and the 4f orbitals of gold. These peaks are absent in the spectra of undoped nanocrystals, confirming that the dopants are incorporated into the nanocrystals and not merely adsorbed on the surface. High-resolution scans of the In 3d region reveal that the binding energy of the main peak remains unchanged for silver and gold doping, but is broadened and slightly shifted in the copper-doped samples, with the appearance of a secondary peak at 444.8 electronvolts and 452.3 electronvolts, which is attributed to a minor surface oxide layer formed during air exposure. The As 3d peak similarly shows a secondary component at 44.2 electronvolts only in the copper-doped samples, consistent with the formation of arsenic oxide. The presence of these oxides is attributed to brief air exposure during sample loading and is not indicative of bulk oxidation, as the same exposure time is applied to all samples and only the copper-doped ones show these features. The Ag 3d peaks appear at 368.2 and 374.2 electronvolts, matching the binding energies reported for both metallic silver and silver(I), suggesting that the silver atoms are in a reduced state. The Au 4f peaks are observed at 83.9 and 87.5 electronvolts, consistent with metallic gold, indicating that gold is incorporated in its zero-valent state.

Inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy is used to quantify the number of dopant atoms per nanocrystal. The doped nanocrystals are dissolved in concentrated nitric acid, and the resulting solution is diluted with triply distilled water to ensure complete ionization of all elements. The concentrations of indium, arsenic, and the dopant metal are measured by monitoring their characteristic emission lines at 230.6 and 325.6 nanometers for indium, 189.0 and 193.7 nanometers for arsenic, and 324.8, 328.1, and 208.2 nanometers for copper, silver, and gold, respectively. The number of dopant atoms per nanocrystal is then calculated by taking the ratio of dopant to indium or arsenic atoms and multiplying by the estimated number of indium and arsenic atoms per nanocrystal, which is determined from the nanocrystal diameter using a cubic lattice model. The results show a linear relationship between the metal-to-nanocrystal solution ratio and the number of dopant atoms per nanocrystal, with a correlation coefficient greater than 0.98 for all dopants. For example, at a metal-to-nanocrystal ratio of 0.5, approximately 5 copper atoms, 4 silver atoms, and 3 gold atoms are incorporated per nanocrystal. The precision of this method allows for the determination of dopant concentrations with an uncertainty of less than 10 percent, enabling the direct correlation of doping level with optical shift.

Scanning tunneling microscopy and spectroscopy are performed on individual nanocrystals deposited on a flat gold(111) substrate. The samples are mounted in a cryogenic scanning tunneling microscope cooled to 4.2 kelvins and measured under ultra-high vacuum to minimize contamination. Spatially resolved tunneling spectra, or dI/dV curves, are acquired by positioning the tip above single nanocrystals and measuring the differential conductance as a function of bias voltage. The resulting spectra reveal the discrete energy levels of the nanocrystals, with clear conduction and valence band edges and a gap between them. For undoped nanocrystals, the gap is centered at approximately 0.8 electronvolts. Upon copper doping, the conduction band edge shifts to higher energies, corresponding to a reduction in the bandgap, while the valence band edge remains relatively unchanged. This results in a net blue shift of the absorption edge, consistent with the optical measurements. In contrast, silver doping causes the valence band edge to shift to lower energies, increasing the bandgap and resulting in a red shift. Gold doping produces no measurable shift in either band edge, confirming its isovalent character. The magnitude of these shifts is significantly larger than the natural variation between individual nanocrystals of the same size, confirming that the observed effects are due to doping and not to size dispersion. The shifts are also found to be proportional to the number of dopant atoms per nanocrystal, as determined by ICP-AES, establishing a direct quantitative link between dopant concentration and electronic structure.

Theoretical modeling of the doped nanocrystals is performed using a pseudo-crystal model that treats the dopant atoms as deep, spatially distributed impurities with hydrogenic wavefunctions. In this model, each dopant atom contributes a localized energy level with a binding energy ε, and the overlap between neighboring impurity states is described by a hopping integral γ and a characteristic length scale a. The Hamiltonian is diagonalized to obtain the energy spectrum of the doped nanocrystal, and the Fermi level is populated according to the Pauli exclusion principle. The model predicts that for low dopant concentrations, the impurity levels lie below the conduction band edge for donors and above the valence band edge for acceptors, resulting in red shifts in both absorption and photoluminescence. However, at higher concentrations, the impurity levels broaden into a band, and the filling of the ground state leads to a blue shift in the absorption spectrum, while the photoluminescence continues to red shift due to the occupation of higher-energy states. This model successfully reproduces the experimentally observed blue shift for copper doping and red shift for silver doping, while predicting no shift for gold doping due to its isovalent nature. The model further predicts that the degree of localization of the dopant states is high, with γ/ε on the order of 0.1, indicating that the dopants are deep and do not form a fully delocalized band, consistent with the observed quenching of photoluminescence.

## EXPERIMENTAL

The experimental procedures described herein were carried out under strictly controlled conditions to ensure reproducibility, purity, and accuracy of results. All chemicals were used as received from Sigma-Aldrich unless otherwise specified. Indium(III) chloride (InCl₃) with a purity of 99.999+ percent was stored in a desiccator under argon prior to use. Tris(trimethylsilyl)arsine (TMS₃As), synthesized according to published literature, was handled exclusively within a nitrogen-purged glovebox with oxygen and moisture levels maintained below 1 ppm. Trioctylphosphine (TOP) was purified by vacuum distillation at 150 degrees Celsius under argon flow and stored in a sealed glass vial within the glovebox. Gold(III) chloride (AuCl₃), silver nitrate (AgNO₃), silver chloride (AgCl), and copper(II) chloride (CuCl₂) were all of 99 percent purity or higher and were stored in amber glass vials under argon. Dodecylamine (DDA) and didodecyldimethylammonium bromide (DDAB) were used without further purification. Toluene and methanol were obtained in anhydrous form with a purity of 99.8 percent and were dried over molecular sieves prior to use. All glassware was baked at 120 degrees Celsius for 12 hours under vacuum prior to use to remove adsorbed water and organic contaminants.

The synthesis of undoped indium arsenide nanocrystals was performed in a three-necked round-bottom flask equipped with a magnetic stirrer, a reflux condenser, and a septum for inert gas inlet. The flask was connected to a Schlenk line and purged with argon for 30 minutes. A solution of 1.7 grams of a 1.4 molar InCl₃ solution in TOP was prepared by dissolving 0.3 grams of InCl₃ in 1.2 milliliters of purified TOP. This solution was transferred into the flask and heated to 300 degrees Celsius under vigorous stirring. Once the temperature was stabilized, 0.3 grams of TMS₃As, corresponding to 1 millimole, was injected into the hot solution through a syringe fitted with a needle. The solution immediately turned dark brown, indicating rapid nucleation. After 30 seconds, the temperature was lowered to 260 degrees Celsius, and additional precursor solution was injected in 0.1-milliliter aliquots every 30 seconds until the first exciton absorption peak reached the desired energy, as monitored by UV-visible spectroscopy. The reaction was then quenched by removing the flask from the heating block and allowing it to cool to room temperature under argon. The nanocrystals were precipitated by adding 5 milliliters of anhydrous methanol to 2 milliliters of the reaction solution, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 revolutions per minute for 5 minutes. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was redispersed in 2 milliliters of anhydrous toluene. This precipitation-redispersion cycle was repeated three times to narrow the size distribution. The final nanocrystal solution was stored under argon at 4 degrees Celsius.

For the doping procedure, a metal precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 10 milligrams of the chosen metal salt—CuCl₂, AgNO₃, AgCl, or AuCl₃—in 10 milliliters of anhydrous toluene. To this solution, 80 milligrams of DDAB and 120 milligrams of DDA were added, and the mixture was stirred for 1 hour at room temperature to ensure complete dissolution. The resulting solution was then transferred to a syringe and added dropwise over a period of 5 minutes to 2 milliliters of the undoped InAs nanocrystal solution, which was stirred vigorously under argon. After 15 minutes of stirring, the solution was subjected to precipitation using either methanol (for Cu and Au samples) or acetone (for Ag samples). The precipitated nanocrystals were collected by centrifugation, washed three times with toluene and the respective anti-solvent, and then redispersed in 1 milliliter of toluene. The concentration of the nanocrystals was determined by absorbance measurements at the first exciton peak using a known extinction coefficient for undoped InAs nanocrystals.

For UV-visible-near infrared spectroscopy, a JASCO V-570 spectrometer was used with quartz cuvettes of 1-centimeter path length. The samples were measured in toluene at room temperature, and the first exciton peak was identified as the first maximum in the absorption spectrum. Photoluminescence measurements were performed using an Ocean Optics NIR-512 spectrophotometer with a 633-nanometer HeNe laser as the excitation source. The excitation power was kept below 1 milliwatt to avoid photobleaching. The emission spectra were recorded with an integration time of 10 seconds and averaged over five acquisitions. Transmission electron microscopy was performed on a Tecnai T12 G2 Spirit operating at 120 kilovolts. Samples were prepared by depositing one drop of the nanocrystal solution onto a carbon-coated copper grid, allowing it to dry under argon, and then washing the grid with methanol for Cu and Au samples or acetone for Ag samples. The grids were then loaded into the microscope under vacuum. Powder X-ray diffraction patterns were obtained using a Phillips PW1830/40 diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å) operated at 40 kilovolts and 30 milliamperes. Nanocrystal films were prepared by drop-casting the solution onto a low-background quartz substrate and allowing it to dry under argon. Background subtraction was performed using a blank quartz substrate as reference. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy was performed using a Kratos Analytical Axis Ultra XPS instrument with Al Kα radiation (1486.6 electronvolts). Nanocrystal films were deposited on silicon wafers by drop-casting and drying under argon. Survey spectra were collected with a pass energy of 160 electronvolts, and high-resolution scans were performed with a pass energy of 20 electronvolts. All binding energies were referenced to the C 1s peak at 284.8 electronvolts. Inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy was performed using a Perkin-Elmer Optima 3000 instrument. Doped nanocrystals were dissolved in 1 milliliter of concentrated nitric acid at 80 degrees Celsius for 2 hours, then diluted with triply distilled water to a final volume of 10 milliliters. The concentrations of In, As, Cu, Ag, and Au were determined from their respective emission lines: In at 230.6 and 325.6 nanometers, As at 189.0 and 193.7 nanometers, Cu at 324.8 nanometers, Ag at 328.1 nanometers, and Au at 208.2 nanometers. Scanning tunneling microscopy and spectroscopy were performed using a homemade cryogenic STM operating at 4.2 kelvins. Nanocrystals were deposited on a freshly cleaved Au(111) substrate by slow evaporation of a toluene solution under argon. The samples were transferred to the STM chamber within 15 minutes of exposure to air. Tunneling spectra were acquired by positioning the tip above individual nanocrystals and measuring dI/dV as a function of bias voltage using a lock-in amplifier with a modulation frequency of 791 hertz and amplitude of 10 millivolts. The tip was retracted to minimize voltage division, and spectra were acquired at multiple locations on each nanocrystal to ensure statistical validity.

All measurements were performed in triplicate, and the data were averaged with error bars representing the standard deviation. The number of dopant atoms per nanocrystal was calculated from the ICP-AES data using the formula: N_dopant = (C_dopant / C_In) × N_In, where C_dopant and C_In are the concentrations of dopant and indium measured by ICP-AES, and N_In is the number of indium atoms per nanocrystal, estimated from the nanocrystal diameter assuming a cubic zinc blende lattice with a lattice constant of 6.058 angstroms. The estimated number of atoms per nanocrystal ranged from 100 for 2.5-nanometer particles to 1,000 for 4.5-nanometer particles. The calculated dopant concentrations were cross-validated with the optical shifts observed in absorption and photoluminescence, and the agreement between the two methods was within 10 percent for all samples. The absence of metal clusters in TEM and the preservation of the InAs XRD pattern confirmed that the dopants were incorporated into the lattice and not segregated. The linear correlation between dopant concentration and optical shift, the consistency of XPS binding energies, and the reproducibility across multiple synthesis batches confirm the robustness and reliability of the method. The invention, therefore, provides a fully characterized, reproducible, and scalable method for the synthesis of heavily doped semiconductor nanocrystals with unprecedented control over their electronic and optical properties.
