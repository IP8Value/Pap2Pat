# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to systems and methods for self-supervised learning of visual representations from video data, particularly those leveraging multiple complementary modalities or views of the same temporal sequence to improve the semantic quality and generalizability of learned embeddings. More specifically, the invention concerns a cooperative contrastive learning framework that enforces consistency in pairwise similarity relationships across distinct visual views—such as RGB imagery, optical flow, pose heatmaps, and segmentation masks—to guide the joint optimization of view-specific encoders without requiring labeled data. The disclosed method operates by dynamically aligning the geometric structure of inter-instance relationships across views, thereby encouraging the emergence of high-level semantic representations that are invariant to view-specific noise, domain discrepancies, and low-level visual artifacts. This approach is particularly suited for video understanding tasks such as action recognition, video retrieval, and temporal alignment, where traditional contrastive learning methods suffer from an inherent bias toward discriminating between different video clips rather than recognizing semantically equivalent actions across distinct recordings. The invention is applicable to any video dataset containing multiple derivable or observable views, whether generated through physical sensors, computational models, or hybrid systems, and is designed to be integrated with existing self-supervised architectures without requiring architectural overhauls or additional annotation.

## DESCRIPTION OF RELATED ART

Prior approaches to self-supervised visual representation learning have predominantly relied on contrastive learning paradigms that construct positive and negative sample pairs based on structural proximity within a single modality. In the context of video data, these methods typically exploit temporal continuity by treating neighboring frames or spatiotemporal patches from the same video clip as positive examples, while treating samples from unrelated clips as negative instances. This strategy, while effective in capturing low-level motion patterns and local coherence, inadvertently penalizes semantically identical actions that occur in different videos. For instance, two individuals performing identical squats in separate recordings—despite sharing identical kinematic structure, timing, and intent—are treated as dissimilar due to differences in background, lighting, camera angle, or clothing. This limitation leads to representations that are overly sensitive to superficial variations and fail to generalize to unseen instances of the same action, thereby constraining their utility in downstream classification, retrieval, or segmentation tasks. Recent advancements have sought to mitigate this issue through multi-view learning, wherein multiple complementary representations of the same video—such as RGB frames, optical flow, and human pose estimations—are processed by separate encoders and jointly optimized. These methods typically maximize mutual information between corresponding features across views, assuming that shared content across modalities reflects higher-order semantics. However, such approaches still rely on intra-video positive pairs and inter-video negative pairs derived from structural constraints, thereby inheriting the same fundamental flaw: the inability to recognize that two distinct videos may contain the same semantic event. Consequently, while these methods improve representation quality, they do not fundamentally alter the contrastive objective’s bias toward distinguishing video sources rather than recognizing action semantics. Other efforts have explored multi-modal fusion through concatenation, attention mechanisms, or cross-modal reconstruction, but these techniques often require aligned or synchronized inputs, are computationally expensive, or depend on external supervision to establish correspondence. Furthermore, the use of noisy or imperfect views—such as pose heatmaps or segmentation masks generated by off-the-shelf detectors—has been largely avoided due to concerns about error propagation and representation collapse. Existing frameworks either discard such views as unreliable or treat them as auxiliary inputs without leveraging their potential to provide alternative perspectives on the underlying action. As a result, the rich, albeit imperfect, information contained in these derived modalities remains underutilized, and the opportunity to use them as independent sources of semantic guidance remains unexplored. Moreover, no prior method has explicitly enforced consistency in the relative distances between instances across multiple views. While some approaches align feature spaces through projection or correlation, none have formulated the problem as a graph regularization task where the similarity matrix—defined by pairwise distances between all instances in a batch—is synchronized across views to ensure that if two instances are close in one view, they must also be close in all others. This absence of inter-view relational consistency allows each view to develop its own idiosyncratic notion of similarity, leading to fragmented representations that lack cross-modal semantic coherence. The invention addresses these shortcomings by introducing a novel cooperative contrastive learning mechanism that actively encourages all views to agree on which instances are similar and which are dissimilar, thereby transforming the learning process from a collection of independent contrastive objectives into a unified, mutually reinforcing system where each view serves as both a learner and a teacher to the others.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present invention discloses a cooperative contrastive learning framework that improves the quality of self-supervised visual representations by enforcing consistency in pairwise similarity relationships across multiple complementary views of video data. The framework operates by simultaneously training multiple view-specific encoders, each responsible for mapping a distinct modality—such as RGB frames, optical flow, pose heatmaps, or segmentation masks—into a shared embedding space. Unlike prior methods that maximize mutual information between views or align features through direct reconstruction, the invention introduces a synchronization loss that compares the similarity matrices computed independently across each view. Specifically, for every pair of instances in a training batch, the cosine distance between their embeddings is computed in each view, and the resulting distance matrices are compared across all views using a pairwise difference metric. This comparison penalizes discrepancies in the relative ordering of similarities, thereby encouraging all views to agree on which instance pairs are similar and which are dissimilar. To prevent collapse—where all distances converge to a single value—the framework further incorporates a hinge-based similarity loss that explicitly enforces a margin between positive and negative pairs within each view, ensuring that the learned distances remain discriminative. The combined loss function, composed of the synchronization term and the hinge similarity term, is optimized jointly across all encoders, resulting in a feedback loop where improvements in one view refine the similarity structure perceived by the others, which in turn guide further refinement. This cooperative mechanism enables the system to leverage even noisy or imperfect views—such as those generated by off-the-shelf detectors—as valuable sources of semantic signal, as their inconsistencies with other views are systematically corrected through the synchronization process. The resulting embeddings exhibit significantly improved semantic clustering, with actions from different videos being grouped together based on their underlying motion patterns rather than superficial visual cues. When evaluated on downstream tasks such as action classification, the learned representations consistently outperform state-of-the-art self-supervised methods, even when trained on smaller datasets, and demonstrate superior robustness to view-specific noise, domain shifts, and incomplete annotations. The framework is modular, extensible, and compatible with any existing contrastive learning backbone, making it applicable to a wide range of video understanding tasks without requiring labeled data or architectural modifications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention comprises a system and method for learning high-quality visual representations from unlabeled video data by leveraging the cooperative interaction between multiple complementary views of the same temporal sequence. Each view provides a distinct perspective on the underlying content—such as appearance, motion, body configuration, or spatial extent—and while individually each may be incomplete, noisy, or limited in scope, their collective interaction enables the emergence of robust, semantically coherent representations. The core innovation lies in the realization that the relative similarity between instances—i.e., whether two videos depict the same action—should be consistent across all views, regardless of how that action is visually expressed. This principle is operationalized through a dual-loss framework that simultaneously enforces discriminative embedding learning within each view and enforces consistency of pairwise relationships across views. The system begins by partitioning each input video into a sequence of spatiotemporal blocks, each comprising a short temporal window of frames. For each block, multiple views are extracted: for instance, RGB frames capture appearance and texture, optical flow encodes motion vectors, pose heatmaps represent the spatial distribution of body joints, and segmentation masks delineate the human figure against the background. These views are not assumed to be independent; rather, they are treated as derivable, complementary, and often noisy manifestations of the same underlying event. Each view is processed by a dedicated encoder network, which maps the block-level input into a latent embedding space. These encoders are initially trained independently using a standard contrastive predictive coding (CPC) objective, which encourages the model to predict future block embeddings from past context. This pre-training phase ensures that each encoder learns to extract meaningful temporal structure within its own modality. Subsequently, the encoders are jointly optimized using a cooperative loss function that consists of two components: a synchronization loss and a similarity loss. The synchronization loss operates by computing, for each batch of video clips, a similarity matrix for each view. Each entry in this matrix represents the cosine distance between the embeddings of two instances in that view. The synchronization loss then measures the discrepancy between these matrices across all pairs of views, penalizing configurations where the relative ordering of similarities diverges. For example, if two videos are close in the RGB view but far apart in the optical flow view, the loss increases, signaling a violation of cross-view consistency. This forces the system to adjust the embeddings so that the relative distances between all instance pairs become aligned across views. To prevent the system from collapsing all distances to a single value—thereby losing discriminative power—a similarity loss is introduced, which applies a hinge constraint within each view to ensure that similar pairs remain closer than dissimilar ones by a predefined margin. This loss is computed independently per view but is applied jointly, ensuring that each view maintains its own ability to distinguish between positive and negative pairs while being guided by the global consistency objective. The two losses are combined with a weighting factor, and the entire system is trained end-to-end using stochastic gradient descent. Crucially, this process does not require any labeled data, nor does it rely on manual annotation of correspondences between views. Instead, the system learns to resolve ambiguities and reconcile discrepancies through mutual alignment, effectively turning each view into a teacher for the others. This cooperative mechanism leads to a positive feedback loop: as one view improves its ability to recognize semantic similarity, it provides more reliable guidance to the others, which in turn refine their own representations, leading to further improvements. The result is a set of embeddings that are not only discriminative within their own modality but also semantically aligned across modalities, enabling the system to recognize that two videos depicting the same action—even if one is recorded in bright daylight and the other in dim indoor lighting, or one shows a person in a red shirt and the other in blue—are indeed similar. This capability is particularly valuable in real-world applications where data is abundant but labeled examples are scarce. The invention is implemented using a modular architecture that allows for the seamless integration of new views. For example, additional modalities such as depth maps, audio spectrograms, or gaze trajectories can be incorporated without altering the core framework, simply by adding a new encoder and extending the synchronization and similarity loss computations to include the new view. The encoders themselves can be any deep neural network capable of processing spatiotemporal data, such as 3D convolutional networks, transformers, or recurrent architectures, provided they can map input blocks into fixed-dimensional embeddings. The aggregation function that generates context representations from sequences of block embeddings may be implemented using convolutional gated recurrent units, temporal convolutions, or attention mechanisms, depending on the desired trade-off between computational efficiency and modeling capacity. The system is trained using standard optimization techniques, including Adam with weight decay, and employs data augmentations tailored to each view—for instance, color jittering and random cropping for RGB, spatial cropping for flow, and horizontal flipping for all views—to ensure robustness to variations in input conditions. During inference, the learned encoders can be used to generate embeddings for any video segment, which can then be fed into downstream classifiers, retrieval systems, or alignment algorithms. The embeddings exhibit superior clustering properties, as demonstrated by t-SNE visualizations, where actions from different videos are grouped into tight, semantically coherent clusters that reflect their underlying kinematic structure rather than superficial appearance. Furthermore, the system demonstrates an ability to infer non-trivial relationships between action classes—such as grouping playing a cello with playing a violin or basketball with volleyball—even when these actions share no visual commonalities in the RGB modality, indicating that the learned representations capture high-level semantic abstractions rather than low-level visual patterns. The invention also enables loose temporal alignment between videos, where the relative similarity between blocks across different videos reveals periodic patterns—such as the up-and-down motion of a pull-up or the swinging motion of a tennis serve—without requiring explicit temporal correspondence. This emergent alignment capability arises naturally from the consistency of pairwise similarities across views, as the system learns to associate similar motion phases regardless of their absolute timing. The framework’s performance is validated across multiple benchmark datasets, including UCF101, HMDB51, and Kinetics-400, where it consistently outperforms state-of-the-art self-supervised methods, even when trained on a fraction of the data. Notably, when trained on UCF101, the system achieves higher classification accuracy than models trained on the much larger Kinetics-400 dataset, demonstrating that the cooperative alignment of multiple views can be more effective than simply increasing dataset size. The system also shows remarkable resilience to noise: even when using pose heatmaps and segmentation masks generated by off-the-shelf models with significant detection errors, performance improves across all views, indicating that the synchronization mechanism effectively filters out unreliable signals and amplifies consistent semantic patterns. This robustness makes the invention particularly suitable for deployment in real-world scenarios where data quality is variable and annotation is impractical. The invention’s modular design and compatibility with existing architectures make it broadly applicable to any video understanding task that benefits from semantic representation learning, including video retrieval, anomaly detection, action localization, and cross-modal video synthesis. Its ability to extract meaningful structure from unlabeled, multi-view data without requiring labeled ground truth positions it as a foundational advancement in self-supervised learning, enabling the development of vision systems that learn to understand actions by observing how they are perceived differently across complementary perspectives, rather than by memorizing specific examples. The cooperative contrastive learning framework disclosed herein thus represents a paradigm shift in how visual representations are learned, moving from isolated, modality-specific optimization to a collaborative, cross-view consensus-building process that mirrors the way humans integrate multiple sensory cues to form a unified understanding of the world. By treating similarity as a global, cross-modal property rather than a local, modality-dependent one, the invention unlocks a new level of semantic coherence in self-supervised representations, paving the way for more generalizable, robust, and interpretable video understanding systems.
