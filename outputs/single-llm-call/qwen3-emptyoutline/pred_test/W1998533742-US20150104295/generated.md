# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

In the field of turbomachinery, particularly in the design of bladed disks—commonly referred to as blisks—the pursuit of lightweight, high-efficiency structures has led to the adoption of monolithic fabrication techniques and advanced materials that inherently exhibit minimal internal damping. These structural characteristics, while advantageous for aerodynamic performance and mass reduction, introduce significant challenges in the form of uncontrolled vibrational responses under operational loading conditions. The absence of sufficient damping mechanisms renders such components susceptible to high-cycle fatigue, resonance-induced failures, and premature degradation, especially when subjected to periodic excitation forces arising from the interaction between rotating blades and stationary stator vanes. These excitation forces, often termed engine order excitations, are harmonically related to the rotational speed of the rotor and can excite specific vibrational modes characterized by a defined number of nodal diameters in the circumferential direction. The identification and mitigation of these critical modes are paramount to ensuring the structural integrity and operational reliability of modern gas turbines, compressors, and other rotating machinery.

Historically, various passive and active damping strategies have been employed to address excessive vibrations in turbomachinery. Among passive techniques, friction damping through blade-to-blade contact, the use of friction rings, and viscoelastic treatments have demonstrated utility in certain configurations. However, these methods often introduce additional mass, complicate manufacturing and maintenance, or suffer from performance degradation due to environmental factors such as temperature, pressure, and contamination. Active damping approaches, including piezoelectric shunt circuits, have emerged as promising alternatives due to their ability to convert mechanical strain energy into electrical energy, which is then dissipated through an external electrical network. The most commonly studied configurations involve resistive (R-shunt), resistive-inductive (RL-shunt), and more complex switched or active electronic networks. While the R-shunt offers simplicity and robustness, its damping effectiveness is limited to narrow frequency bands and is generally insufficient for high-performance applications. The RL-shunt, by contrast, enables precise tuning to a target resonant frequency through the selection of an inductor whose value satisfies the condition (𝐿𝐶)⁻¹/² ≈ 𝜔ᵢ, where 𝐶 is the capacitance of the piezoelectric transducer and 𝜔ᵢ is the natural frequency of the targeted mode. This configuration provides significantly enhanced damping performance, particularly when optimized with respect to the resistance value, which governs the damping ratio of the coupled electromechanical system.

Despite its superior performance, the practical implementation of RL-shunt damping in rotating machinery is severely constrained by the physical requirements of the inductors. For typical piezoelectric patches used in turbomachinery applications, the required inductance values to achieve resonance with blade-dominated modes often exceed several henries. Such large inductances are not feasible with conventional passive magnetic components due to their prohibitive size, weight, and susceptibility to magnetic saturation under dynamic conditions. Consequently, engineers have resorted to synthetic inductors—electronic circuits based on active components such as operational amplifiers and feedback networks, commonly implemented using Antoniou gyrator topologies—to emulate the behavior of large inductors without the need for bulky magnetic cores. While these synthetic inductors offer a viable laboratory solution, they are fundamentally incompatible with the harsh operational environment of rotating machinery. The presence of active electronic components introduces vulnerabilities to electromagnetic interference, temperature fluctuations, mechanical vibration, and power supply instability. Moreover, the requirement for continuous power, signal conditioning, and control circuitry renders such systems unreliable, maintenance-intensive, and unsuitable for long-term deployment in critical aerospace and power generation systems.

An additional layer of complexity arises from the inherent rotational periodicity of bladed disks. In such structures, vibrational modes manifest as harmonic standing waves in the circumferential direction, characterized by a specific number of nodal diameters, denoted as 𝑛. For a disk with 𝑁 blades, the possible values of 𝑛 range from 0 to 𝑁/2 (for even 𝑁), and each mode typically occurs in a pair: a sine mode and a cosine mode, both sharing the same natural frequency under ideal periodic conditions. The excitation of these modes by rotating forces follows well-defined resonance conditions, which depend on the interaction between the rotational speed, the number of stator vanes, and the spatial harmonic content of the forcing function. This allows engineers to predict and identify the most critical modes that are likely to be excited under normal operating conditions. Targeting these specific modes for damping becomes not only a technical necessity but also a strategic opportunity to optimize the damping architecture. However, conventional RL-shunt implementations treat each piezoelectric patch as an independent transducer, requiring a dedicated inductor per patch. In a bladed disk with hundreds of blades, this leads to an impractical proliferation of inductors, exacerbating the challenges of integration, weight, and reliability.

The present invention addresses these longstanding limitations by introducing a novel topological arrangement of piezoelectric transducers that leverages the spatial symmetry of rotationally periodic vibrational modes to drastically reduce the number of required inductors while preserving the damping efficacy of the RL-shunt configuration. The innovation lies in the systematic grouping of piezoelectric patches into parallel electrical loops, wherein the patches are organized according to the spatial phase distribution of the targeted mode’s mode shape. Specifically, for a mode with 𝑛 nodal diameters, the invention employs 4𝑛 piezoelectric patches arranged in two parallel electrical loops, each containing 2𝑛 patches. Crucially, the patches within each loop are electrically connected in a manner that aligns their polarity and spatial orientation with the alternating regions of positive and negative curvature inherent in the sine and cosine components of the mode shape. By mounting adjacent pairs of patches with inverted polarization and connecting them in parallel, the system effectively doubles the capacitance per loop while maintaining the voltage response proportional to the strain energy of the targeted mode. This geometric and electrical organization ensures that the total induced charge from all patches in a loop is maximized for the targeted mode, while suppressing contributions from other modes due to phase cancellation. As a result, the required inductance per loop is reduced by a factor of 4𝑛² compared to the conventional independent-loop configuration, enabling the use of passive, non-synthetic inductors with feasible physical dimensions and robustness suitable for rotating environments.

This approach fundamentally transforms the paradigm of piezoelectric damping in turbomachinery. Rather than treating each transducer as an isolated sensor-actuator unit, the invention redefines the transducer array as a spatially distributed, electromechanically coupled system whose topology is explicitly designed to match the harmonic structure of the vibration mode it seeks to suppress. The resulting architecture is entirely passive, requiring no active electronics, external power, or real-time control systems. It is inherently scalable, robust, and compatible with the mechanical and thermal constraints of high-speed rotating machinery. Furthermore, the method is not limited to a specific number of blades or a particular mode shape; it is generalizable to any rotationally periodic structure where a critical mode with a known number of nodal diameters can be identified through modal analysis or operational monitoring. The invention thus provides a practical, reliable, and commercially viable solution to one of the most persistent challenges in the design of modern turbomachinery: the effective suppression of resonant vibrations without compromising structural integrity, aerodynamic performance, or system reliability.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

The preferred embodiment of the invention comprises a vibration damping system for rotationally periodic structures, particularly bladed disks used in turbomachinery, wherein a plurality of piezoelectric transducers are arranged in a spatially symmetric configuration and electrically interconnected to form a minimal number of passive RL shunt circuits, each tuned to damp a specific vibrational mode characterized by a defined number of nodal diameters. The system is designed to operate without active components, external power sources, or real-time control circuitry, thereby ensuring long-term reliability in high-speed, high-temperature, and high-vibration environments typical of gas turbines and compressors. The core principle of the invention is the alignment of the electrical topology of the piezoelectric network with the spatial harmonic structure of the targeted vibrational mode, enabling a dramatic reduction in the required inductance values while maintaining or even enhancing the damping performance relative to conventional independent-loop configurations.

The piezoelectric transducers employed in the preferred embodiment are thin, flexible patches fabricated from a piezoelectric ceramic material such as lead zirconate titanate (PZT), operating in the d₃₁ mode, wherein strain along the thickness direction induces a charge across the electrode surfaces. These patches are bonded to a structural surface of the rotating component—typically the inner surface of the blade support rim or the drum structure—such that they are subjected to the strain field associated with the targeted vibrational mode. The patches are arranged in a circumferential pattern that corresponds to the spatial distribution of strain in the mode shape. For a mode with 𝑛 nodal diameters, the circumferential domain is divided into 4𝑛 distinct regions, each corresponding to a lobe of the mode’s harmonic profile. Within each region, a single piezoelectric patch is mounted such that its electrode orientation aligns with the direction of maximum strain. The patches are grouped into two parallel electrical loops, each containing 2𝑛 patches. The patches within each loop are connected in parallel, such that their positive electrodes are electrically joined together and their negative electrodes are similarly joined, forming a single capacitive branch per loop. The key innovation lies in the polarity assignment of the patches: adjacent pairs of patches are mounted with inverted polarization relative to one another, such that the polarity of the first patch in a pair is opposite to that of the second, the third opposite to the fourth, and so forth. This inversion is not merely a physical orientation of the ceramic material but is achieved by reversing the direction of poling during fabrication or by reversing the electrical connections during assembly, ensuring that the charge generated by mechanical deformation in one patch is additive to the charge generated in its paired counterpart, rather than canceling it.

The spatial arrangement of the patches is critical to the functionality of the system. For a mode with 𝑛 nodal diameters, the circumferential angle between adjacent antinodes is 2𝜋/𝑛 radians. The patches are positioned such that every pair of patches separated by an angular displacement of 𝜋/𝑛 radians belongs to the same electrical loop and is connected in parallel with inverted polarity. This configuration ensures that the strain-induced charge generated in one patch is always in phase with the charge generated in its paired counterpart, regardless of whether the mode is a sine or cosine harmonic. The result is that the total effective capacitance of each loop is equal to 2𝑛 times the capacitance of a single patch, while the effective electromechanical coupling factor is maximized for the targeted mode. The inductance value required to tune each loop to the natural frequency 𝜔ᵢ of the targeted mode is then determined by the equation 𝐿 = 1/(𝜔ᵢ² × 2𝑛𝐶), where 𝐶 is the capacitance of a single piezoelectric patch. This represents a reduction in inductance demand by a factor of 4𝑛² compared to a conventional configuration in which each of the 4𝑛 patches is connected to an independent RL shunt circuit, each requiring an inductance of 1/(𝜔ᵢ²𝐶). For example, in a bladed disk with 76 blades targeting a mode with 7 nodal diameters, the conventional approach would require 28 independent inductors each of approximately 0.7 henries, whereas the preferred embodiment reduces this requirement to four inductors of approximately 140 millihenries each, or, if the patches are arranged optimally with inverted polarity pairs, to just two inductors of 280 millihenries. These inductance values are well within the range achievable with passive, air-core or ferrite-core inductors that are compact, lightweight, and mechanically robust.

The resistance value in each RL shunt circuit is selected to maximize the damping ratio of the targeted mode. The optimal resistance is determined by the formula 𝑅 = √(2𝐿 / (𝐾ᵢ² × 2𝑛𝐶)), where 𝐾ᵢ is the total effective electromechanical coupling factor for the targeted mode, defined as the square root of the sum of the squared coupling factors of all patches in the loop. In practice, since the patches are identical and uniformly distributed, the coupling factor for each patch is assumed to be equal, and the total coupling factor is simply proportional to the number of patches per loop. The resistance value is chosen to critically damp the electrical resonance, ensuring that the poles of the coupled electromechanical system are placed at the optimal location for maximum energy dissipation. Importantly, the resistance value is not sensitive to minor variations in the natural frequency of the mode, nor does it require precise calibration for each individual patch. This tolerance to manufacturing variability and environmental drift is a significant advantage over active control systems, which often require continuous feedback and recalibration.

The physical implementation of the system begins with the selection of the piezoelectric patches, which are manufactured to precise dimensions and electrical characteristics to ensure uniformity across the array. The patches are bonded to the structural surface using a high-temperature, high-strength epoxy adhesive that maintains mechanical integrity under thermal cycling and centrifugal loading. The bonding process is performed such that the patches conform to the curvature of the surface and are aligned with the principal strain directions of the targeted mode. Electrical connections are made using flexible, high-temperature insulated wires that are routed along the structural surface to minimize stress concentrations and avoid interference with aerodynamic flow. The inductors are mounted externally on a stationary housing adjacent to the rotating component, connected via slip rings or wireless power and signal transmission systems if necessary, although in the preferred embodiment, the inductors are placed on the stationary portion of the machine and connected via low-inductance, shielded cables to the rotating piezoelectric array through a rotary joint designed for high-speed operation. The entire system is encapsulated in a protective coating to guard against moisture, oxidation, and mechanical abrasion.

The system is designed to be modular and scalable. For structures with a different number of blades or a different targeted mode, the number of patches and their spatial arrangement can be adjusted accordingly. The number of patches must be a multiple of 4𝑛 to ensure symmetry and optimal performance, but the system can accommodate additional patches beyond the minimum requirement, provided they are grouped into additional parallel loops, each tuned to a different mode. For example, if a structure requires damping of two distinct modes—one with 3 nodal diameters and another with 5 nodal diameters—the system may be configured with two sets of parallel loops: one set of two loops for the 3-nodal-diameter mode, using 24 patches (4×3×2), and another set of two loops for the 5-nodal-diameter mode, using 40 patches (4×5×2), for a total of 64 patches. Each set operates independently, with its own pair of inductors and resistors, allowing simultaneous damping of multiple critical modes without interference.

The performance of the system is evaluated through modal analysis and frequency response measurements. Under operational conditions, the vibrational response of the structure is monitored using non-contact laser vibrometry or accelerometers mounted on stationary reference points. The frequency response function between the excitation force and the structural velocity is measured for various electrical configurations: open-circuit (no shunt), independent RL shunts (for comparison), and the proposed parallel-loop configuration. In all cases, the damping effect is quantified by the reduction in peak amplitude of the frequency response at the targeted mode and the corresponding decrease in the root-mean-square (RMS) vibration energy over the operating frequency band. Experimental results demonstrate that, for modes with high circumferential harmonic content and minimal mistuning, the damping performance of the parallel-loop configuration is nearly identical to that of the independent-loop configuration, despite requiring a fraction of the inductance. In cases where blade mistuning is present, the performance may be slightly reduced due to the introduction of non-harmonic spatial components in the mode shape, but the system remains effective as long as the dominant harmonic component corresponds to the targeted mode. The system is particularly effective for drum-dominated modes, where the mode shapes remain highly harmonic even in the presence of moderate mistuning, and for blade-dominated modes with low to moderate mistuning levels.

The invention further includes a method for determining the optimal number of patches and their spatial arrangement based on the mode shape of the targeted vibration. This method involves performing a finite element simulation of the rotating structure under free vibration conditions to extract the mode shape corresponding to the critical mode. The spatial distribution of strain along the circumferential direction is then analyzed to identify the locations of maximum positive and negative strain lobes. The patches are positioned such that each lobe is covered by a single patch, and the polarity of adjacent patches is inverted to ensure constructive charge summation within each loop. The electrical connections are then routed to form two parallel branches, each containing half of the total patches, with the inductors and resistors connected across the terminals of each branch. This procedure ensures that the system is inherently adaptive to the specific geometry and mode shape of the structure, rather than relying on a generic, one-size-fits-all configuration.

The system is designed for integration into existing turbomachinery without requiring major modifications to the base structure. The piezoelectric patches are thin and lightweight, adding negligible mass to the rotating component. Their placement on the inner surface of the drum or blade support rim avoids interference with the aerodynamic flow path, preserving the efficiency and performance of the compressor or turbine. The passive nature of the RL shunt circuits ensures that the system requires no maintenance, no power supply, and no control algorithms, making it suitable for deployment in remote or inaccessible locations such as deep within a jet engine. The inductors and resistors are housed in a sealed, temperature-stabilized enclosure mounted on the stationary casing, protected from the extreme thermal and mechanical gradients experienced by the rotating parts. The entire system is designed to operate reliably over the full range of engine operating conditions, from idle to maximum power, and through repeated thermal cycles without degradation in performance.

In addition to its mechanical and electrical advantages, the invention provides significant economic and operational benefits. The reduction in the number of required inductors by a factor of 4𝑛² translates directly into lower material costs, reduced assembly complexity, and improved system reliability. The elimination of synthetic inductors and active electronics removes a major source of failure in previous damping systems, thereby increasing the mean time between failures and reducing lifecycle maintenance costs. The system is also inherently scalable to larger structures with more blades, as the number of inductors required grows only linearly with the number of targeted modes, rather than quadratically with the number of patches. This scalability makes the invention particularly attractive for next-generation turbomachinery with higher blade counts and more complex mode structures.

The preferred embodiment further encompasses a method for verifying the effectiveness of the damping system during commissioning. This involves applying a controlled, broadband excitation to the structure using non-contact actuators such as voice coil devices or electromagnetic shakers, while monitoring the vibrational response with a laser scanning vibrometer. The excitation is shaped to match the spatial harmonic content of the targeted mode, ensuring that the system is tested under conditions representative of actual operation. The frequency response is then compared across the open-circuit, independent-loop, and parallel-loop configurations. A successful implementation is confirmed when the peak damping at the targeted mode in the parallel-loop configuration is within 5% of the damping achieved by the independent-loop configuration, and when the damping of non-targeted modes is negligible. This verification protocol ensures that the system performs as intended in the real operating environment, accounting for variations in material properties, assembly tolerances, and environmental conditions.

Finally, the invention includes provisions for future adaptability. Although the preferred embodiment is designed for passive operation, the architecture is compatible with the future addition of active control elements. For instance, the resistors in each RL loop could be replaced with digitally programmable resistors or variable impedance networks, allowing for real-time tuning of the damping characteristics in response to changing operating conditions. Similarly, the inductors could be replaced with tunable magnetic cores or switched-capacitor banks to enable frequency tracking. However, in the preferred embodiment, such enhancements are not required, as the passive configuration provides sufficient damping performance under all anticipated operating conditions. The system is therefore designed as a “fit-and-forget” solution, requiring no intervention after initial installation and calibration, making it ideal for mission-critical applications where reliability and longevity are paramount.
