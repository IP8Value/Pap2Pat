# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of automated image captioning has long been grounded in the principle of generating natural language descriptions that align closely with human-provided reference captions. Early approaches relied heavily on supervised learning paradigms where models were trained to maximize the likelihood of producing exact textual matches to annotated captions, typically using maximum likelihood estimation (MLE). These methods, while effective in producing fluent and syntactically correct sentences, suffered from a fundamental limitation: the reference captions themselves were often sparse, generic, and focused only on the most salient or obvious elements within an image. As a result, models trained under such constraints learned to prioritize recall of commonly occurring phrases over the generation of rich, discriminative, and contextually nuanced descriptions. This led to a pervasive phenomenon wherein diverse images—despite containing unique, intricate, or subtle details—were assigned nearly identical captions, reducing the utility of such systems in applications requiring fine-grained visual understanding, such as accessibility tools for the visually impaired, content-based image retrieval, or automated visual indexing in large-scale multimedia archives.

To address this shortcoming, researchers introduced reward-based training frameworks that moved beyond simple n-gram overlap metrics like BLEU, ROUGE, and METEOR, which were known to penalize semantically equivalent but syntactically distinct phrasings. The CIDEr metric, which leverages TF-IDF weighted n-gram matching against multiple references, represented a significant advancement by emphasizing consensus among human annotations. However, even CIDEr remained constrained by the limited number of reference captions per image, typically five, and thus continued to reflect the biases inherent in those annotations rather than the full visual content of the image. Subsequent efforts sought to incorporate semantic similarity through embeddings derived from pre-trained language models such as BERT, yielding metrics like BERTScore, which improved robustness to paraphrasing. Yet these approaches still required reference captions as ground truth, thereby inheriting their limitations.

More recently, multi-modal models such as CLIP, trained on vast collections of image-text pairs scraped from the internet, have emerged as powerful tools for measuring semantic alignment between visual and textual representations. CLIP’s architecture, which jointly encodes images and text into a shared embedding space through contrastive learning, enables the computation of similarity scores between any image and any caption without requiring paired training data. This property has inspired novel training strategies in which the CLIP similarity score itself is used as a reward signal to guide caption generation. By maximizing this score, models are incentivized to produce captions that are not merely similar to existing references but are instead highly discriminative—capturing details that uniquely characterize the input image and distinguish it from others. This shift from reference-based to similarity-based reward mechanisms represents a paradigmatic evolution in captioning, as it decouples the training objective from the quality or completeness of human annotations and instead aligns it directly with the perceptual relevance of the generated text to the visual content.

Despite these advances, a critical challenge persists: CLIP’s text encoder was not trained with a language modeling objective and lacks the grammatical coherence and structural integrity expected in natural human language. Consequently, when used as a reward function, CLIP-based captioning models frequently generate text that is semantically rich but syntactically flawed—exhibiting phenomena such as word repetition, malformed clauses, redundant phrases, or syntactic incoherence. These artifacts, while not necessarily reducing the semantic relevance of the caption as measured by CLIP similarity, severely degrade its usability in real-world applications where grammatical correctness is non-negotiable. For instance, a caption such as “a blue car a blue car parked in front of a house” may score highly on CLIP-Similarity due to the presence of key visual elements, but its repetition renders it unusable for accessibility interfaces, search engine indexing, or human consumption. Previous attempts to mitigate such issues have relied on hybrid reward functions that combine CLIP similarity with traditional n-gram or embedding-based metrics, necessitating careful tuning of weighting coefficients and often leading to unstable training dynamics. Moreover, these hybrid approaches still depend on reference captions for computing the auxiliary text similarity component, thereby reintroducing the very biases the CLIP-based reward sought to eliminate.

There remains, therefore, a critical gap in the state of the art: a method for training image captioning models that fully eliminates dependence on reference captions during reward computation while simultaneously ensuring grammatical fluency and syntactic integrity in the generated output. Such a method would enable the generation of captions that are not only more descriptive and distinctive than those produced by current systems but also linguistically natural and suitable for deployment in high-stakes applications. Furthermore, the absence of a standardized evaluation framework capable of measuring caption quality across fine-grained dimensions—such as background context, object attributes, spatial relationships, and holistic coherence—has hindered meaningful comparison between competing approaches. Existing benchmarks, including MS COCO, are ill-suited for this purpose, as they were designed for general-purpose captioning and do not systematically capture the granularity required to assess whether a model captures subtle visual distinctions. Consequently, there is a pressing need for both a novel training methodology that unifies discriminative reward learning with grammatical regularization, and a comprehensive evaluation protocol that enables rigorous, multi-faceted assessment of caption quality beyond superficial textual overlap.

This invention addresses these limitations by introducing a self-contained, reference-free training framework for image captioning that leverages CLIP’s multi-modal similarity as the sole reward signal, augmented by a novel, synthetic, annotation-free method for fine-tuning the CLIP text encoder to correct grammatical degeneracies. The invention further introduces a new benchmark dataset, FineCapEval, specifically designed to evaluate captioning performance across four distinct dimensions of visual description: overall coherence, background context, object attributes, and inter-object relationships. Together, these components form a unified system that generates captions of unprecedented descriptive fidelity and linguistic quality without requiring any human-annotated reference captions during training or evaluation. The resulting system not only surpasses prior methods in quantitative metrics such as text-to-image retrieval recall and CLIP similarity scores but also demonstrates superior performance in human evaluations across all aspects of descriptive accuracy, establishing a new standard for automated image captioning.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a novel system and method for generating high-fidelity, grammatically correct, and visually distinctive image captions without reliance on human-annotated reference captions during either training or evaluation. The system operates by employing a multi-modal contrastive encoder, specifically CLIP, to compute a similarity score between an input image and a generated caption, and uses this score as the sole reward signal to guide the training of an image captioning model. This approach eliminates the need for any reference captions, thereby removing the inherent biases, sparsity, and coverage limitations associated with human annotations. The captioning model is optimized using a reinforcement learning framework based on the REINFORCE algorithm with a self-critical baseline, ensuring stable and efficient gradient estimation during training. The reward function is defined as the cosine similarity between the image embedding and the text embedding produced by CLIP’s dual encoders, scaled by a learned parameter to balance sensitivity and convergence behavior.

To address the linguistic incoherence that arises when CLIP’s text encoder is used as a reward function—such as word repetition, syntactic fragmentation, and grammatical errors—the invention further introduces a novel technique for fine-tuning the CLIP text encoder using synthetic negative captions. These negative captions are generated automatically by applying a set of controlled perturbations to existing reference captions, including token repetition, deletion, insertion, swapping, and shuffling, without requiring any additional human labeling or linguistic annotations. The perturbed captions are then presented to the CLIP text encoder alongside their corresponding original captions, with the encoder trained to assign higher similarity scores to the original captions and lower scores to the synthetic negatives. This process effectively injects grammatical and syntactic knowledge into the text encoder, enabling it to better distinguish between fluent and degenerate text, thereby improving the quality of the reward signal during caption generation. The fine-tuned text encoder is then used in conjunction with the captioning model during inference, resulting in captions that retain the discriminative richness of CLIP-based rewards while exhibiting significantly improved grammatical structure.

The invention further encompasses a new evaluation framework, termed FineCapEval, designed to measure caption quality across four distinct and complementary dimensions: overall description, background context, object attributes, and spatial relationships between objects. FineCapEval comprises one thousand images selected from publicly available datasets, each annotated by five human evaluators with four corresponding phrases: one for each of the four criteria. These annotations are collected under strict guidelines to ensure consistency, avoid known entities, and emphasize local and global visual details. The evaluation protocol employs a word-level recall metric for the background, object, and relationship criteria, measuring the proportion of key descriptive terms from the human annotations that are accurately captured in the generated caption. For the overall description, the CIDEr metric is applied to maintain compatibility with existing benchmarks. Human evaluation is conducted via pairwise preference testing, where annotators compare captions generated by the proposed system against those produced by baseline models, selecting the superior caption across five criteria: overall quality, background accuracy, object identification, attribute specificity, and relational correctness. The results demonstrate that captions generated by the proposed system consistently outperform those from models trained with traditional text similarity objectives, including CIDEr and MLE, across all evaluation dimensions.

The system is implemented using a transformer-based image captioning architecture, with the image encoder initialized from a pre-trained ResNet-50 backbone and the text decoder initialized from a transformer architecture aligned with CLIP’s text encoder. During training, the model generates captions via beam search, and the CLIP similarity score is computed for each candidate caption. The self-critical baseline is derived from the greedy-decoded caption, ensuring that the reward signal is normalized relative to a low-variance reference point. The CLIP text encoder is fine-tuned independently on a separate corpus of synthetic negative captions, with the objective of maximizing the margin between original and perturbed text embeddings. This fine-tuning is performed once, prior to captioning model training, and the resulting encoder is frozen during subsequent captioning optimization. The entire pipeline operates without any human reference captions during training, evaluation, or reward computation, making it fully self-contained and scalable to any visual domain where reference annotations are unavailable, incomplete, or biased.

The invention provides a substantial advancement over prior art by achieving higher text-to-image retrieval performance than the original reference captions paired with images in the MS COCO dataset, demonstrating that the generated captions are not only more descriptive but also more effective at uniquely identifying the corresponding image. Furthermore, the system produces captions that are linguistically fluent, free from degeneration artifacts, and rich in fine-grained visual detail, making it uniquely suited for applications in accessibility, visual search, automated image tagging, and multimedia indexing. The combination of reference-free reward computation, synthetic grammar correction, and fine-grained evaluation constitutes a holistic, end-to-end solution that redefines the standard for automated image captioning.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Image Search System

The image search system described herein is a fully automated, self-contained architecture designed to generate high-quality, discriminative, and grammatically correct textual descriptions of images for the purpose of enabling accurate, scalable, and semantically rich image retrieval. The system operates independently of any human-annotated reference captions, eliminating the biases, incompleteness, and variability inherent in traditional supervised captioning datasets. At its core, the system integrates a multi-modal contrastive encoder, a reinforcement learning-based caption generator, and a grammatically enhanced text encoder, all operating in concert to produce captions that are not only semantically aligned with the visual content of the image but also linguistically coherent and suitable for real-world deployment. The system is designed to be trained once and deployed across diverse visual domains without requiring re-annotation or domain-specific tuning, making it uniquely scalable and robust.

The system begins with an input image, which is processed by a convolutional neural network encoder, specifically a pre-trained ResNet-50 architecture, to extract a high-dimensional visual feature representation. This representation is then passed to a transformer-based decoder, which generates a sequence of tokens representing a natural language caption. The decoder is initialized with weights derived from the CLIP text encoder, ensuring alignment between the captioning model’s vocabulary and the multi-modal embedding space used for reward computation. Unlike conventional captioning models, which are trained to minimize the cross-entropy loss between generated and reference captions, this system is trained to maximize the similarity between the generated caption and the input image as measured by the CLIP model. This similarity is computed by encoding the image and the generated caption into their respective embedding spaces using CLIP’s image and text encoders, and then computing the cosine similarity between the two vectors. The resulting scalar value, referred to as CLIP-S, serves as the sole reward signal for training the captioning model.

To optimize the captioning model under this reward signal, the system employs a reinforcement learning framework based on the REINFORCE algorithm, augmented with a self-critical baseline to reduce variance in gradient estimation. Specifically, for each input image, the model generates a set of candidate captions via beam search, and the CLIP-S score is computed for each candidate. The baseline reward is derived from the caption produced by greedy decoding, which is typically less diverse but more stable than beam-searched outputs. The difference between the reward of the sampled caption and the baseline reward is used as a policy gradient signal, guiding the model to favor captions that yield higher CLIP-S scores than the greedy output. This mechanism ensures that the model is incentivized to generate captions that are not merely fluent but also more distinctive and informative than the most probable output under standard decoding. The reward function is scaled by a learned weight parameter, set to 2.5, to balance the sensitivity of the gradient updates and prevent premature convergence to suboptimal solutions. This scaling factor is determined empirically through ablation studies and remains fixed during training to ensure stability.

A critical innovation of the system lies in its ability to mitigate the grammatical degradation that arises when CLIP’s text encoder is used as a reward function. Although CLIP excels at aligning semantically relevant image-text pairs, its text encoder was not trained with a language modeling objective and therefore lacks the structural integrity required to produce fluent, grammatically correct sentences. When used as a reward, this leads to the generation of captions plagued by word repetition, syntactic redundancy, and malformed clauses. To resolve this issue, the system introduces a novel fine-tuning procedure for the CLIP text encoder that does not require any additional human annotations. Synthetic negative captions are generated by applying a series of controlled perturbations to existing reference captions from publicly available datasets. These perturbations include: (1) repeating one or more tokens within the caption, (2) removing one or more tokens, (3) inserting random tokens from the vocabulary, (4) swapping the positions of two adjacent tokens, and (5) shuffling the order of tokens within the caption. Each perturbation is applied with a predefined probability, ensuring diversity in the types of grammatical errors introduced. The resulting synthetic captions are paired with their original, unmodified counterparts and used to train the CLIP text encoder to assign higher similarity scores to the original captions and lower scores to the perturbed versions. This process effectively teaches the text encoder to recognize and penalize common degeneration patterns, thereby improving its ability to discriminate between fluent and degraded text.

The fine-tuning of the CLIP text encoder is performed as a separate, offline step prior to training the captioning model. During this phase, the image encoder of CLIP is frozen, and only the text encoder is updated using a contrastive loss function that maximizes the margin between original and synthetic caption embeddings. The loss is computed as the sum of the negative log-likelihood of the original caption having a higher similarity score than each of its synthetic variants, ensuring that the encoder learns to assign higher confidence to grammatically correct text. Once fine-tuned, the text encoder is frozen and used exclusively for computing the CLIP-S reward during captioning model training and inference. This separation of concerns ensures that the grammatical refinement of the text encoder does not interfere with the learning dynamics of the captioning model, while still enabling the generation of linguistically coherent captions. The result is a captioning system that generates descriptions rich in visual detail—capturing subtle elements such as weather conditions, object materials, spatial arrangements, and environmental context—while maintaining the grammatical integrity expected in natural human language.

The system is evaluated using a newly constructed dataset, FineCapEval, which is specifically designed to measure caption quality across four distinct dimensions: overall description, background context, object attributes, and relational structure. FineCapEval comprises one thousand images drawn from the MS COCO test2015 and Conceptual Caption validation splits, each annotated by five human evaluators who are instructed to write one phrase for each of the four criteria. The annotations are collected under strict guidelines that prohibit the use of proper nouns, temporal references, known landmarks, or subjective emotional states, ensuring that the descriptions remain objective, generalizable, and focused on visual attributes. For the background, object, and relationship criteria, performance is measured using a word-level recall metric, R_word, which computes the proportion of key descriptive terms from the human annotations that appear in the generated caption. For the overall description, the CIDEr metric is applied to maintain compatibility with existing benchmarks. Human evaluation is conducted via pairwise preference testing, where annotators compare captions generated by the proposed system against those from baseline models trained with CIDEr or MLE objectives. Each comparison is made across five criteria: overall quality, background accuracy, object identification, attribute specificity, and relational correctness. The results demonstrate that the proposed system consistently outperforms all baselines, with human annotators selecting its captions as superior in over 75% of cases across all dimensions.

The system’s performance is further validated through text-to-image retrieval experiments, in which generated captions are used as queries to retrieve their corresponding images from a large-scale database using a pre-trained CLIP ViT-B/32 model. The recall@K metric is computed for K = 1, 5, and 10, measuring the proportion of times the correct image is retrieved among the top K results. Remarkably, the captions generated by the proposed system achieve higher retrieval recall than the original reference captions paired with the images in the MS COCO dataset. This indicates that the generated captions are not merely more descriptive but are, in fact, more effective at uniquely identifying the corresponding image than the human-written references themselves. This finding underscores the system’s ability to extract and articulate fine-grained visual distinctions that are often overlooked or omitted in human annotations.

The system is implemented using standard deep learning frameworks and is trained end-to-end on a single GPU cluster using mixed-precision arithmetic to accelerate convergence. The captioning model is trained for 50 epochs with a learning rate of 5e-5, using the AdamW optimizer with weight decay. The CLIP text encoder is fine-tuned for 10 epochs on a separate batch of synthetic captions, using a learning rate of 1e-5. The entire pipeline requires no human annotation during training, evaluation, or reward computation, making it fully autonomous and scalable to any visual domain. The system can be deployed in real-time applications such as image search engines, accessibility tools for the visually impaired, automated content tagging for social media platforms, and multimedia indexing systems for archival repositories. Its ability to generate captions that are both semantically rich and grammatically fluent, without reliance on reference data, represents a fundamental advancement in the field of automated image description and establishes a new benchmark for performance, scalability, and linguistic quality.
