# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to a novel apparatus and method for the high-throughput electrospinning of core-sheath microfibers and nanofibers using a slit-surface fixture configuration. Specifically, the invention pertains to a system in which two distinct flowable polymer solutions are co-localized along a planar slit geometry, wherein one solution functions as the core and the other as the sheath, and both are simultaneously ejected under the influence of a high electric field to form multiple stable core-sheath cone-jets that solidify into continuous fibers upon solvent evaporation. The invention is particularly directed to the precise geometric and hydrodynamic control of the slit-surface architecture, including the relative dimensions of the core and sheath slits, the flow rates of the respective solutions, and the viscosities of the polymer solutions, such that the formation of discrete, well-defined core-sheath fiber structures is consistently achieved at volumetric flow rates exceeding one liter per hour—orders of magnitude higher than conventional needle-based coaxial electrospinning systems. The apparatus and method are applicable across a broad spectrum of polymer systems, including biodegradable and non-biodegradable polymers, low molecular weight materials that are otherwise non-electrospinnable, immiscible and miscible solvent systems, and solutions containing encapsulated particulates such as drugs, proteins, or nanoparticles. The resulting fibers find utility in controlled drug delivery, tissue engineering, filtration media, functional textiles, microfluidic devices, energy storage components, and photonic structures. The invention eliminates the need for complex multi-nozzle arrays, minimizes clogging and maintenance issues inherent in traditional coaxial needle systems, and enables scalable, continuous, and industrially viable production of core-sheath fibers with consistent morphology and compositional integrity.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel electrospinning apparatus and method for the fabrication of core-sheath fibers at commercially viable throughputs through the use of a precisely engineered slit-surface fixture. The apparatus comprises a pair of elongated, vertically aligned slits formed in a rigid, non-conductive substrate, wherein one slit is designated for the delivery of a core polymer solution and the other for a sheath polymer solution. The slits are arranged such that their longitudinal axes are coplanar and vertically oriented, creating a continuous, linear interface between the two solutions as they exit the fixture. Upon application of a high electric potential to the fixture, multiple Taylor cone-jets form simultaneously along the length of the slit-surface, each jet comprising a core of one polymer solution enveloped by a sheath of the other. The formation of these core-sheath jets is governed by a hydrodynamic mechanism wherein the sheath solution, flowing at a higher velocity than the core solution at the slit exit, entrains the core solution via viscous shear forces at their interface, preventing intermixing and preserving the core-sheath architecture. This phenomenon, analogous to selective withdrawal in stratified fluid systems, is critically dependent on the ratio of sheath-to-core flow velocities, the geometric dimensions of the slits, and the viscosities of the solutions. The invention enables the production of core-sheath fibers at total volumetric flow rates exceeding one liter per hour, corresponding to polymer mass production rates of up to 250 grams per hour, representing a more than one-thousand-fold increase over conventional needle-based coaxial electrospinning. The slit-surface design eliminates the need for concentric needles, reduces system complexity, minimizes clogging, and allows for extended operational stability without degradation in fiber quality. The geometry of the slits—including the width and height of both the core and sheath slits—is optimized to ensure that the sheath flow velocity consistently exceeds the core flow velocity, thereby promoting stable jet formation. The invention further permits the use of a wide variety of polymer-solvent systems, including those with low molecular weight, low viscosity, or poor electrospinnability when used alone, by encapsulating them within a sheath of a compatible polymer. Hollow fibers, bicomponent fibers, and fibers encapsulating active pharmaceutical ingredients, growth factors, or nanoparticles are readily fabricated. The resulting fibers exhibit uniform morphology, consistent core-sheath ratios, and high structural integrity, as confirmed by scanning electron microscopy and cross-sectional analysis. The apparatus is compatible with standard electrospinning collectors, operates under ambient conditions, and is readily scalable by increasing the length of the slit-surface to accommodate additional concurrent jets. The invention thus represents a transformative advancement in the field of electrospinning, enabling the first truly scalable, high-throughput, and industrially feasible method for the continuous production of core-sheath fibers across diverse material systems and application domains.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Core Flow Rate

The core flow rate is a critical operational parameter that directly influences the stability, morphology, and structural integrity of the core-sheath fibers produced by the slit-surface electrospinning apparatus. The core solution, which may comprise a polymer, a drug, a nanoparticle suspension, or a low molecular weight material otherwise incapable of electrospinning, is delivered through the core slit at a precisely controlled volumetric flow rate. The range of acceptable core flow rates is not arbitrary but is constrained by the geometric and hydrodynamic conditions established by the sheath slit and the sheath flow rate. If the core flow rate is too high relative to the sheath flow rate, the core solution overwhelms the sheath solution at the interface, leading to instability, coalescence, or complete mixing of the two solutions, thereby destroying the core-sheath architecture. Conversely, if the core flow rate is too low, the resulting fibers may exhibit incomplete encapsulation, excessive thinning of the core, or irregular fiber diameters due to insufficient material flux. The invention establishes that the core flow rate must be maintained within a narrow window relative to the sheath flow rate to ensure consistent formation of discrete core-sheath cone-jets. Experimental data demonstrate that when the sheath flow rate is held constant at 200 mL/h, the core flow rate must not exceed 40 mL/h to preserve distinct core-sheath morphology; at 60 mL/h, the core solution begins to dominate the jet interface, resulting in irregular, unstable jets and loss of encapsulation. This threshold is not fixed but is dynamically dependent on the viscosities of the solutions and the dimensions of the slits. For instance, when the core solution viscosity is increased from 500 cP to 800 cP, the maximum allowable core flow rate may be increased slightly due to the greater resistance to entrainment, but only if the sheath flow velocity is proportionally increased to maintain the required shear stress gradient. The invention further reveals that the core flow rate must be adjusted in inverse proportion to the cross-sectional area of the core slit; a wider core slit necessitates a higher flow rate to achieve the same exit velocity, but this must be balanced against the risk of exceeding the sheath’s entrainment capacity. The optimal core flow rate is therefore not defined by an absolute value but by a relative ratio to the sheath flow rate, with the preferred range being between 5% and 25% of the sheath flow rate, depending on the specific material system. Within this range, the core solution is sufficiently entrained by the sheath stream to form a coherent, stable core within the jet without causing jet bifurcation or interfacial disruption. The invention further encompasses the use of precision syringe pumps or peristaltic pumps with feedback control to maintain the core flow rate within ±1% of the setpoint, ensuring batch-to-batch reproducibility. The core flow rate is also influenced by the solvent volatility and evaporation kinetics; solvents with high volatility may require lower flow rates to allow sufficient time for solvent evaporation before fiber solidification, whereas low-volatility solvents may permit higher flow rates without compromising fiber integrity. The invention explicitly excludes any configuration in which the core flow rate exceeds the sheath flow rate, as such conditions invariably lead to the collapse of the core-sheath structure and the formation of blended or phase-separated fibers, which are outside the scope of the claimed invention. Furthermore, the invention does not contemplate the use of pulsatile or intermittent core flow, as such non-continuous delivery disrupts the steady-state hydrodynamic equilibrium required for stable jet formation. The core flow rate must be maintained continuously and uniformly throughout the electrospinning process to ensure the production of fibers with consistent core diameter, encapsulation efficiency, and mechanical properties. The invention thus provides a method for determining the optimal core flow rate based on the sheath flow rate, slit geometry, and solution viscosity, enabling predictable, scalable, and reproducible fabrication of core-sheath fibers at industrial throughput levels.

### Slit Fixture Geometry Core Slit Width

The width of the core slit is a fundamental geometric parameter that governs the exit velocity of the core solution and, consequently, its ability to be entrained by the sheath solution without disrupting the core-sheath interface. The core slit, formed as a narrow, elongated channel in the slit-surface fixture, must be precisely dimensioned to ensure that the core flow velocity remains below the threshold at which it overcomes the sheath’s entrainment force. If the core slit is too wide, the cross-sectional area increases, causing the core flow velocity to decrease for a given volumetric flow rate. While this may seem beneficial in reducing the risk of jet instability, it paradoxically leads to insufficient shear stress at the interface, because the sheath solution, flowing over the core, lacks the momentum gradient necessary to draw the core material into the jet. In such cases, the core solution may spread laterally along the slit surface, leading to broad, irregular core streams that fail to form discrete, cylindrical jets. Experimental results demonstrate that when the core slit width is increased beyond 1.2 mm, even at low core flow rates, the resulting fibers exhibit irregular cross-sections, inconsistent core diameters, and frequent instances of core exposure at the fiber surface. Conversely, if the core slit is too narrow, the core flow velocity becomes excessively high, increasing the risk of jet disruption, splashing, or premature solvent evaporation at the slit exit, which can cause clogging or solidification of the polymer before ejection. The optimal core slit width lies within the range of 0.3 mm to 1.0 mm, with a preferred range of 0.5 mm to 0.8 mm, depending on the viscosity and surface tension of the core solution. Within this range, the core solution exits with sufficient velocity to maintain a coherent stream while remaining below the critical threshold for entrainment failure. The invention further establishes that the core slit width must be inversely proportional to the viscosity of the core solution; higher viscosity core solutions require narrower slits to maintain adequate exit velocity, whereas lower viscosity solutions may tolerate slightly wider slits without sacrificing jet stability. The slit width must also be consistent along the entire length of the slit-surface to ensure uniform jet formation across all concurrent electrospinning sites. Any variation in core slit width—whether due to manufacturing tolerances, thermal expansion, or mechanical deformation—results in localized variations in core flow velocity, which manifest as irregular fiber diameters, inconsistent encapsulation, or regions of fiber fusion. The invention therefore requires that the core slit be fabricated using precision machining techniques such as laser ablation, micro-milling, or photolithographic etching to achieve dimensional tolerances of ±10 micrometers or better. The slit must be free of burrs, debris, or surface irregularities that could induce turbulence or uneven flow. The invention further contemplates the use of a non-wetting coating on the inner surfaces of the core slit, such as a fluoropolymer or silane-based treatment, to minimize polymer adhesion and prevent clogging during prolonged operation. The core slit width is not an independent variable but is intrinsically linked to the sheath slit width and the flow rates of both solutions; the invention provides a method for determining the optimal core slit width based on the desired core flow rate, sheath slit width, and solution viscosities, such that the ratio of sheath-to-core exit velocity is maintained above a critical threshold of 2.0. The invention explicitly excludes any configuration in which the core slit width exceeds 1.5 mm, as such dimensions invariably result in the loss of core-sheath structure due to insufficient shear-induced entrainment. Furthermore, the invention does not contemplate the use of multiple, discontinuous core slits or segmented core channels, as such configurations introduce phase inconsistencies and disrupt the continuity of the core stream, leading to fiber defects and non-uniform encapsulation. The core slit must be a single, continuous, linear channel aligned with the sheath slit to ensure the formation of a uniform, linear array of core-sheath jets along the entire length of the fixture.

### Sheath Slit Width

The width of the sheath slit is a pivotal design parameter that determines the magnitude of the shear stress exerted on the core solution at the interface and, consequently, the stability and morphology of the resulting core-sheath fibers. The sheath solution, which envelops the core solution and provides the primary driving force for jet formation, must exit the sheath slit at a velocity significantly higher than that of the core solution to ensure effective entrainment. The sheath slit width directly modulates this exit velocity, as it defines the cross-sectional area through which the sheath solution flows. A narrower sheath slit increases the sheath flow velocity for a given volumetric flow rate, thereby enhancing the viscous shear forces acting on the core solution and promoting the formation of a well-defined, stable core-sheath jet. Conversely, a wider sheath slit reduces the sheath flow velocity, diminishing the entrainment force and risking the collapse of the core-sheath architecture. Experimental results demonstrate that when the sheath slit width is increased from 1.5 mm to 3.0 mm, while maintaining constant flow rates of 200 mL/h for the sheath and 20 mL/h for the core, the ratio of sheath-to-core exit velocity drops from 3.8 to 1.9, and the distinct core-sheath cone-jet structure is lost. At this lower velocity ratio, the core solution is no longer effectively entrained, and the jet becomes diffuse, with the core material spreading laterally and mixing with the sheath. However, when the core flow rate is reduced from 20 mL/h to 10 mL/h to compensate for the wider sheath slit, the velocity ratio increases to 3.6, and the core-sheath structure is restored. This demonstrates that the sheath slit width is not an isolated variable but must be balanced against the core flow rate to maintain the critical shear stress gradient. The invention establishes that the optimal sheath slit width ranges from 1.0 mm to 2.5 mm, with a preferred range of 1.5 mm to 2.2 mm, depending on the viscosity of the sheath solution and the desired throughput. Within this range, the sheath solution achieves sufficient exit velocity to generate the necessary shear forces for core entrainment while maintaining a stable, laminar flow profile. The invention further reveals that the sheath slit width must be proportionally increased when the sheath solution viscosity is reduced, to ensure that the exit velocity remains sufficiently high to overcome the lower momentum of the fluid. For instance, when the sheath solution viscosity is decreased from 760 cP to 280 cP, the sheath slit width must be narrowed from 2.2 mm to 1.5 mm to maintain the same velocity ratio and preserve core-sheath integrity. The invention also requires that the sheath slit be fabricated with high dimensional precision, with tolerances of ±10 micrometers, to ensure uniform jet formation across the entire length of the fixture. Any deviation in sheath slit width—whether due to tool wear, thermal distortion, or material creep—results in localized variations in jet morphology, leading to inconsistent fiber diameters and encapsulation efficiency. The invention further contemplates the use of a hydrophilic or hydrophobic surface treatment on the inner walls of the sheath slit to control wetting behavior and minimize polymer adhesion. The sheath slit must be aligned with the core slit along a single vertical plane to ensure that the core solution is uniformly enveloped by the sheath solution as it exits the fixture. The invention explicitly excludes any configuration in which the sheath slit width is less than 0.8 mm, as such narrow dimensions lead to excessive pressure buildup, jet instability, and frequent clogging, particularly with high-viscosity or particulate-laden solutions. Similarly, the invention does not permit the use of segmented or discontinuous sheath slits, as such designs disrupt the continuity of the sheath stream and introduce phase inconsistencies that degrade fiber quality. The sheath slit width must be optimized in conjunction with the core slit width and the flow rates of both solutions to ensure that the sheath-to-core exit velocity ratio is maintained above 2.0, which is the empirically determined threshold for stable core-sheath jet formation. The invention thus provides a systematic method for determining the optimal sheath slit width based on the desired throughput, solution viscosities, and core slit dimensions, enabling the reliable, high-throughput production of core-sheath fibers with consistent morphology and structural integrity.

### Sheath Slit Width and Core Slit Width

The relative dimensions of the sheath slit and the core slit constitute a synergistic design parameter that governs the hydrodynamic equilibrium necessary for the formation of stable, discrete core-sheath cone-jets. The invention recognizes that neither the sheath slit width nor the core slit width can be optimized in isolation; their relationship defines the velocity ratio between the two solutions at the slit exit, which is the primary determinant of core entrainment and jet stability. The ratio of sheath slit width to core slit width directly influences the sheath-to-core exit velocity ratio, which, as demonstrated by experimental data, must exceed a critical threshold of 2.0 to ensure consistent core-sheath fiber formation. When the sheath slit width is too large relative to the core slit width, the sheath flow velocity becomes excessively high, leading to jet instability, excessive thinning of the core, or even rupture of the core stream. Conversely, when the core slit width is too large relative to the sheath slit width, the core flow velocity becomes too high, overwhelming the sheath’s ability to entrain it, resulting in intermixing, phase separation, or complete loss of the core-sheath architecture. The invention establishes that the optimal ratio of sheath slit width to core slit width lies between 1.5:1 and 4.0:1, with a preferred range of 2.0:1 to 3.0:1. Within this range, the sheath solution exerts sufficient shear stress to entrain the core solution without inducing jet disruption or excessive core deformation. For example, when the sheath slit width is maintained at 2.2 mm and the core slit width is set to 0.8 mm, the resulting width ratio of 2.75:1 corresponds to a sheath-to-core exit velocity ratio of approximately 3.0, which consistently produces fibers with uniform core diameters and complete encapsulation. When the width ratio is reduced to 1.2:1, as in the case of a 2.2 mm sheath slit and a 1.8 mm core slit, the velocity ratio falls below 2.0, and core-sheath structure is lost. Similarly, when the width ratio is increased beyond 4.0:1, as with a 3.2 mm sheath slit and a 0.7 mm core slit, the extreme velocity differential causes the core stream to be pinched into an unstable, filamentous form that breaks up into droplets or fragments before solidification. The invention further reveals that the relationship between slit widths must be adjusted in response to changes in solution viscosity. For instance, when the sheath solution viscosity is increased, the optimal width ratio may be reduced slightly, as the higher viscosity enhances the shear stress even at lower velocity differentials. Conversely, when the core solution viscosity is increased, the width ratio must be increased to compensate for the greater resistance to entrainment. The invention provides a method for calculating the optimal slit width ratio based on the viscosities of the two solutions, the desired flow rates, and the target fiber morphology. This method involves determining the exit velocity of each solution using the formula v = Q/A, where Q is the volumetric flow rate and A is the cross-sectional area of the slit, and then adjusting the slit widths until the ratio of sheath-to-core exit velocity exceeds 2.0. The invention explicitly excludes any configuration in which the sheath slit width is less than or equal to the core slit width, as such configurations invariably result in the failure of core entrainment and the formation of blended or non-core-sheath fibers. The invention also excludes any configuration in which the slit widths are not parallel or are misaligned along the vertical axis, as such geometrical imperfections introduce asymmetries in the flow field that lead to irregular jet formation and fiber defects. The slit widths must be maintained with high precision along the entire length of the fixture, with dimensional tolerances of ±10 micrometers, to ensure uniform jet formation across all electrospinning sites. The invention further contemplates the use of a single, monolithic slit-surface fixture fabricated from a non-conductive, chemically inert material such as polytetrafluoroethylene, polyetheretherketone, or ceramic, to ensure dimensional stability under prolonged exposure to organic solvents and high electric fields. The invention thus provides a novel and non-obvious approach to the design of slit-surface electrospinning fixtures by recognizing that the relative dimensions of the sheath and core slits are not independent variables but are interdependent parameters that must be co-optimized to achieve stable, high-throughput core-sheath fiber production. This systematic approach to slit geometry represents a significant advancement over prior art, which has focused on individual parameters without accounting for their synergistic interaction.

### Core Slit Height

The height of the core slit is a critical, yet previously unappreciated, geometric parameter that governs the three-dimensional flow dynamics of the core solution as it exits the slit-surface fixture and interacts with the sheath solution. While prior electrospinning systems have focused exclusively on slit width as the primary geometric variable, the invention demonstrates that the height of the core slit—defined as the dimension perpendicular to the direction of flow and orthogonal to the slit width—plays a decisive role in determining the stability, uniformity, and morphology of the core-sheath jet. The core slit height influences the velocity profile of the core solution across the vertical plane of the slit, which in turn affects how the sheath solution entrains the core material. If the core slit height is too great, the core solution exits with a broad, laminar profile that is susceptible to lateral spreading and mixing with the sheath solution, particularly at the upper and lower edges of the slit. This results in irregular core diameters, incomplete encapsulation, and the formation of fiber segments with exposed core material. Conversely, if the core slit height is too small, the core solution experiences excessive shear stress within the slit itself, leading to premature solvent evaporation, polymer solidification, or clogging at the slit exit. The invention establishes that the optimal core slit height lies within the range of 0.1 mm to 0.5 mm, with a preferred range of 0.2 mm to 0.3 mm. Within this range, the core solution exits with a thin, uniform sheet-like profile that is efficiently enveloped by the sheath solution without excessive lateral dispersion. Experimental data demonstrate that when the core slit height is increased from 0.2 mm to 0.6 mm, while maintaining constant width and flow rate, the resulting fibers exhibit a 40% increase in core diameter variability and a 30% reduction in encapsulation efficiency. This is attributed to the increased surface area of the core stream, which allows for greater interaction with ambient air and the surrounding sheath solution, leading to instability. Conversely, when the core slit height is reduced below 0.1 mm, the core solution experiences a pressure drop that causes solvent evaporation within the slit, resulting in polymer precipitation and clogging. The invention further reveals that the core slit height must be inversely proportional to the viscosity of the core solution; higher viscosity solutions require smaller heights to maintain adequate exit velocity and prevent stagnation, whereas lower viscosity solutions may tolerate slightly greater heights without compromising jet stability. The invention also requires that the core slit height be uniform along the entire length of the slit to ensure consistent fiber morphology across the entire electrospun mat. Any variation in height—whether due to machining imperfections, thermal expansion, or mechanical deformation—results in localized variations in core diameter and encapsulation efficiency, leading to non-uniform fiber mats that are unsuitable for commercial applications. The invention further contemplates the use of precision fabrication techniques such as laser micromachining or photolithographic etching to achieve the required dimensional tolerances of ±5 micrometers. The core slit height must be maintained in a fixed relationship with the core slit width to define the aspect ratio of the slit, which the invention determines must be between 1:1 and 5:1, with a preferred range of 2:1 to 3:1. This aspect ratio ensures that the core solution exits as a thin, planar stream that is easily entrained by the sheath solution without excessive lateral spreading. The invention explicitly excludes any configuration in which the core slit height exceeds 0.6 mm, as such dimensions invariably lead to the loss of core-sheath structure due to excessive interfacial mixing. Similarly, the invention does not permit the use of tapered, conical, or curved core slits, as such geometries introduce non-uniform velocity profiles that disrupt the symmetry of the jet formation. The core slit must be a straight, rectangular channel with parallel walls to ensure laminar, uniform flow. The invention further recognizes that the core slit height interacts with the sheath slit height to define the vertical alignment of the two solutions; the sheath slit height must be equal to or slightly greater than the core slit height to ensure complete envelopment of the core stream. If the sheath slit height is significantly greater than the core slit height, the sheath solution may flow around the core stream rather than enveloping it, leading to incomplete encapsulation. The invention thus provides a novel and non-obvious method for optimizing the core slit height as a function of solution viscosity, flow rate, and slit width, enabling the reliable, high-throughput production of core-sheath fibers with consistent morphology and encapsulation efficiency.

### Viscosities of the Solutions

The viscosities of the core and sheath polymer solutions are fundamental physical properties that govern the hydrodynamic interaction between the two fluids at the slit-surface interface and determine whether a stable core-sheath cone-jet can be formed. The invention recognizes that viscosity is not merely a material characteristic but a dynamic, controllable parameter that must be precisely tuned in relation to the flow rates and slit geometries to achieve consistent fiber formation. The viscosity of the sheath solution must be sufficiently high to generate the shear stress required to entrain the core solution without causing jet instability or excessive drag. If the sheath solution viscosity is too low, the fluid lacks the momentum to draw the core solution into the jet, resulting in phase separation, core exposure, or complete failure of core-sheath structure. Experimental data demonstrate that when the sheath solution viscosity is reduced from 760 cP to 280 cP, while maintaining constant flow rates and slit dimensions, the core-sheath jet structure is lost, even though the sheath flow velocity exceeds the core flow velocity. This occurs because the lower viscosity sheath solution cannot generate sufficient shear stress at the interface to overcome the viscous resistance of the core solution. Conversely, if the sheath solution viscosity is too high, the fluid becomes too resistant to flow, leading to excessive pressure buildup, reduced jet formation frequency, and increased risk of clogging. The invention establishes that the optimal sheath solution viscosity ranges from 300 cP to 1000 cP, with a preferred range of 500 cP to 800 cP. Within this range, the sheath solution generates sufficient shear stress to entrain the core solution while maintaining laminar flow and stable jet formation. The viscosity of the core solution must also be carefully controlled; if the core solution viscosity is too low, it may be overly susceptible to entrainment, leading to excessive thinning or fragmentation of the core stream. If the core solution viscosity is too high, it may resist entrainment entirely, resulting in core expulsion or jet bifurcation. The invention determines that the optimal core solution viscosity ranges from 100 cP to 1000 cP, with a preferred range of 300 cP to 700 cP. The relative viscosity ratio between the sheath and core solutions is a critical factor; the invention requires that the sheath solution viscosity be equal to or greater than the core solution viscosity to ensure that the sheath solution can effectively envelop and entrain the core. When the core solution viscosity exceeds that of the sheath solution, the core stream tends to dominate the jet interface, leading to instability and loss of encapsulation. The invention further reveals that the viscosities of the solutions must be adjusted in conjunction with the slit dimensions and flow rates to maintain the critical sheath-to-core exit velocity ratio above 2.0. For instance, when the core solution viscosity is increased from 500 cP to 800 cP, the sheath solution viscosity must be increased from 500 cP to 900 cP to maintain the same entrainment efficiency. The invention provides a method for selecting polymer concentrations and solvent systems to achieve the desired viscosities, taking into account factors such as molecular weight, polymer concentration, solvent volatility, and temperature. The invention explicitly excludes any configuration in which the sheath solution viscosity is less than 200 cP, as such low viscosities invariably result in the failure of core entrainment. Similarly, the invention does not permit the use of solutions with viscosities exceeding 1500 cP, as such high viscosities lead to excessive pressure requirements, reduced jet formation frequency, and frequent clogging. The invention further contemplates the use of viscosity modifiers such as plasticizers, co-solvents, or rheological agents to fine-tune the viscosity of the solutions without altering their chemical composition or electrospinnability. The viscosity of each solution must be measured using a calibrated rotational viscometer under conditions that replicate the electrospinning environment, including temperature and shear rate. The invention requires that the viscosities of both solutions be maintained within ±10% of the target value throughout the electrospinning process, as fluctuations in viscosity lead to variations in jet morphology and fiber quality. The invention thus provides a novel and non-obvious approach to the selection and control of solution viscosities as a key parameter in the formation of stable, high-throughput core-sheath fibers, enabling the processing of a wide range of polymer systems previously considered incompatible with core-sheath electrospinning.

### Core Sheath Fiber Applications

The core-sheath fibers produced by the slit-surface electrospinning apparatus of the invention enable a broad spectrum of advanced applications across multiple industries due to their unique combination of high-throughput production, compositional versatility, and structural integrity. The invention is not limited to any single application but encompasses the use of core-sheath fibers in controlled drug delivery, tissue engineering, filtration media, functional textiles, microfluidic devices, energy storage systems, and photonic components. In the field of pharmaceutical delivery, the invention enables the encapsulation of small molecule drugs, peptides, proteins, nucleic acids, liposomes, and nanoparticles within the core of the fiber, with the sheath polymer serving as a protective barrier that modulates the release kinetics of the encapsulated agent. The high-throughput nature of the invention allows for the continuous production of drug-loaded fibers at scales sufficient for clinical and commercial use, overcoming the limitations of conventional needle-based systems that are incapable of producing quantities adequate for large-scale therapeutic applications. The invention further permits the fabrication of hollow fibers by dissolving the core polymer after electrospinning, leaving behind a porous sheath shell with a high surface-to-volume ratio ideal for catalytic supports, gas separation membranes, or biosensor platforms. The ability to fabricate hollow fibers at industrial throughput levels represents a significant advancement over prior art, which has been restricted to low-yield, labor-intensive methods. In tissue engineering, the invention enables the production of fibrous scaffolds with controlled porosity, mechanical strength, and bioactive component release profiles, where the core may contain growth factors or stem cell signaling molecules and the sheath provides structural support and biodegradability. The invention further permits the fabrication of bicomponent fibers composed of non-biodegradable and biodegradable polymers, such as nylon and PLGA, enabling the creation of composite materials with tailored degradation rates and mechanical properties for implantable devices. The invention also enables the electrospinning of materials that are otherwise non-electrospinnable, such as low molecular weight polydimethylsiloxane, by encapsulating them within a sheath of a compatible polymer. The resulting fibers retain the unique properties of the core material—such as flexibility, hydrophobicity, or thermal stability—while gaining the structural integrity and processability of the sheath polymer. After electrospinning, the sheath may be selectively removed to yield pure PDMS fibers, which are otherwise impossible to produce by conventional electrospinning. The invention further enables the fabrication of fibers with embedded particulates, such as silver nanoparticles for antimicrobial textiles, carbon nanotubes for conductive fabrics, or magnetic nanoparticles for targeted delivery, all with uniform distribution and high encapsulation efficiency. The high throughput of the invention allows for the continuous production of functional textiles with embedded therapeutic, sensing, or protective properties, enabling applications in wound care, protective clothing, and smart textiles. In energy storage, the invention permits the fabrication of core-sheath fibers with conductive cores and insulating sheaths for use in flexible batteries, supercapacitors, or fuel cell membranes. The invention further enables the production of photonic fibers with core materials that exhibit optical properties such as luminescence or refractive index modulation, enabling applications in fiber optics, sensors, and light-emitting devices. The invention thus provides a platform technology that transcends traditional material limitations and enables the scalable production of fibers with tailored functionality across diverse domains. The ability to fabricate these fibers at throughputs exceeding one liter per hour makes the invention uniquely suited for commercial manufacturing, where consistency, reproducibility, and scalability are paramount. The invention does not claim any specific application but encompasses all applications enabled by the production of core-sheath fibers via the slit-surface electrospinning apparatus as described herein.

### Advantages of the Invention as it Relates to High Throughput Open-Bath Monofiber Fabrication Systems

The invention provides a transformative advancement over conventional high-throughput electrospinning systems, particularly those based on open-bath or free-surface configurations, by enabling the continuous, stable, and scalable production of core-sheath fibers at unprecedented volumetric flow rates while maintaining structural fidelity and compositional control. Prior open-bath systems, such as those employing rotating wires, spinning drums, or liquid pools, have been limited to the production of single-component fibers or fibers formed from emulsions, which are inherently restricted in material compatibility and structural complexity. These systems rely on the spontaneous phase separation of immiscible fluids or the mechanical drawing of fibers from a liquid reservoir, which precludes the precise control over core-sheath architecture that is essential for advanced applications. The invention overcomes these limitations by introducing a slit-surface fixture that physically co-localizes the core and sheath solutions in a controlled, linear geometry, allowing for the formation of discrete, stable core-sheath cone-jets under the influence of an electric field. Unlike open-bath systems, which are susceptible to solvent evaporation, fluid turbulence, and inconsistent jet initiation, the slit-surface design ensures that the core and sheath solutions are delivered in a laminar, confined manner, minimizing interfacial mixing and maximizing encapsulation efficiency. The invention further eliminates the need for complex multi-nozzle arrays, which are prone to clogging, misalignment, and maintenance failure, by replacing them with a single, monolithic slit-surface that can be scaled in length to accommodate hundreds of concurrent jets without increasing system complexity. The open-bath systems typically operate at flow rates below 100 mL/h, whereas the invention achieves throughputs exceeding 1 L/h, representing a more than tenfold increase in productivity. Moreover, the invention maintains this high throughput without sacrificing fiber quality, as the slit-surface geometry ensures uniform jet formation across the entire length of the fixture, resulting in fiber mats with consistent diameter, morphology, and encapsulation efficiency. The invention also eliminates the requirement for immiscible solution pairs, which is a fundamental constraint of open-bath systems, by relying on hydrodynamic entrainment rather than interfacial tension to form the core-sheath structure. This enables the use of a vastly broader range of polymer-solvent combinations, including miscible systems, aqueous systems, and low molecular weight materials that are incompatible with emulsion-based approaches. The invention further provides superior process stability, as the slit-surface fixture is resistant to clogging, does not require frequent cleaning, and can operate continuously for extended periods without degradation in performance. In contrast, open-bath systems suffer from solvent evaporation, pool level fluctuations, and contamination from ambient particulates, all of which lead to inconsistent fiber production and frequent downtime. The invention also enables the production of hollow fibers, bicomponent fibers, and fibers encapsulating sensitive bioactive agents with high fidelity, which is not feasible with open-bath systems due to their lack of structural control. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in high-throughput electrospinning, moving from passive, uncontrolled fluid interfaces to an active, engineered hydrodynamic system that enables the industrial-scale production of complex, multi-component fibers. The advantages of the invention are not incremental but fundamental, as it is the first system capable of combining the high throughput of open-bath methods with the structural precision of coaxial needle systems, thereby fulfilling the long-standing promise of electrospun core-sheath fibers for commercial applications.
