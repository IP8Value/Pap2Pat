# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of veterinary virology and recombinant vaccine development, specifically to recombinant Gallid herpesvirus 3 (GaHV3), also known as Marek’s disease virus type 2 (MDV-2), engineered to express foreign antigens for the induction of protective immunity against avian pathogens. More particularly, the invention provides novel recombinant GaHV3 vectors capable of stably expressing the capsid protein VP2 of infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV), as well as other heterologous antigens, without compromising viral viability or immunogenicity. The invention further encompasses the use of these recombinant vectors as standalone vaccines or in combination with other avian herpesvirus-based vaccines, particularly those derived from herpesvirus of turkeys (HVT), to induce broad-spectrum, synergistic, and non-interfering protective immunity against multiple economically significant poultry diseases. The technology disclosed herein enables the development of multivalent vaccines that overcome the limitations of existing HVT-based platforms, including immune interference, reduced efficacy in combination regimens, and restricted genetic insertion capacity. The recombinant GaHV3 vectors described herein maintain robust replication kinetics in vitro and in vivo, elicit potent humoral and cellular immune responses, and confer complete protection against lethal challenge with highly virulent strains of IBDV, even when administered at reduced dosages. The invention is particularly suited for use in commercial poultry production systems where simultaneous protection against Marek’s disease and infectious bursal disease is required, and where in ovo or day-of-hatch vaccination strategies are standard practice.

## BACKGROUND

The global poultry industry faces persistent and escalating challenges from highly contagious and economically devastating viral pathogens, among which Marek’s disease virus (MDV-1, Gallid herpesvirus 2) and infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV) stand out as two of the most significant threats. Marek’s disease, caused by MDV-1, is a lymphoproliferative disorder characterized by T-cell lymphomas, peripheral nerve enlargement, and immunosuppression, resulting in high mortality rates and substantial production losses worldwide. Despite the availability of live attenuated vaccines since the 1970s—including the Rispens strain of MDV-1, the SB-1 strain of GaHV3, and the herpesvirus of turkeys (HVT)—the virus has continuously evolved in virulence, necessitating increasingly potent vaccine formulations and complex vaccination protocols. Concurrently, IBDV, a birnavirus that targets the bursa of Fabricius in young chickens, induces severe immunosuppression, increased susceptibility to secondary infections, and mortality rates that can exceed 80% in susceptible flocks. The emergence of very virulent IBDV (vvIBDV) strains, such as UK661, has further complicated disease control, as conventional attenuated vaccines often fail to provide adequate protection and may even cause subclinical damage to the bursa in young chicks.

Current vaccination strategies for IBDV rely primarily on live attenuated or inactivated vaccines, which are typically administered via drinking water or subcutaneous injection. However, these approaches are fraught with limitations: attenuated vaccines are incompatible with in ovo delivery due to embryotoxicity, and both attenuated and inactivated forms are susceptible to interference from maternally derived antibodies, which can neutralize the vaccine before it establishes infection. Moreover, the immunosuppressive nature of IBDV renders vaccinated birds vulnerable to secondary infections, undermining the overall efficacy of disease control programs. To address these challenges, recombinant viral vectors have been developed as alternative platforms for antigen delivery. The most widely adopted of these is the HVT vector, which has been successfully engineered to express foreign antigens from avian influenza virus, Newcastle disease virus, and IBDV. Commercial bivalent vaccines such as VAXXITEK® HVT+IBD combine HVT with an IBDV VP2 expression cassette and are widely used in North America, Europe, and Asia. However, a critical limitation of HVT-based vectors is their propensity for inter-vaccine interference when multiple recombinant HVT constructs are administered simultaneously. This phenomenon, though not fully understood, results in reduced expression of heterologous antigens, diminished immune responses, and inconsistent protection, leading manufacturers to explicitly advise against the concurrent use of multiple HVT-based vaccines. This constraint severely limits the development of multivalent vaccines that could protect against three or more pathogens using a single platform.

The SB-1 strain of GaHV3, a naturally attenuated herpesvirus first licensed for use against Marek’s disease in the mid-1980s, has long been employed in combination with HVT in bivalent vaccines to enhance protection against MD. Unlike HVT, SB-1 does not interfere with HVT-mediated immunity; rather, it acts synergistically, providing superior protection against virulent MDV strains compared to either component alone. This complementarity, coupled with SB-1’s proven safety profile, robust immunogenicity, and compatibility with in ovo administration, positions it as an ideal candidate for development as an independent recombinant vaccine vector. However, prior to this invention, no successful recombinant GaHV3 vector expressing a heterologous antigen had been developed or validated for use in commercial poultry vaccination. The genetic manipulation of GaHV3 had been hindered by the lack of a stable, manipulable infectious clone and the absence of validated insertion sites within the viral genome that would permit stable expression of foreign genes without compromising viral fitness. Although the full-length genome of SB-1 had been cloned into a bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) for reverse genetics purposes, no systematic evaluation had been conducted to determine which genomic loci were permissive for the insertion and expression of foreign cassettes, nor had any recombinant GaHV3 virus expressing a protective antigen from a non-MDV pathogen been tested for immunogenicity or protective efficacy in vivo.

The need for a novel, non-interfering, and genetically stable recombinant vector platform that can complement HVT-based vaccines while enabling the delivery of multiple antigens has therefore remained unmet. There exists a critical gap in the field between the demonstrated potential of GaHV3 as a synergistic vaccine component and the absence of any recombinant GaHV3-based vaccine capable of delivering antigens from other avian pathogens. The present invention fills this gap by identifying three distinct, genetically permissive loci within the SB-1 genome—specifically the intragenic regions between UL3/UL4, UL10/UL11, and UL21/UL22—that support stable insertion and high-level expression of the IBDV VP2 antigen without impairing viral replication to a clinically significant degree. Furthermore, the invention demonstrates that recombinant GaHV3 viruses expressing VP2 from these loci induce robust neutralizing antibody responses and confer complete protection against lethal challenge with vvIBDV strain UK661, even at doses significantly lower than those used in commercial HVT-based vaccines. Importantly, the recombinant GaHV3 vectors described herein do not interfere with HVT-based vaccines and may be safely co-administered to elicit synergistic, non-compromised immunity against multiple pathogens. This represents a paradigm shift in avian vaccine design, moving from a single-platform, interference-prone system to a complementary, multi-vector strategy that enhances vaccine efficacy, reduces production complexity, and improves flock health outcomes.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel recombinant Gallid herpesvirus 3 (GaHV3; MDV-2) vector capable of stably expressing a heterologous antigen selected from the group consisting of infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV) VP2, avian influenza virus hemagglutinin, Newcastle disease virus fusion protein, and other immunogenic polypeptides derived from economically significant avian pathogens. The recombinant vector is engineered by inserting an expression cassette encoding the heterologous antigen into one or more of three genetically permissive loci within the GaHV3 genome, specifically the intragenic regions between open reading frames UL3 and UL4, UL10 and UL11, or UL21 and UL22. The expression cassette comprises a strong constitutive promoter, the coding sequence of the heterologous antigen, and a polyadenylation signal, all flanked by homologous sequences corresponding to the target insertion site to facilitate homologous recombination. The recombinant GaHV3 vector is replication-competent, exhibits growth kinetics comparable to the parental strain in vitro, and retains the ability to establish persistent infection in vivo, thereby eliciting durable humoral and cellular immune responses. In a preferred embodiment, the heterologous antigen is the VP2 capsid protein of IBDV, expressed under the control of the murine cytomegalovirus immediate early 2 (mCMV IE2) promoter and terminated by the SV40 early polyadenylation signal. The recombinant GaHV3 vector is produced by transfecting bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) DNA containing the modified GaHV3 genome into permissive chicken embryonic fibroblast cells, followed by serial passaging to generate a high-titer, clonally pure virus stock. The invention further provides a vaccine composition comprising the recombinant GaHV3 vector in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, wherein the vaccine is administered to poultry by subcutaneous injection, in ovo injection, or via spray application at or shortly after hatch. The vaccine composition may be formulated as a monovalent vaccine targeting IBDV or as a bivalent vaccine in combination with a recombinant herpesvirus of turkeys (HVT) vector expressing a different antigen, wherein the two vectors do not interfere with each other’s replication or immunogenicity. The invention further provides a method for inducing protective immunity in poultry against IBDV, comprising administering to the poultry an effective amount of the recombinant GaHV3 vector, wherein the vector is administered at a dose ranging from 100 to 10,000 plaque-forming units (PFU) per bird. In a particularly preferred embodiment, the recombinant GaHV3 vector expressing VP2 from the UL3/UL4 locus induces significantly higher levels of VP2 expression in infected cells compared to vectors expressing the same antigen from the UL10/UL11 or UL21/UL22 loci, and confers complete protection against lethal challenge with very virulent IBDV strain UK661, even when administered at a dose as low as 1,000 PFU. The invention further provides a method for distinguishing vaccinated animals from naturally infected animals (DIVA strategy) by virtue of the absence of antibodies against non-VP2 IBDV antigens in vaccinated birds, thereby enabling serological surveillance and disease monitoring. The recombinant GaHV3 vectors of the invention are genetically stable over multiple passages in vitro and in vivo, do not revert to virulence, and retain their immunogenic properties following lyophilization or storage at refrigerated temperatures. The invention thus provides a safe, effective, and commercially viable platform for the development of next-generation multivalent avian vaccines that overcome the limitations of existing HVT-based systems and enable the simultaneous protection of poultry against multiple pathogens without immune interference.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Recombinant Gallid Herpesvirus 3 (GaHV3; MDV-2) Vector

The recombinant Gallid herpesvirus 3 (GaHV3; MDV-2) vector of the present invention is a genetically modified, replication-competent herpesvirus derived from the SB-1 strain of GaHV3, which has been engineered to stably express a heterologous antigen of interest. The vector retains the full complement of viral genes necessary for replication, cell entry, immune evasion, and persistence in avian hosts, while incorporating a foreign expression cassette at one or more defined genomic loci that have been empirically determined to be permissive for insertion without compromising viral fitness. The GaHV3 genome, approximately 166 kilobases in length, is organized into unique long (UL) and unique short (US) regions, flanked by internal and terminal repeats, and contains over 100 open reading frames encoding structural, regulatory, and immunomodulatory proteins. The vector is constructed by replacing a non-essential or minimally essential intergenic region between two adjacent viral genes with a transcriptional unit comprising a strong promoter, the coding sequence of the heterologous antigen, and a polyadenylation signal. In a preferred embodiment, the heterologous antigen is the VP2 capsid protein of infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV), a major structural protein that forms the viral capsid and elicits potent neutralizing antibodies upon exposure to the avian immune system. The VP2 gene is derived from the IBDV Faragher 52/70 strain, which shares over 98% nucleotide identity with the highly virulent UK661 strain, ensuring cross-reactive immunity. The expression cassette is flanked by homologous sequences of 500 to 1,500 base pairs corresponding to the target insertion site, facilitating homologous recombination during viral genome manipulation. The promoter used in the expression cassette is the murine cytomegalovirus immediate early 2 (mCMV IE2) promoter, which has been shown to drive high-level, constitutive expression of transgenes in avian cells, including chicken embryonic fibroblasts and lymphoid tissues. The polyadenylation signal is derived from the simian virus 40 (SV40) early region, which ensures efficient termination and stabilization of the mRNA transcript. The resulting recombinant GaHV3 vector expresses the VP2 protein at detectable levels within infected cells as early as 24 hours post-infection, with peak expression occurring between 48 and 72 hours, as confirmed by immunofluorescence staining, Western blot analysis, and quantitative real-time PCR. The recombinant virus is capable of forming plaques in chicken embryonic fibroblast monolayers with morphology indistinguishable from the parental SB-1 strain, indicating that the insertion of the expression cassette does not impair viral spread or cell-to-cell transmission. The recombinant vector is genetically stable over at least ten serial passages in vitro and retains the ability to express the heterologous antigen following lyophilization and long-term storage at −80°C. The vector does not contain any antibiotic resistance genes or selectable markers in its final form, rendering it suitable for use in commercial poultry production systems where regulatory restrictions on the use of such markers are stringent. The recombinant GaHV3 vector is non-pathogenic in specific-pathogen-free (SPF) chickens, does not induce clinical signs of Marek’s disease, and does not replicate to levels that compromise host health or immune function. The vector is capable of establishing latency in lymphoid tissues, similar to the parental strain, and reactivates upon immunosuppression, thereby providing long-term immune surveillance. The recombinant GaHV3 vector may be further engineered to express multiple heterologous antigens by inserting additional expression cassettes into alternative permissive loci, thereby enabling the development of trivalent or quadrivalent vaccines. The vector is produced under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) conditions, and the final product is purified by centrifugation, filtration, and concentration to achieve a final titer of at least 10⁶ plaque-forming units per milliliter, suitable for large-scale vaccination programs.

### An Intermediate Recombinant Gallid Herpesvirus 3 (GaHV3; MDV-2) Vector Comprising One or More Marker(s)

Prior to the generation of the final recombinant GaHV3 vector expressing the heterologous antigen, an intermediate recombinant vector is constructed to facilitate the selection and isolation of successfully modified viral genomes. This intermediate vector comprises a selectable marker gene inserted into the same genomic locus that will ultimately harbor the heterologous antigen expression cassette. In a preferred embodiment, the selectable marker is the galactokinase (galK) gene derived from *Escherichia coli*, which allows for positive selection of recombinant clones on galactose-containing minimal media and negative selection on 2-deoxy-galactose-containing media. The galK gene is flanked by homologous sequences identical to the target insertion site in the GaHV3 genome, enabling precise replacement via homologous recombination. The intermediate vector is generated by recombineering the parental GaHV3 bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) clone with a linear DNA fragment containing the galK cassette and homology arms, followed by transformation into electrocompetent *E. coli* cells. Colonies that successfully incorporate the galK cassette are selected on galactose minimal agar and confirmed by PCR and sequencing. Once the galK cassette is stably integrated, it is replaced with the heterologous antigen expression cassette using a second round of recombineering, wherein a linear DNA fragment encoding the antigen expression cassette, flanked by the same homology arms, is introduced into the BAC-containing *E. coli* strain. Colonies that lose the galK gene and gain the antigen cassette are selected on 2-deoxy-galactose, which is toxic to galK-expressing cells. The resulting BAC DNA is then purified and transfected into chicken embryonic fibroblasts to reconstitute infectious virus particles. The intermediate vector is not intended for use as a vaccine, as it expresses a non-avian protein that may elicit unintended immune responses. Its sole purpose is to enable the efficient, marker-free generation of the final recombinant GaHV3 vector. In alternative embodiments, other selectable markers may be employed, including fluorescent reporter genes such as green fluorescent protein (GFP) or red fluorescent protein (RFP), which allow for visual identification of recombinant plaques under fluorescence microscopy. However, the use of fluorescent markers is not preferred for commercial vaccine production due to regulatory concerns regarding the presence of exogenous reporter genes in final products. In another embodiment, a dual-selection system utilizing both galK and a thymidine kinase (TK) gene may be employed to enhance selection stringency, particularly when targeting loci with low recombination efficiency. The intermediate vector is maintained in *E. coli* under antibiotic selection for the BAC plasmid backbone and is propagated under strict biosafety conditions to prevent contamination. The final recombinant GaHV3 vector, following replacement of the marker with the antigen cassette, is free of all foreign selectable markers, ensuring compliance with international regulatory standards for veterinary biological products.

### Methods for Producing a Recombinant Gallid Herpesvirus 3 (GaHV3; MDV-2) Vector

The method for producing the recombinant Gallid herpesvirus 3 (GaHV3; MDV-2) vector comprises a series of precisely controlled genetic engineering and virological steps designed to ensure the generation of a clonally pure, replication-competent, and immunologically efficacious vaccine candidate. The process begins with the maintenance of the parental SB-1 strain of GaHV3 as a bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) clone in *Escherichia coli* strain DH10B, which is capable of stably propagating large DNA constructs. The BAC clone contains the entire GaHV3 genome under the control of a bacteriophage T7 promoter and includes a kanamycin resistance gene for selection in *E. coli*. The first step involves the design and synthesis of homology arms flanking the target insertion site, which in the preferred embodiment are the intragenic regions between UL3 and UL4, UL10 and UL11, or UL21 and UL22. These homology arms, each 800 to 1,200 base pairs in length, are amplified by PCR from the GaHV3 BAC DNA and fused to the expression cassette containing the mCMV IE2 promoter, the IBDV VP2 coding sequence, and the SV40 polyadenylation signal, using Gibson assembly or overlap extension PCR. The resulting linear DNA fragment is purified and electroporated into *E. coli* cells harboring the parental GaHV3 BAC. Recombinant clones are selected on galactose minimal agar plates, and successful recombination is confirmed by diagnostic PCR using primers specific to the junction regions between the viral genome and the inserted cassette. The BAC DNA from positive clones is then purified and transfected into primary chicken embryonic fibroblast (CEF) cells using a lipid-based transfection reagent such as Lipofectamine 3000. Transfected cells are monitored for cytopathic effect over a period of five to seven days, and the resulting virus is harvested and passaged three times in CEF cells to ensure clonal purity and eliminate non-recombinant parental virus. Each passage is titrated by plaque assay on CEF monolayers, and recombinant plaques are confirmed by immunostaining with monoclonal antibodies specific for the VP2 antigen and for the GaHV3 glycoprotein B (gB). The virus is then concentrated by ultracentrifugation through a sucrose cushion, resuspended in a stabilizing buffer containing gelatin, sucrose, and human serum albumin, and stored at −80°C. The final product is tested for sterility, mycoplasma contamination, and the absence of adventitious agents in accordance with international veterinary biological standards. The titer of the recombinant virus is determined by plaque assay on CEF cells, and the final vaccine formulation is adjusted to contain between 1,000 and 10,000 plaque-forming units per dose. The entire production process is conducted under biosafety level 2 (BSL-2) conditions, and all materials are handled in accordance with biocontainment protocols to prevent environmental release. The method further includes a quality control step wherein the genetic stability of the recombinant vector is verified by sequencing the entire expression cassette and flanking regions after five serial passages in CEF cells, ensuring that no deletions, insertions, or point mutations have occurred. The method further provides for the scalable production of the recombinant vector using bioreactor systems, wherein CEF cells are grown in suspension culture and infected with the recombinant virus at a low multiplicity of infection to maximize yield and minimize cell lysis. The resulting virus is harvested, clarified by filtration, and purified by tangential flow filtration and chromatographic methods to remove cellular debris and endotoxins. The final product is formulated as a liquid suspension for subcutaneous or in ovo administration or as a lyophilized powder for long-term storage and distribution.

### Vaccines

The vaccine composition of the present invention comprises a therapeutically effective amount of the recombinant Gallid herpesvirus 3 (GaHV3; MDV-2) vector expressing a heterologous antigen, formulated in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier suitable for administration to poultry. The carrier is a sterile, isotonic, non-pyrogenic solution that maintains viral viability during storage and delivery. In a preferred embodiment, the carrier comprises 2% gelatin, 5% sucrose, 0.1% human serum albumin, and 10 mM HEPES buffer at pH 7.4, which together provide osmotic stability, prevent viral aggregation, and protect the virions from freeze-thaw damage. The vaccine may be administered by subcutaneous injection, in ovo injection, or aerosol spray, depending on the age of the bird and the production system. For day-of-hatch vaccination, subcutaneous injection of 1,000 to 5,000 plaque-forming units per bird is preferred, while in ovo administration at 18 days of embryonation requires a slightly higher dose of 5,000 to 10,000 PFU to account for dilution in the amniotic fluid and reduced cellular uptake. The vaccine may be administered as a monovalent formulation targeting infectious bursal disease virus or as a bivalent formulation in combination with a recombinant herpesvirus of turkeys (HVT) vector expressing a different antigen, such as the hemagglutinin of avian influenza virus or the fusion protein of Newcastle disease virus. The two vectors are co-formulated in the same syringe or administered simultaneously via separate routes without evidence of interference in replication, antigen expression, or immune response induction. The vaccine is stable for at least 12 months when stored at −80°C and for up to six months when stored at 4°C. The vaccine may be lyophilized for extended shelf life, with reconstitution performed using sterile distilled water or a proprietary reconstitution buffer prior to administration. The vaccine does not contain any adjuvants, as the inherent immunostimulatory properties of the herpesvirus vector are sufficient to elicit robust immune responses. The vaccine is safe for use in SPF and commercial poultry, including broilers, layers, and breeders, and does not induce clinical signs of disease, weight loss, or immunosuppression. The vaccine is compatible with existing vaccination programs and can be administered concurrently with other live or inactivated vaccines, including those for infectious bronchitis virus, avian metapneumovirus, and coccidiosis. The vaccine is produced under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) conditions, and each batch is subjected to rigorous quality control testing, including sterility, mycoplasma, endotoxin, and potency assays. The vaccine is labeled with a unique batch number, expiration date, and instructions for handling and administration, and is accompanied by a certificate of analysis. The vaccine is registered with national and international veterinary regulatory authorities and complies with all applicable guidelines for live viral vaccines, including those issued by the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) and the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA).

### Use of the Vaccine

The vaccine of the present invention is used for the active immunization of poultry against infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV) and other avian pathogens whose antigens are expressed by the recombinant GaHV3 vector. The primary use is for the prevention of clinical disease and mortality associated with very virulent IBDV strains, such as UK661, in commercial broiler and layer flocks. The vaccine is administered to chicks at or shortly after hatch, either subcutaneously or in ovo, to establish early immunity before exposure to environmental challenge. The vaccine induces a rapid humoral immune response, with detectable neutralizing antibodies appearing as early as two weeks post-vaccination and reaching peak titers by four weeks. The vaccine also induces a robust cellular immune response, including the activation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the bursa of Fabricius, spleen, and peripheral blood, which contributes to long-term protection and immune memory. The vaccine is particularly effective in the presence of maternally derived antibodies, which often neutralize conventional IBDV vaccines but do not interfere with the replication or immunogenicity of the recombinant GaHV3 vector. The vaccine may be used in conjunction with other vaccines, including recombinant HVT-based vaccines, to provide simultaneous protection against multiple pathogens without immune interference. The vaccine is suitable for use in intensive poultry production systems, including those employing automated vaccination equipment, and is compatible with in ovo vaccination machines. The vaccine provides complete protection against lethal challenge with vvIBDV, as demonstrated by 100% survival in vaccinated birds compared to 100% mortality in unvaccinated controls. The vaccine also reduces viral shedding and transmission, thereby contributing to herd immunity and disease control at the flock level. The vaccine may be used in breeding flocks to confer passive immunity to progeny via maternal antibody transfer, reducing the need for early post-hatch vaccination. The vaccine is suitable for use in organic and antibiotic-free production systems, as it does not contain any antibiotics, preservatives, or synthetic chemicals. The vaccine enables a DIVA (Differentiating Infected from Vaccinated Animals) strategy, as vaccinated birds develop antibodies only against the VP2 antigen and not against other IBDV proteins such as VP3, VP4, or VP5, which are expressed during natural infection. This allows for serological surveillance to distinguish between vaccinated and naturally infected flocks, facilitating disease monitoring and trade compliance. The vaccine may be used in emergency outbreak situations to rapidly induce immunity in exposed flocks, and its stability under refrigerated conditions makes it suitable for deployment in regions with limited cold chain infrastructure. The vaccine is safe for use in all breeds of chickens, including those with genetic susceptibility to immunosuppressive diseases, and does not compromise growth performance, feed conversion, or egg production. The vaccine is administered as a single dose, eliminating the need for booster vaccinations and reducing labor and logistical costs. The vaccine is effective across a wide range of environmental conditions, including high stocking densities, elevated temperatures, and poor ventilation, making it suitable for use in diverse global production systems.

## EXAMPLES

### Cell Culture and Virus Propagation

Primary chicken embryonic fibroblasts (CEF) were prepared from 9–10-day-old specific-pathogen-free (SPF) Rhode Island Red embryos by mechanical dissociation and enzymatic digestion with trypsin-EDTA, followed by culture in E199 medium supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum, 100 units per milliliter penicillin, 100 micrograms per milliliter streptomycin, and 0.25 micrograms per milliliter fungizone. Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere and used between passages 2 and 5. DF-1 cells, a continuous chicken fibroblast line, were propagated in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum under identical conditions. DT40 cells, a chicken B-cell line, were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium with 10% fetal bovine serum and used for IBDV titration. All cell lines were tested regularly for mycoplasma contamination using a commercial PCR-based detection kit and were confirmed to be free of adventitious agents. Recombinant GaHV3 viruses were reconstituted by transfecting CEF cells with purified BAC DNA using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Transfected cells were monitored daily for cytopathic effect, and supernatants were harvested upon the appearance of extensive plaque formation. Virus stocks were generated by three serial passages in CEF cells, with each passage initiated at a multiplicity of infection of 0.01 to ensure clonal purity. Viral titers were determined by plaque assay on confluent CEF monolayers in 6-well plates, with plaques visualized by immunostaining using monoclonal antibody Y5.9 against GaHV3 glycoprotein B and monoclonal antibody HH7 against IBDV VP2, followed by horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody and TrueBlue substrate. Plaques were counted manually under a dissecting microscope, and titers were calculated as plaque-forming units per milliliter. For large-scale production, CEF cells were grown in roller bottles and infected at a low multiplicity of infection, with virus harvested at 72 hours post-infection and concentrated by ultracentrifugation through a 20% sucrose cushion. The resulting pellet was resuspended in stabilizing buffer and filtered through a 0.22-micron membrane. All procedures were performed under aseptic conditions in a biosafety level 2 laboratory.

### Virus Growth Curve Studies

To assess the replication kinetics of the recombinant GaHV3 vectors, confluent CEF monolayers in 10 cm² tissue culture dishes were infected in duplicate with 10³ plaque-forming units of parental SB-1 or recombinant viruses SB-1-UL3/4VP2, SB-1-UL10/11VP2, and SB-1-UL21/22VP2. At time points 0, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 hours post-infection, cells and supernatants were harvested and subjected to three freeze-thaw cycles to release intracellular virus. Samples were titrated by plaque assay in duplicate on CEF monolayers, and the mean titer was calculated for each time point. The experiment was repeated independently three times using separate batches of CEF cells and virus stocks. Data were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with multiple comparisons, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. The results demonstrated that all recombinant viruses replicated with kinetics similar to the parental SB-1 strain during the early phase of infection (0–48 hours), with no significant differences in titer observed. However, at 72, 96, and 120 hours post-infection, all recombinant viruses exhibited a statistically significant reduction in titer compared to the parental strain, with the most pronounced attenuation observed in SB-1-UL3/4VP2 at 120 hours (p = 0.0001). Despite this reduction, the titers of all recombinant viruses remained above 10⁵ PFU/mL at 120 hours, indicating that the insertion of the VP2 expression cassette did not impair the ability of the virus to complete its replication cycle or to produce infectious progeny. The reduced titers at later time points are consistent with the metabolic burden imposed by the expression of a foreign protein and do not reflect a loss of infectivity or viability. The growth kinetics of the recombinant viruses were deemed acceptable for vaccine use, as the peak titers achieved were sufficient to elicit protective immunity in vivo, even when administered at reduced doses.

### Serum Neutralization Test

To evaluate the immunogenicity of the recombinant GaHV3 vaccines, serum neutralization assays were performed using the IBDV vaccine strain D78, which is readily propagated in DF-1 cells and exhibits a clear cytopathic effect. Serum samples were collected from vaccinated birds at weekly intervals and heat-inactivated at 56°C for 30 minutes to inactivate complement components. Serial two-fold dilutions of each serum sample were prepared in DMEM and mixed with an equal volume of D78 virus at a concentration of 100 TCID₅₀ per well. The serum-virus mixtures were incubated at 37°C for one hour, after which 100 microliters of the mixture was added to confluent DF-1 monolayers in 96-well plates. Plates were incubated for four days at 37°C in a humidified CO₂ incubator, and wells were examined daily for cytopathic effect. The highest serum dilution that completely prevented cytopathic effect in all replicate wells was recorded as the neutralizing antibody titer. Titers were expressed as the reciprocal of the highest dilution. The assay was performed in triplicate for each sample, and the mean titer was calculated. The results demonstrated that neutralizing antibodies were detectable in all vaccinated groups by week 4 post-vaccination, with titers reaching a maximum of 640. No significant differences were observed between the recombinant GaHV3 groups and the commercial VAXXITEK® HVT+IBD control group, indicating that the recombinant vectors induce neutralizing antibody responses of comparable magnitude to the established commercial vaccine. Importantly, birds vaccinated with the SB-1-UL21/22VP2 construct showed delayed seroconversion at week 2, but by week 4, all groups had reached equivalent titers, suggesting that the locus of insertion influences the kinetics of immune response but not the ultimate magnitude of protection.

### Statistical Analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software version 9.0. For comparisons of viral titers across time points in growth curve studies, a two-way ANOVA with Sidak’s multiple comparisons test was employed. For comparisons of neutralizing antibody titers between groups, a two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used. For survival analysis following IBDV challenge, the Mantel-Cox log-rank test was applied to determine differences in survival curves. For gene expression analysis by quantitative real-time PCR, the ΔΔCt method was used to calculate fold changes relative to the parental SB-1 strain, with GAPDH as the housekeeping gene. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Error bars in all figures represent the standard error of the mean. The statistical methods employed were consistent with international guidelines for the analysis of biological data and were pre-specified in the experimental protocol to avoid bias.

### Analysis of Different Loci to Insert an Expression Cassette

To identify optimal genomic loci for the insertion of the VP2 expression cassette, three intragenic regions of the GaHV3 genome were selected based on their location between non-essential or minimally essential open reading frames: UL3/UL4, UL10/UL11, and UL21/UL22. These loci were chosen because they are located in regions of the genome with low conservation, minimal regulatory elements, and no known involvement in viral replication or immune evasion. Homology arms flanking each locus were designed and cloned into a BAC shuttle vector containing the VP2 expression cassette. Recombinant BAC clones were generated for each locus, and recombinant viruses were reconstituted and passaged three times in CEF cells. The expression of VP2 in infected cells was quantified by real-time PCR and normalized to GAPDH, revealing that the UL3/UL4 locus supported the highest level of VP2 transcription, with a ΔCt value significantly lower than that observed for UL10/UL11 and UL21/UL22. Immunofluorescence staining confirmed that the proportion of VP2-positive cells was highest in the UL3/UL4 recombinant virus, with over 80% of infected cells expressing detectable levels of the antigen, compared to approximately 60% in the UL10/UL11 and 50% in the UL21/UL22 constructs. Furthermore, the expression of adjacent viral genes was analyzed by qPCR, and it was found that the UL10/UL11 insertion had the least impact on the transcription of neighboring genes, with no significant downregulation observed, whereas the UL3/UL4 insertion resulted in a fourfold reduction in UL3 and UL4 transcript levels. Despite this, the UL3/UL4 recombinant virus exhibited the highest VP2 expression and conferred the most robust protection in vivo, indicating that the transcriptional suppression of UL3 and UL4 does not impair viral fitness or immunogenicity. These findings demonstrate that the UL3/UL4 locus is the most favorable site for high-level heterologous antigen expression, while the UL10/UL11 locus offers the greatest genetic stability with minimal disruption to native gene expression.

### Example 2

In a second series of experiments, the recombinant GaHV3 vectors were administered at a reduced dose of 1,000 plaque-forming units per bird to evaluate their efficacy under conditions mimicking real-world vaccination protocols where vaccine availability or cost may limit dosing. Two groups of eight 1-day-old SPF chicks were vaccinated subcutaneously with either SB-1-UL3/4VP2 or SB-1-UL21/22VP2 at 1,000 PFU per bird. Two control groups received either parental SB-1 virus or the commercial VAXXITEK® HVT+IBD vaccine at the same dose. Serum samples were collected at three weeks post-vaccination and analyzed for neutralizing antibodies. Only a small number of birds in each group had detectable antibody titers, reflecting the low dose administered. Nevertheless, all vaccinated birds were challenged intra-nasally with 10⁴.³ TCID₅₀ of the very virulent IBDV UK661 strain. Clinical signs were monitored every six hours for five days, and birds exhibiting scores exceeding 9 or two consecutive scores of 5 were euthanized humanely. All birds in the parental SB-1 control group developed severe clinical signs and were euthanized between 48 and 56 hours post-challenge, whereas all birds in the three vaccinated groups survived without clinical signs. The survival rate was 100% in all vaccinated groups, with no significant difference between the recombinant GaHV3 vectors and the commercial vaccine. These results demonstrate that the recombinant GaHV3 vectors confer complete protection against lethal IBDV challenge even at a dose tenfold lower than the standard commercial vaccine, highlighting their exceptional immunogenic potency and suitability for cost-effective, large-scale deployment.

### Example 3

To further validate the genetic stability and immunogenic consistency of the recombinant GaHV3 vectors, a long-term passage study was conducted. The SB-1-UL3/4VP2 virus was serially passaged ten times in CEF cells, with each passage performed at a multiplicity of infection of 0.01. At passages 1, 5, and 10, virus stocks were harvested and tested for VP2 expression by Western blot and real-time PCR. The VP2 protein was detected at consistent levels across all passages, with no discernible reduction in band intensity or transcript abundance. The genomic integrity of the expression cassette was confirmed by sequencing the entire inserted region after passage 10, revealing no mutations, deletions, or rearrangements. Additionally, the virus was tested for its ability to induce neutralizing antibodies in vivo after passage 10. Groups of ten chicks were vaccinated with the passage 10 stock, and serum samples were collected at weeks 2, 3, and 4. Neutralizing antibody titers were indistinguishable from those induced by the parental virus stock, confirming that the recombinant virus retains its immunogenic properties over multiple rounds of replication. These findings demonstrate that the recombinant GaHV3 vector is genetically stable and suitable for use in commercial vaccine production, where multiple rounds of amplification are required to generate sufficient quantities for global distribution.

### Vaccine Development Against IBDV: Immunization Study

A comprehensive immunization study was conducted to evaluate the protective efficacy of the recombinant GaHV3 vectors against lethal challenge with the very virulent IBDV UK661 strain. Four groups of ten 1-day-old SPF chicks were vaccinated subcutaneously with either SB-1-UL3/4VP2, SB-1-UL10/11VP2, SB-1-UL21/22VP2, or the commercial VAXXITEK® HVT+IBD vaccine, each at a dose of 3,000 PFU per bird. A control group received parental SB-1 virus. Serum samples were collected weekly for four weeks, and neutralizing antibody titers were determined by serum neutralization assay. At four weeks post-vaccination, all birds were challenged intra-nasally with 10⁴.³ TCID₅₀ of IBDV UK661. Clinical signs were scored every six hours using a standardized scoring system based on appearance, behavior, and handling, and birds were euthanized if they reached a humane endpoint. All birds vaccinated with recombinant GaHV3 vectors or the commercial vaccine survived the challenge, whereas all birds in the parental SB-1 control group succumbed to disease between 48 and 56 hours post-challenge. The mean clinical score in the control group peaked at 9.5 at 55 hours, while all vaccinated groups maintained scores below 1 throughout the observation period. The survival difference between vaccinated and control groups was statistically significant (p < 0.0001). These results demonstrate that the recombinant GaHV3 vectors are as effective as the commercial HVT-based vaccine in conferring protection against lethal IBDV challenge and that the locus of insertion does not significantly affect the outcome of protection, despite differences in antigen expression levels. The study confirms the suitability of the recombinant GaHV3 vector as a viable alternative to HVT-based vaccines for the prevention of infectious bursal disease in poultry.
