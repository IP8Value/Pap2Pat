# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The evolution of wireless communication systems has been driven by the escalating demand for higher data rates, improved spectral efficiency, and enhanced user fairness, particularly in dense cellular environments where resources are constrained and interference is prevalent. The Third Generation Partnership Project (3GPP) Long-Term Evolution (LTE) standard was developed to meet these demands by establishing a packet-switched radio access network capable of delivering peak downlink data rates of up to 100 Mbps and uplink rates of 50 Mbps, while simultaneously improving throughput at cell edges and increasing overall spectral efficiency. Central to achieving these objectives is the packet scheduler, which governs the allocation of physical resource blocks (PRBs) across time and frequency domains to multiple user equipment (UE) devices within a single transmission time interval (TTI). The scheduler must balance competing objectives: maximizing aggregate system throughput, ensuring equitable access to limited radio resources, and minimizing energy consumption, particularly under conditions of high user density, poor channel conditions, or constrained transmit power budgets.

Historically, packet scheduling algorithms have been categorized into three primary paradigms: round robin (RR), maximum throughput (MT), and proportional fair (PF). The RR algorithm ensures fairness by cycling through UEs in a fixed sequence, granting each UE a turn to transmit regardless of channel conditions. While this approach guarantees equitable access, it often results in suboptimal spectral efficiency, as UEs with poor channel quality are allocated resources despite their inability to transmit data reliably or efficiently. The MT algorithm, conversely, prioritizes UEs with the best instantaneous channel conditions, typically identified through channel quality indicator (CQI) reports, and assigns PRBs to those capable of achieving the highest data rates. This strategy maximizes aggregate throughput but leads to severe unfairness, as UEs located near the cell center with favorable propagation conditions monopolize the available resources, leaving those at the cell edge—often the most in need of coverage—with minimal or no service. The PF algorithm was introduced as a compromise between these extremes, modifying the MT metric by dividing the instantaneous data rate by the historical average throughput experienced by each UE. This dynamic weighting mechanism allows UEs with poor recent performance, even if their current channel conditions are suboptimal, to gain higher scheduling priority, thereby mitigating the monopolization effect observed in MT scheduling. While PF improves fairness compared to MT, it does not explicitly account for the energy cost of transmission, and its performance remains heavily dependent on the accuracy of historical throughput estimates, which may lag behind rapidly changing channel dynamics.

In practical LTE deployments, packet scheduling is typically implemented in a two-phase architecture: a time domain packet scheduler (TDPS) followed by a frequency domain packet scheduler (FDPS). The TDPS operates at a coarser granularity, selecting a subset of candidate UEs based on higher-layer metrics such as buffer occupancy, delay requirements, quality of service (QoS) class identifiers, and HARQ retransmission status. This phase reduces computational complexity by limiting the number of UEs considered in the subsequent FDPS stage, where the actual assignment of PRBs occurs. The FDPS then evaluates the channel conditions of the candidate UEs across individual PRBs and allocates each PRB to the UE that maximizes the chosen scheduling metric, subject to power constraints and QoS priorities. In mixed traffic environments—where real-time (RT) services such as voice streaming coexist with non-real-time (NRT) services like web browsing or file downloads—the TDPS must further prioritize RT traffic due to strict delay and jitter constraints, reserving sufficient resources to meet guaranteed bit rate (GBR) requirements before allocating remaining capacity to NRT traffic. This hierarchical allocation introduces additional complexity, as the scheduler must simultaneously satisfy latency-sensitive constraints while optimizing the utilization of leftover power and bandwidth.

Despite the widespread adoption of these conventional scheduling paradigms, a critical inefficiency remains unaddressed: the implicit assumption that maximizing data rate per PRB is synonymous with optimal resource utilization. In reality, the transmit power required to achieve a given data rate varies significantly depending on the channel gain, modulation and coding scheme (MCS) level, and noise environment. For instance, a UE with a high CQI may be capable of transmitting a large number of bits using a high-order modulation scheme such as 64-QAM, but the power required to maintain reliable transmission at that rate may be disproportionately high compared to a UE with a lower CQI using a simpler modulation scheme such as QPSK. The latter may achieve fewer bits per PRB but at a fraction of the power cost. Conventional schedulers, including MT and PF, do not explicitly consider this power-per-bit trade-off, leading to scenarios where the system consumes excessive energy to serve a small number of high-rate UEs, while UEs with moderate but power-efficient channel conditions remain underutilized. This inefficiency becomes particularly acute in dense multicell deployments, where transmit power is a scarce and regulated resource, and where interference from neighboring cells further degrades channel quality, forcing UEs to operate at higher power levels to maintain connectivity.

Moreover, the performance of existing schedulers deteriorates significantly under conditions of high user load, where the total demand for PRBs exceeds the available capacity. In such saturation regimes, the power required to serve each additional UE increases nonlinearly, and the marginal gain in throughput from allocating a PRB to a high-rate UE diminishes rapidly. In contrast, allocating the same PRB to a UE with a lower but more power-efficient data rate can yield a higher net gain in system throughput per unit of energy consumed. This insight suggests that a scheduling metric based on the ratio of transmit power to transmitted bits—rather than on instantaneous data rate alone—could fundamentally improve the energy efficiency of the system while simultaneously enhancing fairness and overall throughput. Such a metric would inherently favor UEs that achieve a favorable balance between data volume and energy expenditure, thereby promoting a more sustainable and scalable use of radio resources.

The importance of power efficiency in wireless networks has gained increasing recognition in recent years, driven by environmental concerns, operational cost reduction, and the proliferation of battery-powered mobile devices. However, within the context of LTE downlink scheduling, no prior algorithm has formally incorporated the power-per-bit ratio as a primary scheduling criterion. Existing approaches either treat power as a secondary constraint—applied after resource allocation—or rely on heuristic power control mechanisms that operate independently of the scheduler’s decision logic. As a result, the potential for joint optimization of throughput and energy consumption through intelligent scheduling remains largely untapped. The absence of a scheduling framework that explicitly minimizes the transmit power required per transmitted bit represents a significant gap in the state of the art. This gap is especially pronounced in multicell environments, where interference and non-uniform user distribution create highly heterogeneous power requirements across the cell, and where the cumulative energy consumption of the base station directly impacts network scalability and sustainability.

Furthermore, the performance of current schedulers is critically dependent on the accuracy and timeliness of CQI feedback from UEs. Inaccurate or delayed CQI reports can lead to mismatched MCS selections, resulting in retransmissions, increased latency, and unnecessary power expenditure. While this issue affects all scheduling algorithms, it is particularly detrimental to MT and PF schedulers, which rely heavily on precise instantaneous rate estimates. A power-per-bit-based scheduler, by contrast, is inherently more robust to CQI inaccuracies, as it focuses on the relative efficiency of transmission rather than absolute rate values. Even with imperfect channel estimates, the scheduler can still identify UEs that require less power to transmit a given number of bits, thereby maintaining a degree of performance stability under imperfect feedback conditions.

This background underscores the need for a novel packet scheduling algorithm that transcends the limitations of existing paradigms by introducing a fundamentally different optimization objective: the minimization of transmit power per transmitted bit. Such an algorithm would not only improve the energy efficiency of the network but also enhance fairness by reducing the disparity between cell-center and cell-edge users, increase system throughput under saturation conditions, and provide greater resilience to imperfect channel state information. The following sections describe a novel minimum transmit power-based (MP) packet scheduling algorithm that achieves these objectives by redefining the scheduling metric to prioritize UEs that achieve the highest bit-per-watt efficiency, thereby establishing a new standard for power-aware resource allocation in LTE and beyond.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a novel minimum transmit power-based (MP) packet scheduling algorithm designed for use in wireless communication systems, particularly within the framework of 3GPP Long-Term Evolution (LTE) downlink transmissions, wherein the allocation of physical resource blocks (PRBs) to user equipment (UE) is determined by a scheduling metric that explicitly minimizes the ratio of transmit power to the number of transmitted bits. Unlike conventional scheduling algorithms that prioritize instantaneous data rate or historical throughput averages, the proposed MP algorithm evaluates each candidate UE and PRB pair according to the efficiency with which power is converted into transmitted information, thereby enabling a more sustainable, equitable, and spectrally efficient utilization of radio resources. The algorithm operates within a two-phase scheduling architecture comprising a time domain packet scheduler (TDPS) and a frequency domain packet scheduler (FDPS), wherein the TDPS selects a candidate set of UEs based on buffer status, delay constraints, and HARQ retransmission requests, and the FDPS then assigns individual PRBs to the most power-efficient UE among the candidates, as determined by the proposed metric.

The core innovation of the invention lies in the formulation of a scheduling metric that quantifies the transmit power required per bit of information transmitted over a specific PRB, taking into account the channel gain, noise variance, and the minimum channel gain necessary to support a given modulation and coding scheme (MCS) level. The metric is derived from the relationship between the actual channel gain of a UE-PRB pair and the minimum channel gain required to successfully transmit a specific number of bits, thereby defining an excess channel gain that reflects the margin of reliability beyond the threshold for successful decoding. This excess channel gain is normalized by the power required per bit, which increases exponentially with the number of bits transmitted due to the nonlinear relationship between modulation order and power efficiency. The resulting scheduling metric, expressed as the ratio of excess channel gain to the power-per-bit function, enables the scheduler to preferentially allocate PRBs to UEs that achieve the highest bit-per-watt efficiency, even if their absolute data rate is lower than that of other candidates. This approach inherently favors UEs with moderate channel conditions who can transmit reliably at lower power levels using simpler modulation schemes, thereby reducing the overall transmit power burden on the base station and improving the fairness of resource distribution across the cell.

In practical implementation, the proposed algorithm is integrated into the existing LTE scheduling framework without requiring modifications to the physical layer or the CQI reporting mechanism. The necessary computations for the scheduling metric are precomputed and stored in lookup tables based on standardized MCS levels and channel conditions, allowing for low-complexity real-time execution. The algorithm operates under the assumption that the evolved Node B (eNB) has access to accurate CQI reports for all UEs and that the maximum transmit power of the eNB is sufficient to support the required MCS levels, as is typical in LTE deployments. The MP algorithm is applied independently to both the TDPS and FDPS phases, ensuring that the power-efficiency criterion is consistently enforced throughout the scheduling hierarchy. In mixed traffic environments, the TDPS prioritizes real-time (RT) traffic to meet guaranteed bit rate (GBR) and delay constraints, and the FDPS then allocates remaining PRBs among non-real-time (NRT) traffic using the MP metric, ensuring that the residual power is consumed in the most efficient manner possible.

Simulation results demonstrate that the proposed MP algorithm outperforms conventional scheduling paradigms—including maximum throughput (MT), round robin (RR), and proportional fair (PF)—in all key performance metrics. Under high user density conditions, the MP algorithm achieves a statistically significant increase in average user throughput, surpassing even the MT algorithm by up to 18% when the cell is saturated with 25 concurrent users. This gain is attributed to the algorithm’s ability to extract more total information from the same power budget by favoring power-efficient transmissions over high-rate but energy-intensive ones. Furthermore, the MP algorithm exhibits superior fairness characteristics, reducing the disparity in throughput between cell-center and cell-edge users by distributing resources more uniformly across the spatial domain. In particular, the algorithm achieves a 70% improvement in cell boundary throughput compared to the RR algorithm, while simultaneously maintaining higher aggregate throughput than all other schedulers. The fairness improvement is quantified by the reduced concentration of throughput among the top-performing 5% of UEs, which in the MP algorithm accounts for less than 10% of total cell throughput, compared to over 20% in the MT algorithm. Additionally, the MP algorithm demonstrates substantial power savings, sustaining the same cell throughput as the MT algorithm while reducing transmit power by approximately 8 dBm, a reduction that translates directly into lower operational costs, extended battery life for mobile devices, and reduced electromagnetic emissions.

The invention further provides robustness against imperfect channel state information, as the scheduling metric is less sensitive to minor inaccuracies in CQI reporting than rate-based metrics. Even under degraded feedback conditions, the relative ordering of UEs by power efficiency remains stable, ensuring consistent performance. The algorithm is fully compatible with existing LTE standards, requires no changes to the physical layer, and can be implemented via software updates to existing base station firmware. Its scalability and adaptability make it suitable for deployment in future 5G and beyond systems, where energy efficiency and spectral sustainability are paramount. By fundamentally redefining the optimization objective of packet scheduling—from maximizing rate to minimizing power per bit—the invention establishes a new paradigm for resource allocation in wireless networks, enabling higher throughput, improved fairness, and dramatically reduced energy consumption without compromising system complexity or backward compatibility.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EXEMPLARY EMBODIMENTS

The present invention provides a detailed embodiment of a minimum transmit power-based (MP) packet scheduling algorithm for use in wireless communication systems, specifically tailored for deployment within the 3GPP Long-Term Evolution (LTE) downlink architecture. The algorithm is designed to operate within the established two-phase scheduling framework comprising a time domain packet scheduler (TDPS) and a frequency domain packet scheduler (FDPS), ensuring seamless integration with existing LTE infrastructure without requiring modifications to the physical layer, channel quality indicator (CQI) reporting mechanisms, or hybrid automatic repeat request (HARQ) protocols. The fundamental principle underlying the invention is the recognition that optimal resource allocation in a power-constrained environment is not achieved by maximizing the instantaneous data rate per physical resource block (PRB), but rather by minimizing the transmit power required to transmit each bit of information. This paradigm shift enables the scheduler to prioritize transmissions that yield the highest bit-per-watt efficiency, thereby enhancing system throughput, improving fairness across spatially diverse users, and reducing the overall energy burden on the evolved Node B (eNB).

The operation of the MP algorithm begins with the time domain packet scheduler (TDPS), which functions as a preliminary filter to reduce the computational complexity of the subsequent frequency domain allocation. The TDPS selects a candidate set of user equipment (UE) devices eligible for PRB allocation during a given transmission time interval (TTI), which, in LTE, corresponds to a single subframe of one millisecond duration. The selection criteria for the candidate set are derived from higher-layer metrics that reflect the QoS requirements and buffer status of each UE. In particular, UEs with real-time (RT) traffic, such as voice calls or video streaming, are given priority due to their strict delay and jitter constraints. The TDPS ensures that these UEs are included in the candidate set if their buffer occupancy exceeds a predefined threshold or if they have pending HARQ retransmission requests, which are automatically elevated in priority to maintain service continuity. Non-real-time (NRT) traffic, such as web browsing or file downloads, is also considered, but only after the RT traffic demands have been satisfied, in accordance with the hierarchical scheduling policy mandated by LTE standards. The TDPS does not assign PRBs; rather, it compiles a list of candidate UEs whose channel conditions, buffer states, and QoS profiles warrant further evaluation by the FDPS. This phase effectively reduces the number of UEs subject to the more computationally intensive PRB allocation process, thereby enabling real-time operation within the constraints of the TTI.

Following the TDPS, the frequency domain packet scheduler (FDPS) performs the actual allocation of individual PRBs to the candidate UEs selected in the prior phase. The FDPS operates on a per-PRB basis, evaluating each available PRB across the available bandwidth and assigning it to the UE that minimizes the transmit power required per bit of information transmitted. The scheduling metric employed by the FDPS is defined as the ratio of the excess channel gain to the power-per-bit function, where the excess channel gain represents the margin by which the measured channel gain exceeds the minimum required gain to successfully transmit a specified number of bits under a given modulation and coding scheme (MCS). The minimum required gain, denoted as g_min(b_s,n), is determined by the inverse relationship between the required signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and the number of bits b_s,n that must be transmitted over the PRB n for UE s. This relationship is derived from the Shannon-Hartley theorem and is precomputed for all possible MCS levels and bit counts, as defined in the 3GPP LTE specifications. The power-per-bit function, f(b_s,n), is derived from the minimum transmit power required to achieve reliable transmission of b_s,n bits, and is expressed as a function of the noise variance σ²_s,n and the minimum channel gain g_min(b_s,n), such that f(b_s,n) = σ²_s,n × 2^(b_s,n / C), where C is the channel capacity per subcarrier under ideal conditions. This function grows exponentially with the number of bits transmitted, reflecting the increasing power cost of higher-order modulation schemes such as 64-QAM compared to lower-order schemes such as QPSK.

The scheduling metric M(s,n) for a given UE s and PRB n is therefore formulated as:

M(s,n) = (g_s,n - g_min(b_s,n)) / f(b_s,n) / b_s,n,

where g_s,n is the measured channel gain for UE s on PRB n, and b_s,n is the maximum integer number of bits that can be transmitted reliably over PRB n under the current channel conditions, subject to the constraint that the excess channel gain Δ_s,n = g_s,n - g_min(b_s,n) is non-negative. The metric is designed to be maximized, as a higher value indicates a greater margin of channel reliability relative to the power cost per bit. The FDPS evaluates this metric for every candidate UE and every available PRB, and assigns each PRB to the UE that yields the highest value of M(s,n). In cases where multiple UEs yield identical metric values, the scheduler resolves the tie by selecting the UE with the smallest f(b_s,n)/b_s,n ratio, ensuring that the allocation favors the most power-efficient transmission among equivalent candidates.

The practical implementation of the MP algorithm is made computationally feasible through the use of precomputed lookup tables. The values of g_min(b_s,n) and f(b_s,n) are calculated offline for all possible combinations of MCS levels and bit counts, as defined by the 3GPP LTE standard, and stored in memory within the eNB. During runtime, the FDPS retrieves these values based on the CQI reports received from each UE, which indicate the highest MCS level that can be supported under the current channel conditions. The number of bits b_s,n is then determined as the maximum integer such that the corresponding g_min(b_s,n) does not exceed the measured g_s,n. The excess channel gain Δ_s,n is computed as the difference between g_s,n and g_min(b_s,n), and the power-per-bit function f(b_s,n) is retrieved from the lookup table. The metric M(s,n) is then evaluated using a simple arithmetic operation, and the PRB is assigned to the UE with the highest value. This process is repeated for each PRB in sequence until all available transmit power is exhausted or no further assignments can be made without violating the power constraint. The entire computation is performed within the one-millisecond TTI, ensuring real-time operation and compatibility with LTE timing constraints.

The MP algorithm is particularly effective in mixed traffic environments, where real-time and non-real-time services coexist and compete for limited resources. In such scenarios, the TDPS ensures that RT traffic receives priority, allocating sufficient PRBs to meet guaranteed bit rate (GBR) requirements and delay constraints. Once these requirements are satisfied, the remaining transmit power is allocated to NRT traffic using the MP metric. This two-tiered approach ensures that latency-sensitive services are not compromised while simultaneously maximizing the efficiency of the residual power budget. The result is a system that maintains strict QoS guarantees for critical applications while achieving higher overall throughput and lower energy consumption for best-effort services. This dual optimization is not achievable by conventional schedulers, which either prioritize throughput at the expense of fairness or apply fairness heuristics that do not account for power efficiency.

A key advantage of the MP algorithm is its inherent robustness to inaccuracies in CQI reporting. Unlike the maximum throughput (MT) algorithm, which relies on precise estimates of instantaneous data rates and is highly sensitive to feedback delays or quantization errors, the MP algorithm operates on a relative comparison of power efficiency. Even if the reported CQI is slightly overestimated or underestimated, the relative ranking of UEs by their power-per-bit efficiency remains largely unchanged, as the exponential nature of the power-per-bit function dominates the metric. This stability ensures consistent performance under imperfect channel state information, a common occurrence in practical deployments due to mobility, interference, and feedback latency. Moreover, the algorithm naturally mitigates the “near-far” problem, in which UEs close to the eNB dominate resource allocation due to superior channel conditions. By favoring UEs that achieve high bit-per-watt efficiency rather than high absolute data rates, the MP algorithm enables UEs at the cell edge—where channel conditions are poor but power efficiency is often maximized through lower-order modulation—to compete effectively for resources, thereby improving cell-edge throughput and reducing the disparity between users in different spatial locations.

The performance of the MP algorithm has been validated through extensive simulations conducted under a 19-cell multicell model with wrap-around, consistent with the 3GPP LTE simulation scenarios defined in standard literature. The simulation environment assumes a 5 MHz bandwidth, resulting in 25 available PRBs per TTI, and employs omnidirectional antennas with uniformly distributed UEs. Traffic is generated according to a Poisson arrival process, with admission control limiting the maximum number of concurrent users per cell to prevent excessive interference. Two traffic types are modeled: real-time traffic with a guaranteed bit rate of 64 kbps and non-real-time traffic with best-effort characteristics. The simulation results demonstrate that the MP algorithm, when applied to both the TDPS and FDPS phases (MP-MP configuration), achieves a 18% increase in average user throughput compared to the MT-MT configuration when the cell is saturated with 25 users. This gain is primarily attributable to the algorithm’s ability to extract more total information from the same power budget by favoring transmissions that require less energy per bit, even when their absolute data rate is lower. The improvement is most pronounced in the NRT traffic category, where the algorithm’s power-aware allocation allows more users to be served with the residual power after RT traffic has been accommodated.

In terms of fairness, the MP algorithm demonstrates a marked improvement over all conventional schedulers. The fairness metric, defined as the ratio of the throughput of the top 5% of UEs to the total cell throughput, reveals that the MP-MP configuration reduces this ratio to below 10%, compared to over 20% for the MT-MT configuration. This indicates a far more equitable distribution of resources across the user population. Furthermore, the normalized throughput distribution across UE indices shows that the MP algorithm produces a nearly uniform spread of throughput values, in contrast to the highly skewed distribution observed under MT scheduling, where a small number of high-SNR UEs dominate the throughput. Spatial analysis further confirms this improvement: when normalized throughput is plotted against the distance of each UE from the serving eNB, the MP algorithm exhibits a nearly flat distribution across the entire cell radius, whereas MT and PF algorithms show a steep decline in throughput with distance. This result demonstrates that the MP algorithm effectively eliminates the cell-edge throughput penalty, a longstanding challenge in cellular networks, by enabling UEs at the cell boundary to achieve comparable performance to those near the center through more efficient power utilization.

The energy efficiency of the MP algorithm is quantified by comparing the transmit power required to sustain a given cell throughput. The results show that the MP-MP configuration can maintain a cell throughput of over 10 Mbps with a transmit power of only 30 dBm, whereas the MT-MT configuration requires 38 dBm to achieve the same throughput. This represents a power saving of 8 dBm, which corresponds to a reduction in power consumption by a factor of approximately six. Such savings translate directly into lower operational costs, reduced carbon emissions, and extended battery life for mobile devices, as the eNB can reduce its transmit power without compromising service quality. The power savings are particularly significant in dense urban environments, where interference and high user density necessitate higher transmit power levels to maintain connectivity.

The MP algorithm is fully compatible with existing LTE standards and requires no modifications to the physical layer, CQI reporting protocol, or HARQ mechanism. The algorithm can be implemented as a software update to existing base station controllers, leveraging precomputed lookup tables and minimal additional computational overhead. The complexity of the metric evaluation is comparable to that of the proportional fair algorithm, making it suitable for deployment in current and future generations of LTE and 5G base stations. The algorithm’s scalability and adaptability make it applicable to other wireless systems, including Wi-Fi, LTE-Advanced, and future 6G networks, where energy efficiency and spectral sustainability are critical design objectives.

In summary, the minimum transmit power-based packet scheduling algorithm disclosed herein represents a fundamental advancement in wireless resource allocation by shifting the optimization criterion from data rate maximization to power-per-bit minimization. The algorithm achieves superior performance in throughput, fairness, and energy efficiency compared to all conventional scheduling paradigms, while maintaining full compatibility with existing LTE infrastructure. By enabling more equitable and sustainable use of radio resources, the invention provides a scalable solution to the growing challenges of network congestion, energy consumption, and user inequality in modern wireless communication systems.
