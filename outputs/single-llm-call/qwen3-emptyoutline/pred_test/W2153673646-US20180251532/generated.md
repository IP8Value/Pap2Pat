## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The regulation of programmed cell death, or apoptosis, is a fundamental biological process essential for tissue homeostasis, embryonic development, and the elimination of damaged or infected cells. Dysregulation of this process is a hallmark of numerous human diseases, including cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, autoimmune conditions, and ischemic injuries. Central to the intrinsic pathway of apoptosis is the BCL-2 family of proteins, which govern mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), the decisive event leading to cytochrome c release, caspase activation, and irreversible cell death. Among the pro-apoptotic members of this family, BAX plays a pivotal role as a molecular effector that, upon activation, undergoes conformational changes enabling its translocation to the mitochondrial outer membrane, oligomerization, and formation of pores that disrupt membrane integrity. In its inactive state, BAX exists as a monomeric protein in the cytosol, adopting a closed conformation in which its C-terminal transmembrane helix is sequestered within a hydrophobic groove formed by its core helices. Activation is triggered by interactions with BH3-only proteins such as tBID, BIM, or PUMA, which bind to a conserved surface groove on BAX, initiating a cascade of structural rearrangements that expose its N-terminal domain and enable membrane insertion. Despite decades of research, the precise molecular mechanisms governing the transition from cytosolic monomer to membrane-inserted oligomer remain incompletely understood, and no clinically approved therapeutics directly target BAX to modulate its activity.

Previous efforts to pharmacologically modulate BAX have focused primarily on inhibiting its pro-apoptotic function in diseases characterized by excessive cell death, such as stroke, myocardial infarction, and neurodegeneration, or enhancing its activity in cancers where apoptosis is suppressed. Small molecule inhibitors targeting the canonical BH3-binding groove of anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family members, such as BCL-2, BCL-XL, and MCL-1, have achieved clinical success, exemplified by venetoclax. However, direct targeting of BAX has proven exceptionally challenging due to its structural plasticity, transient activation intermediates, and lack of well-defined, druggable pockets in its inactive conformation. Traditional approaches relying on peptide mimetics or small molecules have yielded limited success, often resulting in low affinity, poor selectivity, or unintended activation of BAX rather than inhibition. Furthermore, structural studies of BAX have historically been constrained by the difficulty of stabilizing its monomeric, cytosolic form for biophysical analysis, as it readily undergoes spontaneous or induced conformational transitions upon purification or exposure to membranes. Consequently, there has been a persistent and unmet need for high-affinity, conformation-specific molecular probes capable of binding to the inactive, monomeric form of BAX without inducing activation, thereby enabling mechanistic dissection of its activation pathway and providing a platform for rational drug discovery.

Antibody-based technologies have emerged as powerful tools for interrogating protein conformational states and for developing therapeutics that target previously intractable proteins. Monoclonal antibodies and antibody fragments, such as antigen-binding fragments (Fabs), have demonstrated unique capabilities in recognizing complex, dynamic, and transiently exposed epitopes that are inaccessible to small molecules. In particular, phage display libraries have enabled the selection of antibodies against challenging targets, including intrinsically disordered proteins, membrane proteins, and conformationally labile states. However, prior to this invention, no antibodies or antibody fragments had been identified that bind specifically and with high affinity to the monomeric, cytosolic form of human BAX without triggering its activation. Existing antibodies targeting BAX, such as the 6A7 clone, recognize epitopes that are only exposed upon conformational activation, rendering them unsuitable for inhibiting the initial steps of BAX activation or for stabilizing the inactive state. Moreover, no structural data had been obtained to define the precise molecular interface between an inhibitory ligand and the inactive BAX monomer, nor had any agent been demonstrated to block the N-terminal activation site—a region critical for the binding of BH3-only activators and the initiation of conformational changes. The absence of such tools has severely limited the ability to probe the earliest events in BAX activation, to validate the N-terminal surface as a druggable site, or to develop inhibitors that prevent mitochondrial translocation—a key step in BAX-mediated apoptosis.

The field has thus been hindered by a critical gap: the lack of molecular reagents capable of selectively engaging the inactive, monomeric form of BAX at its N-terminal activation site to prevent its activation by BH3-only proteins. This gap has impeded both fundamental research into the structural biology of apoptosis and the development of novel therapeutics aimed at modulating BAX activity. While some studies have proposed allosteric inhibitors that bind to distal regions of BAX, such as the α3-α4 or α5-α6 loops, none have directly occluded the N-terminal trigger site, which is the primary site of interaction for physiological activators. Therefore, there exists a compelling and urgent need for novel, high-affinity, conformation-specific inhibitors of BAX that bind to its N-terminal surface, prevent activation by BH3-only proteins, and inhibit mitochondrial translocation—thereby providing not only a therapeutic strategy but also a transformative tool for mechanistic biology.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel class of high-affinity, conformation-specific antibody fragments that bind to the monomeric, cytosolic form of human BAX and inhibit its activation by blocking the N-terminal activation site, thereby preventing mitochondrial translocation and subsequent membrane permeabilization. The invention comprises a family of recombinant antigen-binding fragments (Fabs) derived from a synthetic phage display library, each capable of binding to full-length human BAX with nanomolar affinity, forming a stable, stoichiometric 1:1 complex without inducing conformational changes associated with activation. These Fabs specifically recognize an extended epitope on the N-terminal surface of BAX encompassing helices α1 and α6 and the intervening α1-α2 loop, a region critical for the binding of BH3-only proteins such as tBID, BIM, and PUMA. By occupying this site, the Fabs prevent the initial interaction between BAX and its activators, thereby suppressing the cascade of structural rearrangements required for mitochondrial translocation, oligomerization, and pore formation. This mechanism of inhibition is distinct from all previously described inhibitors of BAX, which act through allosteric mechanisms or target distal surfaces, and represents the first direct blockade of the N-terminal activation site of BAX.

One embodiment of the invention is a Fab fragment designated 3G11, which exhibits a binding affinity for monomeric BAX with a dissociation constant (K<sub>D</sub>) of less than 5 nM, as determined by biolayer interferometry and competitive ELISA. The 3G11 Fab binds to full-length BAX and to a C-terminal truncated variant (BAX ΔC26) with comparable affinity, indicating that its epitope does not involve the C-terminal transmembrane helix and that its inhibitory function is independent of the membrane-bound state. Structural analyses using hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry, nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, and computational docking reveal that 3G11 engages a contiguous surface formed by solvent-exposed residues in helix α1 (including Lys-21) and helix α6 (including Arg-134), as well as residues in the α1-α2 loop, which are known to undergo critical conformational displacement during BAX activation. Mutagenesis studies confirm that substitution of Lys-21 or Arg-134 with glutamic acid substantially reduces or completely abolishes binding of 3G11 to BAX and eliminates its ability to inhibit tBID-induced liposomal permeabilization and mitochondrial cytochrome c release. These findings establish a direct causal relationship between epitope engagement and functional inhibition.

The invention further encompasses a panel of fourteen distinct Fabs, each exhibiting unique complementarity-determining region (CDR) sequences and binding to overlapping epitopes on the N-terminal surface of BAX, with affinities ranging from 2.3 nM to 250 nM. Competitive binding assays demonstrate that the majority of these Fabs inhibit each other’s binding to BAX, confirming that they share a common binding site centered on the N-terminal activation region. Importantly, none of the Fabs activate BAX in the absence of activators, nor do they induce membrane permeabilization on their own, establishing their function as pure inhibitors. The Fabs are capable of inhibiting BAX-mediated liposomal permeabilization in a dose-dependent manner, with the highest-affinity variants achieving complete inhibition at sub-micromolar concentrations. In mitochondrial assays using Bak-deficient mouse liver mitochondria, the 3G11 Fab dose-dependently inhibits both the translocation of BAX to the mitochondrial membrane and the release of cytochrome c, demonstrating functional efficacy in a physiologically relevant context.

The invention further includes a method for identifying and isolating such Fabs using a synthetic phage display library with expanded diversity in the CDR-H3 and CDR-L3 loops, enabling the selection of high-affinity binders against the challenging target of monomeric BAX. The method involves immobilizing purified, monomeric BAX on solid supports, performing multiple rounds of selection under conditions that preserve the native conformation of BAX, and screening for clones that bind with high specificity and affinity without inducing activation. The resulting Fabs are expressed in bacterial systems, purified via affinity chromatography, and characterized using biochemical, biophysical, and functional assays to confirm their inhibitory properties.

The invention also provides a method for inhibiting BAX-mediated apoptosis in a cell or tissue by administering a therapeutically effective amount of a Fab fragment as described herein. This method is applicable to conditions characterized by pathological cell death, including ischemia-reperfusion injury, neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease, acute organ injury, and inflammatory conditions involving mitochondrial dysfunction. The invention further provides a method for screening small molecule libraries for compounds that compete with the binding of these Fabs to BAX, thereby identifying novel small molecule inhibitors that target the same N-terminal activation site. This approach enables structure-guided drug discovery for the development of orally bioavailable, non-peptidic therapeutics that mimic the inhibitory mechanism of the Fabs.

In addition, the invention provides a diagnostic or research reagent comprising a Fab fragment as described herein, conjugated to a detectable label such as a fluorophore, enzyme, or radionuclide, for use in detecting the presence or conformational state of BAX in biological samples, including tissue sections, cell lysates, or circulating cell-free components. The Fabs can be used in immunoassays, flow cytometry, or imaging modalities to distinguish between inactive and activated forms of BAX, offering a novel tool for monitoring apoptotic status in real time.

The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the targeting of BAX, providing the first molecular agents capable of directly and specifically inhibiting its activation at the earliest, cytosolic stage by occluding the N-terminal trigger site. This discovery not only enables the development of novel therapeutics for diseases of excessive apoptosis but also provides an unprecedented structural and functional tool for dissecting the molecular mechanisms of mitochondrial apoptosis.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Introduction

The regulation of apoptosis through the mitochondrial pathway is a tightly controlled process governed by the BCL-2 family of proteins, whose interactions determine whether a cell survives or undergoes programmed death. Among the pro-apoptotic members of this family, BAX serves as a critical executioner protein whose activation is indispensable for mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, the point of no return in intrinsic apoptosis. In its inactive state, BAX exists as a soluble monomer in the cytosol, adopting a compact conformation in which its C-terminal transmembrane helix is buried within a hydrophobic groove formed by its core α-helices. Activation is initiated by the binding of BH3-only proteins such as tBID, BIM, or PUMA, which engage a conserved surface groove on BAX, triggering a series of conformational rearrangements that lead to exposure of its N-terminal domain, translocation to the mitochondrial membrane, oligomerization, and pore formation. The precise molecular events that govern this transition—from a stable monomer to a membrane-inserted oligomer—have remained elusive due to the transient nature of intermediate states and the difficulty of stabilizing BAX in its inactive form for structural analysis. Despite extensive efforts to identify pharmacological modulators of BAX, no clinically viable agents have emerged that directly target its activation mechanism, largely because of the absence of high-affinity, conformation-specific ligands capable of binding to the cytosolic monomer without inducing activation.

This invention addresses this fundamental gap by providing a novel class of recombinant antibody fragments that bind with high affinity and exquisite specificity to the monomeric, inactive form of human BAX, effectively preventing its activation by physiological stimuli. These fragments, derived from a synthetic phage display library, recognize a previously untargeted epitope on the N-terminal surface of BAX, encompassing helices α1 and α6 and the α1-α2 loop—a region directly involved in the binding of BH3-only activators and the initiation of conformational changes. By occupying this site, the fragments sterically block the interaction of activators with BAX, thereby suppressing the entire downstream cascade of events leading to mitochondrial permeabilization. This mechanism of inhibition is fundamentally distinct from all previously reported strategies, which have relied on allosteric modulation or targeting of distal surfaces, and represents the first direct blockade of the N-terminal activation site of BAX.

The significance of this invention lies not only in its therapeutic potential but also in its transformative impact on the fundamental understanding of BAX biology. Prior to this work, no molecular probe had been shown to bind to the inactive BAX monomer at its N-terminal trigger site without inducing conformational changes. The identification of such agents provides a powerful tool for probing the structural dynamics of BAX activation, validating the N-terminal surface as a druggable site, and enabling structure-guided drug discovery. The Fabs described herein are not merely inhibitors; they are molecular scalpels that allow researchers to dissect the earliest steps in BAX activation with unprecedented precision. Furthermore, the ability of these Fabs to inhibit mitochondrial translocation—a key step in BAX-mediated apoptosis—demonstrates their functional relevance in a physiologically authentic context, far beyond what can be achieved with synthetic peptides or small molecules.

The invention also provides a robust and scalable method for generating such inhibitors, leveraging synthetic antibody libraries with expanded diversity in the heavy and light chain complementarity-determining regions to overcome the challenges associated with targeting a conformationally labile protein. The selection process is carefully designed to preserve the native state of BAX, ensuring that only those binders that recognize the inactive monomer are isolated. This approach avoids the pitfalls of previous screening strategies that inadvertently selected for activators or binders to activated conformations. The resulting Fabs are produced in bacterial expression systems, purified to homogeneity, and rigorously characterized using a comprehensive suite of biophysical and functional assays, including biolayer interferometry, hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry, nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, size exclusion chromatography, and liposomal and mitochondrial permeabilization assays. Each of these techniques provides complementary evidence that the Fabs bind with high affinity, form a stable 1:1 complex, and inhibit BAX activation without inducing conformational changes.

The therapeutic implications of this invention are profound. Diseases characterized by excessive apoptosis, such as myocardial infarction, stroke, neurodegenerative disorders, and acute organ injury, represent significant unmet medical needs. Current therapeutic strategies have largely focused on inhibiting upstream regulators of apoptosis, such as caspases or upstream kinases, but these approaches often lack specificity and fail to address the root cause of mitochondrial dysfunction. By directly targeting BAX at its point of activation, the Fabs described herein offer a more precise and potentially more effective intervention. Moreover, because the Fabs act at the cytosolic stage, before BAX integrates into the mitochondrial membrane, they can prevent translocation entirely, thereby halting the apoptotic cascade at its inception. This is a critical advantage over inhibitors that target downstream events, which may be too late to prevent irreversible damage.

Beyond therapeutics, the invention provides a platform for the discovery of small molecule inhibitors. The high-affinity Fabs serve as molecular templates for competitive screening assays, enabling the identification of non-peptidic compounds that bind to the same epitope. This opens the door to the development of orally bioavailable drugs that mimic the mechanism of the Fabs but possess improved pharmacokinetic properties. The Fabs can also be used as detection reagents in diagnostic and research applications, allowing for the visualization and quantification of inactive BAX in tissues, cells, or biological fluids. Their conformation-specific binding makes them uniquely suited for monitoring apoptotic status in real time, offering a novel biomarker for disease progression or therapeutic response.

In summary, this invention represents a groundbreaking advance in the field of apoptosis research and therapeutic development. It provides the first molecular agents capable of directly and specifically inhibiting BAX activation at its N-terminal trigger site, thereby preventing mitochondrial translocation and membrane permeabilization. The Fabs described herein are not only potent inhibitors but also powerful tools for mechanistic discovery, enabling a deeper understanding of BAX biology and paving the way for the next generation of targeted therapies for diseases of excessive cell death.

### Materials and Methods

The invention was developed using a combination of recombinant protein expression, phage display technology, biochemical purification, biophysical characterization, and functional assays designed to interrogate the interaction between human BAX and synthetic antibody fragments. All procedures were performed under conditions that preserved the native, monomeric conformation of BAX to ensure the selection of inhibitors that bind to the inactive state. Human full-length wild-type BAX, as well as a C-terminal truncated variant lacking the last 26 amino acids (BAX ΔC26), were cloned into the pTYB1 vector (New England Biolabs) and fused at the N-terminus to a chitin-binding domain for affinity purification. The constructs were transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 CodonPlus (DE3)-RIPL cells, which were grown in Luria broth medium to an optical density at 600 nm of 0.8–1.0, at which point expression was induced with 1 mM isopropyl 1-thio-β-D-galactopyranoside for four hours at 30°C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5,000 rpm for 25 minutes at 4°C and resuspended in cold lysis buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.6), 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and a protease inhibitor mixture (Roche). Cell disruption was achieved using a microfluidizer, followed by ultracentrifugation at 45,000 rpm for one hour at 4°C to separate the soluble fraction from cellular debris. The supernatant was loaded onto a disposable gravity column containing chitin beads (New England Biolabs) pre-equilibrated with lysis buffer. After washing with lysis buffer and then with lysis buffer supplemented with 50 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), the chitin fusion protein was cleaved overnight at 4°C to release BAX. The eluate was concentrated using a Centricon spin concentrator with a 10-kDa molecular weight cutoff (Millipore) and further purified by size exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 75 column (GE Healthcare Life Sciences) equilibrated with gel filtration buffer (20 mM HEPES, 150 mM KCl, pH 7.2). Fractions corresponding to the monomeric peak, eluting at approximately 12 mL, were pooled, concentrated, and used within 48 hours of purification to ensure structural integrity.

A synthetic phage-displayed Fab library, designated Library F, was employed for the selection of BAX-binding fragments. This library was engineered to contain restricted diversity in CDR-H1 and CDR-H2, primarily encoding tyrosine and serine residues, while CDR-L3 and CDR-H3 were designed with expanded diversity encoding nine amino acids—tyrosine, serine, glycine, alanine, phenylalanine, tryptophan, histidine, proline, and valine—in a 5:4:4:2:1:1:1:1:1 ratio, with variable loop lengths to mimic the natural repertoire of human antibody CDRs. Phage panning was performed in two distinct regimes: one in which BAX was immobilized directly onto 96-well enzyme immunoassay plates (Corning Incorporated), and another in which BAX was biotinylated using the EZ-Link Maleimide-PEG2-Biotin Kit (Thermo Scientific, Pierce Biotechnology) and captured on streptavidin-coated plates. In both cases, 1 μg of BAX per well was used, and the library was cycled through three rounds of selection, with increasing stringency in wash conditions to enrich for high-affinity binders. After the third round, individual clones were isolated, grown in 2YT broth supplemented with carbenicillin and M13K07 helper phage, and culture supernatants were screened for binding to immobilized BAX using direct phage ELISA. Clones exhibiting signal intensities at least tenfold higher than background (3% BSA) were selected for DNA sequencing and further characterization.

The phage display vectors for the selected clones were converted into Fab expression vectors by introducing a stop codon and a hexahistidine tag upstream of the P3 gene fusion. Fabs were expressed periplasmically in E. coli BL21(DE3) cells grown in low-phosphate media at 30°C for 18–22 hours. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and lysed using BugBuster lysis reagent (Novagen), and the lysate was clarified by centrifugation. The supernatant was applied to a nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) resin (Qiagen) equilibrated with TBS buffer (pH 7.0), washed with 20 column volumes of TBS containing 20 mM imidazole, and eluted with TBS containing 250 mM imidazole. The eluted Fabs were dialyzed into PBS (pH 8.0) and further purified by protein A affinity chromatography (Pierce Thermo Scientific). Bound Fabs were washed with PBS (pH 8.0, 15 column volumes) and eluted with 100 mM glycine (pH 2.0), immediately neutralized with 1 M Tris buffer (pH 8.0), and buffer-exchanged into PBS (pH 7.0). Protein concentrations were determined by absorbance at 280 nm using extinction coefficients calculated from the amino acid sequences using ExPASy.

Direct binding ELISAs were performed by immobilizing BAX (1 μg/well) on 96-well plates at room temperature for one hour or overnight at 4°C. Wells were blocked with PBS containing 3% bovine serum albumin for 1.5 hours at room temperature. Fabs were diluted in PBS (pH 7.0) and incubated for one hour, followed by washing and incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-FLAG M2 antibody (binding to the FLAG tag at the C-terminus of the Fab light chain). After additional washing, the substrate 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine was added, and the reaction was quenched with 0.5 M sulfuric acid. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured, and dose-response curves were fitted using a four-parameter logistic equation in GraphPad Prism to determine EC<sub>50</sub> values. Competitive binding ELISAs were performed in two formats: one in which biotinylated 3G11 (b3G11) was competed with increasing concentrations of unlabeled Fabs, and another in which phage-displayed Fabs were competed with soluble Fab proteins. In the first format, mixtures of 50 nM b3G11 and varying concentrations of unlabeled Fabs were added to immobilized BAX, and binding of b3G11 was detected using horseradish peroxidase-streptavidin conjugate. In the second format, phage-displayed Fabs (titer ~10<sup>12</sup> pfu/mL) were mixed with 100 nM soluble Fabs and added to immobilized BAX, and binding of phage was detected using horseradish peroxidase-anti-M13 antibody conjugate. IC<sub>50</sub> values were calculated from the inhibition curves.

Binding kinetics were determined using the ForteBio BLItz system (Pall Corp.) with Ni-NTA biosensors. Fabs were immobilized at a concentration of 10 μg/mL in PBS (pH 7.0), followed by association with BAX at concentrations ranging from 30 to 200 nM and subsequent dissociation in buffer. Global fitting of the association and dissociation phases was performed to derive association rate constants (k<sub>a</sub>), dissociation rate constants (k<sub>d</sub>), and equilibrium dissociation constants (K<sub>D</sub>). Size exclusion chromatography was performed using Superdex 75 10/300 GL and Superdex 200 10/300 GL columns (GE Healthcare Life Sciences) equilibrated with 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.2) and 150 mM KCl. Proteins were injected at concentrations of 1–2 mg/mL, and elution profiles were analyzed to determine complex stoichiometry and stability. Liposome permeabilization assays were conducted using liposomes composed of phosphatidylcholine (48%), phosphatidylinositol (10%), dioleoyl phosphatidylserine (10%), phosphatidylethanolamine (28%), and tetraoleoyl cardiolipin (4%), encapsulating the fluorescent dye ANTS and its quencher DPX. Liposomes were purified by gel filtration, and permeabilization was measured by fluorescence increase (excitation 355 nm, emission 520 nm) upon release of ANTS/DPX into solution. Assays were performed in 96-well plates with 400 nM BAX, 30 nM tBID, and varying concentrations of Fabs, with fluorescence measured over 90 minutes at 32°C using a Tecan Infinite M1000 plate reader. Percent release was calculated as ((F − F<sub>0</sub>)/(F<sub>100</sub> − F<sub>0</sub>)) × 100, where F<sub>0</sub> is baseline fluorescence and F<sub>100</sub> is fluorescence after 1% Triton X-100 treatment.

Mitochondrial translocation and release assays were performed using mitochondria isolated from the livers of Bak−/− mice. Mitochondria were resuspended in experimental buffer (125 mM KCl, 10 mM Tris-MOPS pH 7.4, 5 mM glutamate, 2.5 mM malate, 1 mM K<sub>3</sub>PO<sub>4</sub>, 0.1 mM EGTA-Tris pH 7.4) and treated with 200 nM BAX and 100 nM tBID in the presence or absence of 3G11 Fab. After 20 minutes, supernatants and pellets were separated by centrifugation at 5,500 × g for 10 minutes. Pellets were washed with 0.1 M sodium carbonate (pH 11.5), centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C, and solubilized in 1% Triton X-100/PBS. Supernatants and pellets were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting using anti-BAX antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, catalog no. 2772) and anti-cytochrome c antibody (BD Biosciences, catalog no. 556433). Western blotting was performed using 4–12% NuPAGE gels, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked in PBS with 3% milk, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, and detected with IRdye800-conjugated secondary antibodies using the Odyssey infrared imaging system (LI-COR Biosciences).

NMR spectroscopy was performed on uniformly <sup>15</sup>N-labeled full-length human BAX in 25 mM sodium phosphate, 50 mM NaCl, pH 6.0, in 5% D<sub>2</sub>O, using a Bruker 600 MHz spectrometer equipped with a cryogenic probe. <sup>1</sup>H-<sup>15</sup>N heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC) and transverse relaxation optimized spectroscopy (TROSY) spectra were acquired at 25°C, processed using TopSpin, and analyzed with CCPNMR. Hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry (HXMS) was performed by diluting BAX (1.0 mg/mL) into D<sub>2</sub>O buffer (8.3 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pD 7.2) and incubating at 0°C for 10, 100, and 1000 seconds. Reactions were quenched with ice-cold quench solution (0.8% formic acid, 16% glycerol, 0.08 M guanidine hydrochloride), frozen on dry ice, and digested online using an immobilized pepsin column. Peptides were separated on a reverse-phase C18 column and analyzed using an Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometer operated in positive electrospray ionization mode. Data were analyzed using Proteome Discoverer and HDExaminer software, with deuteration levels corrected for back-exchange. Structure calculations were performed using the HADDOCK web server with ambiguous interaction restraints derived from HXMS data and CDR residues of 3G11. Active residues for BAX were defined as those with significant protection from deuterium exchange and solvent accessibility greater than 50%, including residues 19–45 and 123–142. Active residues for 3G11 were defined as CDRH1 (26–32), CDRH2 (52–54), CDRH3 (96–101), CDRL3 (141–148, 165–168, 206–211). One thousand docking orientations were generated, refined, and clustered, with the most populous cluster (177 structures) used for analysis. Molecular models were rendered using PyMOL (Schrödinger, LLC). Mutagenesis of BAX was performed using site-directed mutagenesis to generate K21E, R134E, and R134E/K21E mutants, which were expressed and purified as described above. Binding affinity and inhibitory activity were assessed using ELISA and liposomal permeabilization assays.

### Results

The invention demonstrates the successful isolation and characterization of a novel family of high-affinity antibody fragments that bind specifically to the monomeric, inactive form of human BAX and inhibit its activation by blocking the N-terminal activation site. A synthetic phage display library, Library F, was screened against purified monomeric BAX under conditions designed to preserve its native conformation. After three rounds of selection, 14 distinct Fab clones were identified, each exhibiting unique complementarity-determining region (CDR) sequences with no detectable homology between any two clones, indicating that the library successfully captured a diverse set of binding solutions to BAX. These Fabs were expressed in bacterial systems and purified to homogeneity, and their binding affinities for BAX were quantified using direct binding ELISA. The EC<sub>50</sub> values ranged from 2.3 nM to 250 nM, with the majority of clones exhibiting single- or double-digit nanomolar affinities. The highest-affinity binders, 3G11, 3E8, and 2B1, displayed EC<sub>50</sub> values of 2.6 nM, 3.1 nM, and 2.3 nM, respectively, while the lowest-affinity clone, 2A5, exhibited an EC<sub>50</sub> of 250 nM. These results confirm that the selection process yielded a panel of Fabs with a broad range of binding strengths, all capable of recognizing BAX in its monomeric, cytosolic state.

Binding kinetics were further evaluated using biolayer interferometry, which provided direct measurements of association and dissociation rates. The highest-affinity Fabs, 3G11, 3E8, and 2B1, exhibited dissociation constants (K<sub>D</sub>) of less than 5 nM, consistent with the ELISA data. Notably, the 3G11 Fab displayed a K<sub>D</sub> of 3.8 nM, with a slow dissociation rate (k<sub>d</sub> = 1.2 × 10<sup>−4</sup> s<sup>−1</sup>), indicating a highly stable complex. In contrast, the low-affinity clone 2A5 exhibited a K<sub>D</sub> of 180 nM, with a rapid dissociation rate, confirming its weaker interaction. The correlation between ELISA and biolayer interferometry data validates the reliability of the binding measurements and establishes that the Fabs bind BAX with high affinity and specificity.

To determine whether the Fabs recognize overlapping or distinct epitopes on BAX, competitive binding assays were performed. In the first format, biotinylated 3G11 (b3G11) was used as a probe, and its binding to immobilized BAX was competed with increasing concentrations of unlabeled Fabs. All Fabs except 2A5 demonstrated significant inhibition of b3G11 binding, with IC<sub>50</sub> values in the low nanomolar range, indicating that they share a common binding site. In a second format, phage-displayed Fabs were competed with soluble Fab proteins, and binding of phage was monitored using anti-M13 antibody. Again, most Fabs inhibited each other’s binding, with the highest-affinity clones (3E8 and 2B1) showing the strongest inhibition across the panel. The exception, 2A5, exhibited minimal competitive inhibition, consistent with its low affinity and distinct binding mode. These results demonstrate that the majority of the 14 Fabs bind to a conserved epitope on the surface of BAX, suggesting that this region is a privileged target for high-affinity interaction.

Functional assays were conducted to determine whether the Fabs activate or inhibit BAX. None of the Fabs induced liposomal permeabilization in the absence of tBID, nor did they affect membrane integrity on their own. In contrast, when BAX was activated by tBID in the presence of liposomes, robust ANTS/DPX release was observed. However, the addition of high-affinity Fabs—3G11, 3E8, and 2B1—dose-dependently inhibited this release, with complete inhibition achieved at 2 μM. The low-affinity clone 2A5 showed no inhibition even at 2 μM, confirming that inhibition is directly correlated with binding affinity. Similar results were obtained in mitochondrial assays using Bak−/− mouse liver mitochondria. In the presence of tBID and BAX, cytochrome c was released into the supernatant, but this release was dose-dependently suppressed by 3G11. Furthermore, immunoblot analysis revealed that 3G11 prevented the translocation of BAX from the cytosol to the mitochondrial membrane, as BAX remained predominantly in the supernatant fraction rather than the pellet. This finding demonstrates that the Fabs inhibit not only the final step of membrane permeabilization but also the upstream event of mitochondrial translocation, which is a prerequisite for BAX oligomerization.

Structural analyses provided mechanistic insight into how the Fabs inhibit BAX. Hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry (HXMS) revealed that binding of 3G11 to BAX resulted in significant protection from deuterium incorporation in helices α1 and α6 and the α1-α2 loop, regions known to be critical for BAX activation. In contrast, other regions of BAX, including the C-terminal helix α9, showed no change in deuterium uptake, indicating that the epitope is localized to the N-terminal surface. Importantly, 3G11 bound with similar affinity to both full-length BAX and the C-terminal truncated variant BAX ΔC26, confirming that its epitope does not involve the transmembrane helix and that its inhibitory function is independent of membrane association. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy of <sup>15</sup>N-labeled BAX titrated with 3G11 showed a dose-dependent loss of cross-peak intensity without the appearance of new peaks, consistent with the formation of a stable 1:1 complex in slow exchange on the NMR timescale. The absence of chemical shift perturbations further supports that 3G11 binding does not induce conformational changes in BAX, but rather stabilizes its inactive state.

Computational docking using the HADDOCK web server, guided by HXMS data and CDR residues of 3G11, generated an ensemble of structural models that consistently placed 3G11 in contact with helices α1 and α6 and the α1-α2 loop. The most populous cluster (177 of 200 structures) showed that 3G11 engages a contiguous surface formed by solvent-exposed hydrophobic and polar residues, including Lys-21 in α1 and Arg-134 in α6. Mutagenesis experiments confirmed the functional importance of these residues: substitution of Lys-21 with glutamic acid (K21E) reduced the inhibitory potency of 3G11, while substitution of Arg-134 with glutamic acid (R134E) completely abolished inhibition. The double mutant R134E/K21E also failed to respond to 3G11, and binding affinity, as measured by ELISA, was drastically reduced. These results establish a direct causal link between epitope engagement and functional inhibition.

Importantly, the binding site of 3G11 overlaps extensively with the region recognized by BH3-only proteins such as tBID and BIM, as determined by prior structural studies. This overlap explains the competitive nature of inhibition: 3G11 prevents activators from binding to BAX by occupying the same site. The fact that 3G11 does not induce conformational changes in BAX, yet still prevents activation, further supports a mechanism of steric blockade rather than allosteric modulation. This is a critical distinction from previously described inhibitors, which bind to distal sites and suppress activation indirectly. The Fabs described herein represent the first agents capable of directly occluding the N-terminal activation site of BAX, thereby preventing the initiation of the entire apoptotic cascade.

### DISCUSSION

The invention described herein represents a transformative advance in the understanding and modulation of BAX-mediated apoptosis. For the first time, a family of high-affinity, conformation-specific antibody fragments has been identified that bind to the monomeric, cytosolic form of human BAX and inhibit its activation by directly occluding the N-terminal activation site. This mechanism of inhibition is fundamentally distinct from all previously described strategies, which have relied on allosteric modulation, targeting of distal surfaces, or stabilization of inactive conformations through indirect interactions. The Fabs described herein bind with nanomolar affinity to a contiguous epitope encompassing helices α1 and α6 and the α1-α2 loop, a region that is the primary docking site for BH3-only activators such as tBID, BIM, and PUMA. By occupying this site, the Fabs prevent the initial interaction between BAX and its physiological activators, thereby suppressing the cascade of conformational changes that lead to mitochondrial translocation, oligomerization, and membrane permeabilization. This direct blockade of the activation trigger site is a paradigm-shifting discovery that not only provides a novel therapeutic strategy but also offers an unprecedented tool for dissecting the molecular mechanics of apoptosis.

The structural and functional data presented here establish a clear causal relationship between epitope engagement and biological inhibition. Hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry, NMR spectroscopy, and computational docking collectively demonstrate that 3G11 binds to the N-terminal surface of BAX without inducing conformational changes, thereby stabilizing the inactive monomer. This is a critical distinction from previous attempts to target BAX, many of which inadvertently activated the protein or bound only to activated conformations. The fact that 3G11 binds with equal affinity to both full-length BAX and the C-terminal truncated variant BAX ΔC26 confirms that its epitope lies entirely outside the transmembrane helix and that its inhibitory function is independent of membrane association. This property is essential for therapeutic utility, as it allows the Fabs to act at the earliest stage of BAX activation—before it translocates to the mitochondria—thereby preventing the entire downstream cascade. The dose-dependent inhibition of mitochondrial translocation and cytochrome c release in a physiologically relevant system further validates the functional relevance of this mechanism.

The competitive binding assays provide additional evidence that the Fabs share a common epitope. The fact that nearly all of the 14 Fabs inhibit each other’s binding to BAX indicates that they recognize overlapping or identical regions on the N-terminal surface. This convergence of diverse antibody sequences onto a single site underscores the biological importance of this region as a druggable target. The high-affinity Fabs, particularly 3G11, 3E8, and 2B1, exhibit IC<sub>50</sub> values in the low nanomolar range, demonstrating that this epitope is accessible to high-affinity ligands despite its dynamic nature. The inability of the low-affinity clone 2A5 to compete effectively with other Fabs further supports the notion that binding affinity correlates with epitope occupancy and functional potency. These findings suggest that the N-terminal surface of BAX is not merely a passive binding site but a highly optimized interface for protein-protein interaction, capable of accommodating diverse molecular architectures with high specificity.

The mutagenesis experiments provide definitive proof that the identified epitope is functionally critical. Substitution of Lys-21 and Arg-134 with negatively charged residues abolishes the ability of 3G11 to inhibit BAX activation, confirming that these residues are essential for Fab binding and that their interaction is necessary for inhibition. The fact that these same residues are known to interact with stapled BH3 peptides and BH3-only proteins further validates the biological relevance of the epitope. This overlap between the Fab-binding site and the physiological activator-binding site is not coincidental; it is the very basis of the inhibitory mechanism. Unlike allosteric inhibitors that suppress activation indirectly by stabilizing non-productive conformations, the Fabs described herein act as competitive antagonists, directly preventing the binding of activators. This mechanism is analogous to the action of monoclonal antibodies that block receptor-ligand interactions in immunology, but applied here to a conformationally dynamic intracellular protein—a feat previously thought to be intractable.

The therapeutic implications of this invention are profound. Diseases characterized by excessive apoptosis—such as myocardial infarction, stroke, neurodegenerative disorders, and acute organ injury—represent a major global health burden. Current therapeutic strategies have largely focused on inhibiting downstream effectors such as caspases or upstream regulators such as BCL-2 family members, but these approaches often lack specificity and fail to address the root cause of mitochondrial dysfunction. By directly targeting BAX at its point of activation, the Fabs described herein offer a more precise and potentially more effective intervention. Because they act at the cytosolic stage, before BAX integrates into the mitochondrial membrane, they can prevent translocation entirely, thereby halting the apoptotic cascade at its inception. This is a critical advantage over inhibitors that target downstream events, which may be too late to prevent irreversible damage. Furthermore, the Fabs can be engineered into full-length antibodies, bispecific molecules, or fusion proteins for enhanced pharmacokinetics, tissue targeting, or half-life extension.

Beyond therapeutics, the invention provides a powerful platform for drug discovery. The high-affinity Fabs serve as molecular templates for competitive screening assays, enabling the identification of small molecule inhibitors that bind to the same epitope. This approach bypasses the need for structural knowledge of BAX’s druggable pockets and instead leverages the validated binding interface of the Fabs to guide the discovery of non-peptidic, orally bioavailable compounds. The Fabs can also be used as detection reagents in diagnostic and research applications, allowing for the visualization and quantification of inactive BAX in tissues, cells, or biological fluids. Their conformation-specific binding makes them uniquely suited for monitoring apoptotic status in real time, offering a novel biomarker for disease progression or therapeutic response.

Finally, the success of this approach suggests that similar strategies can be applied to other members of the BCL-2 family, including BAK, BID, and other BH3-only proteins. The ability to isolate high-affinity antibodies against conformationally labile, intracellular proteins opens new avenues for targeting previously “undruggable” pathways. The methodology described herein—combining synthetic phage display libraries with rigorous biophysical and functional validation—provides a generalizable framework for the discovery of molecular probes against challenging targets in cell death regulation. In sum, this invention not only delivers a novel class of BAX inhibitors but also establishes a new paradigm for the targeting of dynamic, conformationally flexible proteins in apoptosis and beyond.
