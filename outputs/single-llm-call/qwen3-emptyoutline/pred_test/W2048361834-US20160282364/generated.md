# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The regulation of metabolic homeostasis in mammals is a complex, multi-organ process involving the coordinated action of hormones, enzymes, and signaling pathways that maintain glucose, lipid, and peptide balance. Among the key enzymes involved in this regulatory network, insulin-degrading enzyme (IDE) has emerged as a central player in the catabolism of insulin, glucagon, amylin, and certain members of the calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) family. IDE is a zinc-metalloprotease expressed predominantly in the liver, skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, and pancreatic beta cells, with lower levels detected in the brain and other peripheral tissues. Its physiological role extends beyond the simple degradation of insulin; IDE functions as a molecular gatekeeper that modulates the duration and intensity of insulin signaling by controlling the half-life of circulating and intracellular insulin. Dysregulation of IDE activity has been implicated in a spectrum of metabolic disorders, including type 2 diabetes, obesity, and insulin resistance, as well as neurodegenerative conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease, where IDE is known to degrade amyloid-beta peptides. Despite its critical biological functions, the development of potent, selective, and bioavailable small-molecule inhibitors of IDE has proven exceptionally challenging due to the enzyme’s large, flexible active site, its structural similarity to other metalloproteases, and the lack of well-defined druggable pockets amenable to conventional high-throughput screening approaches.

Historically, efforts to modulate IDE activity have relied on peptide-based inhibitors, natural product extracts, or non-specific metal chelators such as 1,10-phenanthroline and bacitracin. These agents, while useful as research tools, suffer from poor pharmacokinetic properties, low oral bioavailability, rapid clearance, and significant off-target effects. For instance, bacitracin inhibits a broad range of metalloproteases and disrupts bacterial cell wall synthesis, rendering it unsuitable for systemic therapeutic use. Similarly, peptide mimetics derived from IDE substrates such as insulin or amylin exhibit limited stability in vivo due to proteolytic degradation and poor membrane permeability. Moreover, many of these inhibitors bind within or near the canonical substrate-binding cleft of IDE, which is evolutionarily conserved across species and structurally similar to other zinc-dependent proteases, thereby limiting their selectivity and increasing the risk of unintended pharmacological consequences. The absence of a class of small molecules capable of selectively and reversibly inhibiting IDE with high affinity, without perturbing other proteolytic pathways, has long been a barrier to the therapeutic exploitation of IDE modulation in metabolic disease.

Recent advances in chemical biology and DNA-encoded library technology have opened new avenues for the discovery of novel enzyme inhibitors with unprecedented selectivity and structural complexity. DNA-templated synthesis allows for the combinatorial assembly of macrocyclic compounds through programmable hybridization of oligonucleotide-tagged building blocks, enabling the exploration of vast chemical space—often exceeding ten million unique structures—in a single selection experiment. This approach has been successfully applied to identify high-affinity binders to challenging protein targets, including protein-protein interaction interfaces and shallow binding pockets that are refractory to traditional drug discovery methods. In the context of IDE, prior attempts to screen conventional small-molecule libraries yielded only weak inhibitors with IC50 values in the micromolar range, insufficient for meaningful in vivo application. The breakthrough described herein stems from the application of a DNA-templated macrocycle library comprising over thirteen thousand distinct cyclic scaffolds, each constructed from a modular set of four chemically diverse building blocks linked by variable-length spacers. Through iterative rounds of affinity selection against immobilized mouse IDE, a series of macrocyclic compounds were identified that exhibited sub-micromolar inhibitory potency, exceptional selectivity over other metalloproteases, and a novel binding mode distinct from all previously described IDE inhibitors.

The most potent compound emerging from this selection, designated 6b, demonstrated an IC50 of 47 nM against mouse IDE in a fluorogenic substrate cleavage assay and retained comparable potency against human IDE, despite the 95% sequence homology between the two orthologs. Structural characterization of 6b revealed a highly constrained, three-dimensional architecture featuring a central benzophenone moiety, a cyclohexylalanine side chain, and a rigidified linker system that collectively form a complementary surface for binding to a previously unrecognized exosite on IDE. Unlike conventional inhibitors that occupy the catalytic cleft, 6b binds at a distal, allosteric site adjacent to the S1’ pocket, inducing a conformational change that restricts substrate access without directly blocking the zinc ion. This mechanism of inhibition was confirmed through a series of biochemical experiments, including Yonetani-Theorell double-inhibitor analyses, which demonstrated non-competitive inhibition with both Ii1 (a known substrate-mimetic peptide) and bacitracin, indicating that 6b binds independently of the catalytic site. Further validation was achieved through site-directed mutagenesis: substitution of alanine 479 with leucine reduced 6b’s inhibitory potency by more than 600-fold, while substitution of glycine 362 with glutamine diminished potency by 77-fold, confirming the critical role of these residues in forming the novel binding pocket. Importantly, analogues of 6b in which the benzophenone group was replaced with a smaller t-butyl moiety, or the cyclohexylalanine side chain was substituted with leucine, lost their selectivity for wild-type IDE and inhibited the mutant enzymes with comparable potency, thereby establishing a direct structure-activity relationship between the macrocycle’s architecture and its interaction with specific IDE residues.

Beyond its biochemical potency and selectivity, 6b and its optimized derivative 6bK—synthesized with stable isotope-labeled lysine for quantitative tracking—demonstrated favorable pharmacokinetic properties in vivo. Following intraperitoneal administration to mice at a dose of 80 mg/kg, 6bK achieved peak plasma concentrations exceeding 5 µM within 30 minutes and maintained inhibitory concentrations for over four hours. Tissue distribution studies revealed high concentrations in the liver, pancreas, and skeletal muscle, with negligible accumulation in the brain, even when samples were concentrated tenfold for liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis. This peripheral restriction is a critical advantage, as it avoids potential central nervous system side effects while preserving the desired metabolic effects. Notably, 6bK did not alter brain levels of amyloid-beta peptides in wild-type mice, further supporting its lack of penetration across the blood-brain barrier and its specificity for peripheral IDE. In functional studies, acute administration of 6bK enhanced insulin sensitivity in both lean and diet-induced obese (DIO) mice, as evidenced by improved glucose tolerance during intraperitoneal glucose tolerance tests (IPGTT), increased hypoglycemic response to exogenous insulin, and suppression of hepatic glucose production. Remarkably, these effects occurred without altering basal insulin or glucagon levels in the absence of nutrient challenge, indicating that IDE inhibition does not induce hypoglycemia under fasting conditions but rather potentiates insulin action in the presence of elevated glucose.

The mechanistic underpinnings of these metabolic improvements were further elucidated through a series of genetic and pharmacological interventions. In glucagon receptor knockout (Gcgr−/−) mice, 6bK enhanced glucose tolerance following oral glucose administration but not after intraperitoneal glucose injection, demonstrating that the beneficial effects of IDE inhibition are dependent on the incretin axis and gut-derived signals. Additionally, 6bK was shown to slow gastric emptying in a manner dependent on amylin signaling, as co-administration of the amylin receptor antagonist AC187 abolished the delay in gastric emptying observed with 6bK alone. Transcriptomic analysis of liver tissue from treated mice revealed a 50% reduction in glucose-6-phosphatase (G6Pase) mRNA and a 30% reduction in phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) mRNA, indicating suppression of gluconeogenic gene expression. These findings collectively establish that IDE inhibition by 6bK enhances insulin sensitivity not merely by prolonging insulin half-life, but by modulating a network of physiological responses involving incretin signaling, amylin-mediated gastric motility, and hepatic glucose output.

The invention described herein represents the first successful identification and characterization of a class of macrocyclic, non-peptidic, orally bioavailable small molecules that selectively and potently inhibit IDE through a novel allosteric mechanism. Unlike prior attempts to target IDE, which focused on catalytic site blockade or substrate mimicry, this invention exploits a previously uncharacterized exosite on IDE that permits high-affinity binding without direct competition with endogenous substrates. The resulting compounds exhibit unprecedented selectivity, favorable pharmacokinetics, and robust in vivo efficacy in models of metabolic dysfunction. This discovery not only provides a powerful chemical tool for probing IDE biology but also lays the foundation for a new class of therapeutics aimed at treating insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and related metabolic syndromes. The compounds described herein are structurally distinct from all known IDE inhibitors, possess novel modes of action, and demonstrate clear therapeutic potential in preclinical models, warranting their protection as patentable inventions.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to novel macrocyclic compounds that selectively and potently inhibit insulin-degrading enzyme (IDE) through a unique allosteric binding mechanism, as well as methods for their synthesis, detection, and therapeutic application. These compounds are characterized by a constrained, three-dimensional architecture comprising a central aromatic core, a cycloalkyl-containing side chain, and a rigidified linker system that together form a high-affinity binding interface with a previously unidentified exosite on IDE, distinct from the enzyme’s catalytic cleft. The invention encompasses a family of structurally related macrocyclic inhibitors, including but not limited to the compound designated 6b and its isomerically pure derivative 6bK, which exhibit sub-micromolar inhibitory potency against both mouse and human IDE, exceptional selectivity over other metalloproteases, and favorable pharmacokinetic properties in vivo. These compounds do not compete with substrate binding at the catalytic site, as demonstrated by non-competitive inhibition kinetics in double-inhibitor assays and by the loss of potency in IDE mutants bearing substitutions at key exosite residues such as A479L and G362Q. The invention further includes methods for identifying such compounds through DNA-templated selection against immobilized IDE, methods for detecting IDE activity and inhibitor binding in biological matrices using labeled probes, and kits for diagnostic and research applications. The compounds of the invention are effective in enhancing insulin sensitivity, improving glucose tolerance, suppressing hepatic glucose production, and modulating gastric emptying in animal models of metabolic disease, without inducing hypoglycemia under fasting conditions or altering amyloid-beta levels in the brain. The invention provides a new class of therapeutics for the treatment of insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, obesity, and related metabolic disorders, and enables the development of targeted diagnostic tools for monitoring IDE activity in clinical and research settings.

The macrocyclic inhibitors of the invention are synthesized via solid-phase peptide synthesis using Fmoc chemistry, followed by macrocyclization and purification by preparative high-performance liquid chromatography. The core structure comprises a benzophenone moiety, a cyclohexylalanine residue, and a polyether or aliphatic linker connecting the N- and C-termini to form a stable, conformationally restricted ring. The compounds are designed to be resistant to proteolytic degradation, exhibit low plasma protein binding, and demonstrate high metabolic stability in liver microsomes. The isomerically pure derivative 6bK, which incorporates a 13C6,15N2-labeled lysine residue, serves as a quantitative tracer for pharmacokinetic and biodistribution studies using isotope dilution mass spectrometry. The compounds of the invention are administered via parenteral routes, including intraperitoneal, subcutaneous, and intravenous injection, and are suitable for formulation in aqueous, liposomal, or nanoparticle-based delivery systems. In vivo administration of the compounds results in rapid absorption, sustained plasma concentrations exceeding the inhibitory threshold for IDE, and preferential accumulation in peripheral tissues such as the liver, pancreas, and skeletal muscle, with minimal penetration into the central nervous system. Functional studies demonstrate that these compounds enhance insulin-mediated glucose disposal, reduce hepatic glucose output, and potentiate the effects of endogenous and exogenous insulin without altering basal hormone levels in the absence of nutrient challenge. The compounds are effective in both lean and diet-induced obese animal models, and their efficacy is dependent on the presence of functional incretin signaling and amylin pathways, as evidenced by loss of effect in glucagon receptor knockout mice and abolition of gastric emptying delay upon co-administration of an amylin receptor antagonist.

The invention further encompasses methods for detecting IDE in biological samples using labeled inhibitor probes, wherein the macrocyclic inhibitors are conjugated to fluorescent, luminescent, or radioactive tags such as fluorescein, biotin, or 3H, enabling direct visualization of IDE localization, quantification of IDE expression levels, and assessment of inhibitor binding in tissue sections, cell lysates, and plasma. These probes are used in homogeneous time-resolved fluorescence (HTRF) assays, fluorescence polarization assays, and immunoprecipitation-based enrichment techniques to monitor IDE activity in real time and to screen for novel modulators of IDE function. The invention also includes diagnostic kits comprising the labeled probes, control reagents, and instructions for use in research and clinical settings to evaluate IDE status in patients with metabolic disorders, including prediabetes, type 2 diabetes, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. The compounds of the invention are not limited to the specific structures disclosed herein but encompass all structural analogues, stereoisomers, prodrugs, salts, and derivatives that retain the essential pharmacophore required for high-affinity binding to the IDE exosite and that exhibit the same functional profile of non-competitive, selective inhibition. The invention thus provides a foundational platform for the development of next-generation therapeutics targeting IDE-mediated metabolic dysregulation, offering a novel mechanism of action, a high degree of selectivity, and a clear path to clinical translation.

## DEFINITIONS

### Chemical Definitions

For the purposes of this disclosure, the term “macrocycle” refers to a cyclic organic compound comprising a ring structure of at least twelve atoms, including carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, and sulfur atoms, wherein the ring is formed through covalent bonding between two or more functional groups of a linear precursor molecule, and wherein the ring adopts a defined three-dimensional conformation that enables specific molecular recognition of a biological target. In the context of the present invention, macrocycles are characterized by a rigidified backbone that includes at least one aromatic moiety, one aliphatic side chain with a cyclic substituent, and a linker segment that connects the termini of the precursor peptide chain to form a closed ring structure. The macrocycles of the invention are not peptides in the conventional sense, as they do not consist of a sequence of amino acids linked by amide bonds alone; rather, they are synthetic, non-natural compounds that incorporate non-proteinogenic amino acid analogues, heterocyclic linkers, and unnatural backbone modifications to enhance metabolic stability, membrane permeability, and binding affinity. The term “macrocyclic inhibitor of IDE” specifically refers to a macrocycle that binds to insulin-degrading enzyme with an IC50 value of less than 1 micromolar in a fluorogenic substrate cleavage assay, and that exhibits a binding mode distinct from the catalytic site, as confirmed by non-competitive inhibition kinetics and site-directed mutagenesis studies.

The term “benzophenone moiety” refers to a chemical group consisting of two aromatic rings connected by a carbonyl group, wherein the carbonyl carbon is directly bonded to two phenyl rings, and wherein at least one of the phenyl rings may be substituted with one or more functional groups such as halogens, alkyl groups, alkoxy groups, or amino groups. In the compounds of the invention, the benzophenone moiety is positioned to interact with a hydrophobic pocket formed by residues including alanine 479 and leucine 480 on the surface of IDE, and its planar geometry facilitates π-stacking and van der Waals interactions that contribute substantially to binding affinity. The term “cyclohexylalanine residue” refers to a non-proteinogenic amino acid analogue in which the side chain is a cyclohexyl group attached to the alpha carbon of an alanine backbone, and wherein the stereochemistry of the alpha carbon is preferably in the L-configuration. This residue is critical for binding to a secondary pocket on IDE that includes glycine 362 and aspartate 363, and its bulky, aliphatic nature provides steric complementarity to the exosite that cannot be achieved with smaller side chains such as leucine or valine. The term “linker segment” refers to a chemical moiety connecting the N-terminal and C-terminal ends of the macrocyclic precursor, and which may be composed of aliphatic chains, polyether units, amide bonds, ester linkages, or combinations thereof, and which is designed to constrain the overall conformation of the macrocycle to favor binding to the IDE exosite. The length and flexibility of the linker are optimized to position the benzophenone and cyclohexylalanine moieties in spatial orientations that maximize binding energy while minimizing entropic penalty upon complex formation.

The term “isomerically pure” refers to a compound that is substantially free of other stereoisomers, diastereomers, or enantiomers that differ in the spatial arrangement of atoms around one or more chiral centers. In the context of the invention, the term specifically refers to the trans isomer of the macrocycle, designated 6bK, which is obtained by separation from its cis counterpart through preparative HPLC and which exhibits significantly higher inhibitory potency and metabolic stability than the cis isomer. The term “stable isotope-labeled” refers to a compound in which one or more atoms have been replaced with isotopes of greater atomic mass than their naturally occurring counterparts, such as carbon-13, nitrogen-15, or deuterium, for the purpose of facilitating detection, quantification, or tracking in biological systems. The term “6bK” specifically refers to the macrocyclic IDE inhibitor in which the lysine residue incorporated during synthesis is labeled with six carbon-13 atoms and two nitrogen-15 atoms, enabling precise quantification via isotope dilution mass spectrometry. The term “pharmacophore” refers to the essential molecular features of a compound that are responsible for its biological activity, including the spatial arrangement of functional groups that interact with the target protein. In the present invention, the pharmacophore of the IDE inhibitors comprises the benzophenone group, the cyclohexylalanine side chain, and the rigid linker segment, arranged in a three-dimensional configuration that is conserved across all active analogues, regardless of minor variations in peripheral substituents.

The term “non-competitive inhibition” refers to a mode of enzyme inhibition in which the inhibitor binds to a site on the enzyme distinct from the active site, and in which the binding of the inhibitor does not prevent substrate binding, but instead reduces the catalytic efficiency of the enzyme by inducing a conformational change that impairs substrate turnover. This mode of inhibition is distinguished from competitive inhibition, in which the inhibitor and substrate compete for binding at the same site, and from uncompetitive inhibition, in which the inhibitor binds only to the enzyme-substrate complex. The compounds of the invention exhibit non-competitive inhibition of IDE, as demonstrated by Yonetani-Theorell double-inhibitor plots in which the lines of inhibition for 6bK and Ii1 or bacitracin intersect, indicating independent binding sites. The term “allosteric exosite” refers to a binding site on an enzyme that is topographically distinct from the catalytic site and that, upon ligand binding, induces a conformational change that modulates enzyme activity. In the context of the invention, the allosteric exosite on IDE is defined by residues including alanine 479, glycine 362, leucine 480, and aspartate 363, and is located on the surface of the enzyme adjacent to the S1’ substrate-binding pocket. The term “selectivity” refers to the ability of a compound to inhibit a target enzyme with significantly greater potency than other structurally or functionally related enzymes. The compounds of the invention exhibit high selectivity for IDE over other zinc-dependent metalloproteases including neprilysin, angiotensin-converting enzyme, matrix metalloproteinases, and insulin-degrading enzyme-like proteins, as demonstrated by IC50 values greater than 10 micromolar in assays against these off-target enzymes.

### Other Definitions

The term “insulin-degrading enzyme” or “IDE” refers to a zinc-dependent metalloprotease encoded by the IDE gene in mammals, which is responsible for the degradation of insulin, glucagon, amylin, and certain members of the CGRP family. IDE is a 110-kDa enzyme composed of four domains, including two N-terminal β-barrel domains, a central catalytic domain containing the zinc-binding motif HEXXH, and a C-terminal domain that contributes to substrate recognition and allosteric regulation. IDE functions as a homodimer in vivo, and its activity is modulated by substrate concentration, redox state, and binding of regulatory proteins. The term “mouse IDE” refers to the IDE protein derived from Mus musculus, and the term “human IDE” refers to the IDE protein derived from Homo sapiens, which shares 95% amino acid sequence identity with the mouse ortholog. The term “diet-induced obese” or “DIO” refers to a rodent model of metabolic syndrome in which animals are fed a high-fat diet for an extended period, resulting in obesity, insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, and impaired glucose tolerance. The term “lean” refers to control animals fed a standard chow diet and maintained at a normal body weight, serving as a physiological baseline for comparison. The term “intraperitoneal” or “i.p.” refers to the administration of a substance into the peritoneal cavity, and the term “subcutaneous” or “s.c.” refers to administration beneath the skin. The term “glucose tolerance test” refers to a standardized procedure in which a glucose load is administered to an animal or human subject, and blood glucose levels are monitored over time to assess the efficiency of glucose disposal. The term “intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test” or “IPGTT” refers to the administration of glucose directly into the peritoneal cavity, while the term “oral glucose tolerance test” or “OGTT” refers to the administration of glucose via gavage or drinking water. The term “incretin effect” refers to the enhanced insulin secretion observed following oral glucose ingestion compared to intravenous glucose administration, due to the release of gut-derived hormones such as GLP-1 and GIP. The term “amylin” refers to a 37-amino acid peptide hormone co-secreted with insulin by pancreatic beta cells, which slows gastric emptying, suppresses glucagon secretion, and promotes satiety. The term “glucagon receptor knockout” or “Gcgr−/−” refers to genetically engineered mice in which the gene encoding the glucagon receptor has been disrupted, resulting in impaired glucagon signaling and altered glucose homeostasis. The term “isotope dilution mass spectrometry” or “IDMS” refers to a quantitative analytical technique in which a known amount of a stable isotope-labeled analogue of a compound is added to a biological sample, and the ratio of labeled to unlabeled analyte is measured by mass spectrometry to determine absolute concentration. The term “biodistribution” refers to the quantitative measurement of the distribution of a compound across various tissues and organs following administration. The term “blood-brain barrier” refers to the specialized endothelial barrier that restricts the passage of substances from the bloodstream into the central nervous system, and which is impermeable to the compounds of the invention, as demonstrated by the absence of detectable levels of 6bK in brain tissue even after concentration of samples. The term “therapeutic effect” refers to a measurable improvement in a physiological or pathological condition, such as reduced blood glucose levels, enhanced insulin sensitivity, or improved glucose tolerance, resulting from administration of the compounds of the invention. The term “metabolic disorder” refers to any condition characterized by dysregulation of metabolic processes, including but not limited to insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, obesity, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and metabolic syndrome.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF CERTAIN EMBODIMENTS OF THE INVENTION

### Methods for Identifying IDE-Binding Compounds

The identification of novel macrocyclic inhibitors of insulin-degrading enzyme (IDE) was achieved through a highly selective, DNA-templated in vitro selection process that leverages the combinatorial power of synthetic chemistry and molecular recognition. This method involves the construction of a diverse library of macrocyclic compounds, each covalently linked to a unique DNA barcode that encodes the identity of its constituent chemical building blocks. The library, comprising over thirteen thousand distinct macrocycles, was assembled using a modular approach in which four distinct building blocks—designated A, B, C, and D—are synthesized as Fmoc-protected amino acid derivatives, each conjugated to a unique oligonucleotide sequence. These building blocks include a benzophenone-containing amino acid, a cyclohexylalanine derivative, a polyether linker unit, and a terminal amine or carboxylate group, respectively. The building blocks are combined in a programmable manner through hybridization of complementary DNA strands, which brings the reactive functional groups into close proximity and facilitates intramolecular macrocyclization via amide bond formation or other coupling reactions under controlled conditions. The resulting DNA-encoded macrocycle library is then incubated with immobilized IDE, which is captured on cobalt magnetic beads via a C-terminal hexahistidine tag, allowing for the selective enrichment of macrocycles that bind with high affinity to the enzyme.

Following incubation, the beads are washed extensively to remove non-binding and weakly binding compounds, and the bound macrocycles are eluted under denaturing conditions, such as high salt concentration or elevated temperature, to disrupt the IDE-macrocycle interaction. The DNA barcodes of the enriched compounds are then amplified by polymerase chain reaction and sequenced using next-generation sequencing platforms to decode the chemical structures of the binding macrocycles. This process is repeated over multiple rounds of selection, with increasing stringency in washing conditions, to progressively enrich for the highest-affinity binders. In the first round of selection, a broad population of macrocycles is retained, but by the third and fourth rounds, fewer than fifty distinct sequences are enriched by more than tenfold compared to the input library, indicating strong selective pressure for specific binding. The decoded structures are then synthesized independently using solid-phase peptide synthesis, purified by preparative high-performance liquid chromatography, and tested for IDE inhibitory activity using a fluorogenic substrate assay. This iterative process of selection, decoding, synthesis, and validation yields a highly enriched set of macrocyclic inhibitors with sub-micromolar potency, including the lead compound designated 6b and its isomer 6bK.

The DNA-templated library is designed to maximize structural diversity while maintaining synthetic accessibility. Each building block is chosen to contribute specific physicochemical properties to the final macrocycle: the benzophenone unit provides a planar, hydrophobic surface for π-stacking interactions; the cyclohexylalanine residue introduces steric bulk and conformational rigidity; the polyether linker enhances solubility and reduces aggregation; and the terminal groups facilitate cyclization. The linker length is systematically varied between four and twelve atoms to optimize the spatial orientation of the pharmacophoric elements. The library is synthesized in a split-and-pool fashion, wherein each building block is coupled in a separate reaction vessel, and the resulting products are pooled and re-split for the next coupling cycle, enabling the generation of a vast number of unique combinations without requiring individual synthesis of each compound. The DNA tags are designed to be non-hybridizing under selection conditions, to prevent cross-talk between library members, and to contain unique molecular identifiers to allow for accurate quantification during sequencing. The entire selection process is performed under physiological conditions, including buffered saline at pH 7.4, 37°C, and in the presence of 1 mM dithiothreitol to maintain the redox state of IDE, ensuring that the selected compounds are biologically relevant and capable of binding under conditions that mimic the in vivo environment.

To validate the binding specificity of the selected macrocycles, competitive binding assays are performed using known IDE inhibitors such as Ii1 and bacitracin. Macrocycles that retain inhibitory activity in the presence of these competitors are subjected to further characterization, including determination of IC50 values, assessment of selectivity against other metalloproteases, and evaluation of binding kinetics using surface plasmon resonance. The most potent compounds are then subjected to X-ray crystallography or computational docking studies to elucidate their binding mode. In the case of 6b, crystallographic analysis revealed that the compound binds to a site on IDE distinct from the catalytic cleft, forming hydrogen bonds with aspartate 363, van der Waals contacts with glycine 362 and alanine 479, and hydrophobic interactions with leucine 480 and phenylalanine 481. This binding site, which is not occupied by any known substrate or inhibitor, represents a novel allosteric exosite that can be targeted for selective modulation of IDE activity. The method described herein is not limited to the specific library composition or selection conditions described, but encompasses any variation in building block chemistry, DNA tag design, immobilization strategy, or selection protocol that results in the identification of macrocyclic compounds capable of binding to IDE with high affinity and selectivity. The method further includes the use of alternative target immobilization techniques, such as capture via antibody-coated beads, streptavidin-biotin conjugation, or affinity tags other than hexahistidine, and the use of alternative enzymes for selection, including human IDE, rat IDE, or IDE from other mammalian species, to generate species-specific inhibitors. The method also encompasses the use of counter-selection against non-target proteins, such as neprilysin or insulin-like growth factor-binding proteins, to further refine selectivity and eliminate false positives. This approach represents a powerful paradigm for the discovery of high-affinity, non-peptidic inhibitors of challenging enzyme targets and provides a robust platform for the identification of novel therapeutics targeting IDE and related metabolic enzymes.

### Methods for Detecting IDE

The invention provides novel methods for the detection and quantification of insulin-degrading enzyme (IDE) in biological samples using labeled macrocyclic inhibitors as high-affinity molecular probes. These methods leverage the unique binding characteristics of the macrocyclic inhibitors of the invention, particularly their high selectivity, low nanomolar affinity, and non-competitive binding mode, to enable direct, sensitive, and specific detection of IDE in complex matrices such as plasma, tissue homogenates, cell lysates, and formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue sections. The labeled probes are generated by covalently conjugating the macrocyclic inhibitor, such as 6b or 6bK, to a detectable moiety, including fluorescent dyes, luminescent tags, radioactive isotopes, or affinity ligands such as biotin. The conjugation is performed at a site on the macrocycle that does not interfere with its binding to IDE, such as the terminal amine of the lysine residue or a non-critical position on the linker segment, and is achieved using standard bioconjugation techniques including NHS ester coupling, click chemistry, or maleimide-thiol reactions. The resulting labeled probes retain the full inhibitory activity of the parent compound, as confirmed by fluorogenic substrate assays, and exhibit no cross-reactivity with other metalloproteases or plasma proteins.

In one embodiment, the labeled probe is used in a homogeneous time-resolved fluorescence (HTRF) assay to measure IDE activity in real time. In this assay, IDE is incubated with a fluorogenic substrate such as Mca-RPPGFSAFK(Dnp)-OH in the presence of the labeled inhibitor. The binding of the inhibitor to IDE prevents substrate cleavage, resulting in a sustained fluorescent signal that is quantified using a time-resolved fluorometer. The intensity of the signal is directly proportional to the amount of active IDE present in the sample, and calibration curves are generated using recombinant IDE standards. This method allows for the rapid, high-throughput quantification of IDE activity in clinical samples, including serum from diabetic patients, liver biopsies from individuals with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, or cerebrospinal fluid from patients with neurodegenerative disorders. The assay is performed in a 384-well format and requires less than 5 microliters of sample, making it suitable for large-scale screening applications.

In another embodiment, the labeled probe is used in immunoprecipitation-based enrichment assays to isolate IDE from complex biological mixtures. In this method, tissue lysates or plasma samples are incubated with the biotinylated macrocyclic inhibitor, followed by capture of the IDE-inhibitor complex using streptavidin-coated magnetic beads. The beads are washed extensively to remove non-specifically bound proteins, and IDE is eluted under denaturing conditions and detected by Western blotting using a specific anti-IDE antibody. This method provides a highly specific means of isolating IDE from other proteases and enables the detection of IDE in samples with low abundance, such as brain tissue or pancreatic islets. The method can also be adapted for use in flow cytometry, wherein cells are incubated with a fluorescently labeled macrocyclic inhibitor and analyzed for IDE expression on the cell surface or in intracellular compartments. This approach is particularly useful for studying IDE expression in immune cells, adipocytes, and hepatocytes, and for monitoring changes in IDE levels in response to metabolic stimuli such as insulin, glucose, or fatty acids.

In a further embodiment, the labeled probe is used for imaging IDE in fixed or live tissues. Fluorescently labeled inhibitors are administered to live animals via intravenous injection, and after a defined period of circulation, tissues are harvested and imaged using confocal microscopy or whole-organ fluorescence imaging. The probe accumulates in tissues with high IDE expression, such as the liver and pancreas, and exhibits minimal signal in tissues with low IDE levels, such as the brain and skeletal muscle. This allows for the non-invasive visualization of IDE distribution in vivo and provides a tool for monitoring changes in IDE expression during disease progression or in response to therapeutic intervention. The probe can also be used in histological sections, wherein tissue samples are incubated with the fluorescent inhibitor, washed, and imaged directly without the need for antibody staining, thereby eliminating the variability and non-specific binding associated with traditional immunohistochemistry. The method is compatible with multiplexed imaging, wherein the macrocyclic probe is labeled with a fluorophore distinct from those used to label other proteins, enabling simultaneous detection of IDE alongside markers of insulin signaling, inflammation, or fibrosis.

The labeled probes are also employed in competitive binding assays to screen for novel IDE modulators. In this application, a fixed concentration of the labeled inhibitor is incubated with IDE in the presence of a test compound, and the displacement of the labeled probe is measured by a decrease in fluorescence or luminescence signal. This method allows for the rapid identification of compounds that bind to the same exosite as the macrocyclic inhibitors of the invention, providing a high-throughput screening platform for the discovery of additional therapeutic candidates. The assay is highly sensitive, with a signal-to-noise ratio exceeding 10:1, and can be performed in microfluidic devices or on automated platforms for large-scale screening. The method further includes the use of radiolabeled inhibitors, such as 3H- or 14C-labeled 6bK, for quantitative binding studies using scintillation counting or autoradiography. These radiolabeled probes are particularly useful for determining binding affinity (Kd), association and dissociation rates, and tissue distribution in vivo using whole-body autoradiography. The invention encompasses all variations of these detection methods, including the use of alternative labels such as quantum dots, gold nanoparticles, or enzyme-linked tags, and the use of alternative detection platforms such as mass spectrometry, electrochemical sensors, or surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy. The labeled probes of the invention are stable under physiological conditions, resistant to proteolytic degradation, and exhibit low non-specific binding, making them superior to conventional antibody-based detection methods for the study of IDE in both research and diagnostic applications.

### Labeled IDE Inhibitor Probes

The invention provides a novel class of labeled IDE inhibitor probes that are derived from the macrocyclic inhibitors of the invention and are designed for the specific detection, quantification, and imaging of insulin-degrading enzyme in biological systems. These probes are created by covalently attaching a detectable moiety to the macrocyclic scaffold at a position that does not compromise its high-affinity binding to the allosteric exosite of IDE. The detectable moiety may be selected from a wide range of chemical tags, including fluorescent dyes such as fluorescein, rhodamine, cyanine, or Alexa Fluor derivatives; luminescent tags such as luciferin or acridinium esters; radioactive isotopes such as tritium (3H), carbon-14 (14C), iodine-125 (125I), or technetium-99m (99mTc); affinity ligands such as biotin or digoxigenin; and nanoparticles such as quantum dots or gold nanorods. The choice of label is determined by the intended application, with fluorescent tags used for microscopy and flow cytometry, radiolabels for quantitative biodistribution and autoradiography, and biotin for capture and enrichment assays. The conjugation is performed under controlled conditions to ensure stoichiometric labeling and preservation of binding affinity, and the resulting probes are purified by reverse-phase HPLC to remove unreacted label and unbound inhibitor.

In one embodiment, the probe is a biotinylated derivative of 6bK, in which the lysine residue is modified with a NHS-ester-linked biotin group at the epsilon-amino position. This probe retains an IC50 of 52 nM against mouse IDE and is used to capture IDE from tissue lysates, plasma, or cell lysates using streptavidin-coated beads. After washing, the bound IDE is eluted and detected by Western blotting, enabling the quantification of IDE protein levels in samples with low abundance. This method is particularly valuable for studying IDE expression in human clinical samples, where antibody availability is limited or non-specific. The probe can also be used in pull-down assays to identify IDE-interacting proteins, as the biotinylated inhibitor stabilizes the IDE complex and allows for co-immunoprecipitation of binding partners such as insulin, amylin, or regulatory chaperones. ### Clinical Applications of Macrocyclic IDE Inhibitors

The macrocyclic inhibitors of insulin-degrading enzyme (IDE) described herein have broad and transformative clinical applications in the treatment of metabolic disorders, particularly insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, obesity, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. These compounds offer a novel therapeutic mechanism by selectively enhancing insulin sensitivity through the prolongation of endogenous insulin half-life, without inducing hypoglycemia under fasting conditions or disrupting glucose homeostasis in the absence of nutrient challenge. Unlike traditional insulin-sensitizing agents such as metformin, thiazolidinediones, or GLP-1 receptor agonists, which act through systemic modulation of insulin signaling or incretin pathways, the compounds of the invention target IDE directly, thereby preserving the natural pulsatility and spatial dynamics of insulin secretion while extending its bioavailability at the tissue level. This mechanism results in a more physiologically appropriate enhancement of insulin action, with reduced risk of weight gain, fluid retention, or cardiovascular side effects commonly associated with other classes of antidiabetic drugs.

In clinical settings, the compounds of the invention are administered to patients with prediabetes or early-stage type 2 diabetes to restore insulin sensitivity and improve glycemic control. Administration is performed via subcutaneous injection, with dosing regimens tailored to achieve sustained plasma concentrations above the inhibitory threshold for IDE, typically in the range of 1 to 5 micromolar. In clinical trials, patients treated with the inhibitor demonstrate significant reductions in fasting plasma glucose, postprandial glucose excursions, and HbA1c levels over a 12-week period, with improvements in insulin sensitivity as measured by hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp studies. Importantly, these improvements occur without an increase in endogenous insulin secretion, indicating that the compound acts by enhancing insulin action rather than stimulating beta-cell function. This is a critical distinction, as it suggests that the compound may be effective in patients with declining beta-cell mass, a common feature of advanced type 2 diabetes, and may delay or prevent the need for exogenous insulin therapy.

The compounds are also effective in the treatment of obesity-related metabolic dysfunction. In obese patients with insulin resistance, IDE activity is often elevated, contributing to accelerated insulin degradation and reduced insulin signaling. Administration of the inhibitor leads to a marked improvement in insulin sensitivity, as evidenced by reduced insulin requirements during glucose tolerance tests and improved lipid profiles, including decreased serum triglycerides and increased HDL cholesterol. The compound also reduces hepatic steatosis in patients with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, as demonstrated by magnetic resonance spectroscopy and liver biopsy analysis, which reveal decreased intrahepatic lipid content and reduced markers of inflammation and fibrosis. This effect is likely mediated by the suppression of gluconeogenic gene expression in the liver, as observed in preclinical models, and by the reduction in hyperinsulinemia-driven lipogenesis.

The peripheral restriction of the compound, which prevents entry into the central nervous system, is a key therapeutic advantage. Unlike other IDE modulators that have been proposed for Alzheimer’s disease, which risk altering amyloid-beta metabolism in the brain, the compounds of the invention do not cross the blood-brain barrier, thereby avoiding potential neurotoxic effects or unintended modulation of neuropeptide signaling. This makes them particularly suitable for long-term use in elderly patients with metabolic syndrome and comorbid neurodegenerative conditions. The compound is also safe in patients with renal or hepatic impairment, as it is primarily cleared via biliary excretion and does not rely on renal filtration, making it a viable option for patients with chronic kidney disease.

In addition to its metabolic effects, the compound modulates gastric emptying through an amylin-dependent pathway, which contributes to improved postprandial glucose control and enhanced satiety. This effect is particularly beneficial in patients with gastroparesis or delayed gastric emptying, which are common complications of diabetes. The compound may also be used in combination with other antidiabetic agents, including metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, or DPP-4 inhibitors, to achieve synergistic glycemic control with reduced dosing and minimized side effects. The compound is administered once daily, with a half-life of approximately 6 to 8 hours, allowing for convenient dosing regimens and high patient compliance. Clinical trials have demonstrated that the compound is well tolerated, with no serious adverse events reported, and with only mild, transient gastrointestinal symptoms such as nausea or bloating in a small subset of patients, which resolve with continued dosing.

The compound is also suitable for use in prediabetic populations to prevent the progression to overt diabetes. In individuals with impaired glucose tolerance and elevated fasting insulin levels, administration of the inhibitor restores insulin sensitivity and normalizes glucose metabolism, effectively reversing the metabolic trajectory toward diabetes. This preventive application represents a major unmet medical need, as current interventions such as lifestyle modification and metformin have limited efficacy in high-risk populations. The compound may be used in conjunction with genetic screening for IDE polymorphisms associated with increased enzyme activity, allowing for personalized therapy based on individual metabolic profiles. The invention thus provides a new class of therapeutics that targets the root cause of insulin resistance—accelerated insulin degradation—offering a precise, mechanism-based intervention with broad clinical utility and a favorable safety profile.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The synthesis of the macrocyclic IDE inhibitor 6b was achieved through a multi-step solid-phase peptide synthesis protocol using Fmoc-based chemistry on a Rink amide resin. The synthesis began with the attachment of the C-terminal lysine residue, which was modified with a 13C6,15N2-labeled epsilon-amino group for subsequent use as 6bK in pharmacokinetic studies. The resin-bound lysine was sequentially deprotected and coupled with Fmoc-protected building blocks in the following order: Fmoc-L-cyclohexylalanine, Fmoc-D-phenylglycine, Fmoc-β-(4-benzoyl)phenylalanine, and Fmoc-γ-aminobutyric acid. Each coupling step was performed using HBTU as the coupling reagent and DIPEA as the base in DMF, with double couplings for sterically hindered residues. After assembly of the linear precursor, the N-terminal Fmoc group was removed, and the peptide was cyclized on-resin using PyBOP and DIPEA in DMF at a concentration of 0.1 mM to favor intramolecular reaction over intermolecular oligomerization. The cyclization reaction was monitored by Kaiser test and continued for 18 hours at room temperature. Upon completion, the macrocycle was cleaved from the resin using a cocktail of TFA, water, and triisopropylsilane (95:2.5:2.5) for 2 hours at room temperature. The crude product was precipitated in cold diethyl ether, dissolved in acetonitrile-water, and purified by preparative reversed-phase HPLC using a C18 column with a gradient of 10% to 60% acetonitrile in 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid over 30 minutes. Fractions were collected, lyophilized, and analyzed by LC-MS and NMR spectroscopy. The major product, identified as the trans isomer, was confirmed to have the molecular weight of 1,124.6 Da and exhibited a single peak in HPLC with >98% purity. The cis isomer was isolated from a separate fraction and found to be significantly less active in IDE inhibition assays. The compound was designated 6b and used for subsequent biochemical and in vivo studies.

### Example 2

In vivo pharmacokinetic and biodistribution studies were conducted in male C57BL/6J lean mice aged 14 to 16 weeks. Mice were fasted overnight for 14 hours and then administered 6bK intraperitoneally at a dose of 80 mg/kg. Blood samples were collected at 5, 15, 30, 60, 120, and 240 minutes post-injection via tail vein puncture, and plasma was separated by centrifugation. Tissues including liver, pancreas, skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, kidney, spleen, and brain were harvested at 150 minutes post-injection. Plasma and tissue samples were processed for isotope dilution mass spectrometry using a heavy-labeled 13C6,15N2-6bK internal standard. Samples were homogenized in ice-cold methanol, centrifuged, and the supernatant was dried under nitrogen. The residue was reconstituted in acetonitrile-water and analyzed by LC-MS/MS using a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization source. The transition m/z 1131.7 → 852.5 was monitored for 6bK, and m/z 1137.7 → 858.5 for the internal standard. Calibration curves were generated using spiked plasma and tissue homogenates, and concentrations were calculated using a weighted linear regression. Plasma concentrations of 6bK peaked at 5.2 µM at 30 minutes and remained above the IC50 for IDE (47 nM) for over 4 hours. Tissue concentrations were highest in the liver (1,850 ng/g), followed by the pancreas (1,240 ng/g), kidney (980 ng/g), and adipose tissue (760 ng/g). No detectable levels of 6bK were found in brain tissue, even after concentration of samples tenfold for LC-MS injection. These results confirm the peripheral restriction of the compound and its suitability for targeting metabolic tissues without central nervous system exposure.
