# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The measurement of gravitational acceleration with high precision has long been a cornerstone of geophysics, fundamental physics, and inertial navigation systems. Traditional gravimeters, particularly those based on mechanical spring systems or superconducting sensors, have demonstrated remarkable stability and accuracy under controlled laboratory conditions. However, their performance degrades significantly in environments subject to ambient vibrational noise, such as field deployments, airborne platforms, or mobile laboratories. The advent of atom interferometry has revolutionized the field by offering a fundamentally quantum-based approach to inertial sensing, leveraging the wave nature of matter to achieve unprecedented sensitivity to accelerations. In particular, the Kasevich-Chu interferometer, which employs stimulated Raman transitions to coherently manipulate atomic wave packets, has emerged as the gold standard for absolute gravimetry due to its direct traceability to fundamental constants and its immunity to many classical drift mechanisms. Nevertheless, the practical utility of atom interferometers remains constrained by their extreme susceptibility to vibrational disturbances. Even minute mechanical vibrations, on the order of micrometers or less, induce phase shifts in the interferometer that exceed the quantum interference fringe, leading to signal decoherence and loss of measurement fidelity. This sensitivity arises because the atoms, as free-falling quantum objects, respond to the total acceleration experienced by the interferometer’s reference frame, including both gravitational and inertial components. Consequently, the phase difference between the two arms of the interferometer becomes dominated by the time-varying inertial noise rather than the static gravitational signal of interest. To mitigate this, current state-of-the-art systems rely on bulky, active vibration isolation platforms, which are expensive, power-intensive, mechanically complex, and often impractical for field applications or miniaturized systems. These isolation systems, while effective at attenuating high-frequency noise, are inherently limited in their ability to suppress low-frequency vibrations below one hertz, precisely the band where many environmental disturbances—such as seismic tremors, building sway, or traffic-induced ground motion—predominate. Moreover, the isolation systems themselves introduce additional sources of systematic error, including thermal drift, magnetic coupling, and mechanical hysteresis, which further complicate calibration and long-term stability. Alternative approaches have been explored, including differential interferometry using multiple atomic ensembles, temporal filtering of noisy data, and post-processing techniques based on external accelerometer arrays. However, these methods suffer from inherent limitations: differential schemes require precise spatial and temporal alignment of multiple interferometers, which is challenging to maintain over extended durations; temporal filtering sacrifices measurement bandwidth and introduces latency; and external accelerometer arrays, while useful for noise characterization, do not provide a common-mode reference to the atoms themselves, leading to residual coupling errors due to spatial separation and non-identical dynamic responses. Furthermore, none of these existing methods enable real-time, in situ correction of the atom interferometer signal using a sensor that shares the exact same mechanical reference as the atoms. This fundamental disconnect between the atomic probe and the inertial reference has remained an unresolved bottleneck in the field, preventing atom interferometers from achieving their full potential in noisy, real-world environments. There exists, therefore, a critical need for a novel sensing architecture that integrates a high-fidelity inertial reference directly into the interferometer’s optical path, enabling simultaneous, co-located measurement of the inertial disturbances affecting both the atoms and the optical components, and thereby permitting a direct, coherent subtraction of the noise component from the interferometric signal. Such a system would not only eliminate the necessity for external isolation systems but also unlock new operational regimes for quantum sensors, including continuous, unattended operation in urban environments, deployment on moving platforms, and integration into compact, field-deployable instruments. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a hybrid quantum-inertial sensor that fuses an atom interferometer with a monolithic optomechanical resonator, where the resonator’s test mass is mechanically coupled to the same retroreflector that defines the phase reference for the atom interferometer. By doing so, the inertial forces acting on the atoms and the optical mirror are experienced identically and simultaneously, allowing for a direct, real-time, and physically grounded correction of the interferometric phase. The optomechanical resonator, constructed from a high-finesse optical cavity formed between a fiber tip and a fused silica test mass mounted on a stiff cantilever, provides a highly sensitive, broadband, and low-noise measurement of mirror displacement induced by ambient accelerations. Its optical readout, based on a stabilized telecom-wavelength laser and differential interferometry, enables sub-nanometer resolution of mirror motion with minimal thermal and magnetic interference. Crucially, the resonator is fabricated and mounted such that its sensitive axis is perfectly aligned with the direction of the atom interferometer’s beam propagation, ensuring that the measured displacement corresponds precisely to the acceleration component that couples into the interferometric phase. The combination of these two systems—atom interferometer and optomechanical resonator—operating in tandem under identical environmental conditions, constitutes a novel sensor fusion paradigm that transcends conventional noise suppression techniques. Unlike prior hybrid systems that rely on statistical correlation or external calibration, this invention exploits a shared physical reference to achieve true common-mode rejection of inertial noise, resulting in a differential signal that isolates the gravitational acceleration with unprecedented fidelity. The system operates without active isolation, without external reference sensors, and without requiring prior knowledge of the noise spectrum. It is capable of uninterrupted, high-precision gravimetric measurements in environments where conventional atom interferometers would fail entirely. Moreover, the architecture is inherently scalable, compatible with fiber-optic integration, and amenable to miniaturization, opening pathways to chip-scale quantum inertial sensors for navigation, geophysical surveying, and space-based applications. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in quantum sensing, transforming the atom interferometer from a laboratory curiosity into a robust, field-deployable instrument capable of operating in the real world.

## SUMMARY OF THE EMBODIMENTS

The present invention relates to a hybrid quantum-inertial sensor for the precise measurement of gravitational acceleration, comprising an atom interferometer and an optomechanical resonator mechanically coupled to a common reference structure, wherein the optomechanical resonator provides a real-time, high-fidelity measurement of inertial disturbances affecting the interferometer, enabling the suppression of vibrational noise through direct phase correction. The atom interferometer is configured to manipulate a cloud of cold atoms using a sequence of laser pulses that coherently split, redirect, and recombine atomic wave packets, thereby generating an interference pattern whose phase is sensitive to the local gravitational acceleration and to any inertial accelerations acting on the interferometer’s reference frame. The optomechanical resonator is constructed as a high-finesse optical cavity formed between a first reflective surface mounted on the end of a polarization-maintaining optical fiber and a second reflective surface formed on a rigid test mass, the test mass being mechanically coupled to a retroreflector that directs the laser pulses of the atom interferometer. The resonator is designed such that its mechanical resonance frequency and optical finesse are selected to provide a broadband, high-sensitivity response to accelerations within the operational bandwidth of the atom interferometer, typically ranging from 0.1 Hz to 100 Hz. The test mass and retroreflector are rigidly bonded or otherwise integrally mounted to ensure that any inertial displacement of the retroreflector is transmitted with negligible phase lag or mode mismatch to the test mass of the optomechanical resonator, thereby establishing a shared inertial reference between the atoms and the optical path. A laser source, operating at a wavelength compatible with telecommunications standards, is coupled to the optomechanical resonator to generate an optical readout signal proportional to the displacement of the test mass, which is detected using a balanced photodetector configuration that cancels common-mode intensity noise from the laser source. The detected signal is digitally sampled at a rate sufficient to capture the dynamics of the interferometer’s cycle, typically at intervals centered on the mid-point of the interferometric sequence, and is processed using a high-pass filter to remove low-frequency drift and a low-pass filter to restrict the bandwidth to the interferometer’s response limit. The filtered signal is then scaled by a pre-determined acceleration sensitivity function, derived from the geometry and dynamics of the atom interferometer, to convert the measured displacement into an equivalent inertial acceleration. This calculated acceleration is subtracted from the raw interferometric phase signal to yield a corrected phase that reflects the true gravitational acceleration, free from the confounding effects of ambient vibration. The system operates continuously without the need for external vibration isolation, enabling uninterrupted measurement sequences lasting many hours, even in environments with high levels of mechanical noise. The optomechanical resonator is fabricated from non-magnetic, low-thermal-emissivity materials such as fused silica and fiber-optic components, ensuring that it introduces no spurious magnetic fields or blackbody radiation that could perturb the atomic ensemble. The entire sensor head is compact, with a volume on the order of cubic millimeters, and is compatible with all-fiber optical integration, facilitating deployment in portable, airborne, or space-based platforms. The invention further encompasses embodiments wherein multiple optomechanical resonators are deployed with differing resonance frequencies or orthogonal sensitive axes, enabling simultaneous measurement of multi-axis inertial noise and extending the system’s bandwidth and dynamic range. The method of operation includes the steps of generating a coherent atomic interferometer signal, simultaneously measuring the inertial displacement of the retroreflector via the optomechanical resonator, filtering and scaling the resonator signal to obtain an acceleration estimate, and applying the estimate as a real-time correction to the interferometric phase. The resulting corrected signal exhibits a reduction in short-term instability by a factor exceeding eight compared to the uncorrected signal, and enables the achievement of a white noise floor of less than ten nanometers per square root hertz in acceleration sensitivity, making it suitable for applications requiring high precision in noisy environments. The invention further includes a method for calibrating the acceleration sensitivity function by applying known accelerations to the system and correlating the resonator output with the interferometer phase shift, thereby establishing a transfer function that is stable over time and insensitive to environmental drift. The system is further configured to operate in a k-reversal mode, wherein the momentum transfer direction of the Raman pulses is alternated between two orientations, and the resulting phase differences are averaged to cancel systematic offsets, further enhancing the accuracy of the gravitational measurement. The invention is not limited to rubidium atoms and is applicable to any atomic species suitable for laser cooling and Raman transitions, including cesium, potassium, or ytterbium. It is also extendable to other quantum interferometric systems, such as matter-wave interferometers using molecules or Bose-Einstein condensates, and to classical laser interferometers used in gravitational wave detection or precision metrology, provided that a common mechanical reference exists between the test mass and the interferometer’s optical path. The invention thus provides a novel, integrated, and scalable architecture for quantum inertial sensing that overcomes the fundamental limitations of existing systems by fusing quantum measurement with classical inertial sensing at the physical level, yielding a sensor that is simultaneously precise, robust, compact, and field-deployable.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE EMBODIMENTS

The hybrid quantum-inertial sensor of the present invention comprises a sophisticated integration of atomic quantum interferometry and optomechanical transduction, unified through a shared mechanical reference that enables unprecedented noise suppression in gravitational measurements. The core architecture is built around a Kasevich-Chu atom interferometer, which utilizes laser-cooled atoms of 87Rb as quantum probes of inertial motion. These atoms are confined in a magneto-optical trap and subsequently launched vertically using optical molasses, forming a coherent atomic wave packet that evolves under free fall. The interferometric sequence consists of three precisely timed, counter-propagating Raman laser pulses, each of which imparts a momentum transfer of 2ℏk_eff to the atoms, where k_eff is the effective wavevector of the two-photon Raman transition. The first pulse, a π/2 pulse, coherently splits the atomic wave function into two momentum states, creating a spatial superposition. The second pulse, a π pulse, redirects the trajectories of the two components, causing them to exchange momentum and reverse their relative velocity. The third pulse, another π/2 pulse, recombines the two wave packets, producing an interference pattern whose phase depends on the total acceleration experienced by the atoms during the interrogation time T, which is typically set to 10 milliseconds. The population difference between the two output ports of the interferometer is measured via state-selective fluorescence detection, yielding a sinusoidal signal whose amplitude and phase encode the gravitational acceleration. However, in the presence of ambient vibrational noise, the phase of this interference pattern becomes corrupted by inertial disturbances that couple to the atoms through the motion of the retroreflector mirror, which defines the optical path length for the Raman beams. This mirror, which is part of the interferometer’s optical reference frame, is subject to the same environmental accelerations as the atoms, but in conventional systems, there is no direct means to measure these disturbances at the same location and with the same time resolution as the atomic measurement. The present invention resolves this limitation by integrating an optomechanical resonator directly onto the retroreflector mirror itself. The optomechanical resonator is constructed as a high-finesse Fabry-Pérot cavity formed between two highly reflective surfaces: one mounted on the cleaved end of a polarization-maintaining single-mode optical fiber, and the other formed as a flat, polished surface on a rigid, low-mass test mass fabricated from fused silica. The test mass is mechanically coupled to the retroreflector mirror via a stiff, U-shaped cantilever structure, which serves to constrain the motion of the test mass to a single axis—namely, the direction normal to the retroreflector’s surface. This alignment is critical, as the interferometer’s phase sensitivity is maximized along the direction of the laser beam propagation, and any misalignment would introduce cross-coupling of orthogonal accelerations, leading to systematic errors. The cantilever is engineered to have a high mechanical stiffness, ensuring that the test mass behaves as a near-ideal harmonic oscillator with a well-defined resonance frequency of approximately 678.5 Hz, corresponding to an angular frequency of 2π × 678.5 rad/s. The mechanical quality factor Q of the resonator is measured to be 630, indicating that the system exhibits low internal damping and high energy storage, which is essential for achieving high displacement sensitivity. The optical cavity is interrogated by a tunable laser source operating at a wavelength of 1560 nm, chosen for compatibility with standard telecommunications fiber components and for its low absorption in silica materials. The laser light is delivered via a polarization-maintaining fiber, and a quarter-wave plate is inserted into the optical path to convert the polarization state of the reflected light, enabling the use of a polarizing beam splitter to separate the signal reflected from the resonator from the incident light. A small fraction of the laser light is tapped off prior to the resonator using a 90:10 optical coupler and directed to a reference photodetector, while the majority of the light is coupled into the resonator and reflected back toward a balanced photodetector pair. The balanced detection scheme, comprising two matched photodiodes connected in differential mode, cancels out common-mode intensity noise from the laser source, which is a dominant source of technical noise in high-sensitivity optical measurements. The output of the balanced detector is thus a voltage signal proportional to the intracavity optical phase shift, which, in turn, is directly related to the displacement of the test mass relative to the fiber tip. The displacement sensitivity of the resonator is calibrated using a known acceleration source, such as a commercial force-balance accelerometer, to establish a linear relationship between the detected optical signal and the applied inertial acceleration. This calibration reveals that the resonator exhibits a flat frequency response below its mechanical resonance, meaning that for frequencies below approximately 600 Hz, the displacement of the test mass is directly proportional to the applied acceleration, as predicted by the dynamics of a harmonic oscillator. This flat response is ideal for the purposes of noise correction, as ambient vibrational noise in urban and field environments typically spans the 0.1 Hz to 50 Hz range, precisely where the atom interferometer’s sensitivity is highest. The optical signal from the resonator is digitized using a high-speed analog-to-digital converter operating at a sampling rate of 10 kHz, with data acquisition synchronized to the interferometer’s cycle timing. For each interferometric cycle, a 60-millisecond window of the resonator signal is captured, centered on the midpoint of the interferometric sequence, thereby ensuring that the measured displacement corresponds to the inertial motion experienced by the atoms during the critical phase accumulation period. The acquired signal is then subjected to digital filtering to remove unwanted frequency components. A high-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 0.8 Hz is applied to eliminate slow drifts arising from thermal expansion, mechanical creep, or laser frequency drift, which are not correlated with the interferometer’s dynamics. A low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 50 Hz is subsequently applied to restrict the bandwidth to the interferometer’s corner frequency, which is determined by the interrogation time T and is given by 1/(2T). This filtering ensures that only the noise components that are physically coupled into the interferometric phase are retained, while higher-frequency components, which the interferometer cannot respond to due to its finite bandwidth, are discarded. The filtered signal is then multiplied by a pre-computed acceleration sensitivity function, which is derived from the known geometry of the interferometer and the mechanical properties of the resonator. This function accounts for the fact that the interferometer’s phase response to acceleration is not uniform across frequency but rather follows a low-pass characteristic, with maximum sensitivity at low frequencies and diminishing response above the corner frequency. The sensitivity function is determined experimentally by applying known accelerations to the system and measuring the resulting phase shifts in the atom interferometer, while simultaneously recording the resonator output. The resulting transfer function is stored as a digital lookup table or analytical expression and is applied in real time to convert the resonator’s displacement signal into an equivalent inertial acceleration. This estimated acceleration is then subtracted from the raw interferometric phase signal, yielding a corrected phase that reflects the true gravitational acceleration, free from the confounding influence of ambient vibration. The corrected signal is then fitted to a sinusoidal model to extract the gravitational acceleration for each measurement cycle, and the results are averaged over time to improve statistical precision. The system is operated in a k-reversal mode, wherein the direction of the momentum transfer imparted by the Raman pulses is alternated between two orientations—positive and negative—with respect to the vertical axis. This technique cancels out systematic phase offsets that arise from asymmetries in the laser beams, magnetic field gradients, or residual light shifts, thereby improving the absolute accuracy of the measurement. The k-reversal protocol is implemented by flipping the phase of one of the Raman beams between consecutive measurement cycles, and the resulting phase difference between the two orientations is used to compute the net gravitational acceleration. The combination of k-reversal and optomechanical noise correction yields a measurement that is both highly stable and highly accurate, with a short-term instability reduced by a factor of eight compared to the uncorrected signal. The Allan deviation of the corrected signal, computed over integration times from 1 second to 10,000 seconds, demonstrates a white noise floor of 1 × 10^-5 m/s²/√Hz between 10 Hz and 50 Hz, with pink noise dominating below 10 Hz and Brownian noise becoming apparent below 1 Hz. The Brownian noise, which arises from thermal fluctuations in the optical fiber used to deliver the laser light to the resonator, is identified as the dominant low-frequency limitation, and its origin is traced to the mechanical dissipation in the fiber’s cladding and coating. This noise source is not intrinsic to the resonator itself but is an artifact of the current readout implementation, and it is expected to be mitigated in future iterations through the use of low-loss, low-thermal-expansion fiber designs or free-space optical delivery. The system’s performance is further enhanced by the choice of materials and construction techniques. The test mass and retroreflector are fabricated from fused silica, a material with negligible magnetic susceptibility, zero net magnetic moment, and extremely low blackbody radiation emission at ambient temperatures. This eliminates the possibility of spurious magnetic coupling to the atomic spins or thermal radiation-induced phase shifts, both of which are common sources of systematic error in conventional atom interferometers. The entire sensor head, including the resonator, retroreflector, and mounting structure, occupies a volume of less than 500 cubic millimeters, making it orders of magnitude smaller than conventional vibration isolation platforms. The system is fully compatible with all-fiber optical integration, meaning that the laser delivery, interferometer beam steering, and resonator readout can all be accomplished using standard telecom-grade fiber components, enabling robust, alignment-free operation in field-deployable systems. The absence of moving parts, high-power electronics, or cryogenic systems further enhances reliability and reduces power consumption. The invention is not limited to the specific parameters described herein. In alternative embodiments, the mechanical resonance frequency of the optomechanical resonator may be tuned to a higher value, such as 1500 Hz, to increase the usable bandwidth and reduce susceptibility to low-frequency noise, at the expense of slightly reduced sensitivity. This trade-off is acceptable in applications requiring higher dynamic range, such as airborne gravimetry or inertial navigation, where the noise spectrum is broader and more complex. The optical finesse of the resonator may also be improved from its current value of approximately two to values exceeding 1600 through the application of high-reflectivity dielectric coatings, which would enhance the displacement sensitivity by an order of magnitude. Furthermore, the readout scheme may be upgraded from simple intensity modulation to advanced locking techniques such as Pound-Drever-Hall locking, which would enable sub-picometer resolution of mirror motion and further reduce technical noise. The system may also be extended to multi-axis operation by incorporating multiple optomechanical resonators, each aligned along a different orthogonal axis, thereby enabling the simultaneous measurement of acceleration in three dimensions. This would be particularly valuable for inertial navigation applications, where full attitude and motion tracking is required. The sensor may also be adapted for use with other atomic species, such as cesium or ytterbium, by adjusting the Raman laser wavelengths accordingly, or for use with molecular interferometers, where the mass of the probe particles is larger and the recoil momentum is correspondingly smaller. The same architecture may also be applied to classical laser interferometers used in gravitational wave detectors, where the test masses are subject to seismic noise, by replacing the conventional seismic isolation system with a co-located optomechanical sensor that provides real-time noise correction. In such applications, the optomechanical resonator would be mounted directly on the test mass of the interferometer, and its output would be used to correct the interferometric phase in real time, effectively transforming a passive interferometer into an active, noise-compensated sensor. The invention further encompasses a method for calibrating the acceleration sensitivity function in situ, without the need for external reference accelerometers. This is achieved by applying controlled, periodic accelerations to the system using a piezoelectric actuator mounted on the baseplate, and measuring the resulting phase shifts in both the atom interferometer and the optomechanical resonator. The ratio of the interferometer phase change to the resonator displacement change yields the sensitivity function directly, eliminating the need for external calibration and ensuring long-term stability. The system may also be operated in a continuous mode, where the interferometer cycles at a rate of 1 Hz or higher, and the noise correction is applied in real time, enabling the generation of a continuous, high-resolution time series of gravitational acceleration. This capability opens new possibilities for monitoring subsurface density changes, detecting underground voids, or tracking volcanic activity, all from a stationary or mobile platform. The invention’s ability to operate without active vibration isolation represents a paradigm shift in quantum sensing. It removes the need for large, expensive, and fragile isolation systems, enabling the deployment of atom interferometers in environments previously considered too noisy for such measurements. The sensor is robust, compact, scalable, and compatible with existing fiber-optic infrastructure, making it ideal for integration into portable gravimeters, airborne survey systems, or even satellite-based quantum sensors. The fusion of quantum measurement with classical inertial sensing at the physical level—where the noise is measured and corrected using a sensor that shares the exact same reference frame as the quantum probe—constitutes a novel and powerful approach that transcends conventional signal processing techniques. It is not merely a filtering or averaging method; it is a physical cancellation of noise at its source, enabled by the intimate coupling of two distinct physical systems. The resulting sensor is not just an improvement over existing technology; it is a fundamentally new class of instrument, capable of achieving quantum-limited precision in the real world. The embodiments described herein are illustrative and not exhaustive; numerous variations and enhancements are possible within the scope of the invention, including the use of cryogenic environments to further reduce thermal noise, the integration of microfabricated resonators for chip-scale deployment, or the coupling of multiple interferometers to a single resonator for differential measurements. The invention thus provides a comprehensive, integrated, and scalable solution to the longstanding problem of vibrational noise in quantum inertial sensors, paving the way for the widespread practical application of atom interferometry in science, industry, and defense.
