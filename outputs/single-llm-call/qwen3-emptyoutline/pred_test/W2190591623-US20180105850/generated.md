# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of metabolic engineering and synthetic biology, specifically to the high-yield production of L-ornithine in the eukaryotic microorganism Saccharomyces cerevisiae through a coordinated, multi-module reconfiguration of cellular metabolism. The invention encompasses genetically modified strains of Saccharomyces cerevisiae engineered to overproduce L-ornithine by systematically rewiring endogenous biosynthetic pathways, modulating subcellular compartmentalization, attenuating competing metabolic fluxes, and optimizing precursor supply under industrial fermentation conditions. The strains and methods described herein enable the efficient conversion of glucose into L-ornithine at titers exceeding one gram per liter, with minimal by-product formation, thereby providing a scalable, cost-effective, and environmentally sustainable platform for the industrial biosynthesis of L-ornithine and its downstream derivatives, including L-arginine, polyamines, and pyrroline-5-carboxylate. The invention further extends to the use of these strains in bioreactor-based fermentation processes for the commercial production of L-ornithine as a nutraceutical, pharmaceutical intermediate, or precursor for bio-based chemicals.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

L-Ornithine is a non-proteinogenic amino acid that serves as a critical intermediate in the urea cycle and as a precursor for the biosynthesis of L-arginine, proline, and polyamines such as putrescine and spermidine. Its physiological roles in nitrogen metabolism, cellular detoxification, and protein synthesis have rendered it a valuable compound in the pharmaceutical, food, and agricultural industries. Traditionally, L-ornithine has been produced industrially through chemical synthesis or microbial fermentation using bacteria such as Corynebacterium glutamicum, which naturally overproduces amino acids due to their robust metabolic networks and well-characterized regulatory systems. However, bacterial systems are often limited in their ability to perform complex post-translational modifications, tolerate high osmotic stress, or operate under the stringent sterility and scalability requirements of large-scale industrial fermentation. Furthermore, the use of pathogenic or non-GRAS (Generally Recognized As Safe) bacterial hosts raises regulatory and consumer acceptance concerns in food and nutraceutical applications.

Saccharomyces cerevisiae, commonly known as baker’s yeast, has emerged as an attractive alternative host for amino acid production due to its well-established genetic tractability, GRAS status, tolerance to low pH and high osmolarity, and compatibility with industrial fermentation infrastructure. Despite these advantages, the native metabolism of S. cerevisiae is highly regulated and compartmentalized, with L-ornithine biosynthesis occurring primarily within the mitochondria and tightly coupled to the biosynthesis of L-arginine through a series of feedback-inhibited enzymatic steps. The endogenous pathway is further constrained by the spatial separation of key metabolic intermediates across distinct subcellular compartments—namely, the cytosol and mitochondria—resulting in inefficient transport, metabolic bottlenecks, and futile cycling. Moreover, under conditions of high glucose availability, S. cerevisiae exhibits the Crabtree effect, a phenomenon wherein excess carbon is diverted toward ethanol fermentation rather than oxidative metabolism, thereby severely limiting the flux of glucose-derived carbon into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and, consequently, into α-ketoglutarate, the essential precursor for L-ornithine biosynthesis.

Previous attempts to engineer L-ornithine production in S. cerevisiae have been largely unsuccessful due to the inability to overcome these intrinsic regulatory and structural constraints. Strategies involving the complete deletion of genes responsible for L-ornithine consumption, such as ornithine carbamoyltransferase (ARG3) or arginase (CAR1), have resulted in L-arginine auxotrophy, necessitating costly supplementation of L-arginine in fermentation media and rendering such strains unsuitable for large-scale industrial use. Other efforts focused on overexpressing individual enzymes in the L-ornithine pathway, but these approaches failed to significantly increase titers due to the lack of coordinated regulation across the entire biosynthetic network. Additionally, attempts to relocate parts of the pathway to the cytosol were hampered by the absence of compatible heterologous enzymes, incompatible cofactor availability, or the disruption of essential mitochondrial functions. The prevailing understanding in the field has been that the compartmentalization of L-ornithine biosynthesis in yeast is an insurmountable obstacle to achieving high-yield production, and that the Crabtree effect fundamentally limits the carbon efficiency of any amino acid production system in this organism under high-glucose conditions.

Consequently, there exists a long-felt but unmet need in the biotechnology industry for a genetically engineered strain of Saccharomyces cerevisiae capable of efficiently converting glucose into L-ornithine at industrially relevant titers without requiring exogenous supplementation, without producing significant ethanol by-products, and without compromising cellular growth or viability. There is also a critical need for a systematic methodology that enables the simultaneous optimization of multiple metabolic modules—including precursor supply, biosynthesis, consumption, and subcellular trafficking—through a modular, iterative, and scalable approach. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel and comprehensive strategy for metabolic rewiring that overcomes each of these longstanding barriers, resulting in a strain that achieves unprecedented levels of L-ornithine production and sets a new standard for amino acid biosynthesis in eukaryotic microbial cell factories.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a genetically modified strain of Saccharomyces cerevisiae engineered for the high-yield production of L-ornithine through a coordinated, multi-module metabolic rewiring strategy that simultaneously addresses the limitations of endogenous regulation, subcellular compartmentalization, and carbon overflow metabolism. The strain is characterized by a combination of genetic modifications that include: (1) the fine-tuned downregulation of ornithine carbamoyltransferase (ARG3) expression to establish a leaky L-arginine auxotrophy that permits low-level L-arginine biosynthesis for cellular growth while minimizing feedback inhibition on L-ornithine production; (2) the deletion of ornithine aminotransferase (CAR2) to prevent the conversion of L-ornithine to L-glutamate γ-semialdehyde; (3) the overexpression of mitochondrial transporters, including the ornithine exporter Ort1p and the glutamate/aspartate exchanger Agc1p, to enhance the flux of L-ornithine and its precursor L-glutamate across the mitochondrial membrane; (4) the introduction of a synthetic, heterologous cytosolic L-ornithine biosynthetic pathway derived from Escherichia coli and Corynebacterium glutamicum, comprising the genes argAEC, argBEc, argJCg, argCCg, and argDCg, which collectively convert L-glutamate directly into L-ornithine in the cytosol, thereby bypassing mitochondrial constraints and eliminating the need for intercompartmental transport; (5) the overexpression of the truncated form of the transcriptional regulator MTH1-ΔT to attenuate glucose uptake and thereby suppress the Crabtree effect, resulting in the redirection of carbon flux from ethanol fermentation to the TCA cycle and ultimately to α-ketoglutarate; and (6) the overexpression of arginase (CAR1) to catalyze the degradation of L-arginine into L-ornithine and urea, thereby reducing intracellular L-arginine levels and further alleviating feedback inhibition on the biosynthetic pathway.

The resulting strain exhibits a synergistic enhancement in L-ornithine production, achieving titers exceeding 1.0 gram per liter in shake-flask cultures and up to 5.1 grams per liter in fed-batch fermentations, representing a more than twenty-fold increase over previously reported strains and a dramatic improvement in carbon efficiency. The strain operates without the need for exogenous L-arginine supplementation, produces negligible quantities of ethanol, maintains robust growth kinetics, and is fully compatible with standard industrial fermentation processes. The invention further encompasses a method for producing L-ornithine by cultivating the genetically modified strain in a nutrient medium containing glucose as the sole carbon source under controlled aerobic conditions, wherein the metabolic flux is directed toward L-ornithine synthesis through the combined effects of the aforementioned genetic modifications. The invention also includes the use of the strain in continuous, fed-batch, or chemostat fermentation systems for the scalable and economically viable production of L-ornithine for use in pharmaceutical, nutraceutical, and industrial applications.

The invention is distinguished from prior art by its integrated, modular approach to metabolic engineering, which recognizes that the successful overproduction of a pathway intermediate such as L-ornithine in a compartmentalized eukaryotic host requires not merely the overexpression of biosynthetic enzymes, but the systematic reconfiguration of upstream precursor supply, downstream consumption, subcellular trafficking, and carbon partitioning. The invention demonstrates that the most effective strategy for L-ornithine overproduction is not the enhancement of the native mitochondrial pathway, but rather the complete translocation of the biosynthetic machinery to the cytosol, coupled with the attenuation of glucose uptake to eliminate overflow metabolism. This paradigm shift in metabolic engineering strategy—moving from localized enzyme overexpression to whole-pathway relocation and global carbon flux control—represents a fundamental advancement in the field and establishes a new framework for the production of other mitochondrial-derived metabolites in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and related eukaryotes.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a genetically modified strain of Saccharomyces cerevisiae capable of overproducing L-ornithine at industrially significant levels through a series of precisely coordinated genetic modifications that collectively rewire the organism’s central metabolism to favor the accumulation of L-ornithine as a primary end product. The strain is constructed through a modular pathway rewiring strategy that divides the metabolic network into three distinct functional modules: Module 1, governing the consumption and degradation of L-ornithine and its derivatives; Module 2, responsible for the biosynthesis of L-ornithine from α-ketoglutarate; and Module 3, controlling the supply of α-ketoglutarate from glucose through glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid cycle. Each module is independently optimized in a sequential manner, with the output of one module serving as the foundation for the subsequent optimization of the next, ensuring that each modification is evaluated in the context of a progressively more efficient metabolic background. This serial engineering approach, combined with the strategic use of heterologous genes, subcellular re-localization, and transcriptional fine-tuning, results in a strain that achieves unprecedented levels of L-ornithine production without compromising cellular fitness or requiring costly media supplements.

The first critical modification involves the attenuation of L-ornithine consumption pathways to prevent its conversion into competing metabolites while maintaining sufficient L-arginine biosynthesis for cell viability. In wild-type Saccharomyces cerevisiae, L-ornithine is rapidly consumed through two primary routes: the conversion of L-ornithine to L-citrulline by ornithine carbamoyltransferase (encoded by ARG3) and the transamination of L-ornithine to L-glutamate γ-semialdehyde by ornithine aminotransferase (encoded by CAR2). Complete deletion of ARG3, a common strategy in bacterial systems, leads to L-arginine auxotrophy, necessitating the addition of exogenous L-arginine to the growth medium, which is economically prohibitive for large-scale fermentation. To circumvent this limitation, the invention employs a novel strategy of promoter replacement to reduce, rather than eliminate, ARG3 expression. Specifically, the native promoter of ARG3 is replaced with either the HXT1 promoter, which is glucose-regulated and exhibits low activity under high-glucose conditions, or the KEX2 promoter, which is inherently weak and constitutively repressed. This results in a strain that synthesizes L-arginine at levels sufficient to support normal growth and protein synthesis, while simultaneously limiting the flux of L-ornithine into the arginine biosynthetic pathway. The resulting leaky auxotrophy creates a metabolic imbalance in which L-ornithine accumulates intracellularly due to reduced consumption, while the cell remains viable without external supplementation. The intracellular concentration of L-arginine in this strain is maintained at approximately 70% of wild-type levels, indicating that the pathway is not fully blocked but rather fine-tuned to favor accumulation of the precursor. This strategy is further enhanced by the deletion of CAR2, which eliminates the alternative route of L-ornithine degradation to L-glutamate γ-semialdehyde, thereby preventing a futile cycle that would otherwise consume L-ornithine without contributing to its net accumulation. The resulting strain, designated M1c, serves as the foundational platform for all subsequent modifications.

The second module of the invention focuses on the biosynthesis of L-ornithine from α-ketoglutarate, a process that in wild-type yeast occurs exclusively within the mitochondria and involves five enzymatic steps: the acetylation of L-glutamate by N-acetylglutamate synthase (ARG5,6), the conversion to N-acetylglutamate semialdehyde by N-acetylglutamate reductase (ARG7), the formation of N-acetylornithine by N-acetylornithine aminotransferase (ARG8), the deacetylation of N-acetylornithine to ornithine by N-acetylornithine deacetylase (ARG2), and the regeneration of the acetyl group through the recycling of the acetyl moiety. In wild-type cells, this pathway is constrained by the limited availability of L-glutamate within the mitochondria and the inefficient export of L-ornithine to the cytosol. To overcome these limitations, the invention implements a multi-pronged strategy that includes the overexpression of the mitochondrial biosynthetic enzymes ARG5,6, ARG7, ARG8, and ARG2, which collectively enhance the catalytic capacity of the pathway. However, single-gene overexpression of any one of these enzymes fails to produce a significant increase in L-ornithine titer, indicating that the pathway is not limited by the activity of any single enzyme but rather by the coordinated flux through the entire cycle. The most substantial improvement is achieved through the overexpression of ARG2, the enzyme responsible for the deacetylation step, which increases the L-ornithine titer by 36% compared to the parent strain. This suggests that the recycling of the acetyl group is a critical bottleneck in the pathway and that increasing the flux through this step enhances the overall catalytic efficiency of the cycle. To further enhance precursor supply, the invention overexpresses the mitochondrial glutamate transporter AGC1, which facilitates the import of cytosolic L-glutamate into the mitochondria, and the ornithine exporter ORT1, which mediates the efflux of L-ornithine from the mitochondria into the cytosol. The combined overexpression of AGC1 and ORT1 in the M1cM2h strain results in a 44% increase in L-ornithine titer, demonstrating that the transport of intermediates across mitochondrial membranes is a major rate-limiting factor in the pathway. Despite these improvements, the mitochondrial pathway remains constrained by the compartmentalization of metabolism and the limited availability of cofactors such as NADPH, which is required for the function of certain enzymes in the pathway.

To circumvent these inherent limitations of the mitochondrial pathway, the invention introduces a novel and transformative strategy: the complete translocation of the L-ornithine biosynthetic pathway from the mitochondria to the cytosol. Rather than attempting to optimize the native yeast pathway, the invention constructs a synthetic, heterologous pathway derived from two highly efficient amino acid-producing organisms: Escherichia coli and Corynebacterium glutamicum. This synthetic pathway consists of five genes: argAEC and argBEc from E. coli, which encode N-acetylglutamate synthase and N-acetylglutamate reductase, respectively, and argJCg, argCCg, and argDCg from C. glutamicum, which encode N-acetylornithine aminotransferase, N-acetylornithine deacetylase, and ornithine carbamoyltransferase, respectively. These genes are assembled into a single expression cassette under the control of strong, constitutive promoters and integrated into the yeast genome. The resulting strain, designated M1cM2q, is capable of converting L-glutamate directly into L-ornithine in the cytosol, bypassing the need for mitochondrial import, acetylation, and export entirely. This strategy is validated by the successful complementation of an L-arginine auxotrophic yeast strain lacking ORT1, demonstrating that the cytosolic pathway is fully functional and capable of supplying sufficient L-ornithine for arginine biosynthesis. The strain M1cM2q achieves a L-ornithine titer of 192 mg/L, representing an 11% increase over the best-performing mitochondrial strain and establishing the cytosolic pathway as the superior configuration for L-ornithine production. This result is further corroborated by the observation that attempts to relocate the native glutamate dehydrogenase Gdh1p to the mitochondria—intended to increase mitochondrial L-glutamate supply—result in a significant decrease in L-ornithine titer, suggesting that the mitochondrial environment is incompatible with the efficient function of these enzymes. The success of the cytosolic pathway underscores a fundamental principle of metabolic engineering in eukaryotes: the relocation of entire biosynthetic pathways to the cytosol can be more effective than the incremental optimization of compartmentalized native pathways, as it eliminates transport barriers, reduces cofactor mismatches, and prevents the diversion of intermediates into competing pathways.

The third and final module of the invention addresses the supply of α-ketoglutarate, the essential precursor for L-ornithine biosynthesis, which is derived from the tricarboxylic acid cycle. In wild-type Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the majority of glucose carbon is diverted toward ethanol fermentation under high-glucose conditions due to the Crabtree effect, a phenomenon driven by the high activity of glucose transporters and the saturation of mitochondrial respiration. This results in a severe limitation of carbon flux into the TCA cycle and, consequently, into α-ketoglutarate. Previous attempts to overcome this limitation through the overexpression of TCA cycle enzymes such as PDA1, PYC2, CIT1, ACO2, and IDP1 yielded only modest improvements in L-ornithine titer, indicating that simply increasing enzyme capacity is insufficient to redirect carbon flux. Similarly, the overexpression of alternative NADH oxidases such as HaAOX1 and NDI1, which enhance respiratory chain capacity, improved L-ornithine production but did not eliminate ethanol formation. The breakthrough in this module comes from the realization that the root cause of the Crabtree effect lies not in the capacity of the TCA cycle, but in the rate of glucose uptake. The invention introduces the expression of a truncated variant of the transcriptional regulator MTH1, designated MTH1-ΔT, which is resistant to degradation and thereby maintains the repression of hexose transporter genes such as HXT1, HXT2, HXT3, and HXT4. This results in a significant reduction in the maximum glucose uptake rate—from 2.2 g glucose per gram dry cell weight per hour in the parent strain to just 0.2 g glucose per gram dry cell weight per hour in the engineered strain—while simultaneously eliminating ethanol production entirely. This reduction in glucose influx allows the cell to shift from fermentative to respiratory metabolism, even under high-glucose conditions, thereby channeling nearly all carbon flux into the TCA cycle and ultimately into α-ketoglutarate. The resulting strain, M1cM2qM3e, achieves a L-ornithine titer of 778 mg/L, representing a fourfold increase over the previous best strain and demonstrating that the attenuation of glucose uptake is the most effective strategy for overcoming the Crabtree effect in this context. This finding is further validated by the observation that deletion of KGD2, encoding a subunit of the α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex, leads to a near-complete collapse in L-ornithine production and a dramatic increase in ethanol formation, confirming that the TCA cycle is indeed the primary sink for carbon flux in the engineered strain.

The final modification in the invention involves the overexpression of arginase (CAR1), which catalyzes the conversion of L-arginine into L-ornithine and urea. In wild-type yeast, arginase activity is tightly regulated and occurs primarily under conditions of nitrogen starvation or arginine excess. By overexpressing CAR1 in the M1cM2qM3e strain, the invention introduces a novel mechanism for recycling L-arginine back into L-ornithine, thereby reducing the intracellular pool of L-arginine and further relieving feedback inhibition on the biosynthetic pathway. The resulting strain, M1dM2qM3e, achieves a final L-ornithine titer of 1,041 mg/L, representing a 34% increase over the previous strain and a 23-fold improvement over the initial M1c strain. This enhancement is likely due not only to the direct conversion of L-arginine to L-ornithine but also to the potential formation of an “epi-arginase” complex between arginase and ornithine carbamoyltransferase, which inhibits the latter enzyme’s activity and further prevents the consumption of L-ornithine. The strain is capable of sustained growth in the absence of L-arginine supplementation, maintains a high biomass yield, and exhibits no signs of metabolic stress or growth inhibition, demonstrating that the combined genetic modifications are fully compatible with cellular physiology.

The invention further encompasses a method for the industrial production of L-ornithine using the genetically modified strain described above. The strain is cultivated in a defined minimal medium containing glucose as the sole carbon source, supplemented with essential nitrogen sources, minerals, and vitamins, under controlled aerobic conditions in a bioreactor. The fermentation process is conducted using a fed-batch strategy, wherein glucose is fed incrementally to maintain a low, non-overflowing concentration in the medium, thereby preserving the respiratory metabolism established by the MTH1-ΔT modification. Under these conditions, the strain achieves a final L-ornithine titer of 5.1 g/L and a cell dry weight of 56 g/L, demonstrating the scalability of the process. The L-ornithine is recovered from the fermentation broth through standard downstream processing techniques, including centrifugation, ultrafiltration, ion-exchange chromatography, and crystallization. The resulting product is of pharmaceutical grade, free from ethanol and other fermentation by-products, and suitable for use in nutraceutical formulations, parenteral nutrition, and as a precursor for the synthesis of polyamines and other bioactive compounds.

The invention is further distinguished by its systematic, modular approach to metabolic engineering, which contrasts sharply with the traditional, single-gene overexpression strategies that have dominated the field. By treating the metabolic network as a series of interdependent modules and optimizing each in sequence, the invention ensures that each modification is evaluated in the context of a progressively more efficient metabolic background. This approach not only maximizes the cumulative effect of individual modifications but also minimizes the risk of unintended metabolic consequences. The invention further demonstrates that the most effective strategy for L-ornithine overproduction is not the enhancement of the native mitochondrial pathway, but rather its complete replacement with a synthetic cytosolic pathway, coupled with the global attenuation of glucose uptake to eliminate carbon overflow. This paradigm shift in metabolic engineering strategy represents a fundamental advance in the field and establishes a new framework for the production of other mitochondrial-derived metabolites in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and related eukaryotes.

## EXAMPLES

### Strain Construction of Ornithine-Overproducing Strains

The construction of the L-ornithine-overproducing strains described herein was carried out using a modular pathway engineering strategy that enabled the precise, sequential modification of Saccharomyces cerevisiae strains to optimize each component of the metabolic network responsible for L-ornithine biosynthesis. The parental strain used for all genetic modifications was S. cerevisiae CEN.PK 113-11C, a well-characterized laboratory strain with a stable genetic background and full compatibility with standard molecular biology tools. All genetic modifications were performed using DNA assembler and modular pathway engineering (MOPE) techniques, which allow for the seamless integration of multiple genetic elements into the yeast genome without the need for selectable markers to be retained after each modification. This approach enabled the construction of strains with up to seven distinct genetic alterations without the accumulation of extraneous DNA sequences or the introduction of antibiotic resistance genes, ensuring the final strains are suitable for industrial and food-grade applications.

The first step in strain construction involved the downregulation of ornithine carbamoyltransferase (ARG3) expression to establish a leaky L-arginine auxotrophy. This was achieved by replacing the native promoter of ARG3 with either the HXT1 promoter or the KEX2 promoter, both of which are known to exhibit low transcriptional activity under high-glucose conditions. The HXT1 promoter was selected for its glucose-responsive regulation, which ensures that ARG3 expression is minimized during the exponential growth phase when glucose concentrations are highest, while the KEX2 promoter was chosen for its constitutive weakness, which provides a consistent level of repression regardless of carbon source. Both promoter replacements were performed using homologous recombination, with PCR-amplified DNA fragments containing the respective promoter sequences flanked by 50-base pair homology arms targeting the upstream region of ARG3. The resulting strains, designated M1a and M1b, were verified by quantitative RT-PCR to confirm a reduction in ARG3 mRNA levels to approximately 20% of wild-type levels. Both strains were capable of growth in the absence of L-arginine supplementation, confirming that residual ARG3 expression was sufficient to maintain basal L-arginine biosynthesis. The M1b strain, harboring the KEX2 promoter, exhibited a higher L-ornithine titer than M1a, suggesting that the constitutive nature of KEX2 repression provided a more stable and predictable metabolic environment.

The next modification involved the deletion of the ornithine aminotransferase gene (CAR2) to prevent the conversion of L-ornithine to L-glutamate γ-semialdehyde. This was accomplished by replacing the CAR2 open reading frame with a selectable marker cassette, which was subsequently excised using Cre-loxP recombination to leave behind a single loxP site without any foreign DNA. The resulting strain, designated M1c, was confirmed by PCR and sequencing to ensure complete deletion of CAR2 and the absence of off-target effects. The deletion of CAR2 resulted in a modest increase in L-ornithine titer from 42 mg/L to 45 mg/L, indicating that the transamination pathway was not a major sink for L-ornithine under the conditions of low production. However, the deletion was retained as a precautionary measure, given that the transaminase activity may become significant under conditions of elevated L-ornithine accumulation, as would occur in subsequent stages of strain optimization.

The construction of the mitochondrial pathway enhancement strains proceeded in a stepwise manner, beginning with the overexpression of the core biosynthetic genes ARG5,6, ARG7, and ARG8. These genes were cloned into a high-copy plasmid under the control of the strong, constitutive TEF1 promoter and integrated into the genome at the HIS3 locus using a double-crossover recombination strategy. The resulting strain, designated M1cM2f, exhibited a 31% increase in L-ornithine titer compared to M1c, confirming that the overexpression of these enzymes enhanced the flux through the acetylated derivative cycle. The addition of ARG2 overexpression, driven by the same TEF1 promoter, further increased the titer to 80 mg/L, indicating that the deacetylation step was a critical bottleneck in the pathway. The overexpression of the ornithine exporter ORT1 and the glutamate transporter AGC1 was then performed using chromosomal integration at the URA3 locus, with each gene placed under the control of the PGK1 promoter to ensure high-level, constitutive expression. The resulting strain, M1cM2k, achieved a L-ornithine titer of 149 mg/L, demonstrating that the transport of both precursors and products across the mitochondrial membrane was a major limiting factor in the native pathway.

The construction of the cytosolic pathway strain involved the assembly of a synthetic operon containing five heterologous genes: argAEC and argBEc from Escherichia coli, and argJCg, argCCg, and argDCg from Corynebacterium glutamicum. These genes were codon-optimized for expression in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and synthesized as a single DNA fragment with overlapping ends, which was then assembled into a yeast expression vector using Gibson assembly. The resulting construct was integrated into the yeast genome at the TRP1 locus, with each gene under the control of the strong, constitutive ADH1 promoter. The strain, designated M1cM2q, was verified by PCR and Western blot analysis to confirm the presence and expression of all five heterologous proteins. The strain was further validated by complementation assays using an L-arginine auxotroph lacking ORT1, which demonstrated that the cytosolic pathway could fully restore growth in the absence of exogenous arginine. The L-ornithine titer in this strain reached 192 mg/L, confirming that the synthetic pathway was not only functional but superior to the native mitochondrial pathway.

The final module of strain construction focused on the attenuation of glucose uptake and the enhancement of carbon flux into the TCA cycle. The MTH1-ΔT allele, containing an internal deletion that prevents its ubiquitin-mediated degradation, was synthesized and cloned into a centromeric plasmid under the control of the constitutive TEF1 promoter. This construct was integrated into the genome at the LEU2 locus, resulting in the strain M1cM2qM3e. The expression of MTH1-ΔT was confirmed by qRT-PCR, and the resulting strain exhibited a marked reduction in glucose uptake rate and a complete absence of ethanol production, confirming the successful attenuation of the Crabtree effect. The strain was further evaluated in fed-batch fermentations, where it achieved a final L-ornithine titer of 5.1 g/L and a cell dry weight of 56 g/L, demonstrating the scalability of the strain under industrial conditions.

The final strain, M1dM2qM3e, was constructed by overexpressing arginase (CAR1) from the native promoter, which was replaced with the stronger PGK1 promoter to enhance expression. This modification was introduced into the M1cM2qM3e strain using homologous recombination, and the resulting strain was verified by enzymatic assays to confirm increased arginase activity. The strain exhibited a 34% increase in L-ornithine titer over the parent, reaching 1,041 mg/L in shake-flask cultures, and maintained robust growth without supplementation, confirming the compatibility of all genetic modifications.

### L-Arginine Leaky Auxotroph Enables L-Ornithine Overproduction

The invention demonstrates that the establishment of a leaky L-arginine auxotrophy through the fine-tuned downregulation of ornithine carbamoyltransferase (ARG3) expression is a critical and novel strategy for enabling the overproduction of L-ornithine in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. In wild-type yeast, ARG3 catalyzes the conversion of L-ornithine and carbamoyl phosphate into L-citrulline, the first committed step in L-arginine biosynthesis. This reaction is tightly regulated by feedback inhibition from L-arginine, which binds to and inhibits the activity of multiple enzymes in the pathway, including N-acetylglutamate synthase and ornithine transcarbamylase. Complete deletion of ARG3, a strategy commonly employed in bacterial systems such as Corynebacterium glutamicum, results in a complete blockage of L-arginine biosynthesis, rendering the cell auxotrophic for this essential amino acid. While this strategy effectively prevents the consumption of L-ornithine, it necessitates the supplementation of L-arginine in the culture medium, which is economically prohibitive for large-scale industrial fermentation and introduces regulatory and safety concerns in food and pharmaceutical applications.

The present invention overcomes this limitation by replacing the native promoter of ARG3 with either the HXT1 promoter or the KEX2 promoter, both of which are characterized by low transcriptional activity under high-glucose conditions. The HXT1 promoter is regulated by glucose availability and is repressed during the exponential growth phase when glucose concentrations are highest, while the KEX2 promoter is inherently weak and constitutively repressed, providing a consistent level of transcriptional suppression regardless of carbon source. The resulting strains, M1a and M1b, respectively, exhibit a reduction in ARG3 mRNA levels to approximately 20% of wild-type levels, as confirmed by quantitative RT-PCR. Despite this significant reduction in expression, both strains are capable of growth in the absence of exogenous L-arginine, indicating that residual ARG3 activity is sufficient to maintain basal levels of L-arginine biosynthesis necessary for protein synthesis and cellular viability. The intracellular concentration of L-arginine in these strains is maintained at approximately 70% of wild-type levels, demonstrating that the pathway is not fully blocked but rather fine-tuned to create a metabolic imbalance in which L-ornithine accumulates due to reduced consumption.

This leaky auxotrophy strategy is further enhanced by the deletion of ornithine aminotransferase (CAR2), which catalyzes the conversion of L-ornithine to L-glutamate γ-semialdehyde, an intermediate that can be further metabolized to L-glutamate. While the deletion of CAR2 alone results in only a marginal increase in L-ornithine titer—from 42 mg/L to 45 mg/L—it serves as a critical safeguard against the potential activation of this transamination pathway under conditions of elevated L-ornithine accumulation. The small effect observed in the M1c strain suggests that the transaminase activity is negligible at low L-ornithine concentrations but may become significant as the pathway is further optimized, making its deletion a prudent and necessary step in the engineering strategy. The retention of this deletion in all subsequent strains ensures that L-ornithine is not diverted into competing metabolic routes, thereby maximizing its accumulation.

The success of this strategy is further supported by the observation that the intracellular concentration of L-arginine remains sufficiently high to support growth, yet low enough to alleviate feedback inhibition on the upstream biosynthetic enzymes. This delicate balance is achieved without the need for exogenous supplementation, making the strain suitable for industrial fermentation in minimal media. The leaky auxotrophy strategy also triggers a compensatory transcriptional response mediated by the general amino acid control transcription factor Gcn4p, which is activated in response to reduced intracellular amino acid levels. Gcn4p upregulates the expression of multiple genes involved in amino acid biosynthesis, including those encoding enzymes in the L-ornithine pathway, thereby creating a positive feedback loop that further enhances L-ornithine production. This endogenous regulatory response, which is not typically exploited in bacterial systems, represents a unique advantage of using Saccharomyces cerevisiae as a host organism, as it allows the cell to autonomously adapt its metabolism in response to genetic perturbations.

The leaky auxotrophy strategy is further distinguished from prior art by its precision and reversibility. Unlike complete gene deletions, which are permanent and irreversible, promoter replacement allows for fine-tuning of gene expression levels, enabling the optimization of L-arginine biosynthesis to the minimal level required for viability. This approach avoids the metabolic burden associated with the overexpression of compensatory pathways and prevents the accumulation of toxic intermediates that may arise from the complete blockage of a biosynthetic route. The strategy also eliminates the need for antibiotic resistance markers or other foreign DNA sequences, ensuring that the final strain is free of genetically modified elements that may raise regulatory concerns. The resulting strain is not only capable of high-level L-ornithine production but is also fully compatible with industrial fermentation practices, making it an ideal candidate for commercial-scale production.

### Pathway Re-Localization and Subcellular Trafficking Engineering Elevates L-Ornithine Synthesis

The invention demonstrates that the subcellular compartmentalization of L-ornithine biosynthesis in Saccharomyces cerevisiae presents a fundamental barrier to high-yield production, and that the strategic re-localization of metabolic pathways to the cytosol represents a transformative approach to overcoming this limitation. In wild-type yeast, the biosynthesis of L-ornithine from α-ketoglutarate occurs exclusively within the mitochondria, where the key intermediates—L-glutamate, N-acetylglutamate, N-acetylornithine, and L-ornithine—are synthesized and processed through a series of five enzymatic steps. This compartmentalization imposes several constraints on metabolic flux: the transport of L-glutamate into the mitochondria is mediated by the AGC1 transporter, which operates at limited capacity; the export of L-ornithine to the cytosol is mediated by the Ort1p transporter, which is expressed at low levels; and the acetylated intermediates of the pathway are subject to diffusion barriers and potential degradation. Attempts to enhance the native pathway by overexpressing individual enzymes yielded only modest improvements, indicating that the bottleneck lies not in enzyme activity but in the spatial organization of the pathway.

The invention addresses this limitation through a systematic evaluation of three distinct strategies for pathway re-localization. The first strategy involved the overexpression of mitochondrial transporters to enhance the flux of L-glutamate and L-ornithine across the mitochondrial membrane. The overexpression of AGC1, the glutamate/aspartate exchanger, and ORT1, the ornithine exporter, in the M1cM2h strain resulted in a 44% increase in L-ornithine titer, confirming that transport is a major rate-limiting factor in the pathway. However, this improvement was constrained by the limited capacity of the mitochondrial matrix to accommodate increased flux, as well as the cofactor mismatches inherent in the native pathway. The second strategy involved the relocation of L-glutamate biosynthesis into the mitochondria by targeting the NADPH-dependent glutamate dehydrogenase Gdh1p to the mitochondrial matrix. Despite the expectation that this would increase the local concentration of L-glutamate and thereby enhance flux through the pathway, the resulting strain exhibited a significant decrease in L-ornithine titer. This failure is attributed to the low availability of NADPH in the mitochondrial matrix, which is required for Gdh1p activity, and the unfavorable thermodynamic environment for the reductive amination reaction under mitochondrial conditions. Similarly, the targeting of the NADH-dependent Gdh2p to the mitochondria also resulted in reduced L-ornithine production, further confirming that the mitochondrial environment is incompatible with the efficient function of these enzymes.

The third and most successful strategy involved the complete translocation of the L-ornithine biosynthetic pathway to the cytosol, where the precursor L-glutamate is synthesized and where cofactor availability is optimal. This was achieved by constructing a synthetic, heterologous pathway composed of five genes derived from Escherichia coli and Corynebacterium glutamicum: argAEC and argBEc from E. coli, which encode N-acetylglutamate synthase and N-acetylglutamate reductase, respectively, and argJCg, argCCg, and argDCg from C. glutamicum, which encode N-acetylornithine aminotransferase, N-acetylornithine deacetylase, and ornithine carbamoyltransferase, respectively. These genes were codon-optimized for expression in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and assembled into a single expression cassette under the control of strong, constitutive promoters. The resulting strain, M1cM2q, was able to convert L-glutamate directly into L-ornithine in the cytosol, bypassing the need for mitochondrial import, acetylation, and export entirely. The strain was validated by complementation assays using an L-arginine auxotroph lacking ORT1, which demonstrated that the cytosolic pathway could fully restore growth in the absence of exogenous arginine, confirming that the pathway was not only functional but capable of supplying sufficient L-ornithine for cellular metabolism. The L-ornithine titer in this strain reached 192 mg/L, representing an 11% increase over the best-performing mitochondrial strain and establishing the cytosolic pathway as the superior configuration for L-ornithine production.

This result is further supported by the observation that attempts to enhance the native mitochondrial pathway through the overexpression of individual enzymes, including ARG2, ARG5,6, ARG7, and ARG8, yielded diminishing returns, with the most significant improvement coming from the overexpression of ARG2, which catalyzes the deacetylation of N-acetylornithine. The fact that the synthetic pathway outperformed even the most optimized version of the native pathway underscores a fundamental principle of metabolic engineering in eukaryotes: the relocation of entire biosynthetic pathways to the cytosol can be more effective than the incremental optimization of compartmentalized native pathways, as it eliminates transport barriers, reduces cofactor mismatches, and prevents the diversion of intermediates into competing pathways. The success of this strategy is also consistent with recent studies demonstrating that cytosolic pathway re-localization enhances the production of isobutanol and other metabolites in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, further validating the general applicability of this approach.

The invention further demonstrates that the cytosolic pathway is not only more efficient but also more robust than the native pathway. The synthetic enzymes are derived from highly efficient amino acid-producing organisms, and their codon optimization ensures high-level expression in yeast. The pathway is not subject to the complex regulatory mechanisms that govern the native pathway, such as feedback inhibition by L-arginine, which is effectively bypassed by the leaky auxotrophy strategy. The cytosolic localization also allows for the direct coupling of L-ornithine biosynthesis with the cytosolic supply of L-glutamate, which is synthesized by the NADPH-dependent glutamate dehydrogenase Gdh1p, the most efficient route for glutamate biosynthesis in yeast. The resulting strain exhibits no signs of metabolic stress, maintains robust growth kinetics, and is fully compatible with industrial fermentation conditions, making it an ideal platform for the scalable production of L-ornithine.

### ‘Crabtree Negative’ S. cerevisiae Construction Enables Efficient Carbon Channeling to L-Ornithine

The invention introduces a novel and transformative strategy for overcoming the Crabtree effect in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, thereby enabling the efficient channeling of carbon flux from glucose into the biosynthesis of L-ornithine. The Crabtree effect, a well-documented phenomenon in yeast metabolism, refers to the preferential fermentation of glucose to ethanol under aerobic, high-glucose conditions, even in the presence of a functional respiratory chain. This metabolic behavior results in the diversion of a substantial portion of carbon away from the tricarboxylic acid cycle and into ethanol production, severely limiting the availability of α-ketoglutarate, the essential precursor for L-ornithine biosynthesis. Previous attempts to mitigate the Crabtree effect through the overexpression of TCA cycle enzymes, such as PDA1, PYC2, CIT1, ACO2, and IDP1, or through the introduction of alternative NADH oxidases, such as HaAOX1 and NDI1, yielded only modest improvements in L-ornithine titer, indicating that the root cause of the problem lies not in the capacity of the respiratory chain but in the rate of glucose uptake.

The invention addresses this fundamental limitation by introducing the expression of a truncated variant of the transcriptional regulator MTH1, designated MTH1-ΔT, which is resistant to ubiquitin-mediated degradation and thereby maintains the repression of hexose transporter genes such as HXT1, HXT2, HXT3, and HXT4. In wild-type yeast, MTH1 acts as a negative regulator of glucose transporter expression by stabilizing the repressor complex that binds to the promoters of these genes. Upon glucose sensing, MTH1 is rapidly phosphorylated and degraded, allowing for the upregulation of glucose transporters and the initiation of fermentative metabolism. The MTH1-ΔT allele contains an internal deletion that prevents its phosphorylation and degradation, resulting in the constitutive repression of glucose transporter expression even under high-glucose conditions. The resulting strain, M1cM2qM3e, exhibits a dramatic reduction in glucose uptake rate—from 2.2 g glucose per gram dry cell weight per hour in the parent strain to just 0.2 g glucose per gram dry cell weight per hour—while simultaneously eliminating ethanol production entirely. This reduction in glucose influx allows the cell to shift from fermentative to respiratory metabolism, even under high-glucose conditions, thereby channeling nearly all carbon flux into the TCA cycle and ultimately into α-ketoglutarate.

The success of this strategy is further validated by the observation that the strain achieves a L-ornithine titer of 778 mg/L in shake-flask cultures, representing a fourfold increase over the previous best strain and a 23-fold improvement over the initial M1c strain. The complete absence of ethanol production confirms that the Crabtree effect has been effectively attenuated, and the maintenance of robust growth kinetics demonstrates that the strain is not compromised by the reduced glucose uptake. This finding is further corroborated by the observation that deletion of KGD2, encoding a subunit of the α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex, leads to a near-complete collapse in L-ornithine production and a dramatic increase in ethanol formation, confirming that the TCA cycle is indeed the primary sink for carbon flux in the engineered strain.

The invention further demonstrates that the MTH1-ΔT strategy is superior to other approaches for alleviating the Crabtree effect. Overexpression of TCA cycle enzymes, while increasing enzyme capacity, does not redirect carbon flux if glucose uptake remains high. Similarly, the overexpression of alternative NADH oxidases enhances respiratory chain capacity but does not prevent the overflow of carbon into ethanol if the influx of glucose exceeds the capacity of the TCA cycle. The MTH1-ΔT strategy, by contrast, targets the root cause of the problem—the rate of glucose uptake—and thereby enables a fundamental shift in metabolic mode from fermentation to respiration. This strategy is also scalable, as demonstrated by the achievement of a final L-ornithine titer of 5.1 g/L in fed-batch fermentations, where glucose is fed incrementally to maintain a low, non-overflowing concentration in the medium. The strain maintains high biomass yield and exhibits no signs of metabolic stress, confirming that the MTH1-ΔT modification is fully compatible with industrial fermentation practices.

The invention further establishes that the attenuation of glucose uptake is not merely a means of increasing carbon efficiency but a prerequisite for the successful operation of the synthetic L-ornithine pathway. Without the MTH1-ΔT modification, the cytosolic pathway would be unable to compete with the high flux of carbon into ethanol, and the L-ornithine titer would remain low regardless of the efficiency of the biosynthetic enzymes. The combination of the leaky auxotrophy strategy, the cytosolic pathway, and the MTH1-ΔT modification represents a synergistic triad of genetic modifications that together enable the unprecedented production of L-ornithine in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. This strategy is broadly applicable to the production of other TCA cycle-derived metabolites in yeast and establishes a new paradigm for metabolic engineering in eukaryotic hosts.

### Example 5

The final strain constructed in this invention, designated M1dM2qM3e, integrates all previously described genetic modifications into a single, highly optimized chassis capable of producing L-ornithine at titers exceeding one gram per liter in shake-flask cultures and 5.1 grams per liter in fed-batch fermentations. This strain is characterized by the following genetic modifications: (1) the replacement of the native ARG3 promoter with the KEX2 promoter to establish a leaky L-arginine auxotrophy; (2) the deletion of CAR2 to prevent the transamination of L-ornithine; (3) the overexpression of the synthetic cytosolic L-ornithine biosynthetic pathway composed of argAEC, argBEc, argJCg, argCCg, and argDCg; (4) the expression of MTH1-ΔT to attenuate glucose uptake and eliminate ethanol production; and (5) the overexpression of arginase (CAR1) to recycle L-arginine into L-ornithine. The strain was constructed through a sequential, modular engineering process, with each modification introduced into the background of the previously optimized strain to ensure that each genetic alteration was evaluated in the context of a progressively more efficient metabolic network.

The strain exhibits no growth defects, maintains a cell dry weight of 56 g/L in fed-batch fermentations, and produces negligible levels of ethanol, acetate, or other fermentation by-products. The L-ornithine titer of 1,041 mg/L in shake-flask cultures represents a 23-fold improvement over the initial M1c strain and a 34% increase over the M1cM2qM3e strain, demonstrating the additive and synergistic effects of the final modification. The overexpression of CAR1 not only increases the direct conversion of L-arginine into L-ornithine but also reduces the intracellular pool of L-arginine, thereby further alleviating feedback inhibition on the biosynthetic pathway. The strain is fully capable of growth in minimal medium without L-arginine supplementation, confirming that the leaky auxotrophy strategy is sufficient to maintain cellular viability while enabling high-level L-ornithine accumulation.

The strain was subjected to rigorous analytical validation, including high-performance liquid chromatography, gas chromatography–mass spectrometry, and enzymatic assays, all of which confirmed the identity and purity of the produced L-ornithine. The strain was also tested for genetic stability over multiple generations in continuous culture, with no loss of L-ornithine production capacity observed after 20 serial transfers, confirming that the genetic modifications are stably maintained in the absence of selective pressure. The strain is fully compatible with standard industrial fermentation infrastructure, including bioreactors, fed-batch protocols, and downstream processing techniques, and is suitable for use in pharmaceutical, nutraceutical, and food-grade applications.

The invention further encompasses the use of this strain in a continuous, fed-batch, or chemostat fermentation process for the scalable and economically viable production of L-ornithine. The fermentation process is conducted in a defined minimal medium containing glucose as the sole carbon source, supplemented with essential nitrogen sources, minerals, and vitamins, under controlled aerobic conditions. Glucose is fed incrementally to maintain a concentration below the threshold that triggers the Crabtree effect, ensuring that carbon flux is directed toward the TCA cycle and L-ornithine biosynthesis. The L-ornithine is recovered from the fermentation broth through centrifugation, ultrafiltration, ion-exchange chromatography, and crystallization, yielding a product of pharmaceutical grade with a purity exceeding 98%. The resulting process achieves a yield of 0.23 g L-ornithine per gram of glucose, representing a dramatic improvement over previously reported systems and establishing a new benchmark for the industrial biosynthesis of amino acids in eukaryotic hosts.
