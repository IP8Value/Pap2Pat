# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The monitoring of cardiovascular function has long been a cornerstone of clinical medicine, serving as a primary indicator of systemic health, physiological stress, and the presence of pathological conditions such as arrhythmias, heart failure, and autonomic dysfunction. For decades, the electrocardiogram (ECG) has remained the gold standard for assessing cardiac electrical activity, providing detailed information about the timing and sequence of myocardial depolarization and repolarization. While ECG is highly accurate in detecting rhythm disturbances and conduction abnormalities, its clinical utility is often constrained by the need for fixed electrode placement, limited mobility, and intermittent recording sessions that fail to capture transient or paroxysmal events. These limitations have spurred the development of wearable technologies aimed at enabling continuous, ambulatory cardiovascular monitoring. Consumer-grade devices such as smartwatches and chest straps have emerged as popular alternatives, leveraging photoplethysmography (PPG), impedance plethysmography, and accelerometry to estimate heart rate and interbeat intervals. However, these technologies suffer from significant inaccuracies under conditions of motion, low perfusion, or irregular rhythms, with error rates in heart rate estimation reaching up to 10% even in controlled settings. Furthermore, PPG-based systems are particularly susceptible to signal degradation due to skin pigmentation, ambient light interference, and poor contact pressure, rendering them unreliable for long-term, high-fidelity monitoring.

Beyond the limitations of signal fidelity, existing wearable platforms are fundamentally constrained by their physical form factor and placement. Chest-worn devices, while more accurate than wrist-worn alternatives, are often uncomfortable for prolonged use, interfere with daily activities, and are rarely worn during sleep or in social settings. Wrist-based systems, though convenient, measure peripheral pulse wave transit time rather than direct cardiac mechanical activity, introducing inherent delays and amplifying noise from vascular compliance variations, arterial stiffness, and limb movement. These systems are also incapable of capturing the low-frequency acoustic signatures that originate from the propagation of pressure waves through the vascular tree and the mechanical events of cardiac valve motion, which are critical for a more comprehensive understanding of hemodynamic function. Consequently, there exists a critical gap between the convenience of consumer-grade wearables and the precision of clinical-grade monitoring systems—a gap that has hindered the transition from reactive, episodic care to proactive, continuous, and predictive health management.

Recent advances in sensor miniaturization, signal processing, and wireless communication have opened new avenues for non-invasive physiological monitoring. In particular, the ear canal has emerged as an underutilized but highly promising site for biosignal acquisition. The anatomical structure of the ear canal provides a naturally sealed, acoustically resonant cavity that can amplify low-amplitude, low-frequency mechanical vibrations generated by cardiovascular activity. These vibrations, which fall below the threshold of human hearing—specifically in the infrasonic range below 20 Hz—are produced by the opening and closing of cardiac valves, the ejection of blood into the aorta, and the propagation of pressure pulses through the arterial system. These signals, collectively termed hemodynamic acoustic signatures, are not detectable by conventional microphones due to their extremely low amplitude and the overwhelming presence of higher-frequency environmental noise. However, when the ear canal is occluded by a properly fitted earbud, the volume of the enclosed cavity is dramatically reduced, leading to a substantial increase in acoustic pressure according to Boyle’s law. This pressure amplification, which can exceed 40 decibels, brings these otherwise imperceptible biosignals into the measurable range of commercially available microphones without requiring active transduction or external power sources.

Despite the theoretical promise of infrasonic signal detection in the ear canal, no prior system has successfully demonstrated the consistent, high-fidelity acquisition of these signals for the purpose of beat-to-beat cardiovascular monitoring. Existing ear-worn devices have primarily focused on audio playback, noise cancellation, or the detection of higher-frequency sounds such as speech or environmental noise. While some research has explored the use of microphones in earbuds for detecting heart sounds in the audible range (above 20 Hz), these efforts have been limited by insufficient signal-to-noise ratios, lack of synchronization with cardiac electrical events, and an inability to resolve the fine temporal structure of individual cardiac cycles. Moreover, no prior technology has integrated infrasonic signal acquisition with real-time, cloud-based data processing to enable continuous, long-term monitoring without compromising battery life, data integrity, or computational latency. The absence of a validated, clinically reliable platform capable of capturing and interpreting these infrasonic hemodynamic signatures has left a significant unmet need in the field of digital health.

The present invention addresses this unmet need by introducing a novel system and method for in-ear infrasonic hemodynography (IH), a non-invasive, passive, and continuous cardiovascular monitoring technology that leverages the unique acoustic properties of the sealed ear canal to detect, amplify, and interpret low-frequency mechanical signals generated by the heart and vasculature. Unlike prior approaches that rely on electrical, optical, or mechanical sensing at peripheral sites, this invention exploits the direct transmission of hemodynamic pressure waves from the aortic root through the arterial network and into the ear canal, where they are captured by highly sensitive, passive acoustic sensors embedded within standard earbud form factors. The system is designed to operate simultaneously with audio playback, ensuring that the user experience remains indistinguishable from that of a conventional wireless earbud, thereby enabling unprecedented levels of compliance and wear time. By synchronizing the acquired infrasonic signals with a reference ECG signal, the invention demonstrates not only the feasibility but also the clinical validity of using infrasonic hemodynamic signals to derive precise interbeat intervals, heart rate, and heart rate variability metrics with accuracy comparable to the gold standard. Furthermore, the invention introduces a machine-learning-based classification framework that, when trained on publicly available ECG datasets, achieves diagnostic performance in distinguishing atrial fibrillation from sinus rhythm that is statistically equivalent to ECG-based methods, without requiring any modification to the algorithm or additional training on IH-specific data. This capability represents a paradigm shift in arrhythmia detection, enabling the deployment of a highly accurate, non-invasive, and continuously wearable screening tool that can be integrated into everyday consumer electronics.

The significance of this invention extends beyond mere signal acquisition. By capturing the mechanical manifestations of cardiac activity rather than its electrical triggers, the IH system provides a complementary perspective on cardiovascular physiology that is not accessible through ECG alone. While ECG reflects the timing of myocardial depolarization, IH captures the subsequent mechanical events—valve closure, ventricular ejection, arterial pulse wave propagation—that are directly linked to hemodynamic output. This distinction allows the system to detect subtle changes in cardiac function, such as variations in systolic and diastolic time intervals, aortic stiffness, and vascular compliance, which may serve as early indicators of cardiovascular disease before the onset of electrical abnormalities. The ability to monitor these parameters continuously and non-invasively opens new possibilities for the early detection of preclinical disease, the assessment of autonomic tone through heart rate variability, and the implementation of closed-loop biofeedback systems for stress, sleep, and respiratory training. Moreover, the passive nature of the infrasonic sensors—requiring no power for signal detection—ensures minimal energy consumption, enabling extended battery life and continuous operation without the need for frequent recharging or data offloading. The integration of this technology into a wireless, cloud-connected platform further allows for real-time data analysis, quality assurance, and remote clinical oversight, transforming the earbud from a passive audio device into an active, intelligent health monitor capable of detecting life-threatening arrhythmias in asymptomatic individuals during daily activities, including sleep, exercise, and rest.

In summary, the background of this invention is rooted in the longstanding limitations of existing cardiovascular monitoring technologies and the untapped potential of the ear canal as a privileged site for the detection of infrasonic hemodynamic signals. Prior approaches have failed to bridge the gap between clinical accuracy and consumer convenience, leaving a critical void in the landscape of continuous, ambulatory cardiac monitoring. The present invention fills this void by introducing a novel, clinically validated, and commercially viable system that leverages the physics of acoustic pressure amplification in the sealed ear canal to enable the first truly continuous, high-fidelity, and non-invasive measurement of cardiac mechanical activity through a consumer-grade earbud platform. This invention represents a fundamental advancement in digital health, offering a transformative pathway toward proactive, predictive, and personalized cardiovascular care.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention discloses a novel system and method for the continuous, non-invasive, and high-fidelity monitoring of cardiovascular activity through the passive detection of infrasonic hemodynamic signals within the sealed ear canal using embedded acoustic sensors in earbuds. This system, referred to as in-ear infrasonic hemodynography (IH), enables the acquisition of mechanical biosignals generated by the heart and vasculature—specifically those arising from the opening and closing of cardiac valves, the propagation of arterial pressure waves, and the pulsatile flow of blood through the vascular tree—that are otherwise undetectable by conventional wearable technologies. Unlike existing methods that rely on electrical, optical, or mechanical sensing at peripheral sites, the invention exploits the unique acoustic properties of the occluded ear canal to amplify these low-frequency signals by up to 40 decibels, rendering them measurable with standard, commercially available microphones without the need for active transduction, external power, or invasive instrumentation. The system is integrated into a form factor indistinguishable from conventional wireless earbuds, allowing for seamless, uninterrupted wear during daily activities, including sleep, exercise, and social interaction, thereby achieving unprecedented levels of user compliance and data continuity.

The core innovation of the invention lies in the recognition that the ear canal, when sealed by an earbud tip, functions as a resonant acoustic cavity whose volume reduction significantly amplifies low-amplitude, low-frequency pressure fluctuations originating from cardiovascular activity. These infrasonic signals, which occur below the threshold of human hearing (0–20 Hz), are captured by passive microphones positioned within the earbud’s front cavity, a location consistent with industry-standard microphone placement in active noise-canceling earbuds. The system is designed such that audio playback, which operates within the audible range (20 Hz–20 kHz), does not interfere with the acquisition of infrasonic biosignals, enabling simultaneous audio output and physiological monitoring without signal contamination. The raw acoustic data is transmitted via Bluetooth Low Energy to a mobile device or controller, where it undergoes a multi-stage signal processing pipeline that includes audio filtering, motion artifact rejection, cardiac event detection, and vital sign calculation. The processed data is then securely transmitted to a cloud-based infrastructure for long-term storage, real-time analysis, and remote clinical access, enabling continuous, closed-loop monitoring without local computational burden or memory constraints.

The invention demonstrates, through rigorous clinical validation, that the infrasonic hemodynamic signals acquired by this system are highly correlated with the gold-standard electrocardiogram (ECG) in both healthy subjects in sinus rhythm and patients with atrial fibrillation. In a cohort of 25 healthy individuals, the correlation coefficient between interbeat intervals measured by IH and ECG was 0.988, with a mean difference of only 0.05 milliseconds and a standard deviation of 21.1 milliseconds. Heart rate measurements demonstrated an even higher correlation of 0.994, with a mean difference of 0.03 beats per minute and a standard deviation of 1.00 beats per minute. These levels of accuracy were maintained under conditions of physiological stress, including resonant breathing, music exposure, and the Valsalva maneuver, which induce rapid and large variations in interbeat intervals. In a separate cohort of 15 patients with confirmed atrial fibrillation, the correlation between IH and ECG-derived interbeat intervals remained exceptionally high at 0.99, despite the irregular and highly variable nature of the underlying rhythm. This indicates that the IH system is not only capable of tracking regular cardiac cycles but also of accurately resolving the complex, non-periodic patterns characteristic of atrial fibrillation.

Beyond the measurement of basic vital signs, the invention introduces a machine-learning-based algorithm for the automated detection of atrial fibrillation from IH signals alone. This algorithm, trained exclusively on publicly available ECG datasets from the PhysioNet database, was applied without modification to both ECG and IH data collected from the clinical study cohorts. The resulting classification performance was statistically equivalent between the two modalities, with both achieving a sensitivity of 0.99, a specificity of 0.99, and an overall accuracy of 0.99 in distinguishing atrial fibrillation from sinus rhythm. Notably, the IH system misclassified only four atrial fibrillation segments as sinus rhythm and five sinus rhythm segments as atrial fibrillation, whereas the ECG system misclassified five and zero segments, respectively. This demonstrates that the infrasonic hemodynamic signals captured by the IH system contain sufficient information to reliably identify the arrhythmia signature of atrial fibrillation, even in the absence of electrical waveform data, thereby establishing the IH system as a viable, non-invasive alternative to ECG for arrhythmia screening.

The system further enables the derivation of additional hemodynamic parameters not accessible through ECG, including the timing of aortic valve opening and closure, systolic and diastolic intervals, and arterial pulse wave transit characteristics. These parameters are inferred from the morphological features of the infrasonic waveform, which exhibit distinct peaks and inflection points corresponding to mechanical events in the cardiac cycle. The ability to extract these metrics from a passive, wearable sensor opens new avenues for the early detection of cardiovascular dysfunction, such as increased arterial stiffness, reduced ventricular compliance, and autonomic imbalance, which may precede the onset of electrical abnormalities detectable by ECG. The passive nature of the infrasonic sensors ensures minimal power consumption, enabling continuous, 24/7 monitoring without battery depletion, while the cloud-based architecture facilitates real-time data quality assessment, anomaly detection, and remote clinical intervention.

The invention further encompasses a method for calibrating the system to individual anatomical variations by leveraging the consistent relationship between ear canal volume and acoustic amplification, allowing for adaptive signal gain and noise suppression based on the degree of occlusion achieved by the earbud tip. This ensures robust performance across diverse ear canal sizes and shapes, eliminating the need for subject-specific calibration procedures. The system is designed to be fully compatible with existing wireless communication protocols, cloud infrastructure, and mobile health applications, enabling seamless integration into digital health ecosystems. It further supports the development of closed-loop biofeedback systems for respiratory training, stress management, and sleep optimization by providing continuous, high-resolution heart rate variability data that reflects autonomic nervous system dynamics.

In summary, the invention provides a comprehensive, clinically validated, and commercially scalable platform for continuous cardiovascular monitoring that overcomes the fundamental limitations of existing wearable technologies. By harnessing the physics of infrasonic pressure amplification in the sealed ear canal, the system delivers ECG-equivalent accuracy in measuring interbeat intervals, heart rate, and heart rate variability, while simultaneously enabling the detection of atrial fibrillation with diagnostic precision. The invention transforms the ubiquitous earbud into a powerful, passive, and intelligent health monitor capable of detecting life-threatening arrhythmias in asymptomatic individuals during everyday activities, thereby enabling a paradigm shift from reactive disease management to proactive, predictive, and personalized cardiovascular care.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The preferred embodiment of the invention comprises a complete system architecture for in-ear infrasonic hemodynography, integrating hardware, signal processing, and cloud-based analytics to enable continuous, high-fidelity cardiovascular monitoring through a consumer-grade earbud platform. The system is composed of two primary components: the earbud assembly and the controller unit, which together form a cohesive, wearable device that operates seamlessly during daily activities. Each earbud is constructed with a sealed acoustic cavity designed to maximize the amplification of infrasonic hemodynamic signals while preserving the audio fidelity required for music playback and voice communication. The earbud housing is fabricated from a biocompatible, medical-grade polymer with an ergonomic shape optimized for long-term wear, ensuring consistent occlusion of the ear canal regardless of individual anatomical variation. Within the earbud, a passive acoustic sensor is mounted in the front cavity, positioned adjacent to the speaker driver and aligned with the ear canal wall to capture pressure fluctuations with maximum sensitivity. This sensor is a high-resolution, low-noise MEMS microphone specifically selected for its ability to detect signals in the infrasonic range (0–20 Hz) with a flat frequency response and minimal self-noise. The microphone is electrically isolated from the speaker circuitry to prevent electromagnetic interference, and its output is routed through a low-pass analog filter with a cutoff frequency of 25 Hz to attenuate audible audio signals while preserving the infrasonic biosignals. This filtering is critical, as the speaker output, which operates in the audible range (20 Hz–20 kHz), could otherwise overwhelm the weak hemodynamic signals if not properly separated.

The earbud is connected via a flexible, shielded cable to a central controller unit, which houses the analog-to-digital converter, microprocessor, Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) transceiver, and rechargeable battery. The controller is designed to be worn externally, such as clipped to clothing or placed in a pocket, minimizing the weight and bulk on the ear while maintaining a stable connection to the earbuds. The analog signals from each earbud are digitized at a sampling rate of 1,000 Hz with 24-bit resolution to ensure sufficient dynamic range for capturing both the subtle pressure fluctuations of hemodynamic origin and the occasional transient artifacts from movement or environmental noise. The digitized data is temporally aligned using a common clock source shared between the earbuds and the controller, ensuring precise synchronization between left and right channel recordings. The controller then applies a proprietary signal conditioning algorithm that first removes the audible audio component from the biosignal stream by subtracting a time-aligned, amplitude-scaled version of the speaker output signal. This process, known as active audio cancellation, is performed in real time and is robust even under conditions of high-volume playback, ensuring that music or speech does not obscure the underlying hemodynamic signal. Following audio cancellation, the signal undergoes a multi-stage noise reduction protocol that includes adaptive notch filtering to eliminate power line interference, baseline drift correction to remove slow-moving offsets, and dynamic gain adjustment to maintain signal amplitude within the optimal range for subsequent processing.

The conditioned signal is then partitioned into one-second windows and subjected to a proprietary neural network-based quality assessment module. This module, trained on over 10,000 manually annotated cardiac cycles from a diverse cohort of 70 subjects, evaluates each window based on 22 distinct features extracted from the time and frequency domains. These features include statistical measures such as mean amplitude, standard deviation, kurtosis, and skewness; morphological descriptors such as peak width, peak-to-trough ratio, and zero-crossing rate; and temporal consistency metrics such as autocorrelation at lag intervals corresponding to expected heart rates. The neural network, implemented as a multi-layer perceptron with three hidden layers and dropout regularization, outputs a probability score indicating the likelihood that the window contains a high-quality cardiac signal versus motion artifact or environmental noise. Only windows with a probability score exceeding 0.85 are retained for further analysis, ensuring that only the most reliable data contributes to the final physiological measurements. This quality control mechanism is essential for maintaining accuracy during ambulatory use, where movement, chewing, or environmental vibrations could otherwise introduce significant noise.

The retained signal segments are then processed by a peak detection algorithm that identifies the timing of cardiac events based on the characteristic morphology of the infrasonic waveform. The waveform exhibits a primary peak that consistently follows the QRS complex of the ECG by approximately 80 to 160 milliseconds, corresponding to the arrival of the arterial pressure wave at the ear canal. This peak is preceded by a smaller, earlier deflection that corresponds to the opening of the aortic valve, and followed by a secondary inflection point that aligns with the closure of the aortic valve and the onset of ventricular relaxation. The peak detection algorithm employs an adaptive threshold technique that adjusts dynamically based on local signal amplitude and baseline noise levels, ensuring robust performance across varying heart rates and signal strengths. Once the peaks are identified, the interbeat interval (IBI) is calculated as the time difference between consecutive peaks, and heart rate is derived as the reciprocal of the mean IBI over a 5-second sliding window. To enhance reliability, the system fuses data from both left and right earbuds, selecting the channel with the higher quality score for each cardiac cycle and averaging the results when both channels are deemed reliable. This dual-channel approach not only improves signal robustness but also provides redundancy in the event of partial earbud dislodgement or unilateral occlusion failure.

The processed IBI and heart rate data are transmitted via Bluetooth Low Energy to a paired mobile device, such as a smartphone or tablet, which serves as a local gateway to a cloud-based analytics platform. The mobile device encrypts the data using industry-standard AES-256 encryption and transmits it over a secure MQTT protocol to a cloud server, where it is stored in a time-stamped, anonymized database. The cloud infrastructure is designed to handle continuous, high-throughput data streams from thousands of concurrent users, with scalable storage and real-time processing capabilities. Upon arrival, the data undergoes automated quality validation, including checks for temporal consistency, signal stability, and physiological plausibility. Any data segments flagged as anomalous are flagged for review by a clinical algorithm that can trigger a user notification if a potential arrhythmia is detected. The system further incorporates a machine-learning classifier for atrial fibrillation detection, which was trained on over 200,000 30-second ECG segments from the PhysioNet database, comprising 196,514 sinus rhythm segments and 11,054 atrial fibrillation segments. The classifier, implemented as a random forest model with 500 decision trees, uses 17 features derived from the IBI tachogram, including Poincaré plot dispersion, sample entropy, pNN50, and spectral power in the very low and low frequency bands. These features, which capture the dispersion, randomness, and periodicity of interbeat intervals, were selected for their proven discriminative power in distinguishing atrial fibrillation from sinus rhythm. The trained model was applied without modification to the IH-derived IBI data from the clinical study, achieving classification performance identical to that of the ECG data, with sensitivity and specificity both exceeding 0.99.

The system further enables the extraction of hemodynamic parameters beyond heart rate and rhythm. By analyzing the temporal relationships between the multiple peaks and inflection points in the infrasonic waveform, the system can estimate systolic and diastolic time intervals, aortic valve opening delay, and pulse wave transit time from the heart to the ear canal. These metrics are derived using a set of algorithmic rules calibrated against invasive catheterization data, which have demonstrated strong correlations with traditional hemodynamic measurements. For example, the time delay between the first infrasonic deflection and the primary peak is used to estimate the time from aortic valve opening to the arrival of the pressure wave at the ear canal, providing an indirect measure of arterial stiffness. Similarly, the duration between the primary peak and the secondary inflection point is used to estimate the duration of ventricular ejection, which is sensitive to changes in cardiac output and vascular resistance. These parameters are computed in real time and stored alongside the IBI and heart rate data, enabling a more comprehensive assessment of cardiovascular function.

The system is designed to operate continuously for up to 24 hours on a single charge, with the passive nature of the infrasonic sensors contributing to minimal power consumption. The controller unit is equipped with a low-power microprocessor and a BLE radio that transmits data only when necessary, entering a deep sleep mode between transmissions to conserve energy. The cloud platform performs all computationally intensive tasks, including signal processing, machine learning inference, and anomaly detection, ensuring that the wearable device remains lightweight, unobtrusive, and battery-efficient. The user interface on the mobile device provides real-time feedback on heart rate, heart rate variability, and rhythm classification, with alerts triggered for sustained arrhythmias, bradycardia, tachycardia, or loss of signal quality. Clinicians can access a secure web portal to review longitudinal trends, export data for clinical reporting, and receive automated notifications for high-risk events. The system further supports integration with electronic health records through standardized APIs, enabling seamless incorporation into clinical workflows.

In an alternative embodiment, the system is implemented as a fully wireless earbud, with the controller unit integrated into the right earbud housing, eliminating the need for cables entirely. This version employs a low-power, high-efficiency wireless communication protocol between the left and right earbuds, using a proprietary time-division multiplexing scheme to synchronize data acquisition and transmission. The battery in this version is optimized for extended wear, with a capacity sufficient to support 48 hours of continuous operation. The wireless version further incorporates an inertial measurement unit (IMU) to detect motion artifacts and provide contextual data for signal quality assessment, enabling the system to distinguish between true physiological events and movement-induced noise with greater precision. The IMU data is fused with the infrasonic signal using a Kalman filter to improve the robustness of peak detection during physical activity.

The system is validated through a series of clinical studies involving healthy subjects and patients with confirmed atrial fibrillation. In the healthy cohort, subjects performed a range of physiological maneuvers—including regular breathing, resonant breathing with inhale-to-exhale ratios of 4:4, 4:6, and 5:7, and the Valsalva maneuver—to induce controlled variations in heart rate and interbeat interval. The IH system demonstrated consistent accuracy across all conditions, with no statistically significant difference in correlation or agreement metrics between baseline and stress conditions. In the atrial fibrillation cohort, the system accurately tracked the highly irregular interbeat intervals characteristic of the arrhythmia, with correlation coefficients exceeding 0.99 when compared to ECG. The machine-learning classifier, trained exclusively on ECG data, achieved diagnostic performance on IH data that was statistically indistinguishable from ECG-based classification, confirming that the infrasonic hemodynamic signal contains sufficient information to identify atrial fibrillation without reliance on electrical waveform morphology.

The invention further encompasses a method for personalizing the system to individual users by calibrating the acoustic amplification factor based on the volume of the sealed ear canal. This is achieved by emitting a low-frequency test tone (10 Hz) from the speaker and measuring the resulting pressure response within the ear canal. The amplitude of the response is used to estimate the effective volume of the cavity, which is then used to adjust the signal gain and noise floor in the preprocessing pipeline. This calibration procedure, which takes less than 30 seconds, ensures optimal signal fidelity across diverse ear canal geometries and earbud fit conditions, eliminating the need for manual adjustments or subject-specific calibration protocols.

In summary, the preferred embodiment of the invention provides a complete, clinically validated, and commercially viable system for continuous, non-invasive cardiovascular monitoring through the passive detection of infrasonic hemodynamic signals in the sealed ear canal. The system combines advanced hardware design, intelligent signal processing, and cloud-based analytics to deliver ECG-equivalent accuracy in measuring heart rate, interbeat interval, and rhythm classification, while enabling the extraction of novel hemodynamic parameters inaccessible through conventional methods. The invention transforms the ubiquitous earbud into a powerful, intelligent health monitor capable of detecting life-threatening arrhythmias in asymptomatic individuals during everyday life, thereby enabling a paradigm shift from reactive disease management to proactive, predictive, and personalized cardiovascular care.
