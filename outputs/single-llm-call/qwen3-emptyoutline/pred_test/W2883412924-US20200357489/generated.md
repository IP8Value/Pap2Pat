# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of pharmaceutical discovery and high-throughput drug screening, particularly to a deep learning-based computational system for the rapid, accurate, and interference-resistant evaluation of nanoformulated drug efficacy using single-cell imaging data. More specifically, the invention encompasses a novel automated drug screening apparatus and method that leverages convolutional neural networks trained on multimodal single-cell image datasets obtained via image flow cytometry to assess the biological impact of drug compounds and their nanocarrier formulations without reliance on traditional biochemical assays. The system is designed to overcome the inherent limitations of conventional cytotoxicity screening methods—such as the MTT assay and standard flow cytometry—that are susceptible to physicochemical interference from nanoparticles, self-fluorescent compounds, and complex cellular morphological changes. By directly analyzing high-resolution cellular imagery, the invention enables the detection of subtle, early-stage phenotypic alterations induced by drug-nanocarrier interactions, thereby accelerating the drug discovery pipeline and eliminating the need for prolonged incubation periods, manual data interpretation, or chemical reagent-based readouts. The invention is particularly applicable to the evaluation of inorganic and lipid-based nanocarrier systems, including layered double hydroxides, solid lipid nanoparticles, extracellular vesicles, and other emerging nanoscale drug delivery platforms, and is scalable to diverse cell types, therapeutic agents, and cellular endpoints such as apoptosis, membrane integrity, proliferation, and organelle dynamics.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The development and clinical translation of nanoformulated drugs have gained substantial momentum over the past two decades due to their ability to enhance drug solubility, prolong circulation time, improve tissue targeting, and reduce systemic toxicity. However, the evaluation of these advanced therapeutic systems remains a significant bottleneck in pharmaceutical research. Conventional methods for assessing drug efficacy, such as the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assays, and standard flow cytometry, were developed for small-molecule drugs and are fundamentally ill-suited for the unique physicochemical properties of nanoparticles. Nanoparticles often exhibit intrinsic optical activity, high surface area-to-volume ratios, and non-specific adsorption properties that interfere with colorimetric, fluorometric, and impedance-based measurements. For instance, nanoparticles may absorb or scatter light at wavelengths used in MTT assays, leading to false-positive or false-negative results, while their surface charge and composition may alter the binding kinetics of fluorescent probes used in flow cytometry. Furthermore, many nanocarriers themselves—such as lipid nanoparticles or inorganic layered double hydroxides—can induce morphological changes in cells that are indistinguishable from drug-induced effects when analyzed using conventional gating strategies or population-level metrics.

These limitations are exacerbated in early-stage drug screening, where transient or subcytotoxic cellular responses—such as membrane remodeling, cytoskeletal reorganization, or localized organelle stress—are critical indicators of therapeutic potential but remain undetectable by assays requiring hours or days to manifest measurable biochemical changes. The MTT assay, for example, relies on mitochondrial reductase activity, which is not always correlated with cell viability, apoptosis, or membrane integrity, particularly in the presence of drugs that target metabolic pathways or nanoparticles that disrupt mitochondrial function. Similarly, standard flow cytometry quantifies bulk fluorescence intensity or side-scatter properties across entire cell populations, thereby averaging out subtle, heterogeneous, or spatially localized cellular responses that may be critical for understanding mechanism of action. Moreover, the analysis of such data requires extensive manual calibration of voltage settings, compensation parameters, and gating thresholds, introducing significant inter-operator variability and limiting reproducibility.

Recent attempts to integrate machine learning into drug screening have primarily focused on structure-activity relationship modeling, virtual screening of compound libraries, or classification based on predefined molecular fingerprints. These approaches, while valuable, are constrained by the availability and quality of curated chemical databases, the manual selection of molecular descriptors, and the inability to capture emergent, non-linear, or morphological phenotypes induced by drug-nanocarrier interactions. Furthermore, these methods cannot account for the dynamic, context-dependent behavior of cells exposed to nanomaterials, which often exhibit complex, non-uniform responses that defy simple chemical classification.

Image-based cytometry has emerged as a promising alternative, offering the ability to capture thousands of single-cell images with spatial and spectral resolution. However, existing image analysis platforms rely heavily on rule-based algorithms that require pre-defined thresholds for features such as cell size, shape, nuclear intensity, or fluorescence localization. These algorithms are brittle, fail to generalize across cell types or nanocarrier compositions, and are incapable of detecting subtle, high-dimensional patterns that emerge from the interaction of drug, nanocarrier, and cellular microenvironment. As a result, the full potential of image flow cytometry data—rich in spatial, temporal, and morphological information—remains largely untapped in the context of nanoformulated drug screening.

There is therefore a critical and unmet need for a computational system that can autonomously interpret high-throughput single-cell imaging data to evaluate the efficacy of nanoformulated drugs with high precision, minimal human intervention, and robust resistance to physicochemical interference. Such a system must be capable of learning complex, non-linear relationships between cellular morphology and drug response without relying on predefined biochemical markers, must generalize across diverse nanocarrier chemistries and cell lines, and must operate with sufficient speed to enable real-time or near-real-time screening. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a deep convolutional neural network architecture trained exclusively on multimodal single-cell images, enabling the first fully automated, label-agnostic, and interference-resistant platform for the rapid evaluation of nanoformulated drug efficacy.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention discloses a deep learning-based drug screening system, referred to herein as DeepScreen, which autonomously evaluates the biological efficacy of nanoformulated drugs by analyzing single-cell images acquired through image flow cytometry. The system comprises a convolutional neural network trained on a dataset of labeled single-cell images derived from cells treated with drug-nanocarrier formulations and their respective controls. Each image contains multiple optical channels, including bright-field and at least one fluorescent channel, such as Annexin V-APC, to capture morphological and molecular changes at the single-cell level. The neural network is configured to extract hierarchical, high-dimensional features from these images without reliance on manual feature engineering, enabling the classification of drug efficacy into discrete categories such as ineffective, low, medium, and high, based on the cellular phenotypic response.

The system operates by receiving input images of individual cells, preprocessing them through channel concatenation, resizing, and normalization, and then feeding them into a deep convolutional architecture that employs a network-in-network topology with branch-connected convolutional layers, global average pooling, and 1×1 convolutional output layers. This architecture enables the extraction of spatially localized features indicative of drug-induced cellular alterations, including plasma membrane asymmetry, cytoskeletal distortion, organelle redistribution, and nuclear condensation, even when such changes are subtle, transient, or obscured by nanoparticle interference. The system further incorporates advanced training methodologies, including balanced mini-batch sampling, batch normalization, weight decay, and dropout regularization, to ensure robust generalization across diverse cell lines, nanocarrier types, and drug concentrations.

A key innovation of the invention is its ability to function without the need for additional biochemical assays or prolonged incubation periods. Unlike conventional methods that require 24 hours or more of drug exposure to detect metabolic or cytotoxic effects, the DeepScreen system achieves high classification accuracy using only 2 to 6 hours of drug treatment, thereby reducing the screening timeline from days to hours. Moreover, the system demonstrates exceptional resistance to interference from self-fluorescent drugs, nanoparticle optical properties, and non-specific binding, as evidenced by consistent performance across formulations such as LDH-VP16 and SLN-Cur, which exhibit strong intrinsic fluorescence and distinct morphologies. The inclusion of additional fluorescent markers, such as anti-EGFR-FITC, further enhances classification accuracy, demonstrating the system’s adaptability to user-defined biomarkers and experimental objectives.

The invention also incorporates a class activation mapping module that visualizes the regions of the input image most influential to the network’s decision, providing mechanistic insight into the cellular processes driving drug efficacy. These maps consistently highlight areas of the plasma membrane, perinuclear regions, and intracellular structures, confirming that the system detects biologically relevant phenotypic changes rather than artifacts or noise. Importantly, the system does not require prior knowledge of the drug’s mechanism of action, making it applicable to novel compounds with unknown targets or complex nanocarrier systems whose interactions with cells are poorly understood.

The DeepScreen system is implemented as a software platform executable on standard computing hardware, including graphics processing units, and is designed for integration into high-throughput screening workflows. It eliminates the need for manual image analysis, reagent consumption, and lengthy incubation protocols, significantly reducing cost, labor, and variability. The system is scalable to any cell type, any fluorescent marker, and any nanocarrier formulation, provided that a representative dataset of labeled images is available for training. Furthermore, the architecture is modular and can be retrained incrementally to accommodate new drug classes, emerging nanocarriers such as extracellular vesicles, or additional cellular endpoints, including stem cell differentiation, autophagy, or senescence.

In summary, the invention provides a transformative approach to nanoformulated drug screening by replacing traditional, interference-prone, and time-intensive biochemical assays with a fully automated, image-based deep learning system that delivers rapid, precise, and interpretable assessments of drug efficacy. The system enables the early detection of subtle cellular responses, reduces screening timelines by an order of magnitude, eliminates operator bias, and is uniquely capable of handling the complex physicochemical interference inherent to nanomedicine, thereby accelerating the discovery and development of next-generation nanotherapeutics.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

### Example 1 A Deep Learning-Based Quick and Precise High-Throughput Drug Screening System

The preferred embodiment of the present invention is a deep learning-based drug screening system, designated DeepScreen, which is configured to evaluate the biological efficacy of nanoformulated drugs through the automated analysis of single-cell images obtained via image flow cytometry. The system is implemented as a software module executed on a computing platform equipped with at least one high-performance graphics processing unit, such as an NVIDIA GTX 1080Ti or equivalent, and is designed to interface directly with image flow cytometry instruments such as the Amnis Flowsight or similar platforms capable of capturing high-resolution, multi-channel cellular imagery. The system operates without reliance on biochemical reagents, metabolic assays, or manual data interpretation, thereby eliminating the primary sources of variability and interference that plague conventional screening methods.

The core of the DeepScreen system is a deep convolutional neural network architecture that has been specifically designed to process and classify single-cell images derived from drug-treated and untreated cell populations. Each input image consists of two or more optical channels, including a bright-field channel that captures morphological features such as cell shape, size, texture, and internal granularity, and at least one fluorescent channel, such as Annexin V-APC, which binds to phosphatidylserine externalized on the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane during early apoptosis. In certain configurations, additional fluorescent channels may be included, such as anti-EGFR-FITC for epithelial cell line-specific markers, Hoechst 33342 for nuclear staining, or LysoTracker for lysosomal activity, allowing the system to adapt to diverse experimental objectives and cellular contexts.

Prior to input into the neural network, the raw single-cell image data undergoes a standardized preprocessing pipeline. Each individual cell image sample, originally captured as separate single-channel files, is concatenated along the channel dimension to form a multi-channel input tensor. This concatenation preserves the spatial alignment of morphological and molecular information, enabling the network to learn cross-channel correlations that are critical for accurate classification. The concatenated images are then resized to a uniform dimension of 70 pixels in both height and width using bicubic interpolation, ensuring consistency in input size across all training and testing samples. Subsequently, pixel intensities are normalized to a range between zero and one, and channel-wise mean and standard deviation are computed across the entire dataset to standardize the input distribution, thereby improving training stability and convergence speed.

The neural network architecture employed in the DeepScreen system is a modified variant of the “network-in-network” design, which diverges from conventional deep convolutional architectures by replacing fully connected dense layers with convolutional layers and introducing branch structures that allow parallel processing of input features through distinct convolutional and pooling operations. This architecture comprises 46 total layers, including convolutional layers, max-pooling layers, batch normalization layers, dropout layers, and 1×1 convolutional layers, arranged in a hierarchical, non-densely connected topology. The network begins with an initial convolutional layer that extracts low-level features such as edges, textures, and intensity gradients from the input image. Subsequent layers progressively combine these features into higher-order representations, such as contours, cellular compartments, and localized patterns of fluorescence intensity. The use of branch structures allows the network to simultaneously process features at multiple spatial scales and resolutions, enhancing its ability to detect subtle, heterogeneous, or context-dependent cellular responses.

Crucially, the network does not terminate in fully connected dense layers, which are prone to overfitting and require large amounts of training data. Instead, the final layers consist of a global average pooling layer followed by a 1×1 convolutional layer with a number of output filters equal to the number of efficacy classes (e.g., ineffective, low, medium, high). This configuration enables the network to produce a probability distribution over the possible efficacy categories directly from the spatial feature maps, without the need for flattening or dimensionality reduction. The global average pooling layer reduces each feature map to a single scalar value per channel, effectively summarizing the presence and intensity of each learned feature across the entire image. The subsequent 1×1 convolutional layer acts as a linear classifier, mapping these summarized features to the final output classes. This design not only reduces the number of trainable parameters by over 90% compared to traditional architectures but also enhances generalization and robustness to variations in cell size, orientation, and imaging conditions.

The training of the DeepScreen system is performed using a dataset of labeled single-cell images derived from two human cancer cell lines—A549 (human pulmonary adenocarcinoma) and HEpG2 (human hepatocellular carcinoma)—exposed to nanoformulated drugs and their corresponding nanocarrier controls. The drug formulations tested include layered double hydroxide loaded with etoposide (LDH-VP16) and solid lipid nanoparticles loaded with curcumin (SLN-Cur), both of which are known to exhibit strong intrinsic fluorescence and distinct morphological behaviors in cellular environments. Cells were treated for 2 hours and 6 hours at concentrations corresponding to 80%, 50%, and 20% viability as determined by conventional MTT assays after 24-hour exposure. These concentrations were used to assign labels to the image data: ineffective (IE), low efficacy (LE), medium efficacy (ME), and high efficacy (HE). A total of 103,568 and 115,968 single-cell images were collected from A549 and HEpG2 cells, respectively, for training, and 25,892 and 28,992 images were reserved for independent testing. The training set was carefully balanced to ensure equal representation of each efficacy class within every mini-batch, a strategy that improved classification performance by 1–2% and mitigated bias toward dominant classes.

Training was conducted using the TensorFlow framework on two NVIDIA GTX 1080Ti graphics processing units, with the Adam optimizer configured with a learning rate of 0.001, beta1 of 0.9, beta2 of 0.999, and epsilon of 1×10⁻⁸. To prevent overfitting and enhance model generalization, multiple regularization techniques were applied, including L2 weight decay, dropout with a rate of 0.3, and batch normalization after each convolutional layer. The loss function employed was categorical cross-entropy, which is well-suited for multi-class classification tasks. Training continued until validation accuracy plateaued, typically after 150–200 epochs, with early stopping applied to halt training when no improvement was observed over a 10-epoch window.

Upon completion of training, the DeepScreen system achieved classification accuracies of 0.851, 0.864, and 0.908 on mixed-cell, HEpG2, and A549 test sets, respectively, using only bright-field and Annexin V-APC channels. Notably, the system demonstrated no statistically significant difference in performance between mixed-cell and single-cell-line models, indicating that the network is capable of learning cell-type-invariant features of drug efficacy. When tested on images derived from 2-hour and 6-hour drug exposures, the system maintained high accuracy, whereas conventional MTT and flow cytometry assays failed to detect any statistically significant differences under the same conditions. This demonstrates the system’s unique ability to detect early, subcytotoxic phenotypic changes that precede metabolic or biochemical alterations.

The system’s resistance to interference from nanoparticle properties was further validated by comparing its performance on LDH-VP16 and SLN-Cur formulations with and without the inclusion of fluorescent channels. Despite the strong intrinsic fluorescence of curcumin and the optical activity of layered double hydroxides, the DeepScreen system exhibited no degradation in classification accuracy when these agents were present, confirming its robustness to physicochemical interference. Moreover, when the Annexin V-APC channel was removed and the system was trained solely on bright-field images, accuracy remained at 0.705, still surpassing the discriminatory power of conventional assays. This indicates that morphological features alone—such as membrane ruffling, cytoplasmic granularity, or nuclear condensation—are sufficient for the system to infer drug efficacy, rendering it applicable even in the absence of fluorescent markers.

An additional enhancement to the system involves the integration of a class activation mapping module, which provides interpretability by visualizing the regions of the input image that most strongly influence the network’s classification decision. This is achieved by extracting the weights of the final 1×1 convolutional layer and performing a weighted linear combination of the feature maps from the preceding convolutional layer. The resulting class activation maps are overlaid onto the original input images, revealing that the network consistently focuses on the plasma membrane, perinuclear regions, and intracellular structures, even when these areas are partially obscured by nanoparticle aggregates or fluorescent artifacts. This spatial specificity confirms that the system is not merely detecting noise or artifacts but is instead identifying biologically meaningful phenotypic changes associated with drug action.

The system’s flexibility is further demonstrated by its ability to incorporate additional fluorescent markers. When an anti-EGFR-FITC channel was added to the A549 cell model, which expresses epidermal growth factor receptor, classification accuracy increased to 0.966, approaching near-perfect discrimination. This indicates that the system can be readily adapted to target-specific biomarkers, enabling its use in precision oncology, personalized medicine, and mechanistic studies. The same architecture can be retrained with new datasets to accommodate novel nanocarriers such as extracellular vesicles, polymer-based nanoparticles, or dendrimers, without requiring structural modifications to the network.

In practical implementation, the DeepScreen system is deployed as a standalone software application that accepts image files from image flow cytometers, automatically performs preprocessing, and returns a classification output along with a confidence score for each cell. The output can be aggregated at the population level to generate efficacy distributions, dose-response curves, or comparative heatmaps across multiple drug formulations. The system requires no manual gating, compensation, or thresholding, eliminating operator-dependent variability and enabling fully automated, high-throughput screening. A single screening run, from image acquisition to classification output, can be completed in under 15 minutes, compared to the 3–4 days required for conventional MTT assays.

The system’s scalability is further enhanced by its modular architecture. New drug formulations, cell lines, or cellular endpoints can be incorporated by collecting a new dataset of labeled images and retraining the network using the same architecture. The training process is fully automated and requires only a modest amount of labeled data—on the order of tens of thousands of images—making it accessible to laboratories without extensive computational resources. The system’s performance has been validated across multiple independent experiments, multiple cell lines, and multiple nanocarrier types, demonstrating its reliability and reproducibility.

Importantly, the DeepScreen system does not require prior knowledge of the drug’s mechanism of action, nor does it rely on predefined biological markers. It learns directly from the visual phenotype of the cell, making it uniquely suited for the discovery of novel therapeutics with unknown targets or complex nanocarrier-drug interactions. The system’s ability to detect subtle, early-stage changes in cellular morphology enables the identification of drug candidates that would otherwise be missed by conventional assays, thereby expanding the scope of viable candidates in early-stage screening.

In summary, the preferred embodiment of the invention is a fully automated, deep learning-based drug screening system that transforms high-throughput single-cell imaging into a powerful, interpretable, and interference-resistant platform for evaluating nanoformulated drug efficacy. By eliminating the need for biochemical assays, manual analysis, and prolonged incubation periods, the system dramatically accelerates the drug discovery pipeline, reduces costs, and enhances reproducibility. Its adaptability to diverse cell types, nanocarriers, and fluorescent markers ensures broad applicability across pharmaceutical research, toxicology, and translational medicine, establishing a new standard for precision and efficiency in nanoformulated drug screening.
