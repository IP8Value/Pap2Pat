# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The identification of T-cell epitopes remains one of the most challenging frontiers in immunology and translational medicine, primarily due to the extraordinary complexity of the molecular interactions governing antigen recognition by cytotoxic T lymphocytes. The universe of potential epitopes is vast, encompassing an astronomical number of possible peptide sequences derived from the proteomes of pathogens, tumors, or self-antigens. This combinatorial explosion is further compounded by the extreme polymorphism of human leukocyte antigen (HLA) molecules, which dictate the repertoire of peptides that can be presented to T cells. Each individual expresses a unique combination of HLA class I and class II alleles, each with distinct peptide-binding motifs, binding affinities, and structural preferences that govern which peptides are stabilized and displayed on the cell surface. Consequently, an epitope recognized in one individual may be entirely invisible to the T-cell repertoire of another, rendering many predictive algorithms and screening platforms non-generalizable across populations.

Beyond the constraints imposed by HLA diversity, the process of antigen processing and presentation introduces additional layers of biological noise and bias. Intracellular proteolytic machinery, including the proteasome and endoplasmic reticulum-resident aminopeptidases, does not cleave proteins uniformly or predictably. Peptide generation is influenced by local protein folding, post-translational modifications, subcellular localization, and degradation kinetics, all of which contribute to the stochastic nature of epitope production. Even when a peptide is successfully generated and loaded onto an MHC molecule, its immunogenicity is not solely determined by binding affinity. The stability of the peptide–MHC complex, its half-life on the cell surface, the kinetics of T-cell receptor engagement, and the spatial and temporal dynamics of immune synapse formation collectively determine whether a T cell will be activated, anergized, or ignored. These parameters are often overlooked in high-throughput screening methods that rely exclusively on biochemical binding assays or computational prediction tools, which fail to recapitulate the physiological context in which T-cell recognition occurs.

Traditional functional assays for T-cell epitope discovery have historically relied on presenting individual candidate peptides to target cells and measuring downstream T-cell responses such as cytokine secretion, upregulation of activation markers, or target cell lysis. While these methods are biologically relevant, they are inherently low-throughput, requiring the synthesis, purification, and loading of each peptide individually, followed by separate co-culture experiments. Pooling strategies have been introduced to mitigate this limitation, but they introduce significant deconvolution challenges, as the identity of the reactive epitope must be inferred through iterative rounds of subset testing, which exponentially increases experimental time, cost, and resource consumption. Moreover, these approaches are often limited by the availability of synthetic peptides, which may not accurately reflect naturally processed epitopes due to differences in flanking sequences, post-translational modifications, or intracellular processing pathways.

Affinity-based methods, such as pMHC multimer staining or single-chain MHC display technologies, have emerged as scalable alternatives, enabling the interrogation of thousands of potential epitopes simultaneously. However, these techniques are fundamentally limited by their reliance on direct T-cell receptor–peptide–MHC binding as a proxy for functional recognition. They bypass the natural antigen processing pathway entirely, thereby ignoring critical biological steps that determine whether a peptide is actually presented in vivo. Furthermore, these methods are insensitive to the kinetic parameters of TCR engagement, such as on-rates, off-rates, and dwell times, which are now understood to be more predictive of T-cell activation than equilibrium binding affinity alone. The result is a high rate of false positives—epitopes that bind tightly in vitro but are never presented in vivo—and false negatives—epitopes that are presented efficiently and elicit robust T-cell responses but possess low intrinsic affinity for the TCR. This disconnect between biochemical binding and biological function renders many affinity-based platforms inadequate for the discovery of clinically relevant T-cell epitopes.

The need for a high-throughput, functionally grounded, and physiologically faithful method for epitope discovery has never been more urgent. The advent of next-generation sequencing has enabled the cataloging of millions of T-cell receptor sequences from individuals with autoimmune diseases, cancer, and chronic infections, revealing unprecedented diversity and clonal convergence in antigen-specific repertoires. Yet, without knowledge of the antigens these receptors recognize, these vast datasets remain largely inert. The ability to link T-cell receptor sequences to their cognate epitopes is essential for understanding immune surveillance, predicting cross-reactivity, designing personalized vaccines, and developing T-cell-based immunotherapies. Current methods are incapable of bridging this gap at the scale and fidelity required. There is a critical unmet need for a platform that can screen vast, unbiased libraries of candidate epitopes against polyclonal T-cell populations in a single experiment, while preserving the natural biological context of antigen processing, presentation, and cytotoxic recognition. Such a method would revolutionize our ability to decode the T-cell antigen landscape and translate T-cell receptor data into actionable therapeutic insights.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel, high-throughput method for the identification of T-cell epitopes by leveraging the natural cytotoxic machinery of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells to selectively isolate and recover antigen-presenting cells that have been targeted for destruction. This method enables the simultaneous screening of millions of candidate epitopes encoded as minigenes within a heterogeneous population of target cells, without requiring prior knowledge of the epitopes of interest or the use of synthetic peptides. The core innovation lies in the integration of a genetically encoded, granzyme B–sensitive fluorescent reporter system into antigen-presenting cells, which allows for the direct, real-time detection of cytotoxic T-cell activity at the single-cell level through a measurable shift in fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) signal. Upon recognition of a cognate peptide–MHC complex, cytotoxic T cells deliver granzyme B into the target cell via perforin-mediated pore formation. The delivered granzyme B cleaves a specifically engineered peptide linker within a fusion protein consisting of a cyan fluorescent protein (CFP) and a yellow fluorescent protein (YFP), thereby disrupting FRET and resulting in a quantifiable increase in CFP emission and concomitant decrease in YFP emission. This FRET-shift signature is detectable by flow cytometry and serves as a highly specific, non-invasive readout of T-cell-mediated targeting.

The invention further comprises the use of lentiviral vectors to deliver libraries of minigene sequences encoding short, randomized, or defined peptide segments into target cells, ensuring that each cell expresses only a single minigene and its associated reporter construct. This configuration ensures that the FRET-shift signal observed in a given cell is directly attributable to the presence of a single epitope encoded within that cell’s transgene. Following co-culture with a population of cytotoxic T cells of interest, target cells that have undergone FRET-shift are isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), and the integrated minigenes are recovered by PCR amplification and subjected to deep sequencing. The relative enrichment of specific minigene sequences in the FRET-shifted population, as compared to unshifted controls, allows for the unambiguous identification of epitopes that elicit functional T-cell recognition. This approach eliminates the need for peptide synthesis, individual screening, or deconvolution of pooled samples, enabling the screening of libraries containing up to one million distinct minigenes in a single experiment.

The method is uniquely capable of detecting epitopes presented at extremely low frequencies—down to one in ten thousand target cells—while maintaining high specificity, as demonstrated by the near-complete absence of false positives in mixed target populations. The granzyme B–mediated cleavage mechanism ensures that only cells that have been directly engaged by cytotoxic T cells are labeled, as granzyme B does not diffuse appreciably into bystander cells under physiological conditions. Furthermore, the system is compatible with both monoclonal and polyclonal T-cell populations, including tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and peripheral blood-derived effector cells, making it applicable to clinically relevant samples. The invention is not restricted to murine systems and is directly translatable to human cells, as the granzyme B cleavage motif used herein is conserved between species and functions identically in human cytotoxic lymphocytes. The method is scalable, robust, and amenable to automation, and it provides a direct functional readout of T-cell reactivity that reflects the full biological cascade of antigen processing, presentation, and cytotoxic recognition, thereby overcoming the limitations of affinity-based and computational prediction methods. This invention enables the comprehensive mapping of T-cell epitope repertoires from complex, uncharacterized T-cell populations, facilitating the discovery of novel antigens for cancer immunotherapy, infectious disease vaccines, and autoimmune disease diagnostics.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Iterative Determination of T Cell Epitopes

The present invention provides a systematic and iterative method for the determination of T-cell epitopes that enables the discovery of both known and previously uncharacterized antigenic sequences from vast, unbiased libraries of candidate peptides. Unlike conventional approaches that rely on pre-defined peptide panels or computational predictions, this method begins with the generation of a diverse library of minigenes encoding short amino acid sequences, typically ranging from 12 to 48 nucleotides in length, which correspond to peptides of 4 to 16 amino acids. These minigenes are designed to be inserted into a lentiviral vector downstream of a constitutive promoter and upstream of a genetically encoded, granzyme B–sensitive FRET reporter cassette, ensuring that each transduced cell expresses a single minigene fused to the reporter. The resulting lentiviral library is used to transduce a population of antigen-presenting cells, such as murine or human cell lines that express appropriate major histocompatibility complex class I molecules, at a low multiplicity of infection to ensure that the majority of cells receive only a single viral integration event. This configuration ensures that the FRET signal detected in any given cell is attributable to the expression of a single minigene, eliminating ambiguity in epitope assignment.

Following transduction, the target cell population is co-cultured with a population of cytotoxic T cells whose antigen specificity is unknown or incompletely characterized. During co-culture, T cells that recognize a cognate peptide–MHC complex formed by a minigene-encoded epitope will form an immunological synapse, release perforin, and deliver granzyme B into the target cell. The granzyme B cleaves a specifically engineered linker sequence between the cyan and yellow fluorescent protein domains of the reporter, resulting in a measurable shift in fluorescence emission that can be detected and quantified by flow cytometry. Cells exhibiting this FRET-shift signature are isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting and collected into a separate population, while control populations of unshifted cells and non-T-cell-exposed cells are retained for comparative analysis. Genomic DNA is extracted from both the FRET-shifted and unshifted populations, and the integrated minigenes are amplified by polymerase chain reaction using primers that flank the insertion site. The resulting amplicons are subjected to high-throughput sequencing, typically using Illumina paired-end sequencing platforms, to determine the nucleotide sequences of all minigenes present in each population.

The key analytical step involves comparing the relative abundance of each minigene sequence in the FRET-shifted population versus the unshifted control population. Minigenes that are significantly enriched in the FRET-shifted population, as determined by statistical thresholds such as a fold-change exceeding ten standard deviations above the mean background enrichment, are identified as candidate epitopes capable of eliciting functional T-cell recognition. This enrichment metric accounts for stochastic variations in viral transduction efficiency, minigene expression levels, and sequencing depth, ensuring that only those sequences that are consistently and specifically selected by T-cell activity are deemed positive. The iterative nature of the method arises from the ability to use the top candidate epitopes identified in a primary screen as the basis for a secondary, focused library. For example, minigenes that meet the enrichment threshold but are not previously known antigens can be synthesized as a pooled oligonucleotide library and re-introduced into the same lentiviral vector system. This secondary library is then used to transduce a new batch of target cells, which are co-cultured again with the same T-cell population. Sequencing of the secondary screen reveals whether the previously identified candidates remain the most highly enriched sequences, thereby validating their authenticity and eliminating background noise or sequencing artifacts. This two-round screening strategy significantly reduces false positives and enhances confidence in the identification of bona fide epitopes.

The method further permits the refinement of epitope boundaries by testing minigenes of varying lengths and flanking sequences. For example, a minigene encoding a 40-amino-acid peptide may be found to be immunogenic, but the actual epitope may reside within a shorter core sequence. By synthesizing a panel of shorter minigenes that contain the same core sequence in different positional contexts, the minimal epitope required for T-cell recognition can be precisely mapped. This approach also allows for the evaluation of whether flanking residues enhance or inhibit antigen processing and presentation, providing mechanistic insight into the rules governing epitope immunogenicity. Importantly, the method does not require prior knowledge of the HLA restriction element, as the target cells naturally express their endogenous MHC molecules, and the T-cell population will only respond to epitopes presented in the context of compatible HLA alleles. This makes the method inherently adaptable to diverse genetic backgrounds and suitable for use with human primary cells, including those from outbred populations.

### Reporter Cells

The invention employs genetically engineered reporter cells that serve as the cellular platform for epitope presentation and functional detection. These cells are selected based on their ability to efficiently process and present peptides via major histocompatibility complex class I molecules, their compatibility with lentiviral transduction, their stability in culture, and their capacity to express the FRET reporter construct without significant background fluorescence or cytotoxicity. In one embodiment, murine cell lines such as EL4, a T-cell lymphoma derived from C57BL/6 mice, or ID8, a murine ovarian carcinoma line, are used as target cells. These lines are chosen because they express high levels of MHC class I molecules, are readily transduced by lentivirus, and maintain stable transgene expression over multiple passages. In another embodiment, human cell lines such as K562, which can be engineered to stably express HLA-A*02:01 and other common HLA alleles, are utilized to enable screening against human T-cell populations. The reporter construct is stably integrated into the genome of these cells via lentiviral delivery, ensuring long-term expression and uniformity across the population.

The reporter system consists of a fusion protein comprising a cyan fluorescent protein (CFP) and a yellow fluorescent protein (YFP), separated by a peptide linker that contains a specific cleavage site for granzyme B. The CFP and YFP are arranged such that when the fusion protein is intact, excitation of CFP at 405 nm results in energy transfer to YFP, leading to emission at 525 nm—a phenomenon known as Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET). Upon cleavage of the linker by granzyme B, the fusion protein is severed, eliminating FRET and resulting in a detectable increase in CFP emission at 450 nm and a corresponding decrease in YFP emission. This shift in fluorescence signature is quantifiable, reproducible, and highly specific, as granzyme B is not expressed by the target cells themselves and is only delivered upon direct T-cell engagement. The linker sequence is designed to be highly selective for granzyme B, with minimal susceptibility to cleavage by other intracellular proteases such as caspases or calpains, ensuring that the FRET-shift signal is a direct and exclusive readout of cytotoxic T-cell activity.

To minimize background noise and ensure high signal-to-noise ratios, the reporter construct is engineered with a stuffer sequence upstream of the P2A self-cleaving peptide and the CFP-YFP fusion cassette. This stuffer sequence contains multiple in-frame stop codons and is flanked by restriction sites that allow for its replacement with minigene inserts via standard molecular cloning techniques. Only upon insertion of a productive minigene sequence that lacks stop codons and is in-frame with the reporter will the fusion protein be expressed. This design ensures that only cells with functional minigene expression exhibit fluorescence, eliminating false positives from non-productive integrations or truncated transcripts. The reporter construct is further optimized for expression by incorporating a strong, constitutive promoter such as the modified murine stem cell virus (MSCV) long terminal repeat or the phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK) promoter, ensuring consistent expression levels across the transduced population. The entire construct is packaged into a lentiviral vector that includes a woodchuck hepatitis virus post-transcriptional regulatory element (WPRE) to enhance mRNA stability and a central polypurine tract (cPPT) to improve nuclear import and transduction efficiency. The resulting lentiviral particles are used to transduce target cells at a low multiplicity of infection, ensuring that the majority of cells harbor a single integration event, which is critical for accurate epitope assignment.

### Epitope-Encoding Nucleic Acid Libraries

The invention utilizes large, diverse libraries of nucleic acid sequences encoding candidate epitopes as the foundation for high-throughput antigen discovery. These libraries are synthesized as pools of degenerate oligonucleotides designed to encode a vast array of peptide sequences, either randomly or in a biased manner to reflect known antigenic motifs. In one embodiment, the library consists of 48-nucleotide-long oligonucleotides, each encoding a 16-amino-acid peptide with fully randomized codons at 48 positions, generating a theoretical diversity of 10^28 possible sequences. These oligonucleotides are amplified by polymerase chain reaction using primers that incorporate restriction enzyme sites compatible with the lentiviral vector, purified by gel electrophoresis, and ligated into the minigene acceptor site of the reporter construct. The ligation products are transformed into electrocompetent bacterial cells, and the resulting plasmid library is amplified to generate sufficient quantities for lentivirus production. The titer of the resulting lentiviral library is carefully controlled to ensure that the multiplicity of infection is below 0.3, thereby ensuring that the vast majority of transduced cells receive only a single minigene construct.

In another embodiment, the library is constructed from defined sequences derived from known proteomes, such as the entire coding sequence of a tumor antigen, a viral genome, or a human proteome. In this case, the coding sequence is fragmented into overlapping 40- to 60-base-pair segments, each encoding a 13- to 20-amino-acid peptide with a defined reading frame. These fragments are synthesized as double-stranded DNA and cloned into the reporter vector, generating a library that covers the entire proteome with overlapping coverage to ensure that all potential epitopes are represented. The library is then transduced into target cells as described above, and the resulting population is co-cultured with T cells of interest. This approach enables the systematic screening of entire pathogen or tumor proteomes for T-cell-reactive epitopes, without the need for prior epitope prediction or selection.

The library design is further optimized to account for the biological constraints of antigen processing. For example, minigenes are designed to avoid sequences that contain known proteasomal cleavage sites or endoplasmic reticulum signal peptides that could interfere with normal processing. Additionally, the minigene length is chosen to be compatible with the typical length of MHC class I–bound peptides (8–11 amino acids), while still providing sufficient flanking sequence to allow for proper proteolytic processing. The inclusion of flanking residues is critical, as these residues can influence the efficiency of antigen processing and presentation, and their absence in synthetic peptide screens has led to the failure of many predicted epitopes to elicit T-cell responses in vivo. The library is also designed to include multiple variants of each epitope to account for natural sequence variation, such as single-nucleotide polymorphisms or tumor-specific mutations, thereby enabling the discovery of neoantigens and variant-specific T-cell responses.

The size of the library is scalable and can be adjusted based on the desired depth of screening. Libraries containing up to 10^6 unique minigenes have been successfully generated and screened, with the potential to expand to 10^7 or higher through increased transduction volume and sequencing depth. The use of lentiviral transduction, as opposed to plasmid transfection, ensures that each cell receives a single copy of the minigene, eliminating the confounding effects of multi-copy integration that plague other high-throughput screening platforms. This feature, combined with the granzyme B–mediated selection mechanism, allows for the direct and unambiguous identification of epitopes without the need for iterative panning or complex deconvolution algorithms.

### Cytotoxic T-Cells

The method of the invention is designed to function with a wide range of cytotoxic T-cell populations, including monoclonal, polyclonal, and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes derived from both murine and human sources. The T cells used in the assay are activated and expanded ex vivo using standard protocols involving stimulation with anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 antibodies in the presence of interleukin-2. In the case of transgenic T-cell lines such as OT-I or pmel-1, which recognize the SIINFEKL or KVPRNQDWL epitopes, respectively, these cells are isolated from the spleens of transgenic mice and expanded for seven days prior to use in co-culture. For human applications, peripheral blood mononuclear cells are isolated from donors, stimulated with anti-CD3/CD28 beads, and expanded in the presence of recombinant human interleukin-2 for 7 to 14 days to generate sufficient numbers of effector cells.

The invention is particularly advantageous in its ability to function with polyclonal T-cell populations that contain low frequencies of antigen-specific cells. In traditional assays, the detection of rare T-cell clones is limited by the sensitivity of the readout and the background noise from non-reactive cells. Here, the granzyme B–mediated FRET-shift signal is inherently specific to T-cell engagement, and the selection of target cells based on this signal effectively amplifies the signal from even the rarest reactive clones. For example, when a population of T cells containing only 0.03% SIINFEKL-specific cells is co-cultured with a library of target cells, the FRET-shifted population is still enriched for the Ova minigene at levels exceeding ten standard deviations above background. This sensitivity enables the screening of T-cell populations derived directly from clinical samples, such as tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes or peripheral blood from vaccinated individuals, without the need for prior enrichment or sorting of antigen-specific cells.

The method is also compatible with T cells that recognize epitopes presented by non-classical MHC molecules, such as HLA-E or CD1d, provided that the target cells express the appropriate antigen-presenting machinery. The FRET-shift signal is not dependent on the type of MHC molecule but rather on the delivery of granzyme B, which is a conserved effector mechanism across all cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and natural killer cells. This broad applicability makes the method suitable for studying T-cell responses to viral infections, cancer, and autoimmune diseases, where epitopes may be presented by a variety of MHC class I and class Ib molecules.

The ratio of effector to target cells is optimized to ensure maximal detection sensitivity while minimizing non-specific killing. A 1:1 effector-to-target ratio is typically used, although lower ratios, such as 1:10 or 1:100, can be employed when T-cell numbers are limited, as in the case of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. The duration of co-culture is typically 4 to 8 hours, which is sufficient for granzyme B delivery and FRET-shift detection but precedes the onset of widespread apoptosis, allowing for the recovery of viable target cells for downstream sequencing. The use of propidium iodide exclusion during FACS sorting ensures that only live cells are collected, further enhancing the fidelity of the assay.

### Nucleic Acid Sequencing Techniques

The recovery and identification of epitope-encoding minigenes from the FRET-shifted target cell population are achieved through high-throughput nucleic acid sequencing techniques that enable the precise determination of the nucleotide sequences of thousands to millions of minigenes in a single experiment. Following co-culture and FACS sorting, genomic DNA is extracted from both the FRET-shifted and unshifted target cell populations using standard phenol-chloroform or commercial column-based methods. The integrated minigenes are then amplified by polymerase chain reaction using primers that bind to conserved regions flanking the minigene insertion site in the lentiviral vector. These primers are designed to include Illumina sequencing adapters and sample-specific barcodes, allowing for multiplexed sequencing of multiple samples in a single run.

The amplicons are purified by gel electrophoresis or magnetic bead-based methods to remove primer dimers and non-specific products, and then quantified using fluorometric methods. Sequencing is performed on an Illumina MiSeq or NextSeq platform using paired-end 250-base-pair chemistry, which provides sufficient read length to fully encompass the minigene sequence and its flanking regions. To improve cluster formation and sequencing accuracy, PhiX control DNA is spiked into the library at a concentration of 1–5%, and staggered primer strategies are employed to ensure balanced nucleotide distribution across sequencing cycles. The raw sequencing reads are processed using bioinformatic pipelines that include quality filtering, adapter trimming, and read assembly. Paired-end reads are merged into contiguous sequences using tools such as FLASH or VSEARCH, with parameters adjusted to account for the expected length and sequence diversity of the minigenes.

The assembled sequences are then clustered using algorithms such as Starcode or VSEARCH to collapse PCR and sequencing errors into representative operational taxonomic units. Sequences containing stop codons, frameshifts, or other non-productive mutations are removed from further analysis, as these are indicative of non-functional integrations. The remaining sequences are aligned to a reference database of all possible minigenes in the library, and their relative abundances in the FRET-shifted and unshifted populations are calculated. Statistical analysis is performed to determine the enrichment score for each minigene, typically using a fold-change metric normalized to the mean background enrichment and compared against a threshold of ten standard deviations above the mean. This stringent threshold ensures that only the most robustly enriched sequences are considered positive hits, minimizing false positives.

The sequencing depth is calibrated to ensure that each minigene in the library is represented at a sufficient number of copies to allow for reliable detection. For a library of 10^6 unique minigenes, a sequencing depth of 10^7 to 10^8 reads per sample is typically sufficient to achieve adequate coverage. The use of barcoded primers allows for the simultaneous processing of multiple samples, including controls, replicates, and different T-cell populations, in a single sequencing run, greatly enhancing throughput and reducing cost. The resulting data are visualized as enrichment heatmaps, volcano plots, or rank-order lists, enabling rapid identification of candidate epitopes. The method is fully compatible with cloud-based bioinformatics platforms and can be automated to enable high-throughput screening in clinical and industrial settings.

### Assessing Cellular Immunity to Specific Antigens

The method of the invention provides a powerful tool for assessing cellular immunity to specific antigens in a variety of biological contexts, including cancer, infectious disease, autoimmunity, and transplantation. By enabling the direct linkage of T-cell reactivity to specific peptide sequences, the method allows for the comprehensive mapping of the antigenic landscape recognized by T cells in a given individual or disease state. For example, in the context of cancer immunotherapy, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes can be isolated from patient biopsies, expanded ex vivo, and screened against libraries of minigenes derived from the patient’s tumor genome. This approach enables the discovery of patient-specific neoantigens that are recognized by the endogenous T-cell response, providing targets for personalized vaccines or adoptive T-cell therapies.

In infectious disease, the method can be used to screen T cells from individuals who have recovered from viral infections such as influenza, SARS-CoV-2, or HIV for reactivity against libraries of viral proteome fragments. This allows for the identification of conserved epitopes that are recognized across multiple strains, informing the design of broadly protective vaccines. Similarly, in autoimmune diseases such as type 1 diabetes or multiple sclerosis, T cells from affected individuals can be screened against libraries of self-antigens to identify the specific epitopes that drive pathogenic immune responses. The ability to detect these epitopes at low frequencies, even in the presence of a large background of non-reactive T cells, makes this method uniquely suited for studying rare autoreactive clones that are often missed by conventional assays.

The method also enables the study of T-cell cross-reactivity, a phenomenon in which a single T-cell receptor recognizes multiple distinct epitopes. By screening a T-cell population against a library of minigenes encoding peptides from unrelated pathogens, it is possible to identify epitopes that are structurally or chemically similar to the known antigen, revealing potential mechanisms of molecular mimicry that may underlie autoimmune disease or vaccine-induced adverse events. The ability to detect these cross-reactive responses in a high-throughput, unbiased manner provides critical insights into the safety and specificity of T-cell-based therapies.

Furthermore, the method can be used to monitor the evolution of T-cell immunity over time. For example, in vaccinated individuals, T cells can be isolated at multiple time points after immunization and screened against the same antigen library to determine whether the epitope repertoire expands, contracts, or shifts in specificity. This longitudinal analysis provides a dynamic view of immune memory and can inform the timing and composition of booster vaccinations. The method is also applicable to the study of T-cell exhaustion, as T cells from chronically infected or tumor-bearing individuals can be screened to determine whether their antigen recognition profile is altered compared to their healthy counterparts. The ability to assess cellular immunity at this level of resolution represents a transformative advance in immunology, enabling the precise characterization of T-cell responses in ways that were previously impossible.

### Further Applications

Beyond its primary application in epitope discovery, the method of the invention has broad utility across multiple domains of biomedical research and clinical development. One key application is in the development of T-cell receptor gene therapies, where the identification of high-affinity, tumor-specific T-cell receptors is a critical bottleneck. By screening T cells from patients who have spontaneously rejected tumors or responded to immunotherapy against libraries of tumor antigens, the method enables the rapid isolation of T-cell receptors with defined specificity and high functional avidity. These receptors can then be cloned into viral vectors and used to engineer autologous T cells for adoptive cell transfer, bypassing the need for time-consuming and low-yield cloning from single-cell cultures.

Another application lies in the field of vaccine design, where the method can be used to evaluate the immunogenicity of candidate vaccine antigens. Instead of relying on indirect measures such as antibody titers or cytokine production, the method allows for the direct assessment of whether a vaccine induces T cells capable of recognizing the intended epitopes. This is particularly valuable for vaccines targeting intracellular pathogens or cancer, where cytotoxic T-cell responses are the primary correlate of protection. The method can also be used to compare the immunogenicity of different vaccine formulations, adjuvants, or delivery platforms, providing a functional readout of efficacy that is more predictive of clinical outcome than traditional immunogenicity assays.

The invention is also applicable to the study of immune tolerance and the mechanisms underlying autoimmune disease. By screening T cells from healthy individuals and patients with autoimmune disorders against libraries of self-antigens, it is possible to identify epitopes that are normally ignored by the immune system but become targets in disease. This approach can reveal the breakdown of peripheral tolerance and provide insights into the triggers of autoimmunity. Furthermore, the method can be used to screen for epitopes that are shared between pathogens and self-antigens, helping to elucidate the molecular basis of molecular mimicry and its role in triggering autoimmune responses.

In the context of transplantation, the method can be used to identify donor-specific T-cell epitopes that drive graft rejection. By screening recipient T cells against libraries of donor HLA alleles or tissue-specific antigens, it is possible to identify the specific epitopes that are recognized by alloreactive T cells. This information can be used to develop targeted immunosuppressive therapies or to select donors with minimal epitope mismatch, improving transplant outcomes. The method can also be applied to the study of regulatory T cells, by screening them against libraries of antigens to determine whether they recognize the same epitopes as effector T cells, thereby revealing the mechanisms of immune regulation.

Finally, the method has applications in synthetic biology and immunogen design. By screening libraries of computationally designed peptides for T-cell reactivity, it is possible to engineer novel epitopes with enhanced immunogenicity, stability, or cross-reactivity. These epitopes can be incorporated into next-generation vaccines, diagnostic reagents, or T-cell engagers, providing a platform for the rational design of immune modulators. The ability to screen millions of sequences in a single experiment makes this approach uniquely powerful for exploring the vast sequence space of potential immunogens, accelerating the pace of discovery in immunotherapy.

## Example 1

### Model Assays Based on Stably Transfected Mouse Lymphoblastic Cell Lines EL4 and EG7 as Model Reporter Cells

To validate the functionality of the FRET-shift reporter system in a controlled and reproducible setting, murine lymphoblastic cell lines EL4 and EG7 were selected as model reporter cells due to their well-characterized MHC class I expression profiles, high transduction efficiency, and robust growth characteristics. EL4 cells, derived from a C57BL/6 thymoma, express high levels of H-2Kb and H-2Db molecules, making them ideal for screening epitopes restricted by these alleles. EG7 cells, a variant of EL4 stably transfected with the ovalbumin gene, provide a naturally antigen-expressing background for comparison. Both cell lines were transduced with the lentiviral vector encoding the granzyme B–cleavable CFP-YFP reporter and a stuffer sequence upstream of the minigene insertion site. Following transduction, cells were sorted by fluorescence-activated cell sorting to isolate a pure population of reporter-positive cells exhibiting a resting FRET signature, as confirmed by dual-channel excitation at 405 nm and emission detection at 450 nm and 525 nm.

To establish the specificity of the system, EL4 cells were transduced with a minigene encoding the SIINFEKL epitope of ovalbumin, which is known to be presented by H-2Kb and recognized by the OT-I T-cell receptor. Control cells were transduced with a scrambled version of the same sequence, LKNFISEI, which does not bind H-2Kb. Co-cultures were established between these target cells and OT-I CD8+ T cells expanded from splenocytes of OT-I transgenic mice. After four hours of co-culture, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry using the FRET-shift gating strategy. Cells expressing the SIINFEKL minigene exhibited a clear and significant shift in fluorescence, with a marked increase in CFP emission and a corresponding decrease in FRET signal, whereas cells expressing the scrambled minigene remained in the resting FRET population. The proportion of FRET-shifted cells in the SIINFEKL-expressing population was consistently greater than 30%, compared to less than 1% in the scrambled control, with a Cohen’s d effect size exceeding 40, indicating a highly significant and biologically relevant difference.

To assess the sensitivity of detection, a mixed population of SIINFEKL-expressing and scrambled-expressing EL4 cells was created at a 1:1 ratio and co-cultured with OT-I T cells. Following sorting, genomic DNA was extracted from the FRET-shifted and unshifted populations and subjected to quantitative PCR using TaqMan probes specific for each minigene. The results demonstrated that greater than 95% of the FRET-shifted cells contained the SIINFEKL minigene, while the unshifted population maintained a near-equal ratio of the two minigenes, confirming that the FRET-shift signal is highly specific and not the result of bystander effects or non-specific granzyme B diffusion. Similar results were obtained using EG7 cells, which naturally express full-length ovalbumin, demonstrating that the reporter system functions equivalently in cells expressing endogenous antigen versus those expressing a minigene-encoded epitope.

The kinetics of FRET-shift signal development were also characterized, revealing that the signal becomes detectable as early as one hour after co-culture initiation, peaks between six and eight hours, and precedes the onset of apoptosis as measured by propidium iodide staining. This temporal window provides a safe and reliable period for cell sorting, ensuring that target cells are recovered while still viable for downstream genomic analysis. The use of EL4 and EG7 cells as model reporter systems established the foundational parameters for the method, demonstrating that the FRET-shift assay is highly sensitive, specific, and robust in a controlled murine setting, and providing a validated platform for subsequent experiments with more complex libraries and polyclonal T-cell populations.

## Example 2

### Confirming Function of Granzyme B-Sensitive Signal Generation Product

To rigorously validate that the FRET-shift signal observed in the assay is exclusively due to granzyme B–mediated cleavage of the reporter construct and not to other mechanisms of cell death or non-specific protease activity, a series of controlled experiments were performed using pharmacological inhibitors, genetic knockouts, and alternative protease substrates. First, target cells expressing the CFP-YFP reporter and the SIINFEKL minigene were co-cultured with OT-I T cells in the presence of the granzyme B inhibitor Z-AAD-CMK, a cell-permeable, irreversible inhibitor that specifically blocks the catalytic site of granzyme B. Under these conditions, the FRET-shift signal was completely abolished, with the proportion of shifted cells dropping to baseline levels equivalent to those observed in the absence of T cells. In contrast, inhibition of caspase activity using Z-VAD-FMK had no effect on the FRET-shift signal, confirming that apoptosis-induced proteolysis is not responsible for the observed fluorescence change.

To further confirm the specificity of the reporter for granzyme B, target cells were transduced with a modified reporter construct in which the granzyme B cleavage site was replaced with a sequence recognized by caspase-3, a key executioner protease in the apoptotic pathway. When these cells were co-cultured with OT-I T cells, no FRET-shift was observed, despite the fact that the cells eventually underwent apoptosis as measured by annexin V staining. This demonstrated that the FRET-shift is not a general consequence of cell death but is specifically dependent on granzyme B cleavage. Conversely, when target cells were treated exogenously with purified human granzyme B in the absence of T cells, a dose-dependent FRET-shift was observed, confirming that the reporter is directly and specifically cleaved by granzyme B.

To assess whether other proteases present in the target cell cytoplasm could cleave the reporter, a panel of recombinant proteases—including calpain, cathepsin B, and elastase—was incubated with purified reporter protein in vitro. None of these proteases produced a detectable FRET-shift, even at concentrations orders of magnitude higher than those expected in a physiological context. Additionally, target cells transduced with the reporter construct were treated with proteasome inhibitors such as MG132 or bortezomib, which block intracellular protein degradation, and no spontaneous FRET-shift was observed, confirming that the reporter is stable in the absence of granzyme B delivery.

To confirm that the FRET-shift signal is dependent on perforin-mediated delivery of granzyme B, target cells were co-cultured with OT-I T cells derived from perforin-deficient mice. In this setting, no FRET-shift was observed, despite the fact that the T cells were fully activated and capable of producing granzyme B. This demonstrated that the signal requires the physical delivery of granzyme B into the target cell cytoplasm via perforin pores, and not extracellular diffusion or receptor-mediated uptake. These findings collectively establish that the FRET-shift signal is a direct, specific, and exclusive readout of granzyme B delivery following T-cell recognition, and that the reporter construct is not susceptible to cleavage by other intracellular or extracellular proteases. This high degree of specificity is critical for the fidelity of the assay and ensures that the identified epitopes are truly recognized by cytotoxic T cells in a physiologically relevant manner.

## Example 3

### Model Assays Based On Stably Transfected Mouse Ovarian Cell Lines ID8 and ID8.G7-Ova as Reporter Cells

To extend the applicability of the method to a more physiologically relevant tumor model, murine ovarian carcinoma cell lines ID8 and ID8.G7-Ova were employed as reporter cells. ID8 cells, derived from a spontaneous ovarian tumor in a C57BL/6 mouse, express moderate levels of MHC class I and are widely used as a model for ovarian cancer immunology. ID8.G7-Ova is a stable subline of ID8 that has been genetically engineered to constitutively express full-length ovalbumin, providing a natural source of the SIINFEKL epitope. Both cell lines were transduced with the lentiviral vector encoding the granzyme B–cleavable FRET reporter and sorted to obtain a homogeneous population of reporter-positive cells. The expression of the reporter was stable over multiple passages, and the resting FRET signature was consistent across the population, as confirmed by flow cytometry.

Co-cultures were established between ID8 cells transduced with the SIINFEKL minigene and OT-I T cells, and the resulting FRET-shift signal was compared to that observed in ID8.G7-Ova cells, which naturally express the full-length ovalbumin protein. Both cell lines exhibited robust FRET-shift signals upon co-culture with OT-I T cells, with the proportion of shifted cells reaching approximately 25% in the minigene-transduced population and 20% in the ID8.G7-Ova population. The magnitude of the signal was comparable between the two, indicating that the minigene-encoded epitope is processed and presented with similar efficiency to the endogenous epitope derived from full-length ovalbumin. This finding is critical, as it demonstrates that the minigene system faithfully recapitulates the natural antigen processing pathway and does not introduce artificial biases in epitope presentation.

To further validate the system, a mixed population of ID8 cells expressing the SIINFEKL minigene and ID8 cells expressing the scrambled LKNFISEI minigene was created at a 1:1 ratio and co-cultured with OT-I T cells. Following FACS sorting, genomic DNA was extracted from the FRET-shifted and unshifted populations and analyzed by quantitative PCR. The results showed that over 96% of the FRET-shifted cells contained the SIINFEKL minigene, while the unshifted population maintained a near-equal ratio of the two minigenes, confirming the high specificity of the assay in a tumor cell context. The use of ID8 cells as a reporter system also allowed for the assessment of the method’s performance in a more heterogeneous and clinically relevant cellular environment, as these cells exhibit variable MHC expression, adhesion molecule profiles, and cytokine secretion patterns that more closely resemble primary tumors than lymphoblastic cell lines.

The kinetics of FRET-shift development in ID8 cells were similar to those observed in EL4 cells, with signal detectable as early as one hour, peaking at six to eight hours, and preceding apoptosis by two to four hours. This consistency across cell lines confirms that the method is robust and generalizable to different cellular backgrounds. The successful application of the method to ID8 cells demonstrates its utility in the context of solid tumor immunology and provides a platform for screening T-cell responses against tumor-associated antigens in a setting that more closely mimics the in vivo tumor microenvironment.

## Example 4

### Model Assays Based on Lentivirus-Transduced Mouse Ovarian Cell Lines ID8 and ID8.G7-Ova as Reporter Cells

To evaluate the performance of the method using lentiviral transduction as the primary method of minigene delivery, rather than stable transfection, ID8 and ID8.G7-Ova cells were transduced with lentiviral particles encoding the FRET reporter and either the SIINFEKL minigene or the scrambled control. Lentiviral transduction was performed at a low multiplicity of infection to ensure that the majority of cells received only a single integration event, as confirmed by flow cytometry and digital droplet PCR. The transduction efficiency was approximately 60–70%, and cells were sorted to enrich for reporter-positive populations. The resulting populations were then co-cultured with OT-I T cells, and the FRET-shift signal was measured as described previously.

The results demonstrated that lentivirally transduced ID8 cells exhibited a FRET-shift signal comparable in magnitude and kinetics to that observed in stably transfected cells, with approximately 25–30% of SIINFEKL-expressing cells undergoing FRET-shift upon co-culture with OT-I T cells. The scrambled control remained below 1% shift, confirming the specificity of the signal. Importantly, the use of lentiviral transduction allowed for the generation of highly diverse minigene libraries, as the lentiviral vector can accommodate large and complex insert libraries without significant loss of titer or functionality. This is a critical advantage over plasmid transfection, which often results in multi-copy integrations and heterogeneous expression levels that complicate epitope assignment.

To assess the fidelity of epitope presentation in lentivirally transduced cells, a mixed population of ID8 cells transduced with SIINFEKL minigene and ID8.G7-Ova cells expressing full-length ovalbumin was created and co-cultured with OT-I T cells. The FRET-shifted population was enriched for both the SIINFEKL minigene and the endogenous ovalbumin-derived epitope, as confirmed by quantitative PCR and sequencing. This demonstrated that the lentiviral system does not interfere with the natural processing of full-length antigens and that both minigene-encoded and endogenously processed epitopes are equally capable of eliciting a FRET-shift signal. This finding is of great practical importance, as it validates the use of lentiviral transduction for introducing defined epitopes into cells that already express complex endogenous antigens, enabling the study of epitope competition, immunodominance, and antigen processing in a physiologically relevant context.

The use of lentiviral transduction also facilitated the generation of high-diversity libraries, as demonstrated in subsequent experiments with randomized minigene pools. The ability to transduce large numbers of cells with a single lentiviral library, followed by FACS-based selection of FRET-shifted cells, provides a scalable and reproducible platform for high-throughput epitope discovery. The method’s compatibility with lentiviral delivery ensures that it can be readily adapted to human cell lines, including primary antigen-presenting cells, for clinical applications.

## Example 5

### Assay Based on Autologous B-Lymphoblastoid Cell Line (B-LCL) as Reporter Cells

To demonstrate the translational potential of the method in human immunology, autologous B-lymphoblastoid cell lines (B-LCLs) were generated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells of healthy human donors and used as reporter cells in the FRET-shift assay. B-LCLs are Epstein-Barr virus–transformed B cells that express high levels of MHC class I and class II molecules, are capable of efficient antigen processing and presentation, and retain the donor’s full HLA haplotype, making them ideal for studying human T-cell responses in an autologous context. B-LCLs were transduced with the lentiviral vector encoding the granzyme B–cleavable FRET reporter and a stuffer sequence upstream of the minigene insertion site. Following transduction and sorting, cells were co-cultured with autologous CD8+ T cells expanded from the same donor.

To validate the system, a minigene encoding the immunodominant epitope from cytomegalovirus (CMV) pp65, NLVPMVATV, restricted by HLA-A*02:01, was introduced into the B-LCLs. Co-culture with CMV-specific CD8+ T cells from donors with a history of CMV infection resulted in a robust FRET-shift signal, with approximately 20–30% of the target cells exhibiting the characteristic increase in CFP emission. Control B-LCLs transduced with a scrambled minigene showed no shift, confirming the specificity of the response. The FRET-shift signal was dependent on granzyme B, as pre-treatment of T cells with the granzyme B inhibitor Z-AAD-CMK completely abolished the signal.

To assess the sensitivity of the method in a human setting, a mixed population of B-LCLs expressing the NLVPMVATV minigene and B-LCLs expressing a scrambled control was created at a 1:10,000 ratio and co-cultured with polyclonal CD8+ T cells from a CMV-seropositive donor. Following FACS sorting, genomic DNA was extracted from the FRET-shifted and unshifted populations and subjected to deep sequencing. The NLVPMVATV minigene was enriched in the FRET-shifted population by more than 100-fold compared to the unshifted population, and its relative abundance exceeded ten standard deviations above the mean background enrichment, demonstrating that the method is capable of detecting epitopes presented at extremely low frequencies in a human cellular context.

The successful application of the method to autologous B-LCLs establishes its compatibility with human primary cells and its potential for use in clinical settings, including the identification of tumor neoantigens, viral epitopes, and autoantigens in individual patients. The use of autologous B-LCLs ensures that the HLA restriction is preserved, eliminating the need for artificial HLA expression systems and providing a physiologically accurate platform for epitope discovery. This embodiment of the invention represents a critical step toward the clinical translation of the method for personalized immunotherapy and diagnostics.

## Definitions

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms shall have the meanings ascribed below. An “antigen-presenting cell” refers to any cell capable of processing intracellular proteins into peptides and presenting them on the cell surface in association with major histocompatibility complex molecules, including but not limited to dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells, and tumor cell lines engineered to express appropriate MHC molecules. A “cytotoxic T cell” refers to a CD8+ T lymphocyte capable of recognizing peptide–MHC complexes on target cells and inducing target cell death through the release of perforin and granzymes, particularly granzyme B. A “T-cell epitope” refers to a short peptide sequence, typically 8 to 16 amino acids in length, that is presented by MHC class I molecules and recognized by the T-cell receptor of a cytotoxic T cell. A “minigene” refers to a synthetic nucleic acid sequence encoding a peptide of defined length, typically between 12 and 48 nucleotides, that is designed to be inserted into a vector for expression in a target cell and processed into a peptide epitope. A “FRET reporter” refers to a fusion protein comprising a donor fluorescent protein and an acceptor fluorescent protein separated by a cleavable linker, such that fluorescence resonance energy transfer occurs when the protein is intact and is disrupted upon cleavage of the linker. “Granzyme B” refers to a serine protease secreted by cytotoxic T cells and natural killer cells that cleaves specific peptide substrates to initiate apoptosis in target cells. A “FRET-shift” refers to a measurable change in fluorescence emission profile resulting from the cleavage of a FRET reporter by granzyme B, characterized by an increase in donor emission and a decrease in acceptor emission. A “lentiviral vector” refers to a replication-incompetent viral delivery system derived from human immunodeficiency virus type 1, engineered to stably integrate into the genome of target cells and mediate long-term transgene expression. “Deep sequencing” refers to high-throughput nucleic acid sequencing technologies, such as Illumina sequencing, that enable the simultaneous determination of the nucleotide sequences of thousands to millions of DNA fragments in a single experiment. “Enrichment” refers to the relative increase in frequency of a specific nucleic acid sequence in a FRET-shifted population compared to a control population, as determined by sequencing and statistical analysis. “Polyclonal T-cell population” refers to a heterogeneous mixture of T cells derived from a single donor that includes multiple distinct T-cell clones, each recognizing different epitopes. “Autologous” refers to cells or tissues derived from the same individual, ensuring histocompatibility and preservation of native antigen-processing pathways. “High-throughput screening” refers to the automated or parallelized evaluation of large numbers of samples or conditions in a single experiment, enabling the rapid identification of active agents or biological responses. “Cell viability” refers to the proportion of live cells in a population, typically assessed by exclusion of membrane-impermeant dyes such as propidium iodide. “Immunogenicity” refers to the ability of a peptide to elicit a functional T-cell response upon presentation by MHC molecules. “Specificity” refers to the ability of a T-cell response to distinguish between closely related epitopes, such that recognition occurs only in the presence of the cognate antigen. “Sensitivity” refers to the ability of the method to detect rare epitope-presenting cells in the presence of a large background of non-reactive cells. “Non-specific cleavage” refers to proteolytic degradation of the FRET reporter by proteases other than granzyme B, which would result in false-positive signals. “Stuffer sequence” refers to a nucleic acid sequence placed upstream of a minigene insertion site that contains in-frame stop codons and prevents expression of the reporter until replaced by a productive minigene. “Multiplicity of infection” refers to the average number of viral particles per target cell during transduction, used to control the number of minigene integrations per cell. “Next-generation sequencing” refers to high-throughput DNA sequencing technologies that enable massively parallel sequencing of nucleic acid fragments. “Epitope mapping” refers to the determination of the precise amino acid residues within a peptide that are required for T-cell recognition. “Immunotherapy” refers to the use of biological agents to enhance or suppress immune responses for the treatment of disease, including cancer, infectious disease, and autoimmune disorders. “Personalized medicine” refers to the tailoring of medical treatment to the individual characteristics of each patient, including their genetic, immunological, and pathological profile. “Clinical translation” refers to the process of adapting a laboratory-based method for use in human patients, including validation in clinical samples and compliance with regulatory standards.
