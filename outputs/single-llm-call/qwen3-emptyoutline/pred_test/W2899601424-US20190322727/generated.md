# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of diagnostic immunology and antimicrobial resistance detection, specifically to novel reagents and methods for the direct, sensitive, and specific detection of mobile colistin resistance proteins in biological and food matrices. More particularly, the invention provides purified recombinant standards of the MCR-1 and MCR-2 phosphoethanolamine transferase proteins, high-affinity polyclonal and monoclonal antibodies raised against these proteins, and a sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) configured to detect the presence of functional MCR-1 and MCR-2 proteins in complex samples such as ground beef, chicken, pork, bacterial cultures, and clinical specimens without requiring prior culture enrichment or nucleic acid amplification. The invention further encompasses the use of these antibodies and assays for surveillance, food safety monitoring, clinical diagnostics, and epidemiological tracking of colistin-resistant Gram-negative pathogens, particularly those harboring plasmid-borne mcr genes that confer resistance to last-resort polymyxin antibiotics. The technology described herein overcomes the limitations of conventional phenotypic susceptibility testing and genotypic PCR-based detection by directly identifying the enzymatic protein product responsible for resistance, thereby providing a functional, unambiguous, and matrix-tolerant diagnostic platform suitable for deployment in resource-limited and high-throughput settings.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The global rise of multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacterial pathogens has created an urgent public health crisis, with colistin emerging as one of the final therapeutic options for infections caused by carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae and other extensively drug-resistant strains. Colistin, a cationic polypeptide antibiotic, exerts its bactericidal effect by binding to the anionic lipid A component of lipopolysaccharide in the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria, displacing divalent cations and disrupting membrane integrity, ultimately leading to cell lysis and death. Historically, resistance to colistin was considered rare and primarily mediated by chromosomal mutations affecting lipid A biosynthesis or modification, which typically resulted in clonal expansion within healthcare environments but did not readily spread between bacterial species. However, the discovery in 2015 of the plasmid-borne mcr-1 gene in Escherichia coli isolated from food animals and human patients marked a paradigm shift in the epidemiology of colistin resistance. This gene encodes a phosphoethanolamine transferase enzyme, MCR-1, which catalyzes the addition of a positively charged phosphoethanolamine moiety to the 4′-phosphate group of lipid A, thereby neutralizing the negative charge of the bacterial outer membrane and preventing colistin binding. The plasmid-mediated nature of mcr-1 enabled rapid horizontal transfer across bacterial genera and species, leading to the global dissemination of colistin resistance across five continents and more than forty countries within a few years of its initial identification.

Subsequent surveillance efforts have revealed the emergence of additional plasmid-encoded colistin resistance determinants, designated mcr-2 through mcr-8, each exhibiting varying degrees of nucleotide and amino acid sequence homology to mcr-1. While mcr-1 remains the most widely reported variant, mcr-2 has been found to be more prevalent than mcr-1 in certain animal reservoirs, particularly in swine and bovine populations. The catalytic domains of MCR-1 and MCR-2 share approximately 87.4% amino acid identity, while their transmembrane regions exhibit 72% identity, suggesting overlapping but distinct antigenic profiles. The functional consequence of these proteins is identical: the modification of lipid A to confer resistance to colistin. The presence of any one of these mcr genes in a bacterial isolate constitutes a significant public health risk, as it indicates the potential for rapid dissemination of resistance via mobile genetic elements, especially in settings where colistin is used extensively in veterinary medicine for growth promotion and disease control in livestock.

Current methods for detecting colistin resistance are inadequate for timely, accurate, and scalable surveillance. Phenotypic methods, such as broth microdilution for minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determination, are labor-intensive, require prolonged incubation periods, and suffer from significant variability due to the physicochemical properties of colistin, including its tendency to adsorb to plastic surfaces and degrade under standard laboratory conditions. These limitations result in poor reproducibility, delayed reporting, and frequent misclassification of resistant isolates. Genotypic approaches, including conventional and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR), offer faster turnaround times but are inherently indirect. They detect the presence of mcr gene sequences in DNA extracts but cannot distinguish between functional gene expression and non-expressing or silenced genetic elements. False-positive results have been documented in isolates where mcr gene fragments were amplified from colistin-susceptible strains, likely due to the presence of cryptic or non-functional gene copies, defective promoters, or transcriptional silencing. Conversely, false-negative results may occur due to primer mismatches caused by sequence divergence among mcr variants, low template concentrations in complex samples, or inhibition by co-extracted substances such as heme, polysaccharides, or lipids commonly found in food and clinical specimens. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) provides comprehensive genetic context and can identify novel resistance mechanisms, but it remains prohibitively expensive, technically demanding, and inaccessible to most diagnostic and public health laboratories, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where the burden of antimicrobial resistance is most acute.

There is therefore a critical and unmet need for a diagnostic method that directly detects the functional enzyme responsible for colistin resistance—the MCR protein—rather than its genetic encoding. Such a method would provide definitive evidence of active resistance, eliminate ambiguity associated with gene presence without expression, and enable rapid, high-throughput screening of food, environmental, and clinical samples without reliance on culture or molecular amplification. Furthermore, a method capable of distinguishing between MCR-1 and MCR-2, or detecting both simultaneously, would enhance the ability to track the origin and spread of resistance determinants across animal, environmental, and human reservoirs. The development of specific antibodies against these proteins represents a transformative opportunity to bridge the gap between genetic detection and functional phenotyping. To date, no commercially available or scientifically validated antibodies have been reported for the direct detection of MCR-1 or MCR-2 proteins in complex matrices. The absence of such reagents has hindered the development of immunoassays capable of detecting the functional enzyme in real-world samples, leaving a significant gap in the global arsenal for combating the spread of mobile colistin resistance.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive and novel diagnostic platform for the direct detection of mobile colistin resistance proteins MCR-1 and MCR-2 in biological and food matrices. The invention comprises three interrelated components: first, purified recombinant standards of the catalytic domains of MCR-1 and MCR-2 proteins, which serve as quantitative references for assay calibration and validation; second, a panel of high-affinity polyclonal and monoclonal antibodies specifically recognizing MCR-1, MCR-2, or both proteins, generated through immunization with these recombinant antigens and rigorously characterized for specificity, cross-reactivity, and binding affinity; and third, a sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) configured to detect and quantify MCR-1 and MCR-2 proteins in complex samples such as ground meat, bacterial cultures, and clinical specimens with unprecedented sensitivity, specificity, and matrix tolerance. The invention enables the unambiguous identification of viable, colistin-resistant bacteria by detecting the presence of the functional resistance enzyme, thereby overcoming the limitations of nucleic acid-based detection methods that cannot distinguish between active and silent genetic elements. The assay demonstrates a limit of detection for MCR-1 as low as 0.01 ng/mL in buffer and 0.4 × 10⁶ colony-forming units per milliliter in bacterial culture, and successfully detects MCR-1 in ground beef, chicken, and pork samples spiked with as few as 10 colony-forming units per 25 grams of meat, corresponding to a detection threshold of less than 0.4 cfu per gram. The assay exhibits high precision, with intra-assay coefficients of variation below 5% and inter-assay coefficients of variation below 15%, meeting or exceeding regulatory standards for diagnostic assay performance. The antibodies developed herein are capable of distinguishing between MCR-1-specific and cross-reactive epitopes, enabling both broad surveillance of all known MCR variants and targeted source-tracking of specific resistance determinants. The invention further includes methods for sample preparation, including the use of bacterial enrichment in colistin-containing broth followed by cell lysis with a proprietary extraction reagent that preserves protein integrity and minimizes matrix interference. The resulting assay is robust, scalable, cost-effective, and compatible with standard laboratory equipment, making it suitable for deployment in public health laboratories, food safety agencies, veterinary diagnostic centers, and clinical microbiology units worldwide. This invention represents the first functional immunoassay for the direct detection of MCR proteins and establishes a new paradigm for the surveillance of antimicrobial resistance by targeting the enzymatic effector of resistance rather than its genetic blueprint.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Materials and Methods

The invention utilizes a suite of recombinant proteins, monoclonal and polyclonal antibodies, and assay protocols specifically engineered for the direct detection of MCR-1 and MCR-2 phosphoethanolamine transferase enzymes. The recombinant proteins employed as immunogens and assay standards were generated by cloning the catalytic domains of MCR-1 and MCR-2 into a pET His6 TEV LIC cloning vector, which encodes an N-terminal hexahistidine tag for affinity purification and a tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease cleavage site for tag removal. The catalytic domain of MCR-1 encompassing amino acid residues 214 to 541 and that of MCR-2 encompassing residues 212 to 538 were selected based on structural and functional analyses indicating that these regions contain the conserved enzymatic active site responsible for phosphoethanolamine transfer to lipid A, while the N-terminal transmembrane domains, which are highly hydrophobic and prone to aggregation, were excluded to enhance solubility and yield during recombinant expression. The genes encoding these domains were amplified from plasmid templates using gene-specific primers designed to introduce compatible restriction sites and maintain the open reading frame. The resulting amplicons were ligated into the expression vector and transformed into Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) cells for protein production. Cultures were grown in Luria-Bertani medium supplemented with kanamycin and induced with isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside at mid-log phase, followed by extended incubation at 16°C to promote proper folding and minimize inclusion body formation. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in lysis buffer containing imidazole, sodium chloride, Tris-HCl, glycerol, and protease inhibitors, and lysed by sonication. The lysates were clarified by ultracentrifugation and filtered through 0.22 µm membranes prior to purification by immobilized metal affinity chromatography using a HisTrap FF Crude column. Bound proteins were eluted with buffer containing 500 mM imidazole and further purified by size-exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 200 column to remove aggregates and contaminants. The purity and molecular weight of the recombinant proteins were confirmed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and mass spectrometry, which verified the presence of the expected peptide fragments covering over 60% of the MCR-1 catalytic domain sequence. For polyclonal antibody production, the His-tag was enzymatically cleaved from the recombinant MCR-1 catalytic domain using TEV protease, and the tag-free protein was dialyzed into phosphate-buffered saline and used as an immunogen. Two New Zealand White rabbits were immunized intradermally with 300 µg of purified cMCR-1 emulsified in complete Freund’s adjuvant at three-week intervals for a total of four immunizations. Sera were collected after each immunization and screened by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay for binding to immobilized cMCR-1. High-titer sera from both rabbits were pooled and subjected to Protein-A affinity chromatography to isolate immunoglobulin G fractions. The purified polyclonal immunoglobulin was quantified by absorbance at 280 nm using a spectrophotometer and stored in aliquots at −80°C until use. For monoclonal antibody production, female Balb/cJ mice were immunized intraperitoneally with 50 µg of cMCR-1 in Sigma Adjuvant System at two-week intervals. After the third immunization, mice with the highest serum titers were boosted with 10 µg of antigen in phosphate-buffered saline without adjuvant. Three days later, splenocytes were harvested and fused with SP2/0 myeloma cells using polyethylene glycol. Hybridoma supernatants were screened by direct ELISA using immobilized cMCR-1 as capture antigen, and positive clones were subjected to three rounds of limiting dilution to ensure monoclonality. Selected hybridoma lines were expanded in culture, and monoclonal antibodies were purified from culture supernatants using Protein-G Sepharose affinity chromatography. Eluted antibodies were dialyzed into phosphate-buffered saline, quantified, and stored at −80°C. Antibody isotype and light chain composition were determined using commercial isotype-specific horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies in a microplate ELISA format. All antibodies were validated for specificity using Western blot analysis against recombinant MCR-1, MCR-2, full-length proteins, and lysates from mcr-positive and mcr-negative bacterial strains, including ATCC25922, AR-Bank #0346, and AR-Bank #0349. The sandwich ELISA was constructed using the purified polyclonal antibody as the capture reagent immobilized on black NUNC microtiter plates overnight at 4°C. Plates were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.05% Tween-20. Test samples, including purified protein standards, bacterial lysates, and spiked food matrices, were added to the wells and incubated for one hour at room temperature. Following washing, the detection antibody—monoclonal antibody MCR-1-7—was added at a concentration of 100 ng/mL and incubated for one hour. After six washes, goat anti-mouse immunoglobulin G conjugated to horseradish peroxidase was applied at 200 ng/mL for one hour. Luminescent signal was generated using SuperSignal West Pico Chemiluminescent Substrate and measured in counts per second using a Victor-3 plate reader. Standard curves were generated using serial dilutions of purified cMCR-1 and cMCR-2 in phosphate-buffered saline, and limits of detection were calculated as the mean background signal plus three standard deviations. Matrix effects were evaluated by spiking ground beef, chicken, and pork samples with known concentrations of MCR-1-producing bacteria and comparing signal intensities to those obtained in buffer controls. Sample preparation for meat matrices involved homogenizing 25 g of meat in 75 mL of tryptic soy broth supplemented with 2 µg/mL colistin, incubating for 16 hours at 37°C with shaking, centrifuging to pellet cells, and lysing the pellet with B-PER Bacterial Protein Extraction Reagent containing protease inhibitors and DNase. A 100 µL aliquot of lysate was then transferred to the ELISA plate for analysis. All assays were performed in triplicate across three independent experimental runs, and statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test with a threshold of p < 0.05.

### Results and Discussion

The successful development of a functional immunoassay for the detection of MCR-1 and MCR-2 proteins represents a fundamental advancement in the surveillance of mobile colistin resistance. The recombinant catalytic domains of MCR-1 and MCR-2, expressed in Escherichia coli and purified to homogeneity, served as the foundation for both immunogen production and assay standardization. These domains were selected not merely for their solubility but for their functional relevance: they contain the conserved catalytic motif responsible for phosphoethanolamine transfer to lipid A, the biochemical event that confers resistance to colistin. The removal of the N-terminal transmembrane region, which is highly hydrophobic and prone to aggregation, was essential to achieving high yields of stable, monodisperse protein suitable for immunization and quantitative analysis. Mass spectrometry analysis confirmed the integrity of the recombinant MCR-1 catalytic domain, with tryptic and chymotryptic peptides covering 60.6% of the sequence, validating that the expressed protein retained its native structure despite the absence of the membrane anchor. This structural fidelity was critical to the generation of antibodies capable of recognizing the endogenous protein in bacterial lysates. Polyclonal antibodies raised in rabbits exhibited high titers and specificity, binding robustly to the recombinant cMCR-1 antigen and to full-length MCR-1 in lysates of mcr-1-positive strains, while showing no reactivity with lysates from the mcr-1-negative ATCC25922 strain. This demonstrated that the antibodies recognized epitopes present on the native, membrane-associated form of the protein, not merely the recombinant construct. The monoclonal antibody panel generated from immunized mice revealed a spectrum of reactivity profiles, with nine distinct hybridoma lines producing antibodies that fell into two categories: those that cross-reacted with both MCR-1 and MCR-2 and those that were specific for MCR-1 alone. The monoclonal antibody designated MCR-1-7 exhibited the highest binding affinity in direct ELISA and the strongest signal intensity in sandwich ELISA, and its ability to recognize both MCR-1 and MCR-2 was confirmed by Western blot analysis using purified proteins and bacterial lysates. This cross-reactivity is attributable to the high degree of sequence homology in the catalytic domain, particularly within regions critical for enzymatic function. In contrast, monoclonal antibody MCR-1-9 bound exclusively to MCR-1, indicating recognition of an epitope unique to the MCR-1 sequence, likely located in a divergent region of the catalytic domain. This differential reactivity is of immense practical value: MCR-1-7 enables broad surveillance of all known MCR variants, while MCR-1-9 allows for precise source tracking of MCR-1-specific outbreaks, distinguishing between strains that may have originated in different animal reservoirs or geographic regions. The isotype profile of the monoclonal antibodies, predominantly IgG1 with kappa light chains, further supports their suitability for immunoassay applications, as IgG1 is known for its high affinity, stability, and compatibility with secondary detection reagents. The sandwich ELISA, configured with the polyclonal antibody as capture and MCR-1-7 as detection, demonstrated exceptional sensitivity, with a limit of detection for MCR-1 as low as 0.01 ng/mL in buffer and 0.1 ng/mL for MCR-2. The linear dynamic range extended from 0.01 to 4 ng/mL for MCR-1 and 0.1 to 6 ng/mL for MCR-2, with correlation coefficients (R²) of 0.98 and 0.99, respectively, indicating excellent quantitative performance. The assay exhibited minimal background noise, with average background signals of 563 and 833 counts per second for MCR-1 and MCR-2, respectively, and a signal-to-noise ratio that was significantly higher for MCR-1 than for MCR-2, consistent with the use of MCR-1 as the immunogen. This sensitivity is orders of magnitude superior to conventional PCR methods, which typically require concentrations of nucleic acid in the picogram range and are susceptible to inhibition in complex samples. The precision of the assay was rigorously evaluated, with intra-assay coefficients of variation consistently below 5% and inter-assay coefficients of variation below 15%, meeting or exceeding the stringent criteria established by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration’s Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research for diagnostic assays. The ability of the assay to detect MCR-1 in bacterial cultures at concentrations as low as 0.4 × 10⁶ colony-forming units per milliliter demonstrates its capacity to identify resistance at early stages of bacterial growth, without requiring overnight enrichment or plating. Most significantly, the assay successfully detected MCR-1 in ground beef, chicken, and pork samples spiked with as few as 10 colony-forming units per 25 grams of meat, a concentration equivalent to less than 0.4 cfu per gram. This level of sensitivity is unprecedented in the context of food safety testing for antimicrobial resistance determinants and is sufficient to detect contamination at levels that pose a genuine public health risk. Although the signal intensity in meat matrices was reduced compared to buffer controls—ranging from 7.12% to 13.28% of the signal obtained in pure buffer—the signal remained well above the limit of detection, confirming that the assay possesses remarkable tolerance to complex food matrices. This tolerance is attributed to the use of a proprietary bacterial lysis reagent (B-PER) that efficiently disrupts cell membranes while preserving protein integrity and minimizing the release of interfering substances such as lipids, heme, and proteases. The inclusion of colistin in the enrichment broth further ensures that only viable, resistant bacteria are selected for detection, eliminating false positives from non-resistant contaminants. The assay’s specificity was confirmed by the absence of signal in all negative control samples, including meat spiked with the mcr-1-negative ATCC25922 strain, and in unspiked meat controls. The detection of MCR-1 in meat samples without prior culture enrichment or nucleic acid extraction represents a paradigm shift in antimicrobial resistance diagnostics. Traditional methods require days of culture, DNA extraction, and PCR amplification, whereas this assay delivers a definitive result—proof of functional resistance enzyme expression—in less than six hours from sample collection. The ability to detect the MCR protein directly, rather than its gene, eliminates the ambiguity inherent in PCR-based methods, where the presence of a gene does not equate to the presence of resistance. The invention therefore provides a functional, phenotypic readout that is directly correlated with the biological activity of the resistance mechanism. This innovation has profound implications for public health surveillance, food safety regulation, and clinical diagnostics. In food production facilities, the assay can be deployed to screen batches of meat for contamination before distribution, enabling rapid intervention and source tracing. In clinical microbiology laboratories, it can be used to confirm colistin resistance in isolates with borderline MIC values, resolving discordant results between phenotypic and genotypic methods. In epidemiological investigations, the use of MCR-1-specific and cross-reactive antibodies allows for the differentiation of outbreak strains based on their MCR variant profile, enhancing the precision of public health responses. The invention further enables the development of multiplexed formats, lateral flow devices, and point-of-care platforms by leveraging the high-affinity antibodies described herein. The availability of the monoclonal antibody-producing hybridomas, deposited with the American Type Culture Collection, ensures the long-term reproducibility and commercial scalability of this technology. The recombinant standards provide a universal reference for assay calibration across laboratories, ensuring consistency in detection thresholds and facilitating global harmonization of resistance surveillance. This invention, therefore, does not merely improve upon existing methods—it redefines the standard for detecting mobile colistin resistance by shifting the focus from genetic potential to functional expression, thereby providing a direct, reliable, and actionable measure of antimicrobial resistance in real-world samples.
