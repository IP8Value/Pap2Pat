# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to systems and methods for the long-term, continuous, and temporally resolved recording of biological signals within living cells, tissues, and whole organisms, with subsequent readout via conventional immunofluorescence microscopy following fixation. More specifically, the invention provides genetically encoded, self-assembling protein-based molecular recorders that capture the temporal dynamics of cellular states—such as gene expression, signaling activity, or environmental stimuli—by incorporating distinct molecular labels into a growing linear polymer chain within the cytoplasm. The resulting protein assemblies, termed Expression Recording Islands (XRIs), preserve the chronological sequence of biological events as spatially ordered epitope tags along their length, enabling retrospective analysis of dynamic cellular processes without requiring live imaging, cell lysis, or nucleic acid sequencing. This technology bridges the gap between high-resolution, single-cell temporal resolution afforded by live-cell imaging and the high-throughput, spatially scalable readout capabilities of fixed-tissue histology, thereby enabling the reconstruction of complex physiological histories across large populations of cells in intact tissues, including the mammalian brain.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The ability to observe and quantify the temporal evolution of biological processes within living systems is foundational to understanding cellular function, neural circuit dynamics, developmental trajectories, and disease progression. Traditional approaches to monitoring cellular activity rely heavily on live imaging techniques using genetically encoded fluorescent reporters, which permit real-time visualization of molecular events such as calcium flux, kinase activation, or transcriptional induction. However, these methods are inherently limited by the physical constraints of microscopy: they require prolonged instrument occupancy, are susceptible to phototoxicity and photobleaching, and are rarely scalable beyond small fields of view or a few hundred cells at a time. Consequently, longitudinal studies spanning days or weeks are labor-intensive, technically demanding, and often impractical for population-level analyses. 

Alternative methods for capturing cellular history rely on fixed-tissue analysis, such as RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) or immunohistochemistry, which enable high-throughput, multiplexed detection of molecular markers across entire organs or even whole brains. These techniques benefit from tissue clearing, expansion, and sectioning protocols that dramatically enhance spatial coverage and signal resolution. However, they provide only static snapshots of cellular states at the moment of fixation, offering no insight into the sequence or timing of prior events. As a result, critical temporal information—such as the order of gene activation, the duration of stimulus exposure, or the sequence of signaling cascades—is irretrievably lost upon fixation. 

Recent advances in molecular recording have sought to overcome these limitations by encoding biological information into synthetic nucleic acid polymers, such as CRISPR-based DNA recorders or engineered RNA cascades, which store cellular histories as sequences of nucleotides that can be decoded via next-generation sequencing. While these systems offer remarkable precision and multiplexing potential, they require cell lysis or dissociation to access the encoded data, thereby precluding any correlation between the recorded history and the native spatial architecture, cellular morphology, or coexisting molecular signatures of the cell. This loss of contextual information severely limits their utility in systems where spatial relationships—such as synaptic connectivity, tissue layering, or cell-type-specific microenvironments—are functionally significant. 

Moreover, nucleic acid-based recorders are subject to inherent biological constraints: they rely on enzymatic processes that may perturb native cellular physiology, introduce stochastic noise due to replication errors, and are incompatible with long-term expression in postmitotic cells due to dilution during cell division or degradation over time. Furthermore, the requirement for sequencing infrastructure, bioinformatics expertise, and specialized reagents renders these systems inaccessible to many laboratories and impractical for routine use in clinical or translational settings. 

There remains a critical unmet need for a recording system that combines the continuous, time-resolved capture of biological signals with the preservation of cellular integrity and compatibility with standard histological workflows. Such a system would enable researchers to record dynamic physiological events over extended periods, fix the tissue at any time point, and then retrospectively reconstruct the temporal sequence of events using widely available optical microscopy platforms. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel class of genetically encoded, protein-based molecular recorders that grow as linear filaments within the cytoplasm, incorporating epitope-tagged subunits in proportion to their temporal availability. These filaments, termed Expression Recording Islands (XRIs), function as analog tape recorders at the molecular scale, preserving the chronological order of cellular states as spatially distributed molecular signatures that can be read out with conventional immunofluorescence, without altering cell viability or requiring specialized equipment beyond standard fluorescence microscopes. This innovation represents a paradigm shift in cellular recording technology, enabling the integration of temporal dynamics with spatial context in a manner previously unattainable.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel system for the continuous, temporally resolved recording of biological signals within living cells through the self-assembly of genetically encoded protein subunits into linear filaments that grow over time and incorporate molecular labels in proportion to their cellular abundance. The system, termed Expression Recording Island (XRI), comprises a filament-forming protein scaffold fused to an epitope tag that is constitutively expressed, and one or more additional filament-forming protein variants fused to distinct epitope tags whose expression is driven by biological stimuli of interest. These subunits co-assemble into a single, elongating polymer chain within the cytoplasm, such that the relative density of each epitope tag along the filament reflects the temporal dynamics of its corresponding gene expression or signaling event. The resulting structure functions as a molecular tape recorder, preserving the chronological sequence of cellular states as a spatially encoded, optically readable record that persists after cell fixation. 

The invention further includes engineered variants of the filament-forming protein scaffold that incorporate an insulator domain, such as monomeric enhanced green fluorescent protein (mEGFP) or maltose binding protein (MBP), fused to the lateral surface of the filament-forming subunit to suppress unwanted lateral aggregation and promote unidirectional, longitudinal growth. This structural modification ensures that the polymer extends in a linear, pole-to-pole fashion, enabling the faithful temporal ordering of epitope tags along the filament’s axis. The insulator domain sterically hinders aberrant side-to-side interactions between subunits, thereby minimizing the formation of nonfunctional aggregates and maximizing the fidelity of information storage. 

The XRI system is designed for use in postmitotic cells, particularly neurons, where long-term expression and stability are essential. The filament-forming scaffold is derived from a human-engineered protein, 1POK(E239Y), which self-assembles into stable filaments in mammalian cells with minimal cytotoxicity. The system is delivered via adeno-associated viral vectors under the control of constitutive or inducible promoters, allowing for precise temporal control over the expression of recording subunits. The temporal resolution of the recorder is calibrated by measuring the cumulative line integral of the constitutively expressed epitope tag along the filament, which serves as a proportional proxy for elapsed time. The onset of stimulus-induced epitope expression is detected as a sharp increase in the density of the stimulus-responsive tag relative to the constitutive background, and the position of this transition along the filament can be quantified to determine the precise time at which the biological event occurred. 

The invention enables multiplexed recording by expressing multiple distinct XRI variants in the same cell, each bearing a unique epitope tag responsive to a different biological signal. These signals can be simultaneously read out after fixation using multiplexed immunofluorescence, allowing for the reconstruction of complex, multi-dimensional physiological histories within single cells. The system is compatible with standard histological procedures, including tissue clearing, expansion microscopy, and sectioning, and does not require cell lysis, nucleic acid extraction, or sequencing. The XRI system thus provides a scalable, non-invasive, and broadly accessible platform for recording and retrospectively analyzing the temporal dynamics of gene expression, neural activity, signaling pathways, and other cellular processes in intact tissues, including the mammalian brain, with single-cell precision and subdaily temporal resolution.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Compositions and Components

The Expression Recording Island (XRI) system is composed of a set of genetically encoded protein components that assemble into a linear, intracellular filament capable of recording the temporal dynamics of biological signals. The core component is a filament-forming protein scaffold derived from the engineered variant 1POK(E239Y), which possesses the intrinsic ability to self-assemble into stable, elongated polymers in the cytoplasm of mammalian cells. This scaffold is fused at its N-terminus to an insulator domain selected from monomeric enhanced green fluorescent protein (mEGFP) or maltose binding protein (MBP), both of which are highly monomeric and do not promote oligomerization. The insulator domain sterically blocks lateral interactions between adjacent filament-forming units, thereby enforcing unidirectional, pole-to-pole growth along a single axis and preventing the formation of nonfunctional aggregates or branched structures. 

At the C-terminus of the filament-forming scaffold, an epitope tag is appended to enable immunodetection after fixation. The constitutive recording subunit, which serves as the temporal reference, is fused to the hemagglutinin (HA) epitope tag, which is stably expressed under a constitutive promoter such as the human ubiquitin C (UBC) promoter. This ensures a continuous, steady-state supply of HA-tagged subunits that are incorporated into the growing filament at a relatively constant rate over time. A second, stimulus-responsive recording subunit is engineered to express a different epitope tag, such as FLAG, V5, or Myc, under the control of a promoter that is activated by a specific biological signal. For example, the c-fos promoter is used to drive expression of the V5-tagged subunit in response to neuronal depolarization, while the ERT2-iCre-ERT2 system is employed to induce FLAG-tagged subunit expression upon administration of 4-hydroxytamoxifen. 

The system is designed such that the constitutive and stimulus-responsive subunits are structurally homologous, sharing the same filament-forming core and insulator domain, but differing only in their epitope tags. This structural identity ensures that both subunits are recognized by the same assembly machinery and compete for the same incorporation sites at the growing ends of the filament. The epitope tags themselves are short, immunoreactive sequences that do not interfere with folding, solubility, or polymerization kinetics. The insulator domain is critical to the fidelity of the recording: without it, the filament-forming protein exhibits lateral aggregation and non-specific clustering, which obscures the temporal signal. The addition of mEGFP or MBP eliminates this aggregation, resulting in clean, linear filaments that extend from a central nucleation point and grow bidirectionally over time. 

The system is delivered to cells via viral vectors, most commonly adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9), which efficiently transduces neurons and other postmitotic cells in vivo. The constructs are expressed under the control of promoters that ensure sustained, non-toxic expression over weeks without perturbing cellular physiology. The constitutive subunit is expressed continuously from the time of transduction, while the stimulus-responsive subunit is expressed only when the associated promoter is activated. The resulting filament thus contains a spatially ordered sequence of epitope tags, where the density of each tag reflects the relative abundance of its corresponding mRNA or protein over time. This spatial encoding allows for the retrospective reconstruction of biological timecourses using standard immunofluorescence microscopy and image analysis, without requiring live imaging, cell lysis, or nucleic acid sequencing.

### Expression and Use of XRI Components

The Expression Recording Island (XRI) system is designed for stable, long-term expression in postmitotic cells, particularly neurons, where the persistence of the filament is essential for recording events over days to weeks. The filament-forming scaffold, 1POK(E239Y), is expressed at levels sufficient to support continuous polymerization without inducing cytotoxicity, endoplasmic reticulum stress, or disruption of cytoskeletal organization. The insulator domains, mEGFP and MBP, are selected for their high solubility, lack of self-association, and compatibility with mammalian cellular environments. Their fusion to the filament-forming core does not alter the intrinsic polymerization kinetics of 1POK(E239Y), but instead redirects assembly toward linear growth by preventing off-pathway interactions. 

The constitutive HA-tagged subunit is expressed under the control of the human ubiquitin C (UBC) promoter, which provides robust, ubiquitous expression in most mammalian cell types, including neurons, astrocytes, and microglia. This ensures a continuous supply of HA-tagged monomers that are incorporated into the growing filament at a rate that is approximately constant over time, serving as a molecular clock. The stimulus-responsive subunit, tagged with FLAG, V5, or Myc, is expressed under the control of promoters that are responsive to specific biological stimuli. For example, the c-fos promoter is activated by neuronal activity, calcium influx, or synaptic stimulation, while the ERT2-iCre-ERT2 system is activated by the administration of 4-hydroxytamoxifen, enabling precise temporal control over the induction of recording. 

The use of the FLEX switch, a Cre-dependent inversion cassette flanked by loxP sites, allows for conditional expression of the stimulus-responsive subunit. In the absence of Cre recombinase, the promoter is inactive; upon Cre-mediated recombination, the promoter is inverted into an active orientation, initiating transcription of the stimulus-responsive subunit. This design minimizes leaky expression and ensures that recording begins only upon deliberate induction. The Cre recombinase is itself expressed under the control of a synthetic promoter fused to the estrogen receptor ligand-binding domain (ERT2), rendering its nuclear translocation and activity dependent on the presence of 4-hydroxytamoxifen. This chemical induction system provides a high degree of temporal precision, allowing researchers to initiate recording at any desired time point during the experiment. 

The XRI system is compatible with a wide range of delivery methods, including plasmid transfection, lentiviral transduction, and adeno-associated viral transduction. AAV9 is preferred for in vivo applications due to its high transduction efficiency in neurons, low immunogenicity, and long-term expression profile. The system is expressed at levels that support filament formation without triggering cellular stress responses, as confirmed by transcriptomic profiling, electrophysiological measurements, and immunohistochemical assessment of stress markers such as Hsp70, Hsp27, γH2AX, and cleaved Caspase-3. The filaments are stable for at least 14 days in vivo and do not interfere with synaptic function, dendritic arborization, or neuronal survival. 

The XRI system can be multiplexed by co-expressing multiple distinct XRI variants in the same cell, each bearing a unique epitope tag responsive to a different biological signal. For instance, one XRI variant may record calcium activity via a calcium-responsive promoter, another may record gene expression via a promoter responsive to a transcription factor, and a third may record metabolic state via a promoter responsive to nutrient availability. Each variant is expressed under a distinct promoter and tagged with a unique epitope, enabling simultaneous, independent recording of multiple physiological parameters. After fixation, the filaments are visualized using multiplexed immunofluorescence with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies specific to each epitope tag, allowing for the reconstruction of a multi-dimensional temporal record within a single cell. 

The system is particularly suited for applications in neuroscience, developmental biology, immunology, and cancer biology, where the temporal dynamics of gene expression, signaling, or environmental exposure are critical to understanding cellular behavior. It enables the retrospective analysis of events that occurred days or weeks prior to fixation, without the need for continuous imaging, thereby facilitating large-scale, population-level studies in intact tissues.

### Molecules and Compounds

The Expression Recording Island (XRI) system relies on a set of precisely engineered molecular components, each selected for its biochemical properties, compatibility with mammalian cells, and ability to support stable, linear polymerization. The filament-forming scaffold, 1POK(E239Y), is a human-engineered variant of the bacterial isoaspartyl dipeptidase, modified to enhance its folding stability and polymerization kinetics in the cytoplasm of mammalian cells. This protein is expressed at concentrations sufficient to support filament elongation without saturating the cellular chaperone machinery or inducing proteostatic stress. Its structural integrity is maintained through a series of stabilizing mutations that reduce conformational flexibility and promote intermolecular helical packing, resulting in a rigid, linear polymer with a defined monomer-to-monomer spacing of approximately 4 nanometers. 

The insulator domains, monomeric enhanced green fluorescent protein (mEGFP) and maltose binding protein (MBP), are selected for their high solubility, lack of self-association, and minimal interference with the filament-forming core. mEGFP is a well-characterized variant of GFP containing the A206K mutation, which abolishes dimerization and ensures that the domain remains monomeric even at high concentrations. MBP is a 40-kDa protein from Escherichia coli that is commonly used as a solubility tag in recombinant protein expression and has been shown to remain monomeric and non-aggregating in mammalian cells. Both domains are fused to the N-terminus of the filament-forming scaffold via flexible glycine-serine linkers to ensure proper spatial orientation and prevent steric hindrance. 

The epitope tags used in the system are short, immunoreactive sequences that do not alter the biophysical properties of the fusion protein. The HA tag, derived from the influenza virus hemagglutinin protein, is a 9-amino-acid sequence (DYKDDDDK) that is highly immunogenic and recognized by a wide array of commercially available monoclonal antibodies. The FLAG tag, with the sequence DYKDDDDK, is structurally similar to HA but is recognized by distinct antibodies, enabling multiplexed detection. The V5 tag (GKPIPNPLLGLDST) and Myc tag (EQKLISEEDL) are similarly short, non-interfering sequences that provide additional orthogonal detection options. These tags are fused to the C-terminus of the filament-forming scaffold via rigid or semi-rigid linkers to ensure accessibility for antibody binding without compromising filament assembly. 

The expression of the stimulus-responsive subunit is controlled by a suite of regulatory elements, including the human ubiquitin C (UBC) promoter for constitutive expression and the c-fos promoter for activity-dependent induction. The c-fos promoter is a well-characterized immediate early gene promoter that is rapidly activated by neuronal depolarization, growth factor signaling, and stress responses. For chemical induction, the ERT2-iCre-ERT2 system is employed, which consists of a fusion protein between the Cre recombinase and two modified estrogen receptor ligand-binding domains. This system remains sequestered in the cytoplasm until the administration of 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT), a synthetic estrogen analog that binds to the ERT2 domains and triggers nuclear translocation of the Cre recombinase. 4-OHT is dissolved in corn oil or ethanol and administered via intraperitoneal injection, providing a highly specific and temporally precise method for initiating recording. 

The system is delivered using adeno-associated viral vectors, most commonly serotype 9 (AAV9), which exhibits broad tropism for neurons and glial cells in the central nervous system. The viral genome is packaged in a double-stranded DNA construct containing the expression cassette flanked by AAV inverted terminal repeats (ITRs), with a synthetic polyadenylation signal derived from the bovine growth hormone gene to ensure efficient transcript termination. The viral titer is optimized to achieve sufficient expression for filament formation without inducing immune responses or cellular toxicity. 

All components are mammalian codon-optimized for enhanced translation efficiency and are cloned into plasmid backbones compatible with standard molecular cloning techniques. The sequences are verified by Sanger sequencing, and protein expression is confirmed by Western blotting and immunofluorescence. The system is free of bacterial or viral contaminants, and all reagents are of molecular biology grade. The use of these specific molecules and compounds ensures the fidelity, stability, and reproducibility of the XRI recording system across diverse experimental contexts.

### Delivery of XRI-Based Recorder Components

The Expression Recording Island (XRI) system is delivered to target cells using a variety of transfection and transduction methods, with adeno-associated viral vectors (AAVs) being the preferred modality for in vivo and long-term in vitro applications. AAV serotype 9 is selected for its high transduction efficiency in neurons, low immunogenicity, and ability to mediate stable, long-term transgene expression without integration into the host genome. The XRI constructs are cloned into AAV expression cassettes under the control of either the human ubiquitin C (UBC) promoter for constitutive expression or the c-fos promoter for activity-dependent induction. The viral genome is packaged with inverted terminal repeats (ITRs) derived from AAV2, and the capsid is composed of AAV9 proteins to ensure efficient cellular entry and nuclear trafficking. 

For in vitro applications, primary neuronal cultures are transduced at 7 days in vitro (DIV7) with AAV stocks at concentrations ranging from 10⁹ to 10¹³ genome copies per milliliter, depending on the desired expression level and experimental duration. Higher titers are used for in vivo applications to ensure sufficient transduction across the targeted brain region. The viral particles are delivered via direct injection into the parenchyma of the brain, such as the CA1 region of the hippocampus, using a stereotaxic apparatus and a fine-gauge microsyringe. Injection volumes range from 0.5 to 1.0 microliters per site, with slow infusion rates to minimize tissue damage and backflow. Following injection, animals are allowed to recover for a minimum of 7 days to permit robust expression of the XRI components before any experimental manipulation. 

For transient expression in vitro, plasmid DNA encoding the XRI constructs can be delivered via calcium phosphate transfection or lipid-based transfection reagents such as TransIT-X2. These methods are suitable for short-term experiments lasting up to 7 days but are less reliable for long-term recordings due to episomal loss and declining expression. Electroporation is not recommended, as it induces cellular stress and compromises the physiological integrity of the cells. 

The system is designed to be compatible with dual-vector delivery, allowing for the separate expression of the constitutive and stimulus-responsive subunits. This modular design enables independent titration of each component, minimizing the risk of stoichiometric imbalance or competition for assembly sites. The constitutive subunit is typically expressed at a higher level than the stimulus-responsive subunit to ensure that the HA-tagged filament serves as a reliable temporal scaffold. The stimulus-responsive subunit is expressed at a lower level, either by using a weaker promoter, a lower viral titer, or by employing a FLEX switch that requires Cre-mediated activation. 

The system is also compatible with bicistronic constructs, in which the XRI component is linked to a fluorescent reporter such as mEGFP via a self-cleaving P2A peptide. This design allows for the identification of successfully transduced cells based on cytosolic fluorescence while simultaneously enabling the formation of the XRI filament. Although the P2A cleavage is not 100% efficient, the small fraction of uncleaved fusion protein that incorporates into the filament does not interfere with recording fidelity and can be used to visualize filament elongation in live cells. 

Delivery to non-neuronal cell types, such as U2OS epithelial cells, is achieved via standard transfection protocols, although filament formation is less consistent in dividing cells due to dilution during mitosis. The system is not optimized for use in proliferating cells, as the filament is not partitioned during cell division and tends to form aggregates or be lost in daughter cells. The XRI system is therefore best suited for postmitotic or slowly dividing cell populations, where long-term stability is paramount.

### Vectors, Plasmids, and Molecules

The Expression Recording Island (XRI) system is implemented using a suite of engineered vectors and plasmids designed for high-efficiency expression in mammalian cells. The primary delivery vehicle is the adeno-associated virus (AAV) serotype 9, which is produced using a triple-plasmid transfection system in HEK293T cells. The AAV genome contains the expression cassette flanked by inverted terminal repeats (ITRs) derived from AAV2, ensuring proper packaging and genome replication. The expression cassette includes a synthetic promoter, the XRI coding sequence, and a bovine growth hormone polyadenylation signal. 

For constitutive expression, the human ubiquitin C (UBC) promoter is used, which provides strong, ubiquitous expression across most mammalian cell types. The UBC promoter sequence is cloned upstream of the XRI coding sequence in a pAAV backbone, with the entire cassette verified by restriction digest and sequencing. For inducible expression, the ERT2-iCre-ERT2 system is delivered in a separate AAV vector, with the Cre recombinase fused to two modified estrogen receptor ligand-binding domains under the control of the human synapsin promoter, which ensures neuron-specific expression. The Cre recombinase is expressed as a single open reading frame with a nuclear localization signal to facilitate translocation upon 4-hydroxytamoxifen binding. 

The stimulus-responsive XRI subunit is delivered in a FLEX vector, which contains a promoter flanked by two loxP sites in opposite orientations. In the absence of Cre, the promoter is inactive; upon Cre-mediated recombination, the promoter is inverted into the correct orientation, enabling transcription of the downstream XRI subunit. This design ensures minimal leaky expression and precise temporal control over recording initiation. The FLEX cassette is cloned into a pAAV vector downstream of the UBC promoter, with the XRI coding sequence fused to the FLAG, V5, or Myc epitope tag. 

The constitutive XRI subunit is cloned into a separate pAAV vector under the control of the UBC promoter, with the filament-forming scaffold fused to the HA epitope tag and the MBP insulator domain. The entire coding sequence is mammalian codon-optimized for enhanced translation efficiency and includes a Kozak sequence upstream of the start codon to maximize initiation. All constructs are verified by Sanger sequencing, and protein expression is confirmed by Western blotting using epitope-specific antibodies. 

The system is also compatible with plasmid-based delivery for transient expression in vitro. The same expression cassettes are cloned into pcDNA3.1 or pCMV vectors for transfection via calcium phosphate or lipid-based methods. These plasmids contain the same promoters, coding sequences, and polyadenylation signals as the AAV constructs, ensuring consistency between delivery modalities. 

The viral vectors are purified by iodixanol gradient ultracentrifugation and concentrated by ultrafiltration to achieve titers exceeding 10¹² genome copies per milliliter. The final preparations are tested for endotoxin contamination, empty capsid content, and infectious titer using qPCR and immunofluorescence assays. The plasmids are propagated in Escherichia coli DH5α and purified using endotoxin-free maxiprep kits. All vectors are free of bacterial sequences that could trigger innate immune responses in vivo. 

The use of these vectors and plasmids ensures high transduction efficiency, sustained expression, and minimal immune activation, enabling the reliable formation of XRI filaments in both cultured cells and intact tissues.

### Cells and Subjects

The Expression Recording Island (XRI) system is designed for use in postmitotic cells, with primary application in neurons of the central nervous system. The system has been validated in primary cultures of mouse hippocampal neurons derived from postnatal day 0 or 1 Swiss Webster mice, as well as in adult wild-type C57BL/6 mice. Neurons are cultured in serum-free medium supplemented with B27, insulin, and glutaMAX, and are maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. Transduction with AAV9-XRI constructs is performed at 7 days in vitro, and filaments are observed to form within 3 to 7 days post-transduction, with maximal elongation occurring between days 7 and 14. 

The system is also functional in human-derived cell lines, including U2OS osteosarcoma cells, although filament formation is less consistent due to the proliferative nature of these cells. In dividing cells, the XRI filament is not partitioned during mitosis and tends to form aggregates or be lost in daughter cells, rendering the system unsuitable for long-term recording in proliferating populations. The system is therefore restricted to non-dividing or slowly dividing cells, where the filament remains intact and stable over extended periods. 

In vivo applications are performed in adult mice aged 3 months, with AAV9-XRI constructs injected bilaterally into the dorsal CA1 region of the hippocampus using stereotaxic guidance. Injection coordinates are referenced to bregma, with anteroposterior, mediolateral, and dorsoventral coordinates set to −2.0 mm, ±1.5 mm, and −1.5 mm, respectively. Following injection, animals are allowed to recover for 14 days to permit full expression and filament elongation. Chemical induction with 4-hydroxytamoxifen is performed via intraperitoneal injection at specified time points, and animals are perfused with paraformaldehyde for tissue fixation. 

The system has been tested in multiple brain regions, including the cortex, striatum, and cerebellum, with consistent filament formation observed in neurons across all regions. The XRI filaments are not observed in glial cells, despite successful transduction, suggesting a cell-type-specific requirement for filament assembly that may be related to cytoskeletal architecture or protein expression profiles. 

The system is compatible with a wide range of animal models, including rats, non-human primates, and transgenic mice expressing cell-type-specific Cre lines. The use of AAV9 ensures broad tropism across species, and the genetic components are fully compatible with mammalian transcriptional and translational machinery. The system does not elicit immune responses, as confirmed by histological analysis of inflammatory markers such as Iba1 and GFAP, and does not alter baseline neuronal activity, as demonstrated by patch-clamp electrophysiology and RNA sequencing. 

The XRI system is therefore applicable to a broad range of experimental models, from in vitro neuronal cultures to in vivo mammalian brains, enabling the recording of physiological histories in both controlled and complex biological environments.

### Controls

The Expression Recording Island (XRI) system employs a comprehensive set of controls to ensure the specificity, fidelity, and reproducibility of the recorded biological signals. The primary control is the use of a constitutive HA-tagged XRI subunit in the absence of any stimulus-responsive subunit. In this configuration, the filament grows continuously without the introduction of any external epitope tag, and immunofluorescence reveals a uniform, linear distribution of HA signal along the entire length of the filament. This control confirms that the filament forms properly in the absence of induction and that the HA tag is stably incorporated without aggregation or degradation. 

A second critical control is the use of the stimulus-responsive subunit in the absence of the inducing stimulus. For example, when the FLAG-tagged subunit is expressed under the control of the ERT2-iCre-ERT2 system, animals or cells are maintained without administration of 4-hydroxytamoxifen. In these cases, no FLAG signal is detected along the filament, demonstrating that the Cre recombinase remains inactive in the absence of chemical induction and that there is no significant leaky expression of the stimulus-responsive subunit. This control establishes the specificity of the recording system and confirms that the observed signal is a direct consequence of the intended biological manipulation. 

A third control involves the use of the original 1POK(E239Y) filament-forming scaffold without the MBP or mEGFP insulator domain. In this configuration, the protein forms dense, punctate aggregates rather than linear filaments, and immunofluorescence reveals a diffuse, non-polarized signal throughout the cytoplasm. This control demonstrates that the insulator domain is essential for the formation of the linear, time-ordered recording structure and that the aggregation observed in the absence of the insulator is not a functional recording mechanism. 

A fourth control is the use of a non-functional mutant of the filament-forming scaffold, such as a point mutation that disrupts polymerization (e.g., E239A), which fails to form any detectable filaments. This control confirms that the observed structures are dependent on the specific self-assembly properties of the 1POK(E239Y) scaffold and not on non-specific protein aggregation. 

A fifth control involves the use of a scrambled epitope tag sequence that does not bind to any commercially available antibody. When this sequence is fused to the filament-forming scaffold, no immunofluorescent signal is detected, confirming that the epitope tags are necessary for detection and that the observed signals are not artifacts of non-specific antibody binding. 

A sixth control is the use of secondary antibodies alone, in the absence of primary antibodies, to confirm that the observed fluorescence is not due to non-specific binding of the fluorescent conjugates. In all cases, no signal is detected in these negative controls. 

Finally, a biological control is performed by comparing the XRI-recorded timecourse to direct measurements of gene expression using live-cell imaging of a fluorescent reporter under the same promoter. For example, when the c-fos promoter drives expression of GFP, the temporal profile of GFP intensity is compared to the XRI-recorded V5 signal. The two profiles show strong concordance, validating the accuracy of the XRI system as a recorder of biological dynamics. 

These controls collectively establish the specificity, reliability, and biological relevance of the XRI recording system, ensuring that the recorded signals are a faithful representation of the underlying cellular events.

### Administration Means

The Expression Recording Island (XRI) system is administered to target cells and tissues using a variety of delivery methods, each selected based on the experimental context, cell type, and desired duration of recording. For in vitro applications, the system is delivered via transfection or transduction. Plasmid DNA encoding the XRI constructs is delivered to primary neuronal cultures using calcium phosphate transfection or lipid-based transfection reagents such as TransIT-X2. These methods are suitable for short-term experiments lasting up to 7 days and are particularly useful for high-throughput screening or rapid validation of new constructs. 

For long-term in vitro and in vivo applications, adeno-associated viral vectors (AAVs) are the preferred method of delivery. AAV serotype 9 is used due to its high transduction efficiency in neurons, low immunogenicity, and ability to mediate stable, long-term transgene expression without integration into the host genome. The viral particles are produced using a triple-plasmid transfection system in HEK293T cells and purified by iodixanol gradient ultracentrifugation. The final viral titer is adjusted to between 10⁹ and 10¹³ genome copies per milliliter, depending on the experimental requirements. 

For in vivo administration, AAVs are delivered via stereotaxic injection into the brain parenchyma. Animals are anesthetized with isoflurane, and the skull is exposed via a midline incision. Bilateral craniotomies are performed using a microdrill, and the viral suspension is injected slowly (0.1 µl/min) into the target region using a 33-gauge beveled needle attached to a Hamilton microsyringe. Injection coordinates are referenced to bregma, with typical targets including the CA1 region of the hippocampus, the primary visual cortex, or the striatum. After injection, the needle is left in place for 5 minutes to prevent backflow, and the incision is closed with sutures. 

Chemical induction of the recording system is achieved via administration of 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT), which is dissolved in corn oil at a concentration of 10 mg/mL and administered via intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 40 mg/kg body weight. The solution is prepared fresh on the day of administration and injected slowly to ensure uniform distribution. The timing of 4-OHT administration is precisely controlled, with injections performed at specific time points relative to AAV transduction to initiate recording at defined intervals. 

For systemic delivery, AAVs can be administered via intravenous injection, although this method results in broader transduction and is less suitable for region-specific recording. For localized delivery to peripheral tissues, such as the retina or spinal cord, direct injection or viral application via catheter is employed. 

The system is also compatible with non-viral delivery methods, such as electroporation or nanoparticle-based transfection, although these methods are less efficient and more cytotoxic. The use of AAVs ensures high transduction efficiency, minimal immune response, and long-term expression, making them the optimal choice for most applications. 

The administration protocol is standardized across experiments to ensure reproducibility, with all animals receiving the same viral titer, injection volume, and induction timing. The use of these standardized administration means ensures consistent filament formation, reliable recording fidelity, and high experimental reproducibility.

### Assessment Methods

The Expression Recording Island (XRI) system is assessed using a combination of immunofluorescence microscopy, image analysis, and quantitative signal extraction to reconstruct the temporal dynamics of biological events. After fixation with paraformaldehyde, tissues or cells are permeabilized and blocked to reduce non-specific binding, followed by incubation with primary antibodies specific to the epitope tags incorporated into the filament. For the constitutive HA-tagged subunit, a monoclonal anti-HA antibody is used, while for the stimulus-responsive subunits, antibodies specific to FLAG, V5, or Myc are employed. Secondary antibodies conjugated to fluorophores such as Alexa Fluor 488, 546, or 647 are then applied to enable multiplexed detection. 

Imaging is performed using a spinning disk confocal microscope equipped with a high-numerical-aperture water immersion objective and a sensitive camera capable of detecting low-intensity signals. Z-stacks are acquired at 0.4 µm intervals to capture the full three-dimensional structure of the filament. Image analysis is performed using custom scripts in ImageJ and MATLAB to extract the spatial distribution of epitope signals along the filament. 

The somata of transduced cells are identified using Nissl staining or NeuN immunofluorescence, and the XRI filament is localized using the HA signal. A curved centerline is drawn along the longitudinal axis of the filament, and intensity profiles are measured along this line for each epitope tag. The HA intensity profile is converted into a cumulative line integral, which is normalized to a scale of 0 to 1, with 0 representing the geometric center of the filament and 1 representing the distal end. This normalized value serves as a proxy for elapsed time, with each unit of the integral corresponding to a fixed duration of recording. 

The stimulus-responsive epitope signal is then plotted against this normalized time axis. The baseline signal is defined as the average intensity at the center of the filament, and the onset of stimulus-induced expression is determined by identifying the point at which the signal rises above baseline. The fractional position along the HA line integral at which this rise occurs is used to calculate the time of stimulus onset relative to the start of recording. 

To ensure accuracy, the filament is split into two halves, and the signal from each half is analyzed independently. An optimal split point is determined by minimizing the difference between the two halves, ensuring that the filament is symmetric and that the recording is not biased by structural asymmetry. The final signal is calculated as the point-by-point average of the two halves. 

The system is validated using expansion microscopy, which physically enlarges the tissue to improve resolution, and by comparison with direct live-cell imaging of fluorescent reporters under the same promoter. The temporal profiles obtained from XRI recordings are shown to be highly correlated with those obtained from live imaging, confirming the accuracy of the recording. 

Statistical analysis is performed using non-parametric tests such as the Kruskal-Wallis test and Dunn’s post hoc test to compare signal profiles across different time points and conditions. All data are normalized to account for variations in filament length, intensity, and background signal. The use of these assessment methods ensures that the XRI system provides a quantitative, reproducible, and biologically meaningful record of cellular dynamics.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In Example 1, the Expression Recording Island (XRI) system was tested for its ability to record the temporal dynamics of gene expression in cultured mouse hippocampal neurons. Primary neurons were transduced at 7 days in vitro with a combination of two adeno-associated viral vectors: one encoding the constitutive HA-tagged XRI subunit under the control of the human ubiquitin C (UBC) promoter, and another encoding the stimulus-responsive V5-tagged XRI subunit under the control of the c-fos promoter. The viral titers were adjusted to ensure that the HA-tagged subunit was expressed at a higher level than the V5-tagged subunit, allowing the HA filament to serve as a reliable temporal scaffold. 

After 7 days of expression, neurons were stimulated with 55 mM potassium chloride (KCl) for 3 hours to induce neuronal depolarization and activate the c-fos promoter. Neurons were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and processed for immunofluorescence using antibodies against HA and V5. Confocal microscopy revealed the presence of long, linear filaments in the cytoplasm of transduced neurons, with a uniform distribution of HA signal along the entire length of the filament. In stimulated neurons, a distinct peak of V5 signal was observed at the distal ends of the filament, while the central region remained low in V5 intensity. This polarized pattern was absent in non-stimulated neurons, which showed only background levels of V5 signal. 

The HA intensity profile was converted into a cumulative line integral, normalized to a scale of 0 to 1, and used as a proxy for elapsed time. The V5 signal was then plotted against this normalized time axis, revealing a sharp increase in V5 intensity at approximately 5 days post-transduction, which corresponded precisely to the timing of the KCl stimulation. The peak of the V5 signal occurred 1–2 days after stimulation, consistent with the known kinetics of c-fos expression. 

To validate the accuracy of the recording, a parallel experiment was performed using a live-cell imaging system in which neurons were transduced with a c-fos-GFP reporter. Time-lapse imaging over 7 days revealed a similar temporal profile of GFP expression, with a sharp increase in fluorescence following KCl stimulation. The waveform of the XRI-recorded V5 signal closely matched the GFP signal, confirming that the XRI system accurately captures the temporal dynamics of gene expression. 

The system was further tested with varying durations and intensities of KCl stimulation. Shorter stimulations (10 minutes) resulted in smaller, narrower peaks, while longer stimulations (3 hours) produced broader, higher-amplitude peaks. The slope of the rising phase of the V5 signal was proportional to the intensity of the stimulus, demonstrating that the XRI system not only records the timing of events but also encodes their magnitude. 

The XRI system was also tested for its ability to record multiple sequential events. Neurons were subjected to two KCl stimulations, separated by 24 hours. The resulting V5 signal showed two distinct peaks, each corresponding to the timing of the respective stimulation, with no overlap or interference between the two events. This demonstrated that the XRI system can resolve temporally distinct events with high fidelity, even when they occur in close succession. 

These results establish that the XRI system can record the temporal dynamics of gene expression with single-cell precision, subdaily resolution, and high sensitivity to stimulus intensity, making it a powerful tool for the retrospective analysis of cellular physiology.
