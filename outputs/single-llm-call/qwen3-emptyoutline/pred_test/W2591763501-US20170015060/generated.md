## BACKGROUND

The field of additive manufacturing has long sought to bridge the performance gap between conventionally manufactured fiber-reinforced composites and those produced through layer-by-layer fabrication techniques. Traditional composite materials, particularly those employing high-performance carbon fibers embedded in aromatic thermoset matrices, are renowned for their exceptional mechanical strength, thermal stability, and environmental resistance, making them indispensable in aerospace, defense, and high-end automotive applications. However, the fabrication of such materials has historically been constrained by the limitations of conventional molding and layup processes, which restrict geometry to simple, two-dimensional configurations and impose significant constraints on fiber orientation control. While continuous fiber reinforcement offers superior mechanical performance, its integration into complex three-dimensional structures remains prohibitively expensive and technically challenging. Conversely, short fiber composites, though more amenable to molding and injection processes, suffer from isotropic or near-isotropic mechanical behavior due to the random dispersion of fibers within the matrix, resulting in suboptimal load transfer and inefficient material utilization.

Attempts to overcome these limitations through additive manufacturing have yielded mixed results. Fused deposition modeling (FDM) and similar thermoplastic-based techniques have demonstrated the ability to extrude short carbon fibers, but their utility is severely restricted by the inherent thermal and mechanical limitations of thermoplastic matrices such as acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS) or polylactic acid (PLA). These materials lack the high glass transition temperatures, creep resistance, and chemical stability required for structural applications in extreme environments. Moreover, the mechanical properties of thermoplastic-based composites are fundamentally limited by the lower modulus and strength of the matrix itself, rendering them unsuitable for high-stress applications where aromatic thermosets such as epoxies or cyanate esters are the industry standard. Alternative additive manufacturing approaches, including selective laser sintering and stereolithography, have been explored for composite fabrication, but they are generally incompatible with high-volume-fraction, high-aspect-ratio carbon fibers due to the destructive nature of the energy sources involved, which can degrade fibers, induce thermal stress, or cause delamination between layers.

Direct ink writing (DIW), a form of extrusion-based additive manufacturing, has emerged as a promising candidate for the fabrication of fiber-reinforced composites due to its ability to deposit material with high spatial precision and its compatibility with a wide range of rheologically tailored inks. However, prior efforts in DIW have been largely confined to low fiber loadings, typically below 1 volume percent, or have relied on low-aspect-ratio fibers, such as nanotubes or short glass fibers, which do not provide the same level of mechanical reinforcement as high-performance carbon fibers. Even when higher fiber loadings have been attempted, the resulting inks have exhibited poor extrudability, severe nozzle clogging, phase separation between the resin and fiber phases, or insufficient post-extrusion shape retention, all of which prevent the formation of high-resolution, self-supporting structures. Furthermore, the critical phenomenon of shear-induced fiber alignment—essential for achieving anisotropic mechanical and physical properties—has either been neglected, poorly controlled, or demonstrated only in thermoplastic systems where the matrix viscosity is insufficient to effectively transmit shear forces to the fibers over the length scales required for meaningful alignment.

A persistent challenge in the field has been the inability to simultaneously achieve three critical objectives: (1) the use of high-volume-fraction, high-aspect-ratio carbon fibers within an aromatic thermoset matrix; (2) the stable, continuous extrusion of such inks through micro-nozzles without clogging or phase separation; and (3) the reliable, reproducible alignment of fibers along the direction of extrusion to produce orthotropic composite architectures with performance exceeding that of conventionally molded random composites. While some studies have reported partial success in aligning fibers during extrusion, none have demonstrated a system capable of producing fully dense, structurally complex, three-dimensional composite parts with controlled fiber orientation, high fiber loading, and thermoset-level mechanical performance. The absence of a viable ink formulation that satisfies the competing demands of rheological processability, fiber stability, and rapid solidification has rendered the production of high-performance additively manufactured carbon fiber composites an unmet technological goal.

Moreover, the lack of a comprehensive understanding of the fluid dynamics governing fiber motion within the extrusion nozzle has hindered the rational design of printing systems. Prior work has relied on empirical trial-and-error approaches to nozzle geometry and printing parameters, without predictive modeling to optimize shear profiles, flow gradients, or residence time distributions within the extrusion path. This has resulted in inconsistent fiber alignment, unpredictable mechanical anisotropy, and limited scalability. Additionally, the curing chemistry employed in prior DIW systems has often required the mixing of reactive components immediately prior to printing, introducing significant complexity, reducing ink shelf life, and limiting the ability to print large or multi-part structures over extended timeframes. Latent curing systems, which remain stable at ambient conditions yet rapidly polymerize upon thermal activation, have been underutilized in DIW applications, particularly in systems requiring high fiber loading and precise geometric control.

There exists, therefore, a critical and unmet need in the field of advanced manufacturing for a method and system capable of producing three-dimensional, fully dense, high-performance carbon fiber-reinforced thermoset composite structures with controlled, spatially tailored fiber orientation, high fiber volume fractions, and mechanical properties that surpass those of conventionally manufactured counterparts. Such a system must integrate a novel ink formulation with precisely engineered rheological properties, a compatible extrusion architecture that promotes consistent shear alignment, and a latent thermal curing protocol that enables both extended printability and rapid, complete network formation. The invention disclosed herein fulfills this need by providing a unified technological framework that enables the additive manufacturing of orthotropic carbon fiber-reinforced thermoset composites with unprecedented control over microstructure, performance, and functionality.

## SUMMARY

The invention disclosed herein comprises a novel method and system for the additive manufacturing of high-performance, orthotropic carbon fiber-reinforced thermoset composite structures through direct ink writing, wherein the ink formulation, extrusion process, and curing protocol are co-designed to achieve unprecedented control over fiber alignment, structural integrity, and final material properties. The invention enables the fabrication of three-dimensional composite architectures with fiber volume fractions exceeding 3 volume percent and up to 8 volume percent, using high-aspect-ratio carbon fibers with lengths on the order of hundreds of micrometers, embedded within a bisphenol-F epoxy resin matrix modified with colloidal silica to achieve precisely tailored rheological behavior. The ink exhibits thixotropic, non-Newtonian characteristics that permit stable extrusion through micro-nozzles with diameters as small as 250 micrometers, while maintaining sufficient viscosity at rest to prevent fiber sedimentation and ensure immediate shape retention upon deposition. Upon extrusion, the carbon fibers undergo shear-induced alignment along the direction of flow, resulting in a highly anisotropic microstructure wherein 80 to 90 percent of the fibers are oriented parallel to the print path, thereby maximizing load transfer efficiency and enabling mechanical performance that significantly exceeds that of conventionally molded, randomly oriented composites of equivalent fiber loading.

The invention further comprises a latent thermal cure chemistry based on a Lewis acid catalyst, specifically a boron trifluoride derivative, incorporated at low concentrations of 0.1 weight percent or less, which remains chemically inert at ambient temperatures for periods exceeding five days, thereby enabling extended printability and storage stability of the ink. Upon exposure to elevated temperatures ranging from 80 to 200 degrees Celsius, the catalyst is activated to initiate a homopolymerization reaction between the epoxide functionalities of the resin, resulting in a fully crosslinked, high-density thermoset network with mechanical properties comparable to or superior to those of conventional amine-cured epoxy systems. The curing process may be performed in a single, uniform post-printing step, eliminating the need for in-situ mixing of reactive components and enabling the fabrication of complex, multi-layered, and geometrically intricate structures without time constraints imposed by resin pot life.

The invention also encompasses a computational modeling framework that simulates the hydrodynamic behavior of carbon fibers within the extrusion nozzle, enabling the prediction and optimization of fiber alignment as a function of nozzle geometry, flow rate, ink rheology, and fiber dimensions. The model explicitly resolves the motion of discrete fibers as rigid bodies interacting with a non-Newtonian, shear-thinning fluid matrix, incorporating contact forces, wall interactions, and variable viscosity profiles derived from experimental rheological data. This modeling capability allows for the rational design of nozzle geometries that minimize flow instabilities, reduce the risk of clogging, and maximize shear-induced alignment efficiency, thereby transforming the fabrication process from an empirical endeavor into a predictive engineering discipline.

The resulting additively manufactured composite structures exhibit pronounced orthotropic behavior in mechanical, electrical, and thermal properties. In tensile testing, unidirectionally printed specimens demonstrate a Young’s modulus up to 37 percent higher than that of conventionally pressed, randomly oriented composites of identical fiber volume fraction. In compression, the aligned filamentary architecture exhibits superior resistance to buckling and delamination both parallel and perpendicular to the print direction, contrary to conventional assumptions that alignment in one axis necessarily compromises performance in others. Electrical conductivity measurements reveal a directional anisotropy of more than four orders of magnitude, with conductivity parallel to the fiber alignment direction exceeding 1 siemens per centimeter, while conductivity perpendicular to the alignment direction is reduced to less than 10^-4 siemens per centimeter. This level of control over electrical transport enables the design of printed structures with spatially defined conductive pathways, shielding regions, or thermal management channels without the need for secondary processing or embedded metallic elements.

The invention further enables the fabrication of complex, free-form geometries—including curved surfaces, internal channels, and topology-optimized lattices—that would be impossible to produce using traditional layup or molding techniques. By varying the fiber loading, print path, and layer orientation within a single build, the mechanical and physical properties of the composite can be locally tuned to meet specific functional requirements, such as regions of high stiffness adjacent to regions of high toughness, or conductive pathways embedded within insulating matrix material. The ability to print such architectured composites without tooling, molds, or post-processing steps represents a paradigm shift in the design and manufacture of high-performance composite structures, enabling rapid prototyping, customization, and the integration of multiple functions into a single, monolithic component.

The invention is not limited to carbon fiber and bisphenol-F epoxy systems; the underlying principles of rheological stabilization through colloidal modification, shear-induced alignment during microextrusion, and latent thermal curing are broadly applicable to other high-performance fiber-matrix combinations, including aramid, glass, or boron fibers in cyanate ester, phenolic, or bismaleimide matrices. The method is scalable to industrial production through the use of multi-nozzle arrays, automated deposition systems, and integrated curing chambers, and is compatible with existing additive manufacturing platforms. The resulting composite materials are suitable for applications in aerospace, defense, automotive, biomedical, and energy sectors where lightweight, high-strength, functionally graded, and thermally stable structures are required. The invention thus provides a foundational technology for the next generation of additively manufactured composites, enabling the transition from isotropic, mass-produced parts to anisotropic, performance-optimized, digitally designed structures with unprecedented levels of control and functionality.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF SPECIFIC EMBODIMENTS

The invention disclosed herein is directed to a comprehensive system for the additive manufacturing of high-performance, orthotropic carbon fiber-reinforced thermoset composite structures through direct ink writing, wherein the ink formulation, extrusion dynamics, and curing protocol are synergistically engineered to enable the production of structurally complex, fully dense composite architectures with spatially controlled fiber alignment and superior mechanical and physical properties. The invention is not merely an incremental improvement over existing additive manufacturing techniques; it constitutes a fundamentally new approach to composite fabrication that integrates material science, fluid dynamics, and thermal chemistry into a unified manufacturing paradigm. The following detailed description outlines the essential components of the invention, including the composition and preparation of the ink, the design and operation of the extrusion system, the computational modeling of fiber alignment, the latent thermal curing mechanism, and the resulting properties of the printed composite structures, with particular emphasis on the interdependence of these elements and their collective contribution to the invention’s novelty and utility.

The ink formulation at the core of the invention comprises a bisphenol-F diglycidyl ether oligomer as the primary resin matrix, modified by the addition of a low volume fraction of colloidal silica nanoparticles and a high volume fraction of chopped, high-aspect-ratio carbon fibers. The bisphenol-F epoxy resin, selected for its high glass transition temperature, excellent chemical resistance, and compatibility with latent curing agents, serves as the thermosetting medium into which the reinforcing fibers are embedded. The carbon fibers employed are Grade HTS40, obtained from Toho Tenax, with a mean diameter of 6 micrometers, a length of approximately 600 micrometers, and an aspect ratio exceeding 100, providing the necessary mechanical reinforcement potential. However, the direct dispersion of such fibers into an unmodified epoxy resin results in catastrophic phase separation during extrusion, wherein the fibers remain stationary within the barrel and nozzle while the resin flows past them, leading to clogging, inconsistent deposition, and complete loss of structural integrity. To overcome this fundamental limitation, the invention introduces a precisely controlled addition of Cabosil TS530 fumed silica, a high-surface-area pyrogenic silica, at a concentration of 15 weight percent relative to the resin. This modification transforms the Newtonian, low-viscosity bisphenol-F epoxy into a thixotropic, non-Newtonian fluid with a static viscosity at zero shear rate exceeding 8,000 pascal-seconds, while retaining a low shear-thinned viscosity of approximately 2,000 pascal-seconds under the shear rates encountered during extrusion. The colloidal silica particles act as a rheological modifier by forming a transient, reversible network structure within the resin matrix, which imparts yield stress and shear-thinning behavior essential for printability. This network is sufficiently robust to suspend and carry the high-aspect-ratio carbon fibers during extrusion, preventing sedimentation and phase separation, while being sufficiently labile under shear to permit flow through micro-nozzles. The resulting ink exhibits a rheological response that conforms to the Carreau model, with parameters determined experimentally to be a zero-shear viscosity of 8,000 Pa·s, an infinite-shear viscosity of 2.2 Pa·s, a time constant of 45, and a power-law index of 0.1. This specific rheological profile ensures that the ink remains stable during storage and handling, yet flows readily under the controlled shear forces applied during extrusion, allowing for the deposition of continuous, self-supporting filaments with diameters as small as 250 micrometers and as large as 610 micrometers, depending on nozzle geometry.

The extrusion system employed in the invention is designed to exploit the rheological properties of the ink to induce controlled shear alignment of the carbon fibers during the printing process. The ink is loaded into a 3-milliliter syringe barrel, which is connected via a luer-lok fitting to a nozzle assembly fabricated from stainless steel or hardened tool steel, with an internal geometry that transitions from a cylindrical barrel of 5-millimeter diameter to a conical narrowing section of 31 millimeters in length, terminating in a cylindrical tip of 250 to 610 micrometers in diameter. The nozzle is mounted on a precision three-axis positioning stage capable of sub-micron positional accuracy, allowing for the deposition of filaments with spatial control exceeding 10 micrometers. During extrusion, the ink is forced through the nozzle under air pressure provided by a fluid dispenser capable of delivering pressures up to 100 psi. As the ink flows through the constriction of the nozzle, it experiences a steep velocity gradient, with the fluid velocity increasing from near-zero at the walls to a maximum at the centerline, creating a parabolic flow profile. The high viscosity of the silica-modified resin ensures that the carbon fibers are not merely passively transported by the fluid but are actively dragged along by viscous shear forces, resulting in their preferential alignment along the axis of extrusion. This alignment is not a passive consequence of flow but is actively engineered through the rheological design of the ink and the geometric design of the nozzle. The shear forces generated within the nozzle are sufficient to overcome the Brownian motion and inter-fiber entanglement that would otherwise randomize fiber orientation, resulting in a microstructure wherein the majority of fibers are oriented parallel to the print direction. This effect is observed consistently across all printed geometries, from simple unidirectional lines to complex, multi-layered, curved structures such as di-parabolic rocket nozzles, demonstrating the robustness and scalability of the alignment mechanism. The alignment is verified through optical microscopy of cross-sectioned printed samples, which reveal a high degree of fiber orientation parallel to the filament axis, with fiber misalignment angles typically less than 15 degrees from the print direction.

The invention further includes a computational modeling framework that enables the prediction and optimization of fiber alignment during extrusion. This framework employs a distributed Lagrange multiplier technique coupled with discrete element method simulations to resolve the motion of individual carbon fibers as rigid, anisotropic bodies within a non-Newtonian fluid matrix. The simulation domain replicates the geometry of the extrusion nozzle, with the fluid phase modeled as a shear-thinning fluid whose viscosity varies with local shear rate according to the Carreau model derived from experimental rheological data. The carbon fibers, modeled as cylinders with dimensions matching those of the actual fibers used in printing (6 micrometers in diameter and 300 micrometers in length), are randomly distributed at the inlet of the domain with a volume fraction of 8 percent. The simulation incorporates fluid-particle interactions governed by no-slip boundary conditions, particle-particle contact forces with friction and inelasticity, and wall interactions that account for the physical dimensions of the nozzle. The results of these simulations demonstrate that fiber alignment initiates at the nozzle walls, where shear rates are highest, and propagates into the bulk of the fluid domain as the extrusion proceeds, mirroring the parabolic velocity profile of the surrounding fluid. The simulations confirm that the alignment efficiency is not solely a function of nozzle geometry but is critically dependent on the viscosity and shear-thinning behavior of the resin matrix. A resin with insufficient viscosity fails to transmit sufficient shear stress to the fibers, resulting in poor alignment, while a resin with excessive viscosity impedes flow and causes clogging. The invention thus provides a predictive tool for optimizing nozzle design and ink formulation to maximize alignment efficiency, thereby transforming the fabrication process from an empirical trial-and-error endeavor into a rational engineering discipline. This modeling capability is not merely an analytical tool but an integral component of the invention, enabling the design of next-generation nozzles with monotonic tapering profiles, optimized entrance geometries, and internal surface treatments that further enhance alignment and reduce the risk of clogging.

The curing mechanism of the invention is based on latent thermal catalysis, which permits the ink to remain stable and printable at ambient temperatures for extended periods while enabling rapid, complete curing upon thermal activation. The resin system is modified by the addition of a boron trifluoride-based latent catalyst at a concentration of 0.1 weight percent or less. At temperatures below 70 degrees Celsius, the catalyst remains chemically inert, preserving the viscosity and printability of the ink for durations exceeding five days under ambient conditions. Upon exposure to temperatures between 80 and 200 degrees Celsius, the catalyst is activated to generate a strong Lewis acid, which initiates a homopolymerization reaction between the epoxide functionalities of the bisphenol-F resin. This reaction proceeds without the need for a co-reactant such as an amine, eliminating the need for pre-mixing of reactive components and ensuring that the ink remains stable until the moment of curing. The curing process is characterized by a rapid gelation event, occurring in less than five seconds at temperatures above 150 degrees Celsius, followed by complete network formation within 12 hours at 80 degrees Celsius. The resulting thermoset network exhibits a high crosslink density, as confirmed by dielectric relaxometry, which measures the relaxation dynamics of polar groups within the polymer matrix. The dielectric loss tangent (tan δ) and imaginary permittivity (ε”) show a sharp decline over time, indicating the progressive immobilization of molecular segments as the network forms. The final cured material exhibits mechanical properties comparable to those of conventional amine-cured bisphenol-F epoxy systems, with tensile strength exceeding 120 megapascals and Young’s modulus exceeding 15 gigapascals, while also demonstrating superior thermal stability, with a glass transition temperature exceeding 180 degrees Celsius. The curing protocol is compatible with the printed structures, as the low catalyst loading and controlled temperature profile prevent thermal degradation of the carbon fibers or the formation of internal stresses that could lead to warping or delamination. The entire curing process may be performed in a single, uniform post-printing step, enabling the fabrication of large, multi-layered, and geometrically complex structures without time constraints imposed by resin pot life.

The resulting additively manufactured composite structures exhibit pronounced orthotropic behavior in mechanical, electrical, and thermal properties, a direct consequence of the controlled fiber alignment achieved during extrusion. In tensile testing, specimens printed unidirectionally with 8 volume percent carbon fiber exhibit a Young’s modulus of 18.5 gigapascals, compared to 13.5 gigapascals for conventionally pressed, randomly oriented composites of identical fiber loading, representing a 37 percent increase in stiffness. This enhancement is attributed to the fact that 80 to 90 percent of the fibers are aligned parallel to the applied stress direction, whereas in a random composite, only approximately one-third of the fibers are oriented favorably to carry load. In compressive testing, the aligned structures demonstrate superior performance both parallel and perpendicular to the print direction. When compressed axially along the print direction, the aligned filaments resist buckling more effectively than randomly oriented fibers, due to their continuous, aligned nature and the uniform stress distribution along the filament axis. When compressed transversely, perpendicular to the print direction, the aligned structures still outperform random composites, a counterintuitive result that arises from the regular, ordered arrangement of filaments, which provides a more uniform load distribution and reduces the likelihood of localized failure. The aligned architecture effectively creates a “virtual weave” of fiber bundles, wherein the filaments act as interconnected load-bearing elements, even in directions orthogonal to their primary orientation. This behavior is not observed in randomly oriented composites, where fiber-fiber interactions are stochastic and load transfer is inefficient. The invention thus enables the design of composite structures with mechanical properties that are not merely anisotropic but are spatially programmable, allowing for regions of high stiffness, high toughness, and high damping to be incorporated into a single component without the need for hybrid materials or secondary bonding.

The electrical properties of the printed composites are similarly controlled by the fiber alignment. Four-point probe measurements conducted on a 3 volume percent carbon fiber composite printed in a uniaxial, hexagonal close-packed pattern reveal a surface conductivity of 1.1 siemens per centimeter parallel to the fiber alignment direction, while the conductivity perpendicular to the alignment direction is measured at 3.7 × 10^-5 siemens per centimeter, a difference of more than four orders of magnitude. This extreme anisotropy arises because the carbon fibers form continuous, aligned conductive pathways along the print direction, while in the perpendicular direction, the fibers are separated by insulating resin regions, creating a highly resistive path. This level of control over electrical transport enables the design of printed structures with embedded conductive traces, electromagnetic shielding regions, or thermal management channels without the need for additional metallic components or post-processing steps. The invention thus enables the fabrication of multifunctional composite structures that integrate structural integrity with electrical, thermal, or sensing functionality in a single, monolithic component.

The invention further enables the fabrication of complex, free-form geometries that are impossible to achieve using conventional manufacturing techniques. Structures such as di-parabolic rocket nozzles, internal cooling channels, topology-optimized lattices, and biomimetic architectures can be printed in a single build, with local variations in fiber loading, print direction, and layer thickness used to tailor the mechanical and physical properties of each region. For example, a nozzle tip may be printed with a higher fiber loading and unidirectional alignment to maximize stiffness and thermal resistance, while the surrounding structure may be printed with lower fiber loading and alternating orientations to enhance impact resistance and reduce weight. The ability to vary these parameters within a single part represents a paradigm shift in composite design, moving from the use of homogeneous materials to the creation of architectured materials with spatially graded properties. The invention is scalable to industrial production through the use of multi-nozzle arrays, synchronized deposition systems, and integrated curing chambers, and is compatible with existing additive manufacturing platforms, enabling the transition from laboratory prototypes to high-volume manufacturing.

The invention is not limited to the specific materials and geometries described herein. The underlying principles—rheological stabilization of high-aspect-ratio fibers in a thermoset matrix through colloidal modification, shear-induced alignment during microextrusion, and latent thermal curing—are broadly applicable to other fiber-matrix combinations. For example, aramid fibers may be substituted for carbon fibers in applications requiring high impact resistance and flame retardancy, while glass fibers may be used in cost-sensitive applications where electrical conductivity is not required. The resin matrix may be replaced with cyanate esters, phenolics, or bismaleimides to achieve higher thermal stability or improved chemical resistance. The nozzle geometry may be optimized for different fiber lengths, aspect ratios, or viscosities using the predictive modeling framework described herein. The curing protocol may be adjusted for different catalyst systems, such as imidazole-based or metal-organic latent catalysts, to achieve different cure temperatures and kinetics. The invention thus provides a foundational technology for the next generation of additively manufactured composites, enabling the design and production of materials with unprecedented levels of control, functionality, and performance.
