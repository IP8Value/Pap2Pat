# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of structural optimization and computational design, specifically to a method and system for generating fail-safe structural topologies through topology optimization under uncertainty. The invention provides a computationally tractable framework for enforcing structural redundancy and resilience against localized material loss, particularly in three-dimensional load-bearing components subjected to critical service conditions. The method is particularly applicable to aerospace, automotive, and heavy machinery industries where catastrophic failure due to localized damage must be precluded by design. The invention introduces a novel damage population modeling technique that enables the simulation of random, arbitrary material removal events—representing potential failure modes such as member fracture, crack propagation, or impact-induced voids—without requiring an intractable number of finite element analyses. By leveraging a hierarchical, geometrically structured sequence of damage zones, the invention achieves a balance between fidelity of failure representation and computational feasibility, allowing engineers to generate optimized structures that inherently satisfy fail-safe criteria as defined by industry standards for structural integrity under partial or complete loss of discrete load-carrying elements. The system is implemented within commercial finite element analysis software and is compatible with standard topology optimization formulations, including compliance minimization under volume constraints, while supporting advanced constraints such as minimum length scale, manufacturing directionality, and stress limits.

## BACKGROUND

Structural design has long been guided by principles of efficiency, weight reduction, and material economy, particularly in industries where performance is directly tied to mass and stiffness. Topology optimization, since its formal introduction in the late 1980s, has revolutionized the way engineers conceptualize load paths, enabling the automatic generation of highly efficient, often truss-like structures that minimize compliance under prescribed loading conditions. However, these optimized designs frequently exhibit minimal redundancy, rendering them vulnerable to localized failures. A single cracked member, a fractured fastener, or an impact-induced void can lead to catastrophic collapse, despite the structure appearing optimal under nominal conditions. This vulnerability is particularly acute in safety-critical applications such as aircraft, spacecraft, and high-speed rail components, where the consequences of failure are not merely economic but existential.

Historically, fail-safe design has been an afterthought in structural optimization. Traditional approaches to ensuring structural resilience have relied on empirical rules, conservative safety factors, or post-optimization reinforcement—methods that are neither systematic nor optimal. The concept of fail-safe, as codified in aerospace engineering standards, requires that a structure maintain its load-carrying capacity at or above 80% of the limit load even after the complete loss of any single discrete member or the partial loss of material in monolithic regions. This requirement is not merely probabilistic; it is deterministic and exhaustive, demanding that every possible failure mode be accounted for in the design process. Yet, in the context of topology optimization, where structural members do not exist a priori but emerge iteratively from a continuous density field, defining what constitutes a “member” for failure testing becomes a fundamental challenge.

Prior attempts to incorporate fail-safe considerations into topology optimization have been limited by two critical shortcomings. First, many approaches have treated failure as a binary event occurring at predefined locations, such as element centers, without establishing a rigorous mathematical connection to the physical reality of structural member failure. Second, and more significantly, existing methods have required the analysis of a number of failure scenarios equal to the number of finite elements in the model—often exceeding hundreds of thousands—which renders the optimization computationally infeasible for real-world three-dimensional problems. Even with parallel computing, the exponential growth in analysis burden with mesh refinement has prevented practical adoption.

One notable prior effort introduced the concept of removing square-shaped voids at every finite element location to simulate material loss. While this approach captured the essence of localized failure, it did not address the core dilemma: how to define a failure test that is valid for structural members that have not yet been identified. Furthermore, the method’s reliance on mesh-aligned damage zones led to an excessive number of required analyses, rendering it unsuitable for industrial applications. The absence of a systematic method to reduce the number of failure scenarios while preserving the integrity of the fail-safe requirement has left a critical gap in the field.

The present invention resolves these limitations by introducing a mathematically grounded, geometrically structured damage population model that accurately represents the infinite set of possible failure locations through a finite, scalable sequence of damage zones. Unlike prior approaches that treat failure as an arbitrary grid of voids, this invention defines damage as a cube-shaped region of material removal whose placement follows a hierarchical, space-filling sequence. The method establishes a direct relationship between the size of the damage region and the maximum allowable cross-sectional dimension of structural members, thereby ensuring that any member smaller than the damage size is subject to failure testing. By introducing a partial damage population that targets only the geometrically most vulnerable regions—those where material can survive the base layer of damage—the invention achieves a dramatic reduction in computational cost while maintaining or even improving the fidelity of the fail-safe constraint. This innovation transforms fail-safe topology optimization from a theoretical curiosity into a practical engineering tool, enabling the design of structures that are not only lightweight and efficient but inherently resilient to the unpredictable nature of real-world damage.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a method and system for generating fail-safe structural topologies using topology optimization, wherein the structure is designed to maintain structural integrity under the complete loss of any discrete load-carrying member or the partial loss of material in monolithic regions. The method comprises defining a damage region of predetermined geometric shape and size within a design domain, and establishing a hierarchical sequence of damage populations that collectively simulate the infinite set of possible failure locations with finite computational effort. The damage region is modeled as a cube-shaped void of edge length d, representing the minimum size of material loss that would compromise a structural member. A base damage population, designated PA₁, is generated by placing non-overlapping damage cubes in a gapless, grid-aligned arrangement that fully occupies the design domain, ensuring that no point within the domain is unexposed to at least one damage event. A partial enhancement damage population, designated PB₂, is then constructed by placing additional damage cubes at the geometric centers of the junctions formed by adjacent PA₁ damage cubes, thereby targeting the most vulnerable regions where material might otherwise survive the base population. The total number of damage scenarios analyzed is thus limited to the sum of PA₁ and PB₂, which is at most twice the size of PA₁, regardless of the mesh density of the finite element model.

The optimization problem is formulated as a minimization of the maximum compliance across all structural configurations resulting from the removal of each damage cube in the combined population PA₁ ∪ PB₂, subject to a volume constraint on the total material. The structural response under each damage configuration is computed independently using finite element analysis, with elements contained within the damage zone removed from the stiffness matrix. Sensitivity information is computed for each configuration and aggregated by the optimizer to update the material density field iteratively. The method further incorporates a maximum length scale constraint, such that the largest cross-sectional dimension of any structural member is limited to d/2, ensuring that no member can survive a damage event without being fully intersected by at least one damage cube. This constraint guarantees that the fail-safe requirement is satisfied in a mathematically rigorous sense: any member smaller than the damage size is guaranteed to be fully removed by at least one damage cube in the population, and any monolithic region thicker than d/2 is guaranteed to be perforated by a through-hole under the same population.

The invention is implemented within a commercial finite element analysis software platform using a Message Passing Interface (MPI) parallel architecture, wherein each damage scenario is analyzed concurrently on a dedicated computing process. The master process orchestrates the optimization loop, collecting compliance and sensitivity data from all slave processes and updating the design variables. The system automatically generates the damage population based on user-defined damage size and design domain geometry, excludes damage cubes that intersect point loads to preserve boundary conditions, and terminates the optimization if any damage scenario causes compliance to exceed a user-defined threshold, indicating insufficient redundancy. The method supports standard topology optimization formulations, including SIMP-based material interpolation, and is compatible with additional constraints such as symmetry, manufacturing directionality, and stress limits. The result is a structural topology that is simultaneously lightweight, efficient, and inherently fail-safe, with redundant load paths that ensure survival under the complete loss of any single structural element or localized material defect.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention provides a systematic and computationally viable method for generating structural topologies that satisfy fail-safe requirements under the assumption of arbitrary, localized material loss. The core innovation lies in the transformation of an otherwise intractable infinite-dimensional failure space into a finite, geometrically structured set of damage scenarios that collectively ensure complete coverage of all possible failure modes. This is achieved through the introduction of a hierarchical damage population model, which is grounded in geometric analysis and validated through numerical simulation. The method begins with the definition of a design domain, a region of space within which material may be distributed to form a load-bearing structure. This domain is discretized into a finite element mesh, typically composed of tetrahedral or hexahedral elements, with each element assigned a normalized material density variable ranging from zero (void) to one (solid). The optimization objective is to minimize the maximum compliance across all structural configurations induced by the removal of damage zones, subject to a volume constraint on the total material.

The damage zone is defined as a cube-shaped region of predetermined edge length d, representing the smallest size of material loss that would constitute a critical failure event. This cube is not a physical object but a mathematical construct used to simulate the complete removal of material within its volume. The orientation of the cube is fixed relative to the global coordinate system, and its placement is constrained to lie entirely within the design domain. The key insight of the invention is that the infinite set of possible damage locations—any point within the domain where a cube of size d could be centered—is not required to be sampled exhaustively. Instead, a finite set of damage cube placements, carefully chosen based on geometric principles, can guarantee that no structural member smaller than d/2 in any cross-sectional dimension can survive without being fully intersected by at least one damage cube. This guarantee is established through the construction of two damage populations: PA₁ and PB₂.

PA₁, the base damage population, consists of a gapless, grid-aligned arrangement of damage cubes that completely fill the design domain without overlap. The cubes are placed such that their centers are spaced exactly at intervals of d along each Cartesian axis, forming a cubic lattice that partitions the domain into uniform cells. In this configuration, every point within the domain lies within the volume of at least one damage cube, and no region of material is left unexposed. The number of damage cubes in PA₁ is determined solely by the dimensions of the design domain and the size of the damage cube, independent of the finite element mesh density. For example, in a domain of dimensions Lₓ × Lᵧ × L_z, the number of cubes in PA₁ is given by the integer ceiling of Lₓ/d multiplied by Lᵧ/d and L_z/d. This population ensures that any structural member with a cross-sectional dimension less than d will be intersected by at least one damage cube, thereby subjecting it to failure testing. However, geometric analysis reveals that in this configuration, there exist regions—specifically, the corners and edges where eight damage cubes meet—where a representative cube of size d can be positioned such that a significant portion of material survives. The volumetric survival rate in these regions is approximately 12.5%, and the sectional survival rate—critical for load transfer—is approximately 25% along each axis. These regions represent potential loopholes in the failure test, where material may be strategically placed to evade damage.

To close these loopholes without doubling the computational burden, the invention introduces PB₂, a partial enhancement population. PB₂ consists of damage cubes placed at the geometric centers of the junctions formed by adjacent PA₁ cubes. These junctions correspond to the points where eight PA₁ cubes meet, and the centers of these junctions are located at offsets of d/2 along each axis relative to the PA₁ grid. The number of such junctions is equal to the number of internal nodes in the PA₁ grid, which is approximately one-eighth the number of PA₁ cubes in a fully enclosed domain. Thus, PB₂ adds no more than an additional 12.5% of the PA₁ population, resulting in a total damage population size of at most 112.5% of PA₁. Crucially, these PB₂ cubes are positioned precisely where the maximum material survival occurs under PA₁ alone. When combined, PA₁ and PB₂ ensure that no representative cube of size d can be placed anywhere within the domain without intersecting at least one damage cube in such a way that the material survival rate drops to zero. This is not an approximation; it is a geometric certainty derived from the spatial arrangement of the cubes. The combination of PA₁ and PB₂ thus achieves a level of failure coverage that is equivalent to an infinite damage population, provided that the maximum cross-sectional dimension of any structural member is constrained to d/2.

The optimization problem is formulated as a minimax problem: minimize the maximum compliance across all structural configurations induced by the removal of each damage cube in PA₁ ∪ PB₂. Each configuration is treated as an independent finite element model, where elements whose centroids lie within a damage cube are removed from the stiffness matrix, and their contributions to load resistance are nullified. The compliance for each configuration is computed as the dot product of the displacement vector and the applied load vector under static equilibrium. The optimizer seeks to minimize the highest compliance among all configurations, effectively ensuring that the worst-case failure scenario is as benign as possible. The sensitivity of compliance with respect to each design variable is computed using the adjoint method, and the gradients from all damage configurations are aggregated to form a composite sensitivity field that guides the density update. The material interpolation follows the Solid Isotropic Material with Penalization (SIMP) model, where the elastic modulus of each element is interpolated as a power law of its density, with a penalization exponent typically between 3 and 4 to promote binary solutions. A lower bound on density, typically 0.01, is enforced to prevent numerical singularities.

The implementation of the method leverages the inherent parallelism of the problem. Since each damage configuration is independent—no shared degrees of freedom, no coupling between models—the analysis and sensitivity computation for each configuration can be performed simultaneously on separate computing processes. The invention is implemented within a commercial finite element software platform using a Message Passing Interface (MPI) architecture, wherein a master process coordinates the optimization loop and a number of slave processes, equal to the total number of damage cubes plus one for the undamaged baseline, perform the finite element analyses. Each slave process is assigned a unique damage configuration and computes the compliance and sensitivity for its assigned model. The master process collects these results, identifies the maximum compliance, computes the aggregate sensitivity, updates the design variables using a gradient-based optimizer such as Method of Moving Asymptotes (MMA), and broadcasts the updated densities to all slave processes for the next iteration. This architecture enables the solution of problems with hundreds of damage scenarios in a time comparable to a single baseline optimization, provided sufficient computing resources are available.

To ensure physical realism and numerical stability, several practical constraints are enforced. Damage cubes that intersect point loads or prescribed displacements are automatically excluded from the population to preserve boundary conditions. Distributed loads that are partially removed by a damage cube are retained with reduced magnitude, as this reflects realistic load redistribution. A threshold on the material volume within a damage cube is applied to reduce the population size: if the total material volume within a candidate damage cube is below a specified percentage—typically 10%—the cube is omitted, as its removal would not meaningfully affect structural performance. Additionally, for PB₂ cubes, only those entirely contained within the design domain are included, avoiding partial overlaps with domain boundaries. The optimization terminates if any damage configuration causes compliance to increase by more than a user-defined factor—typically ten times the undamaged compliance—indicating that the structure lacks sufficient redundancy to sustain any failure mode. This safeguard prevents the generation of designs that are optimized for nominal conditions but are catastrophically fragile under failure.

The method further supports the imposition of a maximum length scale constraint, which limits the largest cross-sectional dimension of any structural member to d/2. This constraint is implemented using established techniques such as projection filters, Helmholtz-type smoothing, or morphological operations, ensuring that no structural feature larger than half the damage size can emerge. This constraint is not merely a practical expedient; it is a necessary condition for the mathematical guarantee of fail-safe behavior. Without it, a member of size d could theoretically be placed entirely within the intersection of multiple damage cubes and survive, violating the fail-safe principle. With it, any member is guaranteed to be intersected by at least one damage cube in the combined PA₁ and PB₂ population, ensuring that no discrete member can evade failure testing. This constraint also prevents the formation of thick, monolithic plates that could otherwise survive damage by virtue of their bulk, ensuring that all load paths are distributed through multiple, slender members that are subject to the full force of the damage population.

The resulting topologies exhibit a distinct structural character: they are rich in redundancy, featuring multiple parallel load paths, interconnected truss-like elements, and strategically placed stiffening ribs that ensure load redistribution in the event of member loss. The designs are not merely robust; they are inherently fail-safe, with no single point of vulnerability. The optimization process naturally evolves these features without the need for manual intervention or heuristic rules. The method is scalable to complex three-dimensional geometries, as demonstrated in a control arm example with over 300,000 elements, where the damage population consisted of only 73 cubes—less than 0.02% of the number of elements—compared to over 200,000 in prior methods. The computational efficiency of the method makes it suitable for industrial deployment, where turnaround time and resource utilization are critical. The output is a density field that can be post-processed into a smooth iso-surface at a threshold of 0.3 to 0.5, yielding a manufacturable design that retains the fail-safe characteristics of the optimized topology.

The invention is not limited to compliance minimization. While this formulation was used for clarity and reproducibility, the same framework can be extended to stress-constrained optimization, displacement limits, frequency constraints, or multi-objective formulations. The damage population model is agnostic to the objective function; it only requires that the structural response under each damage configuration be computable and differentiable. The method can also be adapted to non-cubic damage shapes, such as spheres or ellipsoids, by adjusting the population geometry accordingly. For spherical damage, the base population PA₁ would consist of a face-centered cubic arrangement, and PB₂ would be constructed at the voids between spheres. The same principles of geometric coverage and redundancy apply. The method can also be extended to dynamic loading, fatigue, or thermal failure modes by incorporating time-dependent analysis and damage evolution models into the failure scenarios. The core innovation—the hierarchical, geometrically optimized damage population—remains valid regardless of the specific failure mechanism being modeled.

## EXAMPLES

The efficacy and practicality of the invention are demonstrated through three distinct numerical examples, each illustrating the transformation of a conventional, non-redundant topology into a fail-safe design under the proposed damage population framework. The first example reproduces a two-dimensional cantilever plate subjected to shear loading, a problem previously studied in the literature to benchmark fail-safe topology optimization. The design domain is a rectangular plate measuring 100 units in length and 50 units in height, discretized into 20,000 quadratic quadrilateral elements. A vertical point load of magnitude 1000 is applied at the center of the bottom edge, while the top edge is fully fixed. The volume fraction constraint is set to 20% of the domain volume, and the damage cube size is defined as 25 units, corresponding to a quarter of the domain height. The base damage population PA₁ consists of eight damage cubes arranged in a 2 × 4 grid, while the partial enhancement PB₂ adds four additional cubes at the junctions between adjacent PA₁ cubes, resulting in a total of twelve damage scenarios. The optimization converges in 26 iterations for the standard case, 39 for PA₁, and 39 for PA₁ ∪ PB₂. The standard design yields a two-bar truss configuration, identical to the Michell truss, with a compliance of 58.72. The fail-safe design under PA₁ eliminates the central bar and introduces two additional diagonal members, forming a four-bar structure with redundant load paths. The addition of PB₂ further refines the topology, introducing a small central rib that bridges the gap between the diagonal members, ensuring that even the most vulnerable regions are reinforced. The maximum compliance under damage increases by approximately 120% compared to the undamaged state, a significant improvement over the 170% increase observed in the three-bar truss benchmark. The design is insensitive to the placement of the damage grid, as demonstrated by running the optimization with a shifted damage population, yielding nearly identical topologies when symmetry is enforced. The number of active damage zones—those that contribute to the maximum compliance within 2%—is four for PA₁ and five for PB₂, confirming that the failure modes are well distributed and not concentrated in a single critical location.

The second example revisits the cantilever plate problem studied by Jansen et al., but with a more realistic damage size and mesh resolution. The domain dimensions and loading conditions are identical, but the damage cube size is increased to 22 units, reducing the PA₁ population to just 26 cubes and PB₂ to 42. In contrast, Jansen et al.’s method required over 5,400 damage scenarios, a computational burden that would render the problem intractable on most modern HPC systems. The resulting topology under PA₁ is a truss-like structure with multiple load paths radiating from the loading point, while the PB₂-enhanced design adds a central web that prevents the formation of a single dominant load path. The maximum compliance under damage increases by approximately 150%, and the number of active damage zones is reduced to three, indicating that the structure is robust and not overly sensitive to any single failure location. When the mesh is refined to 43,200 elements, the topology remains unchanged, confirming mesh independence. The computational cost under the proposed method is less than 1% of that required by Jansen et al.’s approach, demonstrating a dramatic improvement in scalability. The design is not only fail-safe but also manufacturable, with clear, well-defined members that can be fabricated using conventional techniques such as additive manufacturing or casting.

The third example is a three-dimensional control arm, a complex structural component taken from a commercial finite element software tutorial. The design domain measures approximately 450 × 550 × 110 units and is discretized into 327,493 tetrahedral elements. Two load cases are applied: one representing pure bending and the other a combination of bending and torsion. The volume fraction constraint is set to 30%, and the damage cube size is defined as 50 units. The base damage population PA₁ consists of 45 cubes arranged in a 3 × 3 × 5 grid, while PB₂ adds 28 additional cubes at the junctions between adjacent PA₁ cubes, resulting in a total of 73 damage scenarios. The standard optimization yields a design with a single, slender load path leading to the bearing points, with a compliance of 162.3. The fail-safe design under PA₁ ∪ PB₂ introduces a central rib spanning the width of the arm and thickens the regions surrounding the two vertical bearings, creating multiple parallel load paths that ensure redundancy. The maximum compliance under damage increases to 756.8, nearly four times the undamaged value, but this increase is distributed across four active damage zones, all located near the bearings. This result provides clear design insight: the bearing regions are the critical failure points, and additional support must be provided in their vicinity. The optimization does not merely generate a structure; it diagnoses its weaknesses and prescribes solutions. The computational cost under the proposed method is negligible compared to Jansen et al.’s approach, which would require over 200,000 damage scenarios for this mesh. The design is manufacturable under a single draw direction constraint, and the resulting topology exhibits the hallmarks of fail-safe engineering: redundancy, symmetry, and distributed load paths.

In all examples, the optimization converges within fewer than 50 iterations, and the final topologies are stable and repeatable across multiple runs. The use of a maximum length scale constraint of d/2 ensures that no member exceeds the critical size that could evade damage. The MPI implementation enables the solution of the 73-scenario control arm problem in less than 12 hours on a 128-core cluster, a turnaround time comparable to a single baseline optimization. The method is not sensitive to the initial design, the optimizer settings, or the penalty exponent, as long as the damage population is properly constructed. The results confirm that the invention achieves the dual objectives of computational efficiency and fail-safe fidelity, transforming what was previously an intractable theoretical problem into a practical engineering tool. The designs generated are not merely optimized—they are resilient, robust, and inherently safe, embodying the principles of fail-safe engineering in a way that was previously unattainable through automated design methods.
