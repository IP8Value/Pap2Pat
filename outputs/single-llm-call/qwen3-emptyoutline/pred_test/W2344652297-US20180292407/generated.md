# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to a novel method for the isolation and molecular characterization of antigen-specific plasma cells from fixed and intracellularly stained biological samples, and for the subsequent generation of high-affinity monoclonal antibodies against complex and challenging antigens, including post-translationally modified proteins such as phosphorylated peptides. More specifically, the invention provides a systematic, reproducible, and highly sensitive technique for the detection, sorting, and single-cell molecular analysis of rare antigen-specific plasma cells that express immunoglobulins with exquisite specificity for target epitopes, even when those epitopes are present in minute quantities within a heterogeneous cell population. The method is particularly advantageous for the isolation of plasma cells producing antibodies against phosphorylation site-specific epitopes, splice variant-specific domains, or other structurally similar but functionally distinct protein variants that are indistinguishable by conventional antibody screening methods. The invention further encompasses the integration of chemical fixation, intracellular antigen staining, enzymatic reversal of fixation-induced crosslinking, and single-cell cDNA synthesis and amplification to enable the recovery of full-length immunoglobulin variable region genes from fixed cells without loss of sequence fidelity or functional integrity. This approach overcomes longstanding limitations in monoclonal antibody discovery, particularly the inability to isolate rare antigen-specific B cells from immunized animals when the target antigen is poorly immunogenic, intracellular, or subject to post-translational modification. The method is applicable across species, including but not limited to mice, rats, rabbits, guinea pigs, and non-human primates, and is compatible with a wide range of chemically synthesized antigens, including biotinylated peptides, phosphopeptides, and other small molecule haptens. The resulting monoclonal antibodies exhibit exceptional specificity, high affinity, and utility in diagnostic, therapeutic, and research applications, including immunohistochemistry, Western blotting, flow cytometry, and targeted protein detection in complex biological matrices.

## BACKGROUND ART

The production of monoclonal antibodies against specific protein epitopes has long been a cornerstone of biomedical research and therapeutic development. Conventional methods for generating monoclonal antibodies typically involve immunizing an animal with a purified antigen, followed by the fusion of splenic or lymph node B cells with immortalized myeloma cells to generate hybridomas, which are then screened for antigen-specific antibody production. While effective for many targets, this approach is fundamentally limited in its ability to isolate antibodies against antigens that are poorly immunogenic, intracellular, or exist in multiple isoforms with high sequence homology. In particular, the generation of antibodies that distinguish between post-translationally modified forms of a protein—such as phosphorylated, acetylated, methylated, or ubiquitinated variants—has proven exceptionally challenging. This is because the immune system often generates a polyclonal response against the unmodified peptide backbone, and the modified epitope may be presented in such low abundance that antigen-specific plasma cells are exceedingly rare within the total lymphocyte population. Traditional methods for isolating such rare cells, such as fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) of live cells stained with fluorescently labeled antigens, suffer from low signal-to-noise ratios, high background binding, and the inherent instability of intracellular antigens during cell handling. Furthermore, live-cell staining methods are incompatible with the use of chemically synthesized peptides, which are often required for precise epitope targeting, due to their inability to penetrate the plasma membrane and their susceptibility to degradation or non-specific binding in the extracellular environment.

Alternative approaches have attempted to circumvent these limitations by using permeabilized cells to access intracellular immunoglobulins. However, such methods have historically failed to yield reliable results due to the degradation of RNA during the permeabilization process, which renders subsequent molecular cloning of immunoglobulin variable regions impossible. Even when RNA is preserved, the fixation methods traditionally employed—such as methanol, ethanol, or glutaraldehyde—either destroy antigen-binding conformation or induce irreversible crosslinking between RNA and cellular proteins, preventing efficient reverse transcription and polymerase chain reaction amplification. While paraformaldehyde fixation has been shown to preserve protein epitopes and antigen-binding activity, it also induces extensive RNA-protein crosslinks and mono-methylol modifications that severely impair the efficiency of reverse transcriptase enzymes, limiting successful amplification to short cDNA fragments of less than 100 base pairs. This has rendered the recovery of full-length immunoglobulin heavy and light chain variable region genes from fixed, intracellularly stained single cells an unsolved problem in the field. Moreover, existing methods lack the ability to perform subtractive staining to eliminate plasma cells that bind to undesired epitopes, such as the unmodified form of a phosphopeptide or the closely related isoform of a splice variant. As a result, the isolation of monoclonal antibodies with absolute specificity for a single post-translational modification—such as phosphorylation at a single amino acid residue—has remained an elusive goal, despite its critical importance in studying signaling pathways, disease biomarkers, and therapeutic targets.

Prior art has described methods for single-cell antibody gene recovery from live cells, including the use of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) trackers to identify plasma cells based on their high secretory activity. However, these methods rely on the expression of cell surface markers or intracellular organelle markers that are not universally present in all plasma cell subsets and are subject to variability due to cell activation state, cell cycle phase, or tissue microenvironment. Furthermore, live-cell staining requires the use of antigens that can be delivered extracellularly, which precludes the use of synthetic peptides that are not membrane-permeable. The resulting antibodies often lack the desired specificity, as the immunogen may contain impurities or non-native conformations. Additionally, the low frequency of antigen-specific plasma cells in immunized animals—often less than 0.01% of the total lymphocyte population—demands a method with exceptional sensitivity and precision, which existing techniques have failed to achieve. Attempts to combine fixation with enzymatic reversal of crosslinks have been explored in the context of chromatin immunoprecipitation and nucleic acid recovery from fixed tissues, but these protocols have never been adapted to the specific requirements of single-cell antibody gene recovery, where the preservation of both antigen-binding conformation and RNA integrity must be simultaneously maintained. There is therefore a critical and unmet need in the field for a method that enables the isolation of rare antigen-specific plasma cells from fixed and intracellularly stained samples, the reversal of fixation-induced RNA-protein crosslinks without compromising antigen integrity, and the subsequent amplification of full-length immunoglobulin variable region genes from single cells to enable the production of monoclonal antibodies with unprecedented specificity and affinity.

## SUMMARY OF INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and highly effective method for the isolation and molecular characterization of antigen-specific plasma cells from fixed biological samples, enabling the generation of monoclonal antibodies against challenging antigens, including post-translationally modified proteins, splice variants, and other structurally similar epitopes that are inaccessible to conventional antibody discovery platforms. The method integrates a sequence of precisely controlled steps—chemical fixation, intracellular antigen staining, enzymatic reversal of fixation-induced crosslinking, single-cell isolation, and full-length immunoglobulin variable region amplification—that collectively overcome the longstanding technical barriers to isolating rare antigen-specific plasma cells and recovering their cognate antibody genes. The invention is based on the unexpected discovery that paraformaldehyde fixation, when applied under optimized conditions, preserves the antigen-binding conformation of intracellular immunoglobulins while simultaneously enabling the subsequent enzymatic reversal of RNA-protein crosslinks through proteolytic digestion, thereby permitting the recovery of intact, amplifiable RNA from single plasma cells. This finding contradicts prior assumptions that paraformaldehyde fixation irreversibly compromises RNA integrity and renders single-cell molecular analysis impossible. The method further incorporates a subtractive staining strategy that allows for the simultaneous exclusion of plasma cells binding to undesired epitopes, such as unmodified peptides or non-target isoforms, thereby enriching for cells producing antibodies with absolute specificity for the target epitope. This dual-staining approach enables the isolation of plasma cells that recognize a single phosphorylation site within a peptide sequence, even when those cells constitute less than 0.01% of the total lymphocyte population. The invention further includes a streamlined, automated single-cell processing system that integrates cell lysis, reverse crosslinking, mRNA purification, and cDNA synthesis into a single, closed microfluidic workflow, minimizing contamination and maximizing reproducibility. The resulting monoclonal antibodies exhibit dissociation constants in the sub-nanomolar range, demonstrate complete specificity for their intended epitope in the presence of closely related variants, and are fully functional in downstream applications such as Western blotting, immunofluorescence, and flow cytometry. The method is broadly applicable across species and antigen types, and represents a transformative advance in the field of antibody discovery, enabling the generation of high-quality monoclonal antibodies against targets previously considered intractable.

### Solution to Problem

The primary problem addressed by the invention is the inability of existing methods to isolate rare antigen-specific plasma cells from fixed biological samples and to recover full-length immunoglobulin variable region genes from those cells for the purpose of generating monoclonal antibodies with exquisite specificity for post-translationally modified epitopes, splice variants, or other structurally similar antigens. This problem arises from three interrelated technical barriers: (1) the destruction of RNA integrity during intracellular staining due to membrane permeabilization and exposure to endogenous RNases; (2) the irreversible crosslinking of RNA to cellular proteins by paraformaldehyde fixation, which prevents efficient reverse transcription and PCR amplification; and (3) the absence of a reliable method to subtractively enrich for plasma cells that bind only to the desired epitope while excluding those that bind to related, non-target epitopes. The solution provided by the invention lies in the sequential and synergistic application of four key innovations. First, the use of a precisely timed paraformaldehyde fixation protocol at low temperature preserves the tertiary structure of intracellular immunoglobulins while minimizing RNA degradation, allowing for subsequent intracellular staining with fluorescently labeled antigens. Second, the introduction of a protease K-mediated reversal of fixation-induced crosslinks at an elevated temperature effectively liberates RNA from protein adducts without compromising the antigen-binding activity of the immunoglobulins, thereby enabling the recovery of amplifiable cDNA. Third, the implementation of a dual-fluorescent staining strategy—wherein cells are simultaneously stained with a labeled target antigen and a labeled non-target antigen—allows for the precise identification and isolation of plasma cells that bind exclusively to the desired epitope, effectively eliminating background and cross-reactive cells. Fourth, the integration of a microscale, automated single-cell processing system that combines magnetic bead-based mRNA capture, homopolymer tailing, and 5′ RACE PCR amplification enables the robust recovery of full-length immunoglobulin heavy and light chain variable region genes from individual sorted cells. Together, these innovations solve the longstanding problem of isolating rare, epitope-specific plasma cells and converting their antibody genes into functional monoclonal antibodies with unprecedented specificity and affinity, even when the target antigen is a single phosphorylation site within a protein sequence.

## DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENT

### (1) Fixing Step

The fixing step is a critical and highly optimized component of the method, designed to preserve the structural integrity of intracellular immunoglobulins while minimizing RNA degradation and maintaining the capacity for subsequent enzymatic reversal of fixation-induced crosslinks. The fixation process employs paraformaldehyde as the sole fixative, applied at a concentration of approximately 1.5% to 2.5% in phosphate-buffered saline, with a preferred concentration of 2%. The fixation is performed at a temperature between 0°C and 8°C, with optimal results achieved at 4°C, to slow down enzymatic activity and reduce non-specific protein aggregation. The duration of fixation is carefully controlled and ranges from 5 to 20 minutes, with a preferred duration of 8 minutes, to ensure complete penetration of the fixative into the cytoplasm without inducing excessive crosslinking that would render RNA irrecoverable. Longer fixation times, such as 30 minutes, result in a significant reduction in the efficiency of full-length variable gene amplification, whereas shorter times, such as 5 minutes, may lead to incomplete fixation and loss of antigen-binding conformation. The fixation solution is prepared fresh immediately prior to use, using high-purity paraformaldehyde that has been dissolved in warm phosphate-buffered saline and cooled to 4°C prior to application. The cell suspension is mixed gently with the fixation solution to ensure uniform exposure, and the reaction is quenched by the addition of ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline containing 1% bovine serum albumin to prevent over-fixation. Following fixation, cells are centrifuged at low speed, typically between 500 and 800 × g, for 2 to 5 minutes at 4°C to pellet the cells without inducing lysis. The supernatant is carefully removed, and the cell pellet is resuspended in a minimal volume of buffer to minimize dilution and maximize the efficiency of subsequent staining. The fixation protocol is validated by confirming the retention of intracellular immunoglobulin staining with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies, which demonstrates that the antigen-binding sites remain accessible and intact. Importantly, fixation with other agents such as methanol, ethanol, acetone, or glutaraldehyde is explicitly excluded from the method, as these agents either denature immunoglobulins or induce irreversible crosslinking that prevents both antigen binding and RNA recovery. The fixation step is performed under RNase-free conditions, with all reagents and equipment treated with diethylpyrocarbonate or subjected to autoclaving to eliminate exogenous RNase contamination. The fixation protocol is compatible with a wide range of cell types, including hybridomas, primary B cells, plasma cells isolated from lymph nodes, spleens, or bone marrow, and even cells derived from non-mammalian species, provided that the fixation time and concentration are adjusted to account for differences in cell membrane composition and cytoplasmic volume. The success of the fixation step is quantified by the proportion of cells that retain strong intracellular immunoglobulin signal after staining and by the efficiency of subsequent full-length variable gene amplification, which must exceed 70% in control experiments to validate the integrity of the fixation protocol.

### (2) Cell Membrane Lysis Step

Following fixation, the cell membrane is selectively permeabilized to allow the entry of fluorescently labeled antigens into the cytoplasm, where they can bind to intracellular immunoglobulins. This step is performed under strictly controlled conditions to ensure that permeabilization is sufficient to permit antigen access while minimizing RNA degradation and preserving the conformational integrity of the immunoglobulins. The permeabilization is achieved through the use of a non-ionic detergent, specifically Triton X-100, at a final concentration of 0.05% to 0.2%, with a preferred concentration of 0.1%. The detergent is incorporated into a staining buffer composed of phosphate-buffered saline supplemented with 0.1% bovine serum albumin, 0.01% sodium azide, and 400 units per milliliter of RNaseOUT inhibitor to protect RNA from endogenous and exogenous RNases. The permeabilization is carried out simultaneously with the staining step, eliminating the need for a separate permeabilization incubation that could lead to RNA loss. The staining solution is prepared immediately prior to use and contains the fluorescently labeled antigen, a fluorescently labeled secondary antibody specific for the host species of the immunoglobulin, and, in cases where subtractive staining is employed, a second fluorescently labeled antigen corresponding to an undesired epitope. The cells are resuspended in a small volume of staining solution, typically 25 to 100 microliters per 10^6 cells, and incubated on ice for 10 to 20 minutes, with a preferred duration of 15 minutes, to allow for complete diffusion of the antigens into the cytoplasm. During this time, the detergent disrupts the lipid bilayer of the plasma membrane without affecting the integrity of intracellular organelles such as the endoplasmic reticulum, where immunoglobulins are abundantly stored. The staining solution is formulated to be free of nucleases, and all reagents are treated with diethylpyrocarbonate or filtered through 0.22-micron filters to ensure RNase-free conditions. The use of alternative detergents such as saponin, digitonin, or Tween-20 is not preferred, as these agents either induce excessive membrane disruption or fail to provide uniform permeabilization across the cell population. The permeabilization step is validated by the uniformity of intracellular staining across the cell population and the absence of extracellular fluorescence, which would indicate incomplete washing or non-specific binding. After staining, the cells are diluted with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline containing 1 micromolar 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole to label nuclei and facilitate cell identification during flow cytometry. The stained cells are then kept on ice until sorting, and all subsequent handling is performed under RNase-free conditions to preserve RNA integrity. The combination of low detergent concentration, short incubation time, and inclusion of RNase inhibitors ensures that the RNA remains sufficiently intact for downstream amplification, even after exposure to the permeabilizing agent.

### (3) Staining Step

The staining step is a sophisticated, multi-component procedure that enables the simultaneous detection of antigen-specific plasma cells and the exclusion of non-specific or cross-reactive cells through the use of dual-fluorescent labeling. The core of this step involves the intracellular staining of fixed and permeabilized cells with a fluorescently labeled antigen that corresponds to the target epitope, along with a fluorescently labeled secondary antibody that binds to the constant region of the immunoglobulin, thereby serving as a universal marker for plasma cells. In addition, a second fluorescently labeled antigen is included to identify and exclude plasma cells that bind to undesired epitopes, such as the unmodified form of a phosphopeptide or a closely related protein isoform. The antigens used in this step are chemically synthesized peptides, typically 15 to 30 amino acids in length, that are biotinylated at either the N- or C-terminus to facilitate conjugation with streptavidin-linked fluorophores. The target antigen is conjugated with a fluorophore such as DyLight 488, and the non-target antigen is conjugated with a spectrally distinct fluorophore such as DyLight 550 or DyLight 650. The conjugation is performed under controlled conditions using commercially available labeling reagents, followed by purification through size-exclusion chromatography to remove unconjugated dye and ensure a consistent dye-to-protein ratio. The concentration of each labeled antigen is optimized to achieve a signal-to-noise ratio that maximizes the separation between positive and negative populations during flow cytometry, typically ranging from 0.1 to 1.0 micrograms per milliliter. The secondary antibody is conjugated with a fluorophore such as DyLight 650 or Alexa Fluor 647 and is used at a dilution of 1:250 to 1:500 to ensure strong, specific labeling of immunoglobulins without background staining. The staining solution also contains RNaseOUT, a recombinant ribonuclease inhibitor, at a concentration of 400 units per milliliter, to protect RNA from degradation during the staining process. The staining is performed in the dark at 4°C to prevent photobleaching and to maintain protein conformation. After staining, the cells are washed once with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline to remove unbound reagents and then resuspended in a buffer containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole to label nuclei and facilitate cell identification. The staining protocol is validated by the clear separation of distinct cell populations during flow cytometry: cells that are positive for the target antigen and negative for the non-target antigen are identified as the population of interest, while cells that are positive for both antigens are excluded as cross-reactive, and cells that are negative for the target antigen are excluded as non-specific. The use of DsRed as a marker for damaged or sticky cells is optionally included to further reduce background noise, particularly in samples with high levels of cellular debris. The staining step is performed under RNase-free conditions, with all reagents and equipment treated to eliminate exogenous RNase contamination. The success of the staining step is determined by the ability to isolate a discrete, low-frequency population of cells—often less than 0.01% of the total lymphocyte population—that exhibits high fluorescence for the target antigen, low fluorescence for the non-target antigen, and strong immunoglobulin staining. This population is then subjected to single-cell sorting for downstream molecular analysis.

### (4) Cell Isolation Step

The cell isolation step employs high-precision flow cytometry to sort individual cells based on their fluorescent staining profile, enabling the isolation of rare antigen-specific plasma cells from a heterogeneous population with exceptional purity and efficiency. The stained cell suspension is loaded into a flow cytometer equipped with a cell deposition unit capable of single-cell sorting into 96-well or 384-well plates, with each well pre-filled with a lysis buffer containing magnetic oligo(dT) beads and protease K. The flow cytometer is configured with multiple fluorescence detectors to simultaneously measure the intensity of the target antigen, the non-target antigen, the immunoglobulin-specific secondary antibody, and the nuclear stain, allowing for the precise definition of gating parameters that distinguish the desired cell population. The FL-1 channel is typically used to detect the target antigen, the FL-2 channel for the non-target antigen, the FL-5 channel for the immunoglobulin-specific secondary antibody, and the FL-7 channel for the nuclear stain. The threshold for the FL-2 channel is adjusted to minimize autofluorescence caused by fixation-induced background, and the compensation matrix is carefully calibrated to eliminate spectral overlap between fluorophores. The sorting strategy is designed to isolate cells that are positive for the target antigen, negative for the non-target antigen, and positive for immunoglobulin staining, while excluding cells that are positive for DsRed or exhibit high forward or side scatter, which are indicative of debris or dead cells. The sorting speed is maintained at a low rate, typically less than 100 events per second, to ensure high fidelity of single-cell deposition. Each sorted cell is deposited into a well containing 20 microliters of lysis buffer composed of Cell Lysis/Binding solution, 30 micrograms of Dynabeads Oligo(dT)25, and 100 micrograms per milliliter of protease K. The use of magnetic beads allows for the subsequent capture of polyadenylated mRNA, while the protease K ensures that RNA-protein crosslinks are reversed prior to cDNA synthesis. The sorting process is validated by the recovery of a discrete population of cells that correspond to the expected frequency of antigen-specific plasma cells, as determined by preliminary FACS analysis. The success of the isolation step is quantified by the percentage of wells that yield a single cell upon microscopic inspection and by the efficiency of subsequent full-length variable gene amplification. The isolation step is performed under sterile, RNase-free conditions, with the flow cytometer’s fluidics system rigorously cleaned between runs using 0.1% sodium hypochlorite followed by RNase-free water to prevent cross-contamination. The use of automated cell deposition ensures reproducibility and minimizes operator variability, making the method suitable for high-throughput applications. The isolation of cells at this stage is critical, as it enables the downstream molecular analysis to be performed on a clonal basis, ensuring that each recovered antibody gene originates from a single plasma cell and is therefore representative of a unique monoclonal antibody.

### (6) Antigen Specific Monoclonal Antibody Preparing Step

The antigen-specific monoclonal antibody preparing step encompasses the molecular cloning of immunoglobulin variable region genes from single sorted cells and their subsequent expression as functional recombinant antibodies. Following cell isolation, the 96-well plates containing sorted cells and lysis buffer are incubated at 50°C for 60 minutes to reverse the paraformaldehyde-induced RNA-protein crosslinks through proteolytic digestion by protease K. This step is essential for liberating RNA from the crosslinked matrix and enabling efficient reverse transcription. After incubation, the magnetic beads are concentrated using a magnet, and the supernatant is removed, leaving the beads bound to polyadenylated mRNA in a minimal volume of lysis buffer. The beads are then subjected to a homopolymer tailing reaction using terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase and dCTP to add a poly-C tail to the 3′ end of the mRNA, facilitating the subsequent amplification of the variable region genes by 5′ RACE PCR. The cDNA synthesis is performed using a reverse transcriptase enzyme and a primer complementary to the poly-C tail, which simultaneously primes the synthesis of cDNA and incorporates a universal sequence at the 5′ end of the cDNA molecule. The resulting cDNA is amplified using nested PCR primers specific to the constant region of the immunoglobulin heavy and light chains and a primer complementary to the universal sequence introduced during tailing. The amplified fragments are then cloned into linear expression cassettes using TS-jPCR, a technique that enables seamless joining of the variable region gene with a constant region cassette without the need for restriction enzymes or ligases. The resulting immunoglobulin heavy and light chain gene pairs are co-transfected into mammalian expression cells, such as Expi293F or 293FT cells, using a transfection reagent optimized for high-efficiency delivery. The transfected cells are cultured for three to five days, after which the supernatant is harvested and screened for antigen binding using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays with immobilized biotinylated peptides. Positive clones are further purified by protein A affinity chromatography, and the binding affinity of the resulting monoclonal antibodies is determined using surface plasmon resonance or bio-layer interferometry. The specificity of each antibody is validated by competition assays with non-phosphorylated or differently phosphorylated peptides, as well as by immunofluorescence and Western blotting in cells expressing the target protein or its mutants. Antibodies that exhibit high affinity (dissociation constant below 1 nM), complete specificity for the target epitope, and no cross-reactivity with related antigens are selected for further characterization and use. This step enables the rapid generation of monoclonal antibodies against targets that are otherwise inaccessible, including single phosphorylation sites, splice variants, and other post-translationally modified epitopes, with a success rate exceeding 80% in optimized conditions.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In this example, the method was applied to isolate antigen-specific plasma cells from the OKT10 hybridoma cell line, which produces an antibody against CD38. Hybridoma cells were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline for 8 minutes at 4°C, washed, and resuspended in a staining solution containing 0.1% Triton X-100, 0.1 μg/mL DyLight 488-conjugated CD38, 1:250 DyLight 650-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG, and 400 units/mL RNaseOUT. The cells were incubated on ice for 15 minutes, washed, and stained with 1 μM DAPI. Flow cytometry analysis revealed a distinct population of CD38-positive, IgG-positive cells, which were sorted into 96-well plates containing lysis buffer with magnetic oligo(dT) beads and 100 μg/mL protease K. The plates were incubated at 50°C for 1 hour, after which mRNA was captured, reverse transcribed, and amplified by 5′ RACE PCR. Full-length variable region genes for both the heavy and light chains were successfully amplified from 89% of the sorted single cells. The amplified genes were cloned into expression vectors and transfected into 293FT cells. Supernatants were screened by ELISA, and all 12 expressed antibodies bound specifically to CD38 with no cross-reactivity to other antigens. This example demonstrates the feasibility of the method for recovering full-length antibody genes from fixed hybridoma cells with high efficiency and fidelity.

### Example 2

In this example, the method was used to generate monoclonal antibodies against RORγt, a nuclear receptor that differs from its splice variant RORγ by only 21 amino acids at the N-terminus. Guinea pigs were immunized with a KLH-conjugated RORγt peptide, and lymph node cells were collected one week after the final immunization. Cells were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde for 8 minutes at 4°C, permeabilized, and stained with DyLight 488-conjugated RORγt peptide, DyLight 550-conjugated RORγ peptide, anti-guinea pig IgG, and DAPI. Flow cytometry revealed a discrete population of cells that were positive for RORγt, negative for RORγ, and positive for IgG, comprising less than 0.01% of the total lymphocyte population. These cells were sorted into 96-well plates containing lysis buffer with magnetic beads and protease K, incubated at 50°C for 1 hour, and processed for cDNA synthesis and 5′ RACE PCR. Full-length variable region genes were amplified from 85% of the sorted cells. The heavy and light chain genes were co-transfected into 293FT cells, and supernatants were screened by Western blot. Of the 20 expressed antibodies, 19 specifically recognized RORγt and showed no reactivity with RORγ, demonstrating the ability of the method to isolate antibodies with absolute specificity for a splice variant.

### Example 3

In this example, the method was applied to generate monoclonal antibodies against a triphosphorylated p53 peptide phosphorylated at serine 15, threonine 18, and serine 20. Guinea pigs were immunized with a biotinylated triphospho-p53 peptide conjugated to KLH. Lymph node cells were fixed, permeabilized, and stained with DyLight 488-conjugated triphospho-p53 peptide, DyLight 550-conjugated unmodified p53 peptide, anti-guinea pig IgG, and DAPI. Flow cytometry revealed a distinct population of cells that were positive for triphospho-p53, negative for unmodified p53, and positive for IgG, comprising less than 0.01% of the total lymphocytes. These cells were sorted into 96-well plates, processed as described, and full-length variable region genes were amplified from 82% of the sorted cells. The genes were expressed in 293FT cells, and 47 out of 48 antibodies (96%) specifically bound to the triphospho-p53 peptide and showed no reactivity with the unmodified peptide. One antibody, designated clone #23, was further characterized and shown to bind specifically to p53 phosphorylated at threonine 18, with no reactivity to peptides phosphorylated at serine 15 or serine 20. This antibody exhibited a dissociation constant of 0.2 nM and was able to detect endogenous p53 phosphorylated at T18 in etoposide-treated cells by immunofluorescence and Western blot, but not in cells treated with lambda protein phosphatase. This example demonstrates the method’s ability to isolate antibodies with exquisite specificity for a single phosphorylation site within a multi-phosphorylated epitope.

### Comparative Example 1 (Method in NPL 4)

In this comparative example, the method described in the prior art (NPL 4) was applied to the same triphospho-p53 immunized guinea pig lymph node cells. Live cells were stained with ER-tracker, triphospho-p53 peptide, unmodified p53 peptide, and anti-guinea pig IgG, followed by FACS sorting based on ER-tracker positivity and triphospho-p53 positivity. Single cells were sorted into lysis buffer and processed for cDNA synthesis and 5′ RACE PCR. Full-length variable region genes were amplified from only 34% of the sorted cells, and only 16% of the expressed antibodies showed specific binding to the triphospho-p53 peptide. The remaining antibodies showed cross-reactivity with the unmodified p53 peptide or failed to bind at all. This demonstrates that the live-cell staining method fails to effectively isolate rare, epitope-specific plasma cells and results in a high rate of non-specific or cross-reactive antibody production, in stark contrast to the high specificity and efficiency achieved by the present invention.

### Example 3 (Continued)

Following the isolation of clone #23, further characterization was performed to confirm its specificity for p53 phosphorylated at threonine 18. The antibody was tested against a panel of synthetic p53 peptides phosphorylated at various combinations of serine 15, threonine 18, and serine 20. Clone #23 showed strong binding only to the peptide phosphorylated at threonine 18, with no reactivity to peptides phosphorylated at serine 15 or serine 20 alone, or to the unmodified peptide. In immunofluorescence assays, clone #23 labeled the nuclei of etoposide-treated 293FT cells but not untreated cells. When cells were treated with lambda protein phosphatase, the signal was completely abolished, while staining with a pan-p53 antibody remained unchanged. Clone #23 also failed to label Saos-2 cells transfected with a p53 T18A mutant, whereas it strongly labeled cells transfected with wild-type p53. These results confirm that clone #23 is a monoclonal antibody with absolute specificity for p53 phosphorylated at threonine 18, a target previously unattainable by conventional methods.

### Example 4

In this example, the method was used to generate monoclonal antibodies against phosphorylated CHK2 at threonine 68. Rabbits were immunized with a biotinylated CHK2 peptide phosphorylated at T68. Lymph node cells were fixed, stained with DyLight 488-conjugated pT68-CHK2 peptide, DyLight 550-conjugated unphosphorylated CHK2 peptide, anti-rabbit IgG, and DAPI. Flow cytometry revealed a discrete population of cells that were positive for pT68-CHK2, negative for unphosphorylated CHK2, and positive for IgG. These cells were sorted, processed, and full-length variable region genes were amplified from 88% of the sorted cells. Of the 17 expressed antibodies, one, designated clone #34, exhibited complete specificity for pT68-CHK2 and showed no reactivity with unphosphorylated CHK2 or other phosphorylated forms of the protein. Clone #34 exhibited a dissociation constant of 0.08 nM toward the pT68 peptide and successfully detected endogenous phosphorylated CHK2 in cells treated with DNA-damaging agents. This example demonstrates the reproducibility and broad applicability of the method for generating high-affinity, epitope-specific monoclonal antibodies against phosphorylation sites in diverse proteins.

## INDUSTRIAL APPLICABILITY

The method disclosed herein has substantial industrial applicability across multiple sectors of biotechnology, pharmaceuticals, diagnostics, and academic research. In the pharmaceutical industry, the ability to generate monoclonal antibodies against post-translationally modified epitopes—such as phosphorylation sites, acetylated lysines, or methylated arginines—enables the development of novel therapeutics targeting disease-specific signaling pathways in cancer, autoimmune disorders, and neurodegenerative diseases. For example, antibodies against phosphorylated p53 at threonine 18 or phosphorylated CHK2 at threonine 68 can be used to develop diagnostic assays for DNA damage response status in tumors, or as therapeutic agents to selectively inhibit oncogenic signaling in cells with aberrant kinase activity. In the diagnostics sector, the method enables the production of highly specific detection reagents for use in clinical assays, including immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry, and ELISA, where cross-reactivity with unmodified or differently modified forms of a protein can lead to false positives or misdiagnosis. The method is particularly valuable for the development of companion diagnostics for kinase inhibitors, where the presence or absence of a specific phosphorylation event is a biomarker for drug response. In academic research, the method provides a powerful tool for studying post-translational modifications at the single-cell level, enabling the discovery of novel epitopes and the generation of custom antibodies for functional studies without reliance on commercial vendors or lengthy immunization protocols. The method is scalable, automated, and compatible with high-throughput screening platforms, making it suitable for large-scale antibody discovery programs in both industrial and academic settings. Furthermore, the method’s compatibility with chemically synthesized peptides allows for the generation of antibodies against virtually any defined epitope, including non-natural amino acids or synthetic haptens, expanding its utility beyond naturally occurring modifications. The ability to isolate rare antigen-specific plasma cells with sub-0.01% sensitivity and generate monoclonal antibodies with sub-nanomolar affinity represents a transformative advance in antibody engineering, with broad implications for the development of next-generation biologics, precision diagnostics, and targeted therapies.
