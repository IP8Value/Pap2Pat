# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) engineered to possess physical, biochemical, and functional characteristics substantially identical to those of naturally occurring extracellular vesicles (EV) derived from biological sources, while simultaneously incorporating unique, exogenous molecular markers that enable unambiguous detection, quantification, and segregation from endogenous EV populations. Specifically, the invention pertains to a class of biological reference materials derived from the human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1) gag polyprotein fused to a fluorescent reporter protein, which, upon expression in mammalian cells, self-assemble into nanoscale, lipid bilayer-enclosed vesicles that mimic the size, density, surface protein composition, lipid profile, morphology, and refractive index of native EV. These rEV are designed for use as standardized, traceable, and commutable calibration standards in the field of extracellular vesicle research and diagnostics, enabling accurate normalization of EV isolation protocols, calibration of optical and non-optical detection instruments, assessment of recovery efficiencies across laboratories, and quantitative comparison of EV concentrations in clinical biofluids such as plasma, urine, and other bodily fluids. The invention further encompasses methods for the production, purification, storage, and application of these rEV, including post-insertion surface modification with polyethylene glycol (PEG) to prevent protein corona formation and enable selective immunocapture, thereby facilitating downstream molecular analyses of endogenous EV without interference. The rEV of this invention are particularly suited for applications in precision medicine, clinical diagnostics, and quality control in multi-center studies where reproducibility, comparability, and standardization of EV measurements are critical for reliable data interpretation and translational validation.

## BACKGROUND

Extracellular vesicles (EV) are nano-sized, membrane-bound particles released by virtually all cell types into biological fluids, including blood, urine, cerebrospinal fluid, and interstitial fluid. They play essential roles in intercellular communication, immune modulation, and disease pathogenesis, particularly in cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, autoimmune conditions, and infectious diseases. The potential of EV as non-invasive biomarkers for early disease detection, prognosis, and therapeutic monitoring has generated intense interest in their clinical translation. However, the field of EV research suffers from a profound lack of standardization, resulting in widespread inconsistencies in EV isolation, quantification, and characterization across laboratories. This heterogeneity stems from the absence of universally accepted reference materials that accurately mimic the physical and biochemical properties of native EV while allowing for reliable differentiation from endogenous vesicles.

Current reference materials used for EV calibration and normalization are predominantly synthetic, including polystyrene beads, silica nanoparticles, and liposomes. While these materials are commercially available and easy to handle, they fundamentally lack the biological complexity of native EV. Polystyrene beads, for instance, exhibit a refractive index (RI) of approximately 1.61, which is significantly higher than the RI of native EV (~1.37–1.39), leading to systematic underestimation of EV size and concentration when measured by light-scattering techniques such as nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) or high-resolution flow cytometry (HR-FC). Similarly, silica beads have an RI of ~1.45, still deviating from the true RI of EV and thus compromising the accuracy of instrument calibration. Moreover, these synthetic particles do not possess the characteristic surface proteins—such as tetraspanins CD9, CD63, and CD81—or the lipid composition (including phosphatidylserine, phosphatidylcholine, sphingomyelin, and cholesterol) that define EV identity and influence their behavior during separation procedures such as density gradient centrifugation, size exclusion chromatography, or immunoaffinity capture. Consequently, synthetic beads are inadequate for evaluating the performance of EV isolation methods, as they do not behave commutably with native EV under experimental conditions.

Biological reference materials derived from cell culture supernatants or biofluids have been proposed as alternatives, but these suffer from critical limitations. They are inherently heterogeneous, lacking defined molecular composition, and cannot be distinguished from the sample EV under analysis. This renders them unsuitable for calibration, as their signal cannot be differentiated from the target analyte. Furthermore, their production is not standardized, leading to batch-to-batch variability, and they often contain contaminating proteins, lipoproteins, or non-EV particles that confound downstream analyses. No biological reference material currently exists that simultaneously satisfies the essential criteria for an ideal EV reference: (1) structural and biochemical fidelity to native EV, (2) traceability through exogenous, non-endogenous molecular tags, and (3) commutability across diverse biofluids and analytical platforms.

Recent efforts to improve reference materials have included the use of fluorescently labeled EV generated by transfecting cells with membrane-anchored GFP constructs or staining EV with lipophilic dyes. However, these approaches yield only a small fraction of fluorescently labeled particles—typically less than 13%—due to inefficient labeling and the presence of unlabeled endogenous EV. Additionally, these methods do not provide a stable, genetically encoded signal that is consistently incorporated into every vesicle, nor do they allow for quantification at the RNA or protein level beyond fluorescence. The absence of a robust, multi-modal detection system limits their utility in laboratories that lack fluorescence-capable instruments or require absolute quantification.

There is a critical and unmet need for a biological reference material that is not only physically and biochemically analogous to native EV but also engineered to be uniquely identifiable, quantifiable, and compatible with a wide array of analytical techniques commonly employed in EV research. Such a material must be stable under storage conditions, scalable for large-scale production, and applicable across diverse biofluids without altering its behavior relative to endogenous EV. The present invention fulfills this need by leveraging the natural ability of the HIV-1 gag polyprotein to hijack the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT) machinery and to assemble into virus-like particles (VLP) that bud from the plasma membrane with lipid and protein composition nearly identical to that of native EV. By fusing gag to enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP), the resulting recombinant vesicles (rEV) inherit the structural properties of EV while carrying a genetically encoded, fluorescent, and immunologically detectable marker that enables precise quantification via fluorescence, protein, and RNA-based assays. This invention introduces the first biological reference material that meets all criteria for a universal EV standard: biological origin, structural fidelity, traceability, stability, and commutability.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) that are produced by the expression of a gag polyprotein fused to a fluorescent reporter protein in mammalian cells, resulting in the formation of vesicles that are structurally and biochemically indistinguishable from naturally occurring extracellular vesicles while simultaneously carrying a unique, exogenous molecular signature that permits their unambiguous detection and quantification. The gag polyprotein, derived from the human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1), is a multifunctional structural protein that, when expressed in isolation without other viral components, spontaneously oligomerizes at the plasma membrane and recruits the ESCRT machinery to mediate the outward budding and scission of membrane domains enriched in tetraspanins, phosphatidylserine, and other EV-associated lipids and proteins. This process results in the release of nanoscale, lipid bilayer-enclosed vesicles that are morphologically and compositionally equivalent to endogenous EV. The fusion of the gag polyprotein to enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) ensures that every rEV particle contains a detectable fluorescent tag, enabling direct quantification by fluorescence-based methods. The rEV of this invention are produced through transient transfection of HEK293T cells with a plasmid encoding the gag-EGFP fusion protein under the control of a constitutive promoter, followed by purification via density gradient centrifugation to isolate vesicles with a buoyant density of 1.086–1.119 g/mL, which corresponds precisely to the density range of native EV derived from plasma, urine, and cell culture supernatants.

The rEV produced by this method exhibit a mean hydrodynamic diameter of 108.6 ± 9.5 nm, a zeta potential of −32.3 ± 1.6 mV, and a refractive index of approximately 1.37, values that are statistically indistinguishable from those of EV isolated from a variety of biological sources, including breast cancer cell lines, cancer-associated fibroblasts, plasma, and urine. Transmission electron microscopy confirms that rEV display the characteristic cup-shaped morphology associated with native EV, and mass spectrometry-based proteomic analysis reveals that rEV contain the full complement of canonical EV-associated proteins, including ALIX, TSG101, syntenin-1, flotillin-1, CD9, CD81, and CD63, with 89% of the total proteome overlapping with that of EV derived from mock-transfected cells. Lipidomic profiling further demonstrates that rEV contain the same classes of phospholipids, sphingomyelins, and lysophospholipids as native EV, with a Spearman correlation coefficient of 0.921 for lipid abundance, indicating near-perfect compositional fidelity. The cholesterol content per particle is equivalent to that of native EV, and the membrane integrity and orientation are preserved following freeze-thaw cycles, as confirmed by immunoprecipitation using antibodies targeting the extravesicular loop of CD81.

The rEV of this invention are uniquely identifiable due to the presence of two exogenous molecular signatures: the gag-EGFP fusion protein and encapsulated EGFP mRNA. The gag-EGFP protein is incorporated into the vesicle membrane and lumen during biogenesis, enabling detection by fluorescence microscopy, flow cytometry, microplate readers, and immunoblotting. The EGFP mRNA is encapsulated within the lumen of rEV during particle formation, as the gag polyprotein possesses RNA-binding domains that nonspecifically package cellular RNA in the absence of viral genomic RNA. This results in the consistent encapsulation of EGFP mRNA, which can be quantified using reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), providing a highly sensitive, nucleic acid-based method for rEV enumeration. The combination of these two detectable markers allows for multi-modal quantification, ensuring compatibility with a broad range of laboratory instrumentation and analytical workflows. Fluorescent nanoparticle tracking analysis (fNTA) and fluorescence-triggered high-resolution flow cytometry (HR-FC) enable direct, particle-by-particle quantification of rEV, with linear detection ranges spanning from 5 × 10⁸ to 1 × 10¹⁰ particles per sample. Protein-based detection via p24 ELISA provides a linear quantification range from 1 × 10⁶ to 1 × 10⁷ rEV, while western blot analysis of gag-EGFP yields a detection limit of 7 × 10⁷ rEV and a quantifiable range from 4 × 10⁸ to 1 × 10⁹ rEV. RT-qPCR for EGFP mRNA exhibits a semi-logarithmic relationship with rEV concentration over a range of 1 × 10⁷ to 1 × 10⁹ particles, with a coefficient of variation below 1% under optimized conditions, making it the most precise quantification method available.

The rEV of this invention are stable under a variety of storage conditions. They retain their size distribution, fluorescence intensity, buoyant density, and protein composition following storage at −80 °C for up to 12 months, after multiple freeze-thaw cycles, and during storage at 4 °C for up to seven days. Lyophilization in the presence of 5% trehalose as a cryoprotectant preserves rEV morphology, fluorescence, and particle count, enabling long-term storage and global distribution without the need for continuous cold chain logistics. In the absence of trehalose, rEV exhibit aggregation, membrane disruption, and increased size, demonstrating that trehalose is both necessary and sufficient to maintain structural integrity during dehydration. The rEV are non-infectious, as they are produced in the absence of any HIV-1 genomic RNA, proteases, or envelope proteins, and thus pose no biosafety risk.

The rEV of this invention are commutable, meaning they behave identically to native EV when introduced into complex biological matrices. When spiked into phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), cell culture medium, plasma, or urine, rEV co-fractionate with endogenous EV during size exclusion chromatography, density gradient centrifugation, and immunoaffinity capture using antibodies against CD81 or CD63. In plasma, however, rEV exhibit a density shift to higher values (1.141–1.215 g/mL) due to the formation of a protein corona composed of immunoglobulins, particularly IgG and IgM. This shift is reversible upon treatment with proteinase K, which degrades the corona proteins and restores the native density of rEV. To overcome this limitation and enable rEV segregation from endogenous EV in any biofluid without requiring proteinase treatment, the rEV can be post-insertion PEGylated by incubation with 1,2-dimyristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine conjugated to polyethylene glycol (DMPE-PEG 5k). This modification results in a 10 nm increase in mean particle size without altering fluorescence intensity, membrane integrity, or buoyant density. PEGylated rEV (rEV-PEG) resist protein corona formation and float at the same density as endogenous EV in plasma, while simultaneously enabling selective capture using anti-PEG antibodies immobilized on magnetic beads. This allows for complete segregation of rEV-PEG from endogenous EV after separation, ensuring that downstream analyses of the endogenous EV population—such as proteomics, lipidomics, transcriptomics, or functional assays—are not confounded by residual rEV contamination.

The rEV of this invention serve as a universal reference material for evaluating the recovery efficiency of EV isolation methods. When a known quantity of rEV (e.g., 5 × 10¹⁰ particles) is spiked into a biofluid and subjected to a given isolation protocol, the number of rEV recovered after separation can be quantified using any of the detection methods described above. The recovery efficiency is calculated as the ratio of recovered rEV to spiked rEV, expressed as a percentage. Using this approach, the recovery efficiency of differential ultracentrifugation (dUC) from plasma was determined to be between 5% and 10%, while size exclusion chromatography (SEC) demonstrated near-complete recovery (approximately 100%). Density gradient centrifugation yielded a recovery efficiency of approximately 30%, and pelleting of density fractions without SEC reduced recovery to only 5%. These findings validate the utility of rEV for benchmarking and optimizing EV isolation protocols. The coefficient of variation (CV) for rEV quantification using fNTA, RT-qPCR, and p24 ELISA was found to be 5.8%, 0.8%, and 5.7%, respectively, demonstrating high intra-assay precision. When rEV are used to normalize EV counts across users and laboratories, inter-user variability in EV enumeration is reduced by 66%, from 17.5% to 5.9%, thereby enabling reliable comparison of EV concentrations across multi-center studies.

The rEV of this invention enable accurate, normalized enumeration of EV in clinical samples. When 2 × 10¹⁰ rEV are spiked into 2 mL of plasma from breast cancer patients and healthy volunteers, and EV are isolated by density gradient centrifugation, the absolute number of EV measured by NTA does not differ significantly between the two groups. However, when the EV counts are normalized by the recovery efficiency of the spiked rEV, a statistically significant 2.5-fold increase in EV concentration is observed in cancer patients compared to healthy individuals. This normalization corrects for variations in sample handling, centrifugation efficiency, and instrument sensitivity, revealing a biological signal that is otherwise masked by technical noise. The same result is obtained whether rEV quantification is performed via fNTA or p24 ELISA, confirming the robustness and reproducibility of the method. This application of rEV represents a paradigm shift in EV diagnostics, transforming EV enumeration from an unreliable, semi-quantitative measurement into a precise, standardized, and clinically actionable assay.

The rEV of this invention can be further engineered to deliver exogenous RNA or protein cargo. The gag polyprotein’s inherent RNA-binding capacity allows for the encapsulation of any single-stranded RNA molecule longer than 20–30 nucleotides. Therefore, by co-transfecting cells with the gag-EGFP plasmid and a plasmid encoding a gene of interest fused to a 5′ or 3′ untranslated region recognized by gag, it is possible to generate rEV that specifically package and deliver that RNA. Similarly, fusion proteins can be designed to incorporate additional tags, enzymes, or ligands into the rEV membrane or lumen, enabling the creation of custom rEV for targeted delivery, diagnostic sensing, or therapeutic applications. The invention encompasses all such engineered variants, provided they retain the essential physical and biochemical characteristics of native EV and carry at least one exogenous, detectable marker for quantification and segregation.

The production of rEV involves the transient transfection of HEK293T cells with a plasmid encoding the gag-EGFP fusion protein under the control of a cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter, followed by culture in EV-depleted medium for 24–72 hours. The conditioned medium is harvested, clarified by low-speed centrifugation and filtration, concentrated by ultrafiltration, and subjected to discontinuous OptiPrep density gradient centrifugation. Fractions corresponding to a buoyant density of 1.086–1.119 g/mL are collected, pooled, and pelleted by ultracentrifugation. The resulting pellet is resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline and stored as aliquots at −80 °C. For PEGylation, rEV are incubated with DMPE-PEG 5k at a concentration of 0.016 mg/mL for two hours at 40 °C, followed by purification via size exclusion chromatography or ultrafiltration to remove unincorporated PEG. For lyophilization, rEV are mixed with 5% trehalose in PBS and subjected to a controlled freeze-drying protocol that maintains vesicle integrity. The rEV are characterized by nanoparticle tracking analysis, flow cytometry, western blot, mass spectrometry, and lipidomics to confirm compliance with the specifications of this invention.

The rEV of this invention are not limited to the gag-EGFP construct. Other fluorescent proteins, such as mCherry, tdTomato, or mNeonGreen, can be substituted for EGFP without altering the fundamental properties of the rEV. Similarly, the gag polyprotein may be fused to epitope tags such as FLAG, HA, or Myc, enabling detection via antibody-based assays in laboratories without fluorescence capabilities. The invention encompasses all such variants, provided they meet the criteria of structural fidelity, traceability, and commutability.

The rEV of this invention are suitable for use in any setting where EV quantification, standardization, or normalization is required, including clinical laboratories, diagnostic development centers, academic research institutions, and regulatory testing facilities. They are compatible with all standard EV isolation methods, including differential ultracentrifugation, density gradient centrifugation, size exclusion chromatography, immunoaffinity capture, polymer-based precipitation, and microfluidic separation. They are also compatible with all standard EV characterization techniques, including nanoparticle tracking analysis, high-resolution flow cytometry, electron microscopy, western blotting, ELISA, RT-qPCR, mass spectrometry, and lipidomics. The rEV serve as an internal control for every step of the EV workflow, from sample collection to data analysis, ensuring that results are reproducible, comparable, and biologically meaningful.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1: Recombinant Extracellular Vesicles (rEV) Share Physical and Biochemical Characteristics with Endogenous Sample Extracellular Vesicles (Sample EV)

Recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) were generated by transient transfection of HEK293T cells with a plasmid encoding the HIV-1 gag polyprotein fused to enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP). Following 72 hours of culture in EV-depleted medium, the conditioned medium was harvested, concentrated, and subjected to discontinuous OptiPrep density gradient centrifugation. Fractions corresponding to a buoyant density of 1.086–1.119 g/mL were collected and pelleted by ultracentrifugation to yield a purified rEV preparation. This preparation was then compared to endogenous extracellular vesicles (EV) isolated from a variety of biological sources, including plasma, urine, and conditioned medium from breast cancer cell lines (MCF7, 4T1), cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF), and mock-transfected HEK293T cells. Nanoparticle tracking analysis revealed that the mean hydrodynamic diameter of rEV was 108.6 ± 9.5 nm, which was not statistically different from the size distribution of sample EV derived from any of the tested sources, with p-values exceeding 0.0361 in all pairwise comparisons. Similarly, the zeta potential of rEV was measured at −32.3 ± 1.6 mV, a value that was indistinguishable from the zeta potential of sample EV, with p-values greater than 0.0357 across all comparisons. The refractive index of rEV, calculated using Mie theory based on light scattering data obtained by nanoparticle tracking analysis, was determined to be approximately 1.37, matching the refractive index of EV from plasma, urine, and cell culture supernatants. Transmission electron microscopy images confirmed that rEV exhibited the characteristic cup-shaped morphology associated with native EV, with no discernible difference in shape or membrane integrity when compared to sample EV. Western blot analysis demonstrated that rEV contained the full complement of canonical EV-associated proteins, including ALIX, TSG101, flotillin-1, syntenin-1, CD9, CD81, and CD63, with band intensities and migration patterns identical to those observed in sample EV. Immune-electron microscopy using gold-labeled antibodies against CD63 further confirmed the presence of this tetraspanin on the rEV surface, with labeling density and distribution indistinguishable from that of endogenous EV. Mass spectrometry-based proteomic analysis identified 890 proteins in rEV and 892 proteins in EV derived from mock-transfected cells, with 792 proteins (89%) showing equivalent abundance between the two populations, as confirmed by a Spearman correlation coefficient of 0.7712. Hierarchical clustering of proteomic data demonstrated high reproducibility across biological replicates. Lipidomic analysis revealed that rEV contained the same classes of phospholipids, sphingomyelins, and lysophospholipids as sample EV, with a Spearman correlation coefficient of 0.921 for lipid abundance, and the cholesterol content per particle was statistically equivalent to that of EV from plasma, urine, and cell culture supernatants. Together, these findings demonstrate that rEV are not merely similar to native EV but are structurally, biochemically, and physically indistinguishable from them, fulfilling the essential requirement for a biological reference material.

### Example 2: Identification of rEV Detection Methods

Multiple detection modalities were evaluated for their ability to quantify recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) and distinguish them from endogenous extracellular vesicles (EV) based on the presence of the exogenous gag-EGFP fusion protein and encapsulated EGFP mRNA. Fluorescent nanoparticle tracking analysis (fNTA) and fluorescence-triggered high-resolution flow cytometry (HR-FC) were employed for direct particle enumeration, with fNTA utilizing a 500 nm longpass filter to isolate EGFP fluorescence and HR-FC employing a 488 nm laser and logarithmic fluorescence thresholding. Both methods yielded equivalent relative rEV concentrations across a concentration range of 1 × 10⁸ to 1 × 10¹⁰ particles per sample, with a linear correlation coefficient exceeding 0.99. The percentage of fluorescent rEV in the density gradient-purified preparation was 79.7% ± 9.5%, indicating that a subset of particles lacked the gag-EGFP tag. To achieve near-complete purity, velocity gradient centrifugation was employed, which segregated gag-EGFP-negative EV into low-density fractions (1.046–1.068 g/mL) while retaining rEV in high-density fractions (1.076–1.088 g/mL), resulting in a fluorescent particle purity of nearly 100%. For indirect quantification, a microplate reader was used to measure relative fluorescence units (RFU) from rEV suspensions, and a linear relationship was established between RFU and rEV concentration over the range of 5 × 10⁸ to 1 × 10¹⁰ particles, with a coefficient of determination (R²) of 0.98. At the protein level, an anti-p24 ELISA assay demonstrated a linear correlation between p24 concentration and rEV number over the range of 1 × 10⁶ to 1 × 10⁷ particles, with an R² of 0.97. Western blot analysis of gag-EGFP protein bands allowed visualization of rEV at a detection limit of 7 × 10⁷ particles, with densitometry analysis revealing a linear relationship between band intensity and rEV number over the range of 4 × 10⁸ to 1 × 10⁹ particles. At the RNA level, reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) targeting EGFP mRNA exhibited a semi-logarithmic relationship with rEV concentration over the range of 1 × 10⁷ to 1 × 10⁹ particles, with a coefficient of variation below 1% in technical replicates. These findings establish that rEV can be quantified using a wide array of laboratory techniques, from fluorescence-based instruments to protein and nucleic acid assays, ensuring broad accessibility and applicability across diverse research and diagnostic settings.

### Example 3: Identification of Optimized Storage Conditions of rEV

The structural and functional stability of recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) under various storage conditions was systematically evaluated to determine optimal conditions for long-term preservation and distribution. rEV were stored at −80 °C for periods of six and twelve months, thawed at room temperature, and analyzed for particle concentration, size distribution, fluorescence intensity, and buoyant density using nanoparticle tracking analysis and density gradient centrifugation. No significant changes were observed in any of these parameters, indicating that rEV remain stable under long-term cryogenic storage. Multiple freeze-thaw cycles were performed, and immunoprecipitation using antibodies against the extravesicular loop of CD81 confirmed that membrane orientation and integrity were preserved, with no loss of epitope accessibility. Storage at 4 °C for up to seven days also resulted in no measurable degradation in particle count, size, or fluorescence. Lyophilization was tested in the presence and absence of 5% trehalose as a cryoprotectant. In the presence of trehalose, rEV retained their cup-shaped morphology as observed by transmission electron microscopy, their fluorescence intensity as measured by flow cytometry, their size distribution by nanoparticle tracking analysis, and their buoyant density by gradient centrifugation. In the absence of trehalose, rEV exhibited significant aggregation, increased mean particle size, and membrane disruption, as evidenced by a loss of particle count and altered light scattering profiles. Additional experiments demonstrated that trehalose concentrations below 5% were insufficient to prevent damage, while concentrations above 5% did not confer additional benefit. Therefore, 5% trehalose was identified as the optimal and sufficient concentration for lyophilization. The rEV were successfully reconstituted after lyophilization, with recovery rates exceeding 95% and no alteration in protein or lipid composition as confirmed by western blot and mass spectrometry. These results demonstrate that rEV can be stored indefinitely at −80 °C, for up to seven days at 4 °C, or lyophilized at 5% trehalose for room temperature distribution, making them suitable for global deployment in clinical and research settings.

### Example 4: The Use of rEV to Estimate the Efficiency of EV Isolation Methods

The utility of recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) as a tool for quantifying the recovery efficiency of extracellular vesicle (EV) isolation methods was demonstrated by spiking a known quantity of rEV (5 × 10¹⁰ particles) into phosphate-buffered saline, cell culture medium, plasma, and urine, followed by isolation using three commonly employed techniques: size exclusion chromatography (SEC), density gradient centrifugation (ODG), and differential ultracentrifugation (dUC). After isolation, the number of rEV recovered was quantified using fluorescent nanoparticle tracking analysis and anti-p24 ELISA. In SEC, nearly 100% of the spiked rEV were recovered, with the majority eluting in fractions 4–6, coinciding with the elution profile of endogenous EV. In ODG centrifugation, approximately 30% of rEV were recovered in the 1.086–1.119 g/mL density fraction, consistent with the known efficiency of this method for EV isolation. In contrast, dUC yielded only 5–10% recovery, with the majority of rEV lost during pelleting steps. When ODG fractions were pelleted without SEC, recovery dropped to 5%, confirming that the pelleting step itself is a major source of loss. The recovery efficiency of rEV was independent of the initial spike concentration, ranging from 1 × 10⁸ to 1 × 10¹¹ particles, indicating that the method is robust across a wide dynamic range. The coefficient of variation for rEV recovery was 5.7% for SEC, 9.2% for ODG, and 18.0% for dUC, demonstrating that rEV enable precise quantification of method repeatability. Furthermore, when rEV were spiked into plasma and subjected to proteinase K treatment prior to ODG centrifugation, their density shifted back to the native EV range, confirming that the method can be used to assess and correct for corona-induced artifacts. These findings establish rEV as a powerful tool for benchmarking EV isolation protocols, identifying sources of loss, and optimizing workflows for maximum recovery and reproducibility.

### Example 5: rEV Allows for an Accurate Comparison of EV Numbers in Plasma from Cancer Patients Versus Healthy Individuals

The ability of recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) to enable accurate, normalized comparison of extracellular vesicle (EV) concentrations in clinical samples was demonstrated by spiking 2 × 10¹⁰ rEV into 2 mL of plasma from 26 breast cancer patients and 11 sex-matched healthy volunteers. EV were isolated from all samples using density gradient centrifugation, and the total number of EV was quantified by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), while the number of spiked rEV was quantified by fluorescent nanoparticle tracking analysis (fNTA) and anti-p24 ELISA. Absolute EV counts, without normalization, showed no statistically significant difference between cancer patients and healthy individuals (3.91 × 10¹⁰ vs. 2.47 × 10¹⁰ EV/mL, p = 0.1410). However, when EV counts were normalized by the recovery efficiency of the spiked rEV, a statistically significant 2.5-fold increase in EV concentration was observed in cancer patients compared to healthy individuals (1.46 × 10¹¹ vs. 5.83 × 10¹⁰ EV/mL, p = 0.0002). The same result was obtained when rEV quantification was performed using anti-p24 ELISA instead of fNTA, yielding 1.59 × 10¹¹ vs. 7.55 × 10¹⁰ EV/mL (p = 0.0009), confirming that the normalization effect is independent of the quantification method used. This normalization corrects for technical variability introduced during sample handling, centrifugation, and instrument calibration, revealing a biological signal that is otherwise masked by noise. The coefficient of variation in EV counts across samples was reduced from 17.5% to 5.9% after normalization, demonstrating a 66% reduction in inter-user and inter-laboratory variability. These findings establish that rEV enable the transition from unreliable, absolute EV counts to precise, normalized EV concentrations that reflect true biological differences, thereby transforming EV-based diagnostics from a qualitative observation into a quantitative, clinically actionable assay.

### Example 6: Implementing the PSI Sequence to Load EGFP mRNA in rEV

The encapsulation of exogenous RNA into recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) was achieved by exploiting the RNA-binding capacity of the HIV-1 gag polyprotein, which nonspecifically packages cellular RNA during vesicle biogenesis. To demonstrate the feasibility of targeted RNA loading, a plasmid encoding enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) mRNA with a 5′ untranslated region (UTR) containing the psi (Ψ) packaging sequence from HIV-1 was co-transfected into HEK293T cells alongside the gag-EGFP plasmid. The psi sequence, which forms a stem-loop structure recognized by the nucleocapsid domain of gag, was inserted upstream of the EGFP coding sequence. Following transfection, rEV were purified by density gradient centrifugation, and total RNA was extracted and analyzed by reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) using primers specific for EGFP. A significant enrichment of EGFP mRNA was detected in rEV compared to control vesicles produced from cells transfected with gag-EGFP alone, with a 15-fold increase in EGFP transcript levels. The specificity of packaging was confirmed by the absence of EGFP mRNA in rEV produced from cells transfected with a control plasmid lacking the psi sequence. Furthermore, the EGFP mRNA was protected from RNase degradation, as treatment of rEV with RNase A prior to RNA extraction did not reduce EGFP signal, whereas treatment of free RNA in solution resulted in complete degradation. The encapsulated EGFP mRNA was functional, as demonstrated by successful translation of EGFP protein in recipient cells following rEV uptake. These results demonstrate that the gag polyprotein can be harnessed to selectively package any RNA molecule containing the psi sequence, enabling the generation of custom rEV for RNA delivery, diagnostic sensing, or therapeutic applications. The invention encompasses all rEV variants engineered to carry exogenous RNA via the psi sequence or other gag-binding motifs, provided they retain the physical and biochemical characteristics of native EV and carry at least one detectable marker for quantification and segregation.
