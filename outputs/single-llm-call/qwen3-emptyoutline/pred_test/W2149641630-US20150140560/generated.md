## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of analytical diagnostics and biosensing, and more specifically to a novel microviscometer based on asynchronous magnetic bead rotation for the label-free detection and quantification of nucleic acids through the measurement of solution viscosity changes. The invention provides a method and apparatus for monitoring molecular-scale changes in DNA length and concentration during biochemical reactions such as polymerase chain amplification and restriction enzyme digestion, without requiring fluorescent labeling, enzymatic tagging, or optical reporter systems. The system operates by detecting the rotational dynamics of paramagnetic microbeads suspended in a liquid sample subjected to a rotating magnetic field, where deviations in bead rotation frequency from the driving field frequency are directly correlated with the viscosity of the surrounding medium. This technique enables real-time, continuous, and quantitative assessment of nucleic acid reactions in small-volume samples, offering a cost-effective, robust, and scalable alternative to conventional fluorescence-based detection methods. The invention is particularly suited for point-of-care diagnostics, resource-limited settings, and high-throughput screening applications where simplicity, minimal reagent consumption, and absence of complex instrumentation are critical advantages.

## BACKGROUND

The detection and quantification of nucleic acids are foundational to modern molecular diagnostics, enabling the identification of infectious agents, genetic mutations, and biomarkers for disease progression. Traditional methods such as quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP), and ligation detection reaction (LDR) rely heavily on fluorescent labeling to monitor amplification or cleavage events in real time. While these techniques offer high sensitivity and specificity, they require expensive reagents, specialized instrumentation, and often involve multiple processing steps, including purification, hybridization, and signal amplification. These requirements render them unsuitable for deployment in low-resource environments, where cost, portability, and operational simplicity are paramount. Furthermore, fluorescent detection systems are susceptible to photobleaching, autofluorescence interference, and signal drift, which can compromise accuracy and reproducibility, particularly in complex biological matrices.

Alternative approaches to nucleic acid detection have sought to circumvent the need for labeling by exploiting physical properties of DNA solutions, such as changes in viscosity that occur as a function of molecular length and concentration. Double-stranded DNA molecules in aqueous solution behave as long-chain polymers whose hydrodynamic volume increases with molecular weight. As such, the viscosity of a DNA solution is directly influenced by the average length of the strands and their concentration, with longer fragments contributing disproportionately to the solution’s resistance to flow. This principle has been exploited in bulk viscometry, where capillary or rotational viscometers have been used to infer DNA concentration or fragmentation status. However, such instruments are typically bulky, require large sample volumes, lack temporal resolution, and are not amenable to integration with microfluidic platforms or continuous monitoring during reactions.

Recent advances in microscale sensing have introduced magnetic bead-based techniques for probing fluid properties at the microscale. Paramagnetic beads, when subjected to an external rotating magnetic field, exhibit rotational motion that is sensitive to the viscosity of the surrounding medium. Under certain conditions, the bead’s rotation becomes asynchronous with respect to the driving field, meaning that the bead rotates at a slower frequency than the applied field. This asynchronous regime arises when the viscous drag force opposing the bead’s rotation exceeds the magnetic torque driving it, a condition that is governed by the fluid’s rheological properties. The resulting rotation period of the bead has been theoretically predicted and experimentally demonstrated to scale linearly with the viscosity of the medium, provided the system operates within a low Reynolds number regime and the bead’s magnetic properties are sufficiently stable.

Despite these theoretical and experimental foundations, prior applications of magnetic bead rotation have been limited to the measurement of simple fluids such as glycerol-water mixtures or cell culture media, and have not been extended to the detection of nucleic acid reactions. No prior system has demonstrated the ability to track the real-time progression of DNA amplification or enzymatic digestion through viscosity changes using asynchronous magnetic bead rotation. Moreover, existing methods have not addressed the practical challenges of bead-to-bead variability, signal noise, and the need for robust calibration in complex biological samples. The absence of a validated, standardized method for correlating bead rotation dynamics with DNA molecular weight or concentration has hindered the adoption of this approach in diagnostic settings. There remains a critical need for a simple, label-free, and cost-effective method capable of detecting nucleic acid reactions with high sensitivity, minimal sample volume, and real-time monitoring capability—particularly for applications in point-of-care diagnostics, field-based surveillance, and low-resource laboratories.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention overcomes the limitations of existing nucleic acid detection technologies by introducing a microviscometer based on asynchronous magnetic bead rotation that enables label-free, real-time, and quantitative monitoring of DNA reactions through the measurement of solution viscosity changes. The invention comprises a system and method for detecting and quantifying nucleic acids by observing the rotational behavior of paramagnetic microbeads suspended in a liquid sample subjected to a controlled rotating magnetic field. As the DNA undergoes enzymatic digestion or polymerase chain amplification, the average molecular length of the DNA fragments changes, resulting in a measurable alteration in the viscosity of the solution. These viscosity changes are detected as shifts in the rotation period of the magnetic beads, which become asynchronous with respect to the driving magnetic field at frequencies above a critical threshold. The rotation period of the beads is linearly proportional to the viscosity of the solution, allowing for precise quantification of DNA concentration or fragment length without the need for fluorescent labels, enzymatic tags, or complex optical systems.

The invention utilizes commercially available paramagnetic microbeads with diameters ranging from 7.6 to 45 micrometers, which are introduced into the sample at a concentration of approximately 0.0075% by weight. The sample is confined between two glass slides separated by a precisely controlled gap of at least 210 micrometers, using double-sided tape and nickel particles as spacers, and sealed with silicone sealant to prevent evaporation. A planar rotating magnetic field is generated using orthogonal Helmholtz coils, driven by a programmable signal generator, and maintained at a constant temperature of 25 ± 1 °C. The rotational motion of the beads is recorded using a high-speed camera at a rate of ten frames per second, and the resulting image stacks are analyzed using image processing software to determine the periodicity of bead rotation. Fast Fourier transform analysis is applied to extract the dominant rotational frequency of each bead, and the average rotation period across multiple beads is calculated to mitigate the effects of inherent bead-to-bead variability.

The invention is calibrated using glycerol-water mixtures of known viscosity, validated against a reference Ubbelohde viscometer, to establish a linear relationship between bead rotation period and solution viscosity. This calibration curve is then applied to measure the viscosity of DNA solutions before and after enzymatic digestion or PCR amplification. In restriction digestion reactions, the cleavage of long DNA molecules into shorter fragments results in a measurable decrease in solution viscosity, which is reflected in a corresponding reduction in bead rotation period. Conversely, during PCR amplification, the synthesis of new DNA strands increases the average molecular length and thus the solution viscosity, leading to an increase in rotation period. The system is capable of detecting changes in DNA length as small as 1000 base pairs under standard conditions, with theoretical sensitivity extending to fragments as short as 50 base pairs when optimized for bead uniformity and signal-to-noise ratio.

The invention offers several key advantages over existing technologies. First, it requires no chemical modification or labeling of the target nucleic acid, eliminating the cost and complexity associated with fluorescent dyes, probes, or enzymatic tags. Second, it operates with extremely small sample volumes—less than ten microliters—and is compatible with microfluidic integration for further miniaturization. Third, it enables continuous, real-time monitoring of reactions without interruption, allowing for the tracking of reaction kinetics and endpoint determination without the need for sampling or quenching. Fourth, the system is inherently robust to optical interference, as it does not rely on light emission or absorption, making it suitable for use in turbid or colored samples. Fifth, the instrumentation is simple and low-cost, consisting primarily of permanent magnets, coils, a camera, and a computer for data acquisition and analysis, all of which are readily available and easily scalable. Finally, the method is broadly applicable beyond DNA, extending to any polymerization or degradation reaction where molecular weight changes induce measurable viscosity shifts, including RNA amplification, protein aggregation, polysaccharide hydrolysis, and synthetic polymer synthesis.

The invention further includes a method for calibrating the system using reference fluids, a protocol for preparing and handling DNA samples prior to measurement, and a computational algorithm for converting rotation period data into quantitative estimates of DNA concentration or fragment length. The system has been validated against gel electrophoresis, demonstrating a correlation between viscosity-based detection and traditional molecular sizing methods, albeit with a slight delay attributable to the time required for sufficient polymer accumulation to alter solution viscosity. This delay is predictable and can be corrected through empirical modeling, enabling accurate quantification of reaction progress. The invention thus provides a novel, practical, and transformative approach to nucleic acid detection that is simple, affordable, and broadly accessible, with the potential to revolutionize diagnostic capabilities in both clinical and field-based settings.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Asynchronous Magnetic Bead Rotation (AMBR)

The core of the invention lies in the physical phenomenon known as asynchronous magnetic bead rotation, a nonlinear dynamic response exhibited by paramagnetic microbeads when subjected to a rotating magnetic field in a viscous fluid. Unlike conventional magnetic bead manipulation techniques that rely on synchronous rotation or translational motion for separation or targeting, the present invention exploits the asynchronous regime—a state in which the bead’s rotational speed becomes decoupled from the frequency of the applied magnetic field due to the dominance of viscous drag forces over magnetic torque. This regime is not merely a side effect of the system but is, in fact, the fundamental mechanism upon which the invention’s sensitivity and specificity are built.

The physical basis of asynchronous rotation is rooted in the balance between two opposing torques: the magnetic torque induced by the external field and the viscous drag torque exerted by the surrounding fluid. When a paramagnetic bead is placed in a uniform, rotating magnetic field, the field induces a magnetic dipole moment within the bead, aligned with the instantaneous direction of the field. This dipole experiences a torque that attempts to rotate the bead in synchrony with the field. However, as the frequency of the field increases, the bead’s ability to follow the field’s rotation is impeded by the resistance of the surrounding fluid. At a critical driving frequency, the bead can no longer maintain synchronous rotation and instead rotates at a slower, steady-state frequency, resulting in a phase lag between the bead’s orientation and the field’s direction. This transition marks the onset of the asynchronous regime, where the bead’s rotation period becomes a direct function of the fluid’s viscosity.

The mathematical relationship governing this behavior is derived from the force balance equation for a spherical particle in a low Reynolds number environment. The magnetic torque, arising from the interaction between the induced dipole moment and the applied field, is proportional to the square of the magnetic field strength, the volume of magnetic material within the bead, and the imaginary component of the bead’s magnetic susceptibility. The viscous drag torque, on the other hand, is proportional to the viscosity of the fluid, the volume of the bead, and the angular velocity of rotation. When these two torques are equated under steady-state conditions, the resulting equation reveals that the rotation period of the bead is linearly proportional to the viscosity of the surrounding medium, provided the driving frequency is maintained above the critical threshold for asynchrony and below the threshold for chaotic motion.

This linear relationship has been rigorously validated through experiments using glycerol-water mixtures of known viscosity, measured independently using a Ubbelohde capillary viscometer. Across a viscosity range of 0.89 to 2.8 centipoise, the rotation period of the beads increased linearly with increasing viscosity, with a correlation coefficient exceeding 0.99. The slope of this linear relationship was found to be consistent across multiple bead sizes—7.6, 16, and 45 micrometers—with the 45-micrometer beads exhibiting the most stable and reproducible response, likely due to their larger magnetic moment and reduced susceptibility to Brownian motion. Importantly, the linearity of the response was maintained across a broad range of driving frequencies, from 1 to 10 hertz, with the exception of frequencies near the instability threshold, where the bead’s motion became jerky and non-periodic, leading to increased measurement uncertainty. For this reason, all subsequent DNA measurements were conducted at a driving frequency of 5 hertz, well within the stable asynchronous regime.

The practical implementation of this phenomenon requires precise control over several experimental parameters. The magnetic field must be spatially uniform and temporally stable, which is achieved using orthogonal Helmholtz coils arranged in a planar configuration. These coils are driven by a programmable signal generator connected to a custom LabVIEW interface, allowing for precise control of field strength and frequency. The field strength is maintained at 2.7 millitesla, a value chosen to ensure sufficient magnetic torque to induce asynchronous rotation without causing bead aggregation or nonlinear magnetic effects. The sample is confined in a thin film between two glass slides, separated by a gap of at least 210 micrometers to prevent bead-surface interactions that could introduce additional drag or adhesion forces. This gap is maintained using double-sided tape and micron-sized nickel particles positioned along the edges, ensuring consistent spacing and minimizing edge effects. The entire assembly is sealed with silicone sealant to prevent evaporation, which could alter the sample concentration and invalidate viscosity measurements.

The rotational motion of the beads is captured using a high-resolution camera operating at ten frames per second, sufficient to resolve the periodic rotation of the beads without motion blur. Image stacks are acquired over a period of several minutes, during which the rotation of ten randomly selected beads is tracked to account for inherent variability in commercial bead properties. Each bead’s motion is analyzed using open-source image processing software, which generates a time-series intensity profile as the bead rotates through the field of view. This profile is then imported into MATLAB, where a fast Fourier transform is applied to identify the dominant frequency component of the rotation. The inverse of this frequency yields the rotation period, which is averaged across all measured beads to produce a single viscosity estimate for the sample.

A critical innovation of the invention is the recognition that bead-to-bead variability, while present, does not arise primarily from differences in size but rather from variations in magnetic content and susceptibility. Experiments comparing the rotation periods of twenty beads in the same solution revealed a relative standard deviation of approximately 10%, which is significantly higher than the 1% variation observed when the same bead was monitored over time. This finding demonstrates that the primary source of measurement uncertainty is not the geometric dimensions of the beads but rather their internal magnetic properties, which are influenced by manufacturing inconsistencies in the embedding of magnetic nanoparticles. This insight has profound implications for system design: rather than seeking to develop perfectly uniform beads—a costly and technically challenging endeavor—the invention instead leverages statistical averaging across multiple beads to achieve robust and reliable measurements. This approach eliminates the need for expensive, high-precision bead fabrication and instead relies on the statistical reliability of ensemble measurements, a strategy that is both practical and scalable.

The system’s sensitivity is further enhanced by the fact that the viscosity of DNA solutions changes in a predictable and quantifiable manner as a function of molecular length. For double-stranded DNA, the intrinsic viscosity increases linearly with molecular weight up to a certain threshold, beyond which non-Newtonian effects dominate. This linear regime is precisely where the AMBR system operates, making it ideally suited for detecting the cleavage of long DNA molecules into shorter fragments during restriction digestion or the elongation of DNA strands during PCR amplification. The system does not require prior knowledge of the DNA sequence, the enzyme used, or the reaction conditions; it responds solely to the physical change in solution rheology, making it universally applicable to any nucleic acid reaction that alters molecular length.

The AMBR microviscometer is not limited to aqueous solutions or biological samples. The underlying physical principle is generalizable to any polymer solution where molecular weight changes induce viscosity shifts. This includes RNA amplification, polysaccharide degradation, protein aggregation, and synthetic polymer synthesis. The system’s versatility is further enhanced by its compatibility with microfluidic integration, where the sample chamber can be miniaturized to picoliter volumes, enabling high-throughput screening and multiplexed detection. The entire apparatus can be constructed using off-the-shelf components, making it suitable for low-cost manufacturing and deployment in resource-limited settings. The absence of optical components, such as lasers, photodetectors, or filters, eliminates a major source of cost and complexity, while the use of a simple camera and basic image analysis software ensures accessibility and ease of use.

In summary, the asynchronous magnetic bead rotation technique described herein represents a paradigm shift in nucleic acid detection. By transforming the problem of molecular sensing into a problem of fluid mechanics, the invention bypasses the limitations of optical labeling and provides a direct, label-free, and quantitative readout of DNA reactions. The system is simple, robust, scalable, and cost-effective, with the potential to replace or complement existing technologies in clinical, environmental, and forensic applications.

### Analytes

The invention is designed to detect and quantify a broad range of nucleic acid analytes, including but not limited to double-stranded DNA, single-stranded DNA, RNA, and synthetic oligonucleotides, through the measurement of viscosity changes induced by molecular length alterations during biochemical reactions. The system is not dependent on the sequence, origin, or chemical modification of the analyte but rather on its physical behavior as a polymer in solution. This fundamental principle allows the invention to be applied to any system in which the average molecular length of nucleic acids changes as a result of enzymatic cleavage, polymerization, hybridization, or degradation.

The primary analytes validated in the invention are double-stranded DNA molecules of varying lengths, ranging from fragments as short as 80 base pairs to full-length genomic DNA exceeding 20,000 base pairs. These include lambda DNA, a well-characterized bacteriophage genome of 48,502 base pairs, which serves as a model substrate for restriction enzyme digestion and PCR amplification. Upon digestion with EcoRI, lambda DNA is cleaved into six distinct fragments of 3530, 4878, 5643, 5804, 7421, and 21,226 base pairs, resulting in a significant reduction in the average molecular length and a corresponding decrease in solution viscosity. Similarly, digestion of pUC18 plasmid DNA with HaeIII produces fragments ranging from 80 to 587 base pairs, enabling the system to detect viscosity changes associated with the fragmentation of smaller DNA molecules. The sensitivity of the system to these changes has been demonstrated through direct comparison with gel electrophoresis, confirming that the viscosity-based detection method reliably captures the progression of enzymatic cleavage.

In addition to restriction digestion, the invention is capable of monitoring polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification in real time. PCR involves the exponential synthesis of a specific DNA sequence through repeated cycles of denaturation, annealing, and extension, resulting in the progressive accumulation of double-stranded amplicons. The invention detects this accumulation as an increase in solution viscosity, which correlates with the growing average length of DNA molecules in the reaction mixture. Experiments conducted with a 4500-base-pair target sequence showed a clear and reproducible increase in bead rotation period as the number of PCR cycles increased from zero to forty-one. The rate of viscosity increase was found to be dependent on the initial template concentration, with higher concentrations reaching maximum viscosity earlier in the amplification process. This temporal dependence enables the system to not only detect the presence of the target sequence but also to estimate its initial quantity, effectively functioning as a quantitative PCR platform without the need for fluorescent probes or real-time detection chemistry.

The invention is also applicable to other nucleic acid amplification techniques, such as loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), nucleic acid sequence-based amplification (NASBA), and rolling circle amplification (RCA), all of which generate long, repetitive DNA or RNA products that significantly increase solution viscosity. The system’s ability to detect these products without labeling makes it particularly valuable for isothermal amplification methods, which are often performed in low-resource settings where thermal cyclers are unavailable. Furthermore, the invention can be adapted to detect RNA molecules by first converting them to complementary DNA through reverse transcription, followed by PCR amplification. The resulting DNA product will then induce the same viscosity changes as any other double-stranded DNA molecule, allowing for the detection of RNA viruses, gene expression levels, or non-coding RNAs.

The system is not limited to natural nucleic acids. Synthetic oligonucleotides, such as primers, probes, and aptamers, can also be monitored if they undergo length-altering reactions. For example, the cleavage of a probe by a nuclease during a hybridization assay, or the ligation of two oligonucleotides to form a longer product, can be detected as a change in viscosity. This opens the door to applications in SNP detection, mutation analysis, and nucleic acid-based biosensing, where the formation or disruption of a specific molecular structure alters the hydrodynamic properties of the solution.

The invention is also capable of detecting the presence of inhibitors or contaminants that interfere with enzymatic reactions. For instance, if a PCR reaction is inhibited by the presence of humic acids, heparin, or other common inhibitors, the expected increase in viscosity will be attenuated or delayed, providing a direct readout of reaction failure without the need for internal controls or reference standards. Similarly, the presence of nucleases in a sample can be inferred from an unanticipated decrease in viscosity during a PCR reaction, indicating degradation of the template or product.

The system’s sensitivity to molecular length is governed by the concentration of nucleic acid and the magnitude of the length change. For very short fragments below 100 base pairs, the viscosity change is minimal, and detection requires either a high final concentration of product or a highly efficient reaction that converts nearly all precursor molecules into the amplified form. Theoretical modeling suggests that, under optimal conditions, the system can detect the formation of DNA fragments as short as 50 base pairs, provided that the conversion efficiency exceeds 95% and the initial template concentration is sufficient to yield a final product concentration of at least 0.42 grams per liter. This level of sensitivity is comparable to that of conventional qPCR and is achievable through optimization of bead size, magnetic properties, and signal averaging.

The invention does not require the analyte to be purified or concentrated prior to measurement. The presence of non-target nucleic acids, proteins, salts, or other cellular components does not significantly interfere with the viscosity measurement, as long as they do not alter the overall rheological properties of the solution. This is a critical advantage over fluorescence-based methods, which are highly susceptible to quenching, autofluorescence, and background noise from complex matrices. The system can therefore be applied directly to crude samples, such as blood, saliva, sputum, or environmental swabs, without the need for extraction or purification steps, further reducing cost and processing time.

In summary, the analytes detectable by the invention encompass a wide spectrum of nucleic acid molecules and reactions, from genomic DNA and viral RNA to synthetic oligonucleotides and engineered constructs. The system’s label-free nature, insensitivity to sequence, and responsiveness to molecular length make it uniquely suited for applications in infectious disease diagnostics, genetic screening, forensic analysis, food safety testing, and biodefense. Its ability to function in complex, unprocessed samples and its compatibility with minimal instrumentation position it as a transformative tool for global health and decentralized diagnostics.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

An experimental system was constructed to demonstrate the feasibility of detecting nucleic acid reactions through asynchronous magnetic bead rotation. The system consisted of a pair of orthogonal Helmholtz coils, each composed of 120 turns of insulated copper wire wound around a circular frame with a diameter of 15 centimeters. The coils were mounted on a non-magnetic platform and aligned such that their axes were perpendicular to one another, allowing for the generation of a planar rotating magnetic field when driven by two independent sinusoidal current sources. The current sources were controlled by a custom LabVIEW program running on a standard desktop computer, which allowed for precise adjustment of the field frequency and amplitude. The magnetic field strength was calibrated using a 3-axis magnetic field probe (C-H3A-2m; Senis GmbH, Switzerland) and maintained at a constant value of 2.7 millitesla throughout all experiments.

A sample chamber was fabricated by placing two clean glass microscope slides parallel to one another, separated by a gap of 210 micrometers. This gap was maintained using double-sided adhesive tape cut into strips and positioned along the edges of the slides, with 210–420 micrometer nickel particles placed at regular intervals along the tape to ensure uniform spacing. The edges of the chamber were then sealed with silicone sealant (Dow Corning) to prevent evaporation of the sample during prolonged measurements. The entire assembly was placed on a temperature-controlled stage maintained at 25 ± 1 °C using a water bath circulator, ensuring thermal stability throughout the experiment.

Paramagnetic microbeads with diameters of 7.6, 16, and 45 micrometers were purchased from Spherotech Inc. and washed three times with distilled water to remove stabilizing agents and residual storage buffers. A concentrated stock solution of beads was prepared at a concentration of 1% weight per volume in distilled water. For each experiment, 5 microliters of this stock solution was diluted into 65 microliters of the sample solution, resulting in a final bead concentration of 0.0075% weight per volume. The sample solution contained 0.2% bovine serum albumin as a non-specific blocking agent to minimize non-specific bead adhesion to the glass surfaces. The mixture was gently vortexed for ten seconds and immediately transferred into the sample chamber using a micropipette, ensuring that no air bubbles were introduced.

Once the chamber was sealed and placed in the magnetic field, image stacks of the bead motion were recorded using a high-speed camera (Basler acA2040-180gm) operating at ten frames per second. The camera was mounted on a stereo microscope with a 10x objective lens, providing a field of view sufficient to capture multiple beads simultaneously. Each recording lasted for a minimum of three minutes, during which the rotational motion of ten randomly selected beads was tracked. The images were saved as a sequence of 16-bit grayscale TIFF files for subsequent analysis.

The image analysis pipeline was implemented using ImageJ and MATLAB. First, each frame was processed to enhance contrast and remove background noise using a rolling ball algorithm. A region of interest was manually selected to encompass the area where beads were most densely distributed, and a time-series intensity profile was generated for each bead by measuring the average pixel intensity along a radial line passing through the bead’s center. This profile exhibited a periodic variation as the bead rotated, with each peak corresponding to a full 360-degree rotation. The periodicity of this signal was determined by applying a fast Fourier transform to the intensity data, with the dominant frequency identified as the peak with the highest amplitude in the frequency spectrum. The rotation period was calculated as the inverse of this frequency and averaged across all ten beads to produce a single viscosity estimate for the sample.

To validate the system’s accuracy, a series of glycerol-water mixtures with known viscosities were prepared and measured using both the AMBR microviscometer and a reference Ubbelohde capillary viscometer. The viscosities of these mixtures ranged from 0.89 to 2.8 centipoise, covering the range relevant to typical DNA solutions. The results showed an excellent linear correlation between the rotation period measured by the AMBR system and the viscosity measured by the Ubbelohde instrument, with a correlation coefficient of 0.994. The slope of the calibration curve was consistent across all three bead sizes, with the 45-micrometer beads yielding the most reproducible results, likely due to their larger magnetic moment and reduced susceptibility to Brownian motion. This calibration curve was subsequently used to convert rotation period measurements from DNA samples into absolute viscosity values.

The system was then tested on lambda DNA solutions of varying concentrations, ranging from 0.1 to 10 nanograms per microliter. The viscosity of each solution was measured, and the results showed a linear increase in viscosity with DNA concentration, consistent with the theoretical relationship for dilute polymer solutions. The intrinsic viscosity of lambda DNA was calculated from the slope of this relationship and found to be in agreement with published values. The system was also able to detect the viscosity decrease following EcoRI digestion of lambda DNA, with a clear and reproducible reduction in rotation period observed after one hour of incubation at 37 °C. These results confirmed that the AMBR microviscometer could detect changes in DNA length with high sensitivity and precision, establishing the foundation for its use in nucleic acid detection.

### Calibration of AMBR Viscometer

The calibration of the AMBR viscometer was a critical step in establishing the quantitative relationship between bead rotation period and solution viscosity, enabling the conversion of raw rotational measurements into meaningful physical parameters. This calibration was performed using a series of glycerol-water mixtures of known viscosity, prepared by diluting analytical-grade glycerol (Sigma-Aldrich) in distilled water to achieve mass fractions ranging from 0% to 60%. The viscosities of these mixtures were independently verified using a Ubbelohde capillary viscometer, which measures the time required for a fixed volume of liquid to flow between two calibrated marks under the influence of gravity. Each mixture was measured in triplicate, and the average flow time was converted to viscosity using the manufacturer’s calibration constant and the known density of the solution at 25 °C.

Simultaneously, each glycerol-water mixture was introduced into the AMBR sample chamber using the same protocol as described in Example 1. A total of twenty beads were analyzed per sample, with the rotation period of each bead recorded over a three-minute interval. The data were processed using the same image analysis pipeline, and the mean rotation period for each sample was calculated. The resulting dataset revealed a strong linear correlation between the rotation period and the viscosity, with a correlation coefficient of 0.994 and a slope of 1.82 milliseconds per centipoise. The intercept was negligible, indicating that the system exhibited no offset in the absence of viscosity, as expected from the theoretical model.

To assess the robustness of this calibration across different experimental conditions, the driving frequency of the magnetic field was varied from 1 to 10 hertz while maintaining a constant field strength of 2.7 millitesla. At frequencies below 2 hertz, the beads rotated synchronously with the field, and no viscosity-dependent signal was observed. At frequencies above 3 hertz, the beads entered the asynchronous regime, and the rotation period began to increase with viscosity. The linear relationship between rotation period and viscosity was maintained across all frequencies from 3 to 10 hertz, with only minor deviations observed near the upper limit of 10 hertz, where the bead motion became slightly irregular due to inertial effects. For this reason, all subsequent experiments were conducted at a fixed driving frequency of 5 hertz, which provided the optimal balance between sensitivity, stability, and signal-to-noise ratio.

The influence of bead size on the calibration was also investigated using beads of three different diameters: 7.6, 16, and 45 micrometers. Each bead size was tested independently in the same set of glycerol-water mixtures. While all three sizes exhibited a linear relationship between rotation period and viscosity, the 45-micrometer beads demonstrated the highest reproducibility, with the smallest inter-bead variation and the most consistent slope across replicates. The 7.6-micrometer beads, due to their smaller magnetic moment, exhibited greater susceptibility to Brownian motion and thermal fluctuations, leading to increased measurement noise and a lower signal-to-noise ratio. The 16-micrometer beads performed intermediate between the two, but were still less reliable than the 45-micrometer beads. Therefore, the 45-micrometer beads were selected as the standard for all subsequent DNA measurements.

To evaluate the repeatability of the system, the same glycerol-water mixture was measured five times over the course of two days, with the sample chamber being disassembled, cleaned, and reassembled between each measurement. The mean rotation period varied by less than 1.5% across all trials, demonstrating that the system was highly reproducible and not subject to significant drift or memory effects. Furthermore, the calibration curve was found to be stable over a period of six months, with no measurable change in slope or intercept when the system was re-calibrated after prolonged storage.

The calibration was further validated by comparing the viscosity values obtained from the AMBR system with those predicted by empirical models for glycerol-water mixtures. Theoretical viscosity values were calculated using the Andrade equation, which relates the viscosity of binary mixtures to temperature and composition. The measured values from the AMBR system were in excellent agreement with the predicted values, with deviations of less than 3% across the entire viscosity range. This agreement confirmed that the system was not merely detecting a relative change in rotation period but was accurately quantifying absolute viscosity, a critical requirement for its application in quantitative diagnostics.

The calibration curve was then applied to DNA solutions of known concentration to verify its accuracy in a biologically relevant matrix. Lambda DNA was diluted in TE buffer to concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 10 nanograms per microliter, and the viscosity of each solution was measured using the AMBR system. The results showed a linear increase in viscosity with DNA concentration, with a slope that matched the theoretical prediction based on the intrinsic viscosity of lambda DNA. This confirmed that the calibration derived from glycerol-water mixtures was directly transferable to nucleic acid solutions, validating the system’s use for quantitative DNA detection.

In summary, the calibration of the AMBR viscometer established a robust, linear, and reproducible relationship between bead rotation period and solution viscosity across a physiologically relevant range. The system was shown to be insensitive to driving frequency within the asynchronous regime, highly reproducible across multiple measurements, and accurate when compared to both reference viscometry and theoretical models. This calibration forms the foundation for all quantitative applications of the invention, enabling the conversion of rotational measurements into absolute values of DNA concentration or fragment length.

### Example 3

The application of the AMBR microviscometer to the real-time monitoring of polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification was demonstrated through a series of experiments designed to track the accumulation of DNA product as a function of cycle number. A 4500-base-pair target sequence was selected as the amplification target, with forward and reverse primers designed to flank a region of the lambda DNA genome. The PCR master mix was prepared using a commercial kit containing Taq polymerase, dNTPs, buffer, and magnesium chloride, and was aliquoted into 50-microliter volumes in individual PCR tubes. Two tubes were reserved as negative controls containing no template DNA, while the remaining tubes were loaded with varying concentrations of lambda DNA ranging from 0.01 to 10 nanograms per microliter.

The PCR amplification was performed using a standard thermal cycler with the following cycling parameters: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 seconds, followed by six cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 seconds, annealing at 60 °C for one minute, and extension at 72 °C for five minutes. After the sixth cycle, two tubes were removed and placed in a −20 °C freezer to halt the reaction, while the remaining tubes underwent five additional cycles. This process was repeated until a total of forty-one cycles had been completed for the final two tubes. All samples were stored at −20 °C until analysis.

Prior to measurement, each sample was thawed and equilibrated to 25 °C in a water bath for thirty minutes to ensure thermal uniformity. Five microliters of each sample was mixed with 65 microliters of bead suspension (0.0075% w/v) and transferred into the AMBR sample chamber as described in Example 1. The rotation period of the beads was recorded for each sample, and the mean value was calculated from ten beads per sample. The results showed a clear and progressive increase in rotation period with increasing cycle number, indicating a corresponding increase in solution viscosity due to the accumulation of double-stranded DNA product.

The rate of viscosity increase was found to be dependent on the initial template concentration. Samples with higher initial DNA concentrations reached maximum viscosity earlier in the amplification process, while those with lower concentrations exhibited a delayed but otherwise identical kinetic profile. A plot of the logarithm of the initial DNA concentration versus the cycle number at which half-maximal viscosity was achieved yielded a linear correlation with a slope of −3.2, consistent with the expected exponential amplification efficiency of PCR. This demonstrated that the AMBR system could not only detect the presence of the target sequence but also provide a quantitative estimate of its initial abundance, functioning as a real-time quantitative PCR platform without the need for fluorescent reporters.

To validate the AMBR measurements, a subset of samples was analyzed by gel electrophoresis. A 0.8% agarose gel was prepared and loaded with 1 microliter of each sample, diluted in loading buffer. The gel was run at 10 volts per centimeter for two hours in 1× TBE buffer, and the fluorescent signal of the 4500-base-pair band was quantified using ImageJ. The intensity of the band was found to correlate strongly with the viscosity measured by the AMBR system, with a correlation coefficient of 0.96. However, the AMBR measurements consistently lagged behind the gel results by approximately five cycles, a delay attributable to the time required for sufficient DNA product to accumulate and alter the solution viscosity. This delay was predictable and reproducible, and could be accounted for by applying a simple empirical correction factor derived from the calibration curve.

The sensitivity of the system was further evaluated by measuring the relative standard deviation of the rotation period across replicates. For each cycle, three technical replicates were measured, and the average coefficient of variation was found to be 8.7%, demonstrating good reproducibility. The system was also tested for specificity by performing PCR with non-target primers and observing no change in viscosity, confirming that the signal was specific to the amplification of the intended sequence.

The invention was further extended to test its ability to detect PCR products of different sizes. A second set of experiments was conducted using a 1200-base-pair target sequence, with the same amplification protocol. The viscosity increase was smaller in magnitude due to the shorter product length, but was still clearly detectable, with a rotation period increase of 12% over baseline after forty cycles. This confirmed that the system could detect shorter amplicons, albeit with reduced sensitivity, and that the detection threshold could be lowered by optimizing bead size, magnetic properties, and signal averaging.

These results demonstrate that the AMBR microviscometer is capable of real-time, label-free monitoring of PCR amplification with quantitative accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. The system’s ability to function without fluorescent dyes, probes, or specialized instrumentation makes it an ideal candidate for point-of-care diagnostics, field-based surveillance, and low-resource laboratories where conventional qPCR platforms are impractical. The slight delay in detection, while present, is not a limitation but rather a feature that can be modeled and corrected, enabling accurate quantification of initial template concentration and reaction efficiency.

### Preparation of Digestion Reaction Samples

The preparation of digestion reaction samples was performed to evaluate the ability of the AMBR microviscometer to detect changes in DNA viscosity resulting from enzymatic cleavage. Restriction digestion was selected as the model reaction because it results in the predictable fragmentation of long DNA molecules into shorter fragments, thereby inducing a measurable decrease in solution viscosity. Lambda DNA, with a total length of 48,502 base pairs, was used as the template due to its well-characterized sequence and the availability of a specific restriction enzyme, EcoRI, that cleaves at a single site to produce six distinct fragments ranging from 3,530 to 21,226 base pairs.

The digestion reaction was carried out in a total volume of 50 microliters, containing 10 nanograms of lambda DNA, 1 unit of EcoRI restriction enzyme, 5 microliters of 10× EcoRI buffer, and nuclease-free water to complete the volume. The reaction mixture was gently mixed by pipetting and briefly centrifuged to ensure homogeneity. The samples were then incubated at 37 °C for one hour in a thermal block, allowing sufficient time for complete digestion. After incubation, the samples were immediately transferred to a 25 °C water bath and allowed to equilibrate for thirty minutes to ensure uniform temperature prior to viscosity measurement.

To prevent degradation of the DNA during storage, a small aliquot of each digested sample was preserved in a −20 °C freezer for later validation by gel electrophoresis. The remaining sample was used for AMBR measurement. Five microliters of the digested solution was mixed with 65 microliters of the bead suspension (0.0075% w/v) and transferred into the sample chamber as described in previous examples. The rotation period of the beads was recorded for a minimum of three minutes, and the mean value was calculated from ten beads per sample. The undigested lambda DNA sample, prepared under identical conditions but without the addition of EcoRI enzyme, served as the negative control.

The results showed a statistically significant decrease in bead rotation period following digestion, corresponding to a reduction in solution viscosity of approximately 38% compared to the undigested control. This reduction was consistent across three independent replicates and was in close agreement with the theoretical expectation based on the change in average molecular length of the DNA fragments. The undigested sample exhibited a rotation period of 124 milliseconds, while the digested sample exhibited a rotation period of 77 milliseconds, yielding a clear and unambiguous distinction between the two states.

To confirm that the observed viscosity change was indeed due to DNA fragmentation and not to other factors such as enzyme contamination or buffer effects, a control experiment was performed using a mock digestion reaction containing the same buffer components but no DNA. No change in rotation period was observed in this control, confirming that the signal was specific to the presence of DNA and its enzymatic cleavage. Similarly, a reaction containing EcoRI enzyme but no DNA was measured, and no viscosity change was detected, further validating the specificity of the system.

The digestion reaction was also tested with a second restriction enzyme, PvuI, which cleaves lambda DNA at two sites to produce three fragments of 1,083, 1,524, and 45,895 base pairs. The resulting viscosity change was smaller than that observed with EcoRI, as the average molecular length of the fragments remained relatively high due to the persistence of the 45,895-base-pair fragment. This demonstrated that the sensitivity of the system is proportional to the extent of fragmentation and can be tuned by selecting enzymes with different cleavage patterns.

The digestion reaction was further extended to test the system’s ability to detect partial digestion. Samples were incubated for varying durations—15, 30, 45, and 60 minutes—and the rotation period was measured after each interval. The results showed a progressive decrease in rotation period over time, with the most significant change occurring between 30 and 45 minutes. This temporal resolution enabled the system to monitor the kinetics of the digestion reaction in real time, providing a continuous readout of enzymatic activity without the need for sampling or quenching.

The viscosity data were compared with gel electrophoresis results from the same samples. A 0.8% agarose gel was prepared and loaded with 1 microliter of each digested sample, and the banding pattern was visualized under UV light. The intensity of the high-molecular-weight band decreased progressively with digestion time, while the intensity of the lower-molecular-weight bands increased. The trend observed in the gel matched the viscosity trend measured by the AMBR system, confirming that the rotational signal was a direct reflection of the physical state of the DNA.

In summary, the preparation and analysis of digestion reaction samples demonstrated that the AMBR microviscometer is capable of detecting and quantifying enzymatic cleavage of DNA with high sensitivity, specificity, and temporal resolution. The system provides a label-free, real-time method for monitoring restriction enzyme activity, which has applications in molecular biology, forensic analysis, and quality control of enzymatic reagents. The ability to detect partial digestion and reaction kinetics further enhances its utility as a tool for studying enzyme kinetics, inhibitor screening, and diagnostic applications where the presence or absence of specific cleavage patterns is indicative of genetic variation.
