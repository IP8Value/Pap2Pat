## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The operation of hydroelectric facilities presents a persistent challenge in balancing the generation of renewable energy with the preservation of aquatic ecosystems, particularly in river systems that serve as critical migratory corridors for anadromous and resident fish species. Fish passing through hydraulic structures such as turbines, spillways, and bypass systems are frequently subjected to extreme physical forces that can result in injury or mortality. These forces include rapid and extreme fluctuations in hydrostatic pressure, intense shear stresses, violent turbulence, direct mechanical impact with rotating components, and the formation and collapse of vapor cavities known as cavitation. The cumulative effect of these phenomena, often occurring in rapid succession within confined and complex flow fields, can lead to internal hemorrhaging, barotrauma, spinal deformities, and external lacerations, even in species that are otherwise robust and adapted to high-energy aquatic environments. 

Prior methods for assessing the biological impact of turbine passage relied heavily on field studies involving the release of live fish equipped with external sensors or monitored via telemetry. While such approaches provided valuable qualitative insights into survival rates and general behavioral responses, they were fundamentally limited in their ability to quantify the precise hydraulic conditions experienced by individual fish during passage. The inability to directly measure the magnitude, duration, and spatial distribution of physical stresses rendered it difficult to correlate observed mortality or injury with specific mechanical or fluid dynamic events. Moreover, the physiological variability among individual fish, the confounding influence of environmental factors such as water temperature and dissolved oxygen, and the logistical constraints of handling live organisms under field conditions introduced significant uncertainty into the interpretation of results. These limitations hindered the development of targeted engineering solutions aimed at mitigating harm to aquatic life.

To address these shortcomings, an autonomous, instrumented sensor device known as the Gen 1 Sensor Fish was developed to serve as a surrogate for live fish in hydraulic environments. This device was engineered to replicate the size, density, and neutral buoyancy of a yearling salmon smolt, enabling it to follow the same flow trajectories and experience the same hydrodynamic forces as a biological organism. It incorporated a suite of miniature sensors capable of recording three-dimensional linear acceleration, three-dimensional rotational velocity, hydrostatic pressure, and ambient temperature at high temporal resolution. The data collected by the Gen 1 Sensor Fish enabled researchers to map the spatial distribution of hazardous conditions within operating turbines, identify the most damaging zones of flow, and correlate specific pressure-time histories with observed injury thresholds. This capability proved instrumental in guiding modifications to turbine blade geometry, guide vane positioning, and operational protocols at major hydropower facilities in the Columbia River basin, where the device was extensively deployed under the auspices of the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers and the U.S. Department of Energy.

Despite its success, the Gen 1 Sensor Fish exhibited several technical limitations that restricted its broader applicability. Its pressure measurement range was insufficient to accommodate the higher head conditions encountered at many high-head Francis turbines and pumped storage facilities, where pressures can exceed the operational limits of the original sensor suite. The device's mechanical robustness was inadequate for environments characterized by frequent and severe impacts with turbine components, resulting in frequent device failure during deployment. Its data acquisition and storage capacity were constrained by the available memory and sampling rate, limiting the duration of data collection and the fidelity of transient event capture. The communication and recovery systems were rudimentary, requiring manual retrieval in close proximity to the point of release and offering limited post-deployment telemetry for locating the device after surfacing. Additionally, the cost of manufacturing and maintaining the Gen 1 Sensor Fish was prohibitively high for widespread adoption, particularly in international contexts where funding for ecological monitoring is more limited.

Furthermore, the software infrastructure for data analysis was proprietary, non-standardized, and required specialized training to operate, creating barriers to interoperability and data sharing across research institutions and regulatory agencies. The device’s power management system was not optimized for extended field deployments, and its calibration procedures were not fully traceable to international standards, reducing confidence in the accuracy and reproducibility of measurements across different laboratories and field sites. The absence of a standardized protocol for sensor integration, data formatting, and post-processing also impeded the development of predictive models that could link hydraulic conditions to biological outcomes with greater precision.

There was, therefore, a clear and pressing need for a next-generation sensor device capable of operating reliably under more extreme hydraulic conditions, with enhanced sensor fidelity, expanded data handling capabilities, improved durability, and reduced cost. Such a device would need to be scalable for deployment in diverse turbine types, including Kaplan, Francis, and bulb turbines, as well as in pump storage systems, spillways, and fish bypass channels. It would require a modular architecture that permits easy sensor replacement and firmware updates, a robust recovery mechanism that ensures retrieval even after high-impact collisions, and a standardized data interface compatible with widely used analytical platforms. The device must also be manufacturable at a scale sufficient to support international research initiatives and regulatory compliance programs without compromising performance or reliability. The invention described herein fulfills these requirements by integrating advanced microelectromechanical systems, high-reliability mechanical design, and intelligent data acquisition protocols into a compact, field-ready instrument that significantly surpasses the capabilities of its predecessor.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to an autonomous, instrumented sensor device designed for in situ measurement of hydraulic conditions experienced by aquatic organisms during passage through complex hydraulic structures such as turbines, spillways, and fish bypass systems. The device is configured to replicate the physical dimensions, density, and buoyancy characteristics of a juvenile salmonid, enabling it to faithfully follow the same flow paths and experience the same hydrodynamic forces as a biological organism. The sensor device comprises a hermetically sealed cylindrical housing containing a suite of integrated microelectromechanical sensors, a low-power microcontroller, non-volatile memory, a rechargeable battery, a recovery mechanism, and a radiofrequency communication module. The sensors include a three-axis linear accelerometer with a full-scale range of ±200 g and a shock survival rating of 10,000 g, a three-axis gyroscope with a full-scale range of ±2000 degrees per second, a three-axis magnetometer and secondary accelerometer integrated into a single electronic compass module, a high-range pressure sensor capable of measuring up to 12 bars absolute pressure with an overpressure tolerance of 30 bars, and a precision temperature sensor calibrated for operation across a range of -40 to +125 degrees Celsius. All sensors are mounted on a rigid circuit board positioned such that their sensing elements coincide with the geometric center of mass of the device, thereby minimizing measurement errors due to inertial coupling and rotational dynamics.

The microcontroller is programmed to initiate a self-diagnostic sequence upon activation by an external magnetic field, verifying battery voltage, memory integrity, and sensor functionality before commencing data acquisition. Data are sampled at a rate of 2048 Hz and stored in internal flash memory for a duration of up to five minutes, ensuring comprehensive capture of transient events such as blade strikes, pressure spikes, and vortex interactions. The device is neutrally buoyant at deployment and becomes positively buoyant following activation of a recovery mechanism that consists of two independently controlled nichrome wire cutters, each actuated by a programmable current pulse that severs a tensioned fishing line attached to a weighted recovery module. Upon release of the first weight, the device ascends to the surface, where it activates a radiofrequency transmitter operating at 146 MHz and four high-intensity orange light-emitting diodes arranged to ensure visibility from any orientation. The RF signal and LED flashes are pulsed at regular intervals to facilitate visual and electronic detection during recovery operations.

The device is powered by a rechargeable lithium-ion battery that supports continuous operation for the full duration of data acquisition and recovery sequence, with sufficient reserve capacity to ensure reliable performance under extreme environmental conditions. A docking station equipped with a two-pin serial interface enables simultaneous charging and data download for up to four devices, using a transistor-transistor-logic to universal serial bus converter to interface with standard computing platforms. A dedicated software application, developed for data post-processing, converts raw binary sensor outputs into calibrated physical units using factory-provided calibration coefficients, generates time-series plots, exports data in comma-separated value format, and provides diagnostic feedback on sensor health and data integrity. The device is manufactured using corrosion-resistant materials, including a sealed aluminum housing, silicone gaskets, and epoxy encapsulation of sensitive components, ensuring long-term durability in freshwater, brackish, and saltwater environments.

The invention provides a significant advancement over prior art by integrating high-fidelity, high-dynamic-range sensors into a compact, robust, and cost-effective platform that is capable of surviving and accurately recording conditions previously considered too extreme for reliable measurement. The device’s ability to capture pressure transients exceeding 100 bar per second, linear accelerations in excess of 600 g, and rotational velocities approaching 2000 degrees per second enables the identification of previously undocumented hydraulic hazards associated with turbine passage. The recovery mechanism is designed to function reliably even after multiple high-impact collisions, and the dual-weight system ensures surface emergence even if one recovery module fails. The standardized data format and open communication protocol facilitate interoperability with third-party analytical tools and regulatory databases, promoting widespread adoption across international hydropower research communities. The device has been validated in both controlled laboratory environments and real-world field deployments, demonstrating measurement accuracy within ±2% for pressure, ±5% for acceleration and rotation, ±4 degrees for orientation, and ±2 degrees Celsius for temperature. The invention enables the systematic evaluation of turbine design modifications, operational protocols, and fish passage strategies with unprecedented precision, thereby supporting the sustainable development of hydropower infrastructure while minimizing ecological impact.

## DESCRIPTION

The invention comprises an autonomous, instrumented sensor device designed for the in situ measurement of hydraulic conditions experienced by aquatic organisms during passage through high-energy hydraulic structures such as turbines, spillways, and fish bypass systems. The device is engineered to replicate the physical properties of a juvenile salmonid, specifically a yearling smolt, in terms of size, density, and neutral buoyancy, ensuring that it follows the same hydrodynamic trajectories and experiences the same fluid forces as a biological organism. The device is constructed as a cylindrical housing with a diameter of 24.5 millimeters and a length of 89.9 millimeters, weighing approximately 42.1 grams in air and designed to achieve neutral buoyancy in freshwater at the time of deployment. The housing is fabricated from a corrosion-resistant aluminum alloy, machined to precise tolerances and sealed with a double-layer silicone gasket system to maintain watertight integrity under pressures up to 30 bars absolute. The internal components are mounted on a rigid printed circuit board composed of high-temperature fiberglass-reinforced epoxy, with flexible interconnects used to accommodate mechanical stress during impact events. The entire assembly is encapsulated in a thermally conductive epoxy compound to ensure uniform heat transfer between internal components and the outer housing, thereby minimizing thermal gradients that could compromise sensor accuracy.

At the core of the device is a low-power, 32-bit microcontroller that serves as the central processing unit, coordinating data acquisition, timing, memory management, and communication functions. The microcontroller is programmed with firmware that initiates a comprehensive self-diagnostic sequence upon activation by the application of an external magnetic field to a reed switch located on the main circuit board. The diagnostic sequence verifies the integrity of the battery voltage, confirms the absence of residual data in the non-volatile flash memory, and performs a functional check on each sensor channel. If all parameters fall within predefined operational thresholds, the microcontroller activates a status light-emitting diode to indicate a yellow pulse, signaling the commencement of a configurable data acquisition delay period. During this delay, which may be programmed by the user to range from zero to thirty minutes, the device remains in a low-power standby mode, conserving energy while awaiting release into the target hydraulic environment. If any component fails the diagnostic check, the status LED flashes red to indicate a fault condition, and the device remains inactive until manually reset and retested.

Following the delay period, the microcontroller initiates data acquisition by enabling all sensor channels simultaneously at a sampling frequency of 2048 Hertz. The data are digitized using 16-bit analog-to-digital converters integrated within each sensor module and stored in a 128-megabyte non-volatile flash memory array. The sampling rate is selected to ensure sufficient temporal resolution for capturing high-frequency transient events such as pressure spikes, blade strikes, and vortex shedding, which may occur on timescales of milliseconds or less. The memory capacity is sufficient to store approximately five minutes of continuous data, allowing the device to record the full duration of passage through even the most complex hydraulic systems. The microcontroller employs a circular buffer architecture to manage memory allocation, ensuring that the most recent data are retained even if the device is recovered before the full acquisition period has elapsed. Each data sample is timestamped with a high-resolution internal clock, synchronized at the start of acquisition and drift-corrected using temperature-dependent compensation algorithms to maintain temporal accuracy across varying environmental conditions.

The device incorporates a three-axis linear accelerometer based on a chip-scale package analog sensor with a full-scale range of ±200 g and a shock survival rating of 10,000 g. The accelerometer is mounted at the geometric center of the circuit board, which coincides with the center of mass of the entire device, to minimize inertial coupling and ensure that the measured acceleration reflects the true translational motion of the device without distortion from rotational dynamics. The sensor exhibits a bandwidth selectable between 0.5 and 1300 Hertz for the x- and y-axes, and between 0.5 and 1000 Hertz for the z-axis, allowing the user to optimize the frequency response for specific applications. The output signal is conditioned by a low-noise instrumentation amplifier with a fixed gain of 13.0 and an offset correction of 40 millivolts, enabling the full dynamic range to be mapped to the input voltage range of the microcontroller’s analog-to-digital converter. The accelerometer is calibrated prior to assembly using a linear acceleration test track equipped with a triaxial constant-current line drive accelerometer traceable to NIST standards. The calibration procedure involves repeated high-impact collisions against a rigid stopper, with the output of the reference sensor used to derive a polynomial correction function for each axis. The relative error of the acceleration measurements, as determined through repeated testing, is consistently less than 5%, with the majority of measurements falling within ±2%.

A three-axis digital gyroscope is integrated into the device to measure rotational velocity about each principal axis. The gyroscope is a microelectromechanical systems component with a full-scale range of ±2000 degrees per second and includes built-in 16-bit analog-to-digital converters for direct digital output. The device is mounted adjacent to the accelerometer, with its sensing axes aligned to coincide with the same coordinate system, ensuring consistent frame of reference for all measurements. The gyroscope includes an internal low-pass filter with user-selectable bandwidth settings and an inter-integrated circuit serial interface for communication with the microcontroller. The initial zero-rate output is specified at ±40 degrees per second, and the sensitivity to linear acceleration is less than 0.1 degrees per second per g, minimizing cross-axis interference during high-acceleration events. The gyroscope is calibrated using a high-speed videography system in which the device is mounted on a precision rotation fixture capable of generating controlled angular velocities ranging from ±150 to ±2000 degrees per second. The rotational motion is recorded using a digital high-speed camera operating at frame rates up to 16,000 frames per second, and the angular displacement is computed using motion-tracking software. The gyroscope output is compared to the video-derived angular velocity to derive calibration coefficients for each axis. The relative error of the rotational velocity measurements is less than 5%, with the majority of measurements demonstrating accuracy within ±2%.

The device further incorporates an electronic compass module that integrates a three-axis digital accelerometer and a three-axis digital magnetometer into a single package. The accelerometer within the compass module has a maximum full-scale range of ±16 g and is not intended for use during high-impact events but serves to augment the primary accelerometer’s data during low-acceleration phases of passage, improving the accuracy of orientation estimation. The magnetometer has a selectable full-scale range of up to ±8.1 gauss and is used to determine the absolute orientation of the device relative to the Earth’s magnetic field. The module communicates via an inter-integrated circuit interface and includes an on-board temperature sensor for thermal compensation. The compass is calibrated using the same rotation test fixture employed for gyroscope calibration, with the device rotated slowly through a full 360-degree plane at a constant rate of 150 degrees per second. The heading output is compared to a known magnetic north reference established using a precision compass aligned with local geomagnetic field measurements. The accuracy of the orientation determination is within ±4 degrees, enabling the reconstruction of the device’s attitude throughout its passage, which is critical for interpreting the directionality of forces and identifying the orientation of impact events.

The pressure sensor is a micromachined silicon diaphragm sensor of the Wheatstone bridge type, mounted on a ceramic substrate and sealed within a flexible printed circuit board that is bonded directly to the inner surface of the aluminum housing. The sensor has a full-scale measurement range of 12 bars absolute pressure, with an overpressure tolerance of 30 bars, enabling it to operate reliably in high-head turbine environments and during rapid decompression events. The sensor output is a differential voltage signal with a typical full-scale span of 150 millivolts and a zero offset of ±40 millivolts. This signal is amplified by a precision instrumentation amplifier with a gain of 13.0 and an offset correction of 40 millivolts, ensuring that the full pressure range is mapped to the input voltage range of the microcontroller’s analog-to-digital converter. The sensor is calibrated prior to assembly using a pressure chamber equipped with a reference water level logger traceable to NIST standards, with calibration points established at five discrete pressure levels spanning the operational range. After assembly, the complete device is subjected to a hyperbaric chamber test in which the pressure-time history of a turbine passage is simulated, and the sensor output is compared to that of a high-accuracy Honeywell pressure transducer. The relative error of the pressure measurements is consistently less than 2%, demonstrating exceptional fidelity in capturing both static pressure and rapid transients with rates of change exceeding 100 bar per second.

A primary temperature sensor is mounted in close proximity to the pressure sensor, with the intervening space filled with a thermally conductive epoxy compound to ensure efficient heat transfer between the sensor and the metal housing, which is in direct contact with the surrounding water. The temperature sensor is an analog device with a linear temperature coefficient of 6.25 millivolts per degree Celsius and a full-scale operating range of -40 to +125 degrees Celsius. The sensor is calibrated using a precision digital thermocouple thermometer with a stated accuracy of 0.1% and a response time of one second. Calibration is performed in a slush bath of crushed ice and deionized water, maintained at 0 degrees Celsius, and at ambient room temperature. The difference between the sensor reading and the reference thermometer is consistently less than ±2 degrees Celsius across the entire operating range, ensuring that temperature corrections applied to pressure and other sensor measurements are accurate and reliable.

The device includes a dual-recovery mechanism designed to ensure surface emergence even under adverse conditions. Two recovery modules are located at opposite ends of the cylindrical housing, each consisting of a spring-loaded weight tethered by a length of high-strength fishing line that passes over a thin nichrome wire. The nichrome wire is electrically connected to the microcontroller and is configured to carry a brief, high-current pulse sufficient to heat the wire to a temperature exceeding its melting point, thereby severing the fishing line and releasing the weight. The microcontroller is programmed to activate the first recovery module after the completion of data acquisition, initiating a configurable resurface delay period during which the device continues to record environmental conditions. Upon expiration of the resurface delay, the microcontroller triggers the first nichrome wire, releasing the first weight and rendering the device positively buoyant. The device then ascends to the surface, where it activates a radiofrequency transmitter operating at a carrier frequency of 146 megahertz, compatible with existing receiver equipment used by field personnel. Simultaneously, four high-intensity orange light-emitting diodes are activated in a pulsed pattern, oriented radially outward to ensure visibility regardless of the device’s orientation upon surfacing. The color of the LEDs is selected to provide maximum contrast against water and sky, though alternative colors may be substituted for specific operational environments. After the first weight is released, the device continues to transmit RF pulses and flash LEDs at regular intervals until manually deactivated by the application of a magnetic field to the reed switch. If the first recovery module fails to activate, the microcontroller is programmed to trigger the second recovery module after a predetermined timeout, ensuring that the device will still surface even under partial system failure.

The device is powered by a rechargeable lithium-ion battery with a nominal capacity of 1200 milliampere-hours, providing sufficient energy to support continuous data acquisition for five minutes, followed by the activation of the recovery mechanism, RF transmission, and LED flashing for up to 24 hours. The battery is charged via a docking station that connects to the device through a two-pin serial interface located on the download board. The docking station is capable of servicing up to four devices simultaneously and includes a transistor-transistor-logic to universal serial bus converter that enables data transfer to a personal computer running the Sensor Fish Communicator software. The software provides a graphical user interface for configuring device parameters, downloading raw data files, converting binary data into calibrated physical units using embedded calibration coefficients, plotting time-series data for each sensor channel, and exporting results in comma-separated value format for use in third-party analytical packages. The software also performs diagnostic checks on data integrity, flags anomalous sensor readings, and generates a summary report indicating the overall health of the device and the reliability of the collected data. The communication protocol operates at a baud rate of 921.6 kilobits per second, with 8 data bits, 1 start bit, 1 stop bit, and no parity, ensuring rapid and error-free data transfer.

The device is manufactured using a modular assembly process that ensures consistency and traceability across all units. Each sensor is individually calibrated prior to integration, and each assembled device undergoes a final acceptance test that includes a full diagnostic sequence, a pressure chamber test, a high-impact shock test, and a recovery mechanism activation test. The device is designed to withstand up to 100 impact events at accelerations of up to 600 g without loss of functionality, and the recovery mechanism has been demonstrated to operate reliably even after repeated high-velocity collisions with rigid surfaces. The housing is sealed using a double O-ring system with a silicone elastomer that maintains its elasticity across the full operating temperature range, and all internal components are potted in a thermally conductive, non-conductive epoxy to prevent movement and dampen vibration. The circuit board is mounted using shock-absorbing mounts to isolate sensitive components from mechanical stress, and all electrical connections are reinforced with strain relief to prevent wire fatigue.

The device has been validated in both laboratory and field environments. In laboratory tests, the device was subjected to simulated turbine passage conditions in a hyperbaric chamber, and its measurements were compared to those of reference instruments with known accuracy. In field tests conducted at the Ice Harbor Dam on the Snake River, the device was deployed from a depth of 15.2 meters upstream of a spillway opening, and data were successfully recovered from all sensors following passage through the turbulent flow field. The device surfaced as designed, and all sensor data were retrieved without corruption. The recovery mechanism functioned reliably, and the RF transmitter and LEDs were clearly detectable by both electronic and visual means. The device has since been deployed in multiple international locations, including high-head Francis turbine installations and pumped storage facilities, where it has consistently demonstrated superior performance compared to prior-generation devices. The invention provides a standardized, reliable, and cost-effective tool for the quantitative assessment of hydraulic conditions affecting aquatic organisms, enabling engineers and biologists to design and operate hydropower infrastructure with greater ecological awareness and precision.
