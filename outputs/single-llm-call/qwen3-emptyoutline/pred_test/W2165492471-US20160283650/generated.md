# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The biological phenomenon of synthetic lethality has long been recognized as a critical mechanism for understanding genetic interactions, particularly in the context of cellular viability and disease pathogenesis. Synthetic lethality occurs when the simultaneous disruption or inhibition of two nonessential genes leads to cell death, whereas the disruption of either gene alone has no such effect. This concept, first observed in yeast genetics, has since been extended to higher eukaryotes, including humans, where it holds profound implications for therapeutic intervention—especially in oncology. The rationale for exploiting synthetic lethality in cancer therapy is grounded in the principle that cancer cells often harbor specific genetic vulnerabilities, such as mutations in tumor suppressor genes or DNA repair pathways, which render them dependent on compensatory mechanisms encoded by other genes. By pharmacologically targeting these compensatory genes, it becomes possible to selectively kill cancer cells while sparing normal cells that retain functional redundancy. This selective vulnerability forms the foundation of precision medicine strategies aimed at minimizing systemic toxicity while maximizing therapeutic efficacy.

Despite its therapeutic promise, the systematic identification of synthetic lethal gene pairs in humans remains an immense challenge. The sheer combinatorial complexity of the human genome renders exhaustive experimental screening infeasible. With over 20,000 protein-coding genes, the number of unique gene pairs exceeds 200 million, and each pair would require a labor-intensive, context-specific assay to determine synthetic lethal status. Even in well-characterized model organisms such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae, only a fraction of potential synthetic lethal interactions have been empirically validated, and the experimental burden increases exponentially when attempting to replicate such efforts across multiple cellular contexts, tissue types, or disease states. Furthermore, ethical and practical constraints preclude large-scale genetic knockout studies in human subjects, limiting the availability of direct human data. As a result, computational methods have become indispensable for prioritizing candidate gene pairs for downstream experimental validation.

Prior computational approaches to predicting synthetic lethality have largely relied on evolutionary conservation, particularly the assumption that synthetic lethal relationships are preserved between orthologous gene pairs across species. These methods typically map experimentally validated synthetic lethal pairs from model organisms such as yeast to their human homologs, under the premise that functional conservation implies conservation of genetic interaction topology. While this strategy has yielded some successes, its utility is severely constrained by the limited number of genes that are evolutionarily conserved between yeast and humans. Approximately 99% of human genes lack a direct one-to-one ortholog in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, meaning that the vast majority of potential synthetic lethal interactions remain inaccessible to homology-based prediction. Moreover, even among conserved genes, functional divergence due to gene duplication, neofunctionalization, or subfunctionalization can disrupt synthetic lethal relationships. For instance, paralogous gene families that have arisen independently in different lineages may provide compensatory functions that mask or alter synthetic lethal interactions, rendering orthology-based predictions unreliable. Additional methods have attempted to leverage structural, domain, or functional annotations—such as protein fold classification, Pfam domain overlap, or Gene Ontology term similarity—to infer synthetic lethality. While these approaches offer incremental improvements, they are fundamentally limited by their dependence on pre-existing annotations, which are incomplete, biased toward well-studied genes, and often fail to capture the dynamic, context-dependent nature of protein interactions within cellular networks.

Another class of methods has explored network-based metrics, such as information centrality, to quantify the topological relationship between gene pairs. However, these univariate approaches have demonstrated limited predictive power, as they fail to account for the multidimensional nature of biological networks. Synthetic lethality is not a property of individual genes but rather an emergent characteristic of the interaction landscape between two genes within the broader context of cellular wiring. The failure of prior models to capture this complexity has resulted in low sensitivity, poor generalizability across species, and an inability to predict synthetic lethality in organisms with sparse experimental data. Crucially, no existing method has successfully demonstrated the ability to predict synthetic lethal interactions in a species with no prior experimental evidence of such interactions, relying instead on the availability of annotated gene pairs in the target organism. This limitation severely restricts the applicability of computational models to understudied species or novel biological contexts, including rare disease states or non-cancerous tissues.

The fundamental gap in current methodologies lies in their reliance on static, sequence- or annotation-based features that do not reflect the dynamic, systems-level organization of biological networks. Protein-protein interaction networks, which encode the physical and functional relationships between gene products, represent a rich, largely untapped source of information for inferring genetic interactions. These networks capture not only direct binding partners but also indirect functional relationships through shared neighbors, pathway proximity, and topological roles such as hubs or bottlenecks. The hypothesis that synthetic lethality arises from the disruption of complementary network modules—rather than from sequence or functional similarity alone—has remained largely untested at scale. What is needed is a predictive framework that transcends species boundaries by identifying conserved patterns of network connectivity that are indicative of synthetic lethality, independent of gene sequence, protein structure, or functional annotation. Such a framework would enable the translation of knowledge from well-characterized model organisms to humans and other species with minimal prior data, thereby unlocking a new paradigm for systematic discovery of therapeutic targets.

This invention addresses this critical unmet need by introducing a novel computational method—Species-INdependent TRAnslation (SINaTRA)—that leverages the topological architecture of protein-protein interaction networks to predict synthetic lethal relationships across species without reliance on genetic homology, structural similarity, or functional annotation. By training a machine learning model on network-based features derived from the extensively characterized yeast genome and applying it to the human interactome, SINaTRA identifies conserved patterns of connectivity that are predictive of synthetic lethality. The method operates on the principle of connectivity homology: two gene pairs are considered connectivity homologous if their respective nodes exhibit similar patterns of network participation, regardless of whether the genes themselves are evolutionarily related. This approach enables the prediction of synthetic lethal interactions in species with no prior experimental data, with high accuracy and robustness to network incompleteness. Furthermore, by integrating population-level human genomic data to filter out likely false positives, SINaTRA generates a high-confidence catalog of putative synthetic lethal pairs in humans, many of which are enriched in cancer-relevant pathways and correspond to known drug combination targets. The resulting system provides a scalable, species-agnostic platform for the discovery of novel polypharmacological therapies, transforming the way synthetic lethality is studied, predicted, and exploited in biomedical research.

## SUMMARY

This invention discloses a novel computational method for predicting synthetic lethal interactions between pairs of genes in any species, using only a protein-protein interaction network as input and without requiring prior knowledge of synthetic lethal pairs in the target species. The method, termed Species-INdependent TRAnslation (SINaTRA), operates by first deriving a comprehensive set of network-based features from the topology of protein-protein interactions in a well-characterized source organism, such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae, where synthetic lethal interactions have been extensively experimentally validated. These features include eight single-node parameters and four node-pair parameters, each quantifying distinct aspects of network connectivity such as degree centrality, betweenness centrality, shortest path length, communicability, shared neighbors, and shared non-neighbors. The parameters are normalized using rank-based translation to account for differences in network size and density across species, enabling direct comparison of connectivity profiles between organisms. A supervised machine learning model, trained on experimentally confirmed synthetic lethal and non-synthetic lethal gene pairs in the source organism, learns to recognize the multivariate patterns of network connectivity that are statistically associated with synthetic lethality. Once trained, this model is applied to the protein-protein interaction network of a target species, such as Homo sapiens, to generate a continuous SINaTRA score between 0 and 1 for every possible gene pair, representing the probability that the pair exhibits a synthetic lethal relationship.

The invention further includes a post-processing step that enhances prediction accuracy by filtering out gene pairs that are unlikely to be synthetic lethal based on population genomic data. Specifically, gene pairs in which both genes are simultaneously homozygous for severe, deleterious mutations in human populations are identified as non-synthetic lethal, as such mutations would otherwise be incompatible with viability. These pairs are excluded from the final prediction set, thereby reducing the false positive rate and enriching for biologically relevant interactions. The resulting high-confidence synthetic lethal pairs are then analyzed for enrichment in biological pathways, protein complexes, and cancer drug targets, revealing that predicted pairs are significantly more likely to reside within the same pathway or protein complex and to correspond to genes targeted by existing or investigational combination therapies. The invention further enables context-specific prediction by integrating tissue- and cell-type-specific gene expression data, allowing for the refinement of synthetic lethal predictions to particular physiological environments, such as tumor tissues or specific organ systems. The method is robust to incomplete network data, maintaining high predictive performance even when up to 50% of the protein-protein interaction edges are randomly removed, and is not confounded by biases related to gene popularity or experimental study frequency. Importantly, SINaTRA requires no prior knowledge of synthetic lethal interactions in the target species, making it uniquely suited for predicting synthetic lethality in organisms with sparse experimental data, including non-model species and human subpopulations with rare genetic variants. The invention provides a comprehensive, scalable, and species-independent framework for the discovery of synthetic lethal interactions, enabling the rational design of combination therapies that exploit genetic vulnerabilities in cancer and other diseases. The output of the method is a ranked list of gene pairs with associated SINaTRA scores, a database of filtered high-confidence predictions, and a set of tools for identifying "hot zones" of synergistic drug targets, all of which are freely accessible for use in drug discovery, precision medicine, and systems biology research.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention comprises a computational system and method for predicting synthetic lethal interactions between pairs of genes in any species, using only a protein-protein interaction network as input and without requiring prior experimental knowledge of synthetic lethality in the target organism. The method is grounded in the discovery that synthetic lethality is not determined by sequence homology, structural similarity, or functional annotation, but rather by conserved patterns of network connectivity that transcend evolutionary divergence. These patterns, referred to as connectivity homology, describe the degree to which two gene pairs exhibit analogous topological roles within their respective biological networks, even when the underlying genes are not orthologous. The invention leverages this principle to train a machine learning model on synthetic lethal interactions in a well-characterized source species, such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae, and then applies the learned model to the protein-protein interaction network of a target species, such as Homo sapiens, to generate probabilistic predictions of synthetic lethality for all possible gene pairs. The system operates independently of gene sequence, protein structure, or functional annotation, relying solely on the structural topology of biological networks to infer genetic interactions. This approach enables the prediction of synthetic lethal relationships in species with no prior experimental data, overcoming the fundamental limitations of homology-based and annotation-dependent methods that have dominated the field to date.

The method begins with the acquisition of a high-quality protein-protein interaction network for a source species in which synthetic lethal interactions have been extensively validated through experimental screens. In the preferred embodiment, this source species is Saccharomyces cerevisiae, for which a comprehensive and well-curated network has been assembled from multiple high-throughput datasets, including yeast two-hybrid and tandem affinity purification experiments. The network is represented as an undirected graph in which nodes correspond to genes and edges represent physical interactions between their encoded protein products. To ensure network integrity and avoid artifacts arising from disconnected components, the network is pruned to retain only the largest connected component, thereby ensuring that all gene pairs under analysis are topologically interrelated. From this network, a set of twenty network parameters is computed for each gene pair, comprising eight single-node parameters for each gene and four node-pair parameters that describe the relationship between the two genes. The single-node parameters include degree centrality, betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, eigenvector centrality, clustering coefficient, eccentricity, PageRank, and neighborhood connectivity. These parameters collectively capture the topological role of each gene within the network, identifying whether it functions as a hub, a bottleneck, a peripheral node, or a densely connected module member. The node-pair parameters include the shortest path length between the two genes, the number of shared neighbors, the number of shared non-neighbors, and communicability, which quantifies the total number of paths—both direct and indirect—between the two nodes, weighted by path length. These parameters are chosen because they reflect the structural and functional interdependence between gene products, which is hypothesized to underlie synthetic lethality: when two genes occupy complementary topological positions, their simultaneous disruption may collapse essential network functions, leading to cell death.

To enable cross-species translation, the raw values of these parameters are normalized using a rank-based translation method. This normalization is critical because network parameters vary significantly in magnitude and distribution across species due to differences in genome size, network density, and experimental coverage. For example, a degree centrality of 50 in yeast may correspond to a degree of 200 in humans, but the relative position of a gene within its network’s degree distribution may be comparable. Rank normalization addresses this by converting each parameter value into its percentile rank within the distribution of that parameter across all genes (for single-node parameters) or all gene pairs (for node-pair parameters). Thus, each parameter is rescaled to a value between 0 and 1, where 0 represents the lowest observed value and 1 represents the highest. This transformation ensures that connectivity profiles are comparable across species, irrespective of absolute network size or edge density. The resulting feature vectors, each consisting of twenty normalized parameters, are then used to train a supervised machine learning classifier. In the preferred embodiment, a random forest algorithm with 100 decision trees is employed due to its high predictive accuracy, resistance to overfitting, and ability to handle high-dimensional, non-linear data. The classifier is trained using experimentally validated synthetic lethal gene pairs as positive examples and randomly selected non-synthetic lethal pairs as negative examples, with the assumption that the vast majority of uncharacterized pairs are non-synthetic lethal, a reasonable approximation given the estimated frequency of synthetic lethality in diploid organisms is approximately one in one thousand. The model is validated using five-fold cross-validation, ensuring that performance metrics such as the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) reflect robust generalization rather than overfitting to training data. The trained model is then applied to the protein-protein interaction network of the target species, generating a SINaTRA score for every possible gene pair, with higher scores indicating greater likelihood of synthetic lethality.

The invention further incorporates a filtering step to reduce false positives by leveraging population genomic data from human cohorts. Specifically, gene pairs in which both genes are simultaneously homozygous for severe, deleterious mutations in human populations are identified as non-synthetic lethal, as such mutations would otherwise be incompatible with embryonic or postnatal viability. These mutations are identified from publicly available exome and genome sequencing datasets, such as the 1000 Genomes Project and the Sweden-Schizophrenia Population-Based Case-Control Exome Sequencing study, using variant annotation tools to classify mutations as high-impact, including nonsense, frameshift, splice-site, or start-loss variants. Gene pairs meeting these criteria are excluded from the final prediction set, as their co-occurrence in healthy individuals demonstrates that their simultaneous loss does not result in lethality. This filtering step significantly enhances the precision of the predictions, reducing the false discovery rate to less than 0.4% at a SINaTRA score threshold of 0.85. The resulting set of high-confidence synthetic lethal pairs is then subjected to biological validation through enrichment analysis, revealing significant overrepresentation in known biological pathways, protein complexes, and cancer drug targets. For example, gene pairs with SINaTRA scores above 0.95 are found to be significantly enriched for membership in the same KEGG or Reactome pathway, with a greater than threefold increase in within-pathway interactions compared to random expectation. Similarly, gene pairs whose products are components of the same protein complex, as defined by the CORUM database, exhibit significantly higher SINaTRA scores than those not sharing complex membership, with odds ratios exceeding 148 for pairs with scores above 0.95. These findings confirm that the method is not merely identifying random topological correlations but is instead capturing biologically meaningful interactions with established mechanistic relevance.

The invention further enables context-specific prediction by integrating tissue- and cell-type-specific gene expression data from the Human Protein Atlas. By filtering out gene pairs in which either gene is not expressed in a given tissue or cell line, the method generates context-restricted synthetic lethal predictions that reflect the physiological state of the target environment. This capability is particularly valuable in oncology, where synthetic lethality is often context-dependent, manifesting only in cancer cells due to altered gene expression, signaling pathway activation, or metabolic reprogramming. For instance, respiratory epithelial cells and endothelial cells show a significantly lower than expected number of retained synthetic lethal pairs after filtering, suggesting that these tissues may possess inherent robustness to dual-gene disruption, whereas tissues such as the small intestine exhibit a higher-than-expected number of filtered pairs, indicating potential susceptibility to synthetic lethality under certain conditions. These observations open new avenues for tissue-specific therapeutic design and for understanding the biological basis of differential drug toxicity.

The method is also shown to be robust to network incompleteness, a critical feature given that protein-protein interaction networks for most species, including humans, remain substantially incomplete. When the target network is systematically ablated by removing up to 50% of its edges at random, the predictive performance of SINaTRA declines only marginally, with the AUC remaining above 0.79 even under extreme network sparsity. This resilience arises because the method relies on relative connectivity patterns rather than absolute edge counts, making it less sensitive to missing interactions than methods that depend on direct physical proximity or pathway membership. Furthermore, the method is not confounded by biases related to gene popularity or experimental study frequency. Although highly connected genes are more likely to be studied and thus more likely to be included in synthetic lethal screens, the SINaTRA score shows no significant correlation with normalized popularity, defined as the ratio of a gene’s degree to the number of times it appears in the BioGRID database. This indicates that the method is detecting true biological signals rather than artifacts of research bias. The invention further demonstrates superior performance compared to all previously published methods, including homology-based, structural similarity-based, domain-based, functional annotation-based, and information centrality-based approaches, achieving an AUC of 0.91 in cross-species prediction tasks and outperforming the next best method by more than 30 percentage points. The positive predictive value of SINaTRA reaches 17% at a score threshold of 0.95, corresponding to a 170-fold increase over the expected rate of synthetic lethality by chance, and is further enhanced when combined with biological priors such as protein complex membership or pathway co-localization.

The output of the method is a comprehensive database of synthetic lethal predictions for all possible gene pairs in the target species, accompanied by SINaTRA scores, filtered high-confidence pairs, and annotations for pathway membership, complex association, and drug target overlap. The invention further provides tools for identifying "hot zones" of synergistic drug combinations, where multiple gene pairs with high SINaTRA scores are co-targeted by existing or investigational cancer therapies. These hot zones are not only enriched for known combination therapies but also reveal novel, unexplored pairs that are highly predictive of synthetic lethality and represent promising candidates for future drug development. The method is fully automated, scalable, and applicable to any species with a protein-protein interaction network, making it a transformative tool for systems biology, precision medicine, and drug discovery. The invention thus provides a first-of-its-kind, species-independent framework for the systematic discovery of synthetic lethal interactions, enabling the rational design of combination therapies that exploit genetic vulnerabilities with unprecedented precision and scope.

### EXAMPLE 1

In this example, the invention is applied to predict synthetic lethal interactions in Schizosaccharomyces pombe using data derived from Saccharomyces cerevisiae as the source organism. The protein-protein interaction network for S. cerevisiae is constructed from experimentally validated interactions obtained from the BioGRID database, version 3.2.104, and is pruned to retain only the largest connected component, resulting in a network comprising 5,810 genes and 58,732 interactions. From this network, twenty network parameters are computed for each of the 16.8 million possible gene pairs, including eight single-node parameters—degree centrality, betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, eigenvector centrality, clustering coefficient, eccentricity, PageRank, and neighborhood connectivity—and four node-pair parameters—shortest path length, number of shared neighbors, number of shared non-neighbors, and communicability. These parameters are then rank-normalized to ensure comparability across species, with each value scaled to a range between 0 and 1 based on its percentile within the distribution of that parameter across all genes or gene pairs in the source network. The normalized feature vectors are used to train a random forest classifier with 100 decision trees, using 13,196 experimentally validated synthetic lethal pairs as positive examples and an equal number of randomly selected non-synthetic lethal pairs as negative examples. The classifier is validated using five-fold cross-validation, achieving an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.92, demonstrating high accuracy in predicting synthetic lethality within the source species.

The trained model is then applied to the protein-protein interaction network of S. pombe, which is similarly constructed from BioGRID data and contains 1,919 genes and 19,847 interactions. The same twenty network parameters are computed for each of the 1.8 million possible gene pairs in S. pombe and rank-normalized using the same scaling parameters derived from the S. cerevisiae network. The SINaTRA score for each pair is generated by the random forest model, producing a continuous probability score between 0 and 1. The performance of the model in predicting synthetic lethality in S. pombe is evaluated against the 628 experimentally validated synthetic lethal pairs in that organism. The model achieves an AUC of 0.86, significantly outperforming all alternative methods, including homology-based prediction (AUC = 0.60), functional similarity based on Gene Ontology annotations (AUC = 0.81), structural similarity based on SCOP domain classification (AUC = 0.62), and pairwise information centrality (AUC = 0.46). The positive predictive value of the model at a SINaTRA score threshold of 0.85 is 7%, corresponding to a 70-fold increase over the expected rate of synthetic lethality by chance (0.1%), and increases to 17% at a threshold of 0.95, representing a 170-fold enrichment. The model identifies 1,759 gene pairs with SINaTRA scores ≥0.85, of which only 65 had been previously experimentally validated, suggesting that over 1,690 novel synthetic lethal interactions are predicted for the first time. The method is further shown to be robust to network incompleteness: when 50% of the S. pombe network edges are randomly removed, the AUC remains at 0.79, demonstrating that the predictive signal is not dependent on complete network coverage. The model also shows no correlation between SINaTRA score and gene popularity, as measured by the number of times a gene appears in the BioGRID database, confirming that the predictions are not biased toward well-studied genes. Finally, the predicted synthetic lethal pairs are significantly enriched for membership in protein complexes, with a 148-fold increase in odds ratio for pairs within the same complex at a SINaTRA score of 0.95, and for participation in the same biological pathway, with a Fisher’s exact test p-value of less than 2.2e-16. These results demonstrate that the invention successfully translates synthetic lethal predictions from S. cerevisiae to S. pombe with high accuracy, without relying on sequence homology or functional annotation, and identifies a large number of novel, high-confidence interactions that are biologically coherent and mechanistically plausible.

### EXAMPLE 2

In this example, the invention is applied to predict synthetic lethal interactions in Mus musculus using data derived from Saccharomyces cerevisiae as the source organism. Since no comprehensive database of experimentally validated synthetic lethal pairs exists for mouse, the performance of the method is evaluated by comparing its predictions to known biological constraints and to the performance of alternative methods. The protein-protein interaction network for S. cerevisiae is constructed as described in Example 1, with 5,810 genes and 58,732 interactions, and the same twenty network parameters are computed and rank-normalized to form the training feature set. The random forest classifier is trained on the 13,196 experimentally validated synthetic lethal pairs in yeast and validated with an AUC of 0.92. The mouse protein-protein interaction network is obtained from BioGRID, comprising 4,233 genes and 42,156 interactions. The same twenty network parameters are computed for each of the 8.9 million possible gene pairs in mouse and rank-normalized using the scaling parameters derived from the yeast network. The SINaTRA scores for all mouse gene pairs are generated by the trained model, and the resulting distribution is analyzed for biological plausibility. The method is compared to alternative approaches, including Gene Ontology-based functional similarity, which achieves an AUC of 0.687, and homology-based prediction, which is inapplicable due to the lack of direct one-to-one orthologs for the majority of mouse genes. The SINaTRA model achieves an AUC of 0.937, significantly outperforming all other methods. Of the nine experimentally documented synthetic lethal pairs in mouse recorded in BioGRID, eight are predicted by SINaTRA with scores greater than or equal to 0.5, and five have scores greater than or equal to 0.70, indicating that the method correctly identifies nearly all known synthetic lethal interactions in mouse despite having no prior knowledge of them. The method further predicts 1,204 gene pairs with SINaTRA scores ≥0.85, representing high-confidence candidates for synthetic lethality. These pairs are significantly enriched for membership in protein complexes, with a 127-fold increase in odds ratio for within-complex pairs at a score threshold of 0.95, and for participation in the same biological pathway, with a Fisher’s exact test p-value of less than 2.2e-16. The predicted pairs are also enriched for genes involved in DNA repair, cell cycle regulation, and apoptosis—pathways known to be frequently disrupted in mouse models of cancer and developmental disorders. The method is further validated by examining the distribution of SINaTRA scores for gene pairs that are known to be essential for viability in mouse knockout studies. Gene pairs in which one gene is known to be lethal when homozygously knocked out are excluded from the analysis, as synthetic lethality requires both genes to be nonessential individually. The SINaTRA scores for these pairs are found to be significantly lower than those for non-essential pairs, confirming that the method distinguishes between lethal single-gene knockouts and synthetic lethal interactions. The results demonstrate that the invention can accurately predict synthetic lethal interactions in a mammalian species with no prior experimental data, outperforming all existing methods and providing a robust, scalable framework for the discovery of novel genetic interactions in organisms where experimental screens are impractical or ethically constrained.

### EXAMPLE 3

In this example, the invention is applied to predict synthetic lethal interactions in Homo sapiens, with the goal of identifying novel targets for cancer combination therapy. The protein-protein interaction network for S. cerevisiae is constructed and trained as described in Example 1, achieving an AUC of 0.92 in within-species validation. The human protein-protein interaction network is assembled from BioGRID, comprising 14,820 genes and 148,902 interactions. The same twenty network parameters are computed for each of the 109.8 million possible gene pairs in humans and rank-normalized using the scaling parameters derived from the yeast network. The trained random forest model generates a SINaTRA score for each pair, resulting in a continuous probability distribution ranging from 0 to 1. To enhance prediction accuracy, a post-processing filtering step is applied using population genomic data from the 1000 Genomes Project and the Sweden-Schizophrenia Population-Based Case-Control Exome Sequencing study. Gene pairs in which both genes are simultaneously homozygous for high-impact mutations—such as nonsense, frameshift, splice-site, or start-loss variants—in at least one individual and in no more than 5% of the population are identified as non-synthetic lethal, as their co-occurrence in healthy individuals demonstrates that their simultaneous loss is compatible with viability. A total of 450,010 such gene pairs are filtered out, reducing the false discovery rate to 0.36% at a SINaTRA score threshold of 0.85. The remaining high-confidence synthetic lethal predictions include 109,358,780 gene pairs with SINaTRA scores, of which 1,309 pairs have scores greater than 0.95 and are considered ultra-high-confidence predictions. These pairs are significantly enriched for membership in the same biological pathway, with a Fisher’s exact test p-value of less than 2.2e-16 for all cutoffs above 0.80, and for participation in the same protein complex, with a Mann-Whitney U test p-value of less than 2.2e-16. The top-scoring gene pairs are found to be enriched in pathways related to DNA repair, chromatin remodeling, transcriptional regulation, and apoptosis, consistent with known cancer vulnerabilities. The method further identifies "hot zones" of drug synergy by overlaying the SINaTRA scores with data from the Drug Combination Database, revealing that gene pairs targeted by existing or investigational combination therapies have significantly higher SINaTRA scores than those targeted by single agents or not under investigation. For example, gene pairs involving PARP1 and BRCA1/2, known to be synthetic lethal in BRCA-mutant cancers, are predicted with SINaTRA scores of 0.97 and 0.94, respectively, validating the method’s ability to recapitulate known biology. Novel hotspots are identified in regions of the network involving genes such as ATM, ATR, and CHEK1, which are not currently targeted in combination therapies but exhibit high SINaTRA scores and co-expression in tumor tissues. These pairs are further validated for context-specificity by integrating tissue expression data from the Human Protein Atlas, revealing that many high-scoring pairs are expressed in multiple cancer-relevant tissues, including breast, ovarian, and lung epithelium, but not in non-transformed tissues such as liver or muscle. The method also identifies previously unexplored synthetic lethal pairs involving genes such as BAIAP2 and ALDH7A1, which are not known to interact but are predicted with a SINaTRA score of 0.957 and are both implicated in oxidative stress and insulin signaling pathways, suggesting a novel mechanism of synthetic lethality that may be exploitable in metabolic cancers. The invention thus provides a comprehensive, high-confidence catalog of synthetic lethal interactions in humans, enabling the rational design of combination therapies that exploit genetic vulnerabilities with unprecedented precision, while simultaneously identifying novel targets for drug development that are not apparent through conventional approaches.
