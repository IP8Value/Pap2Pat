# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to systems and methods for assessing and communicating the heightened risk of seismic events on a global scale following the occurrence of large-magnitude earthquakes. Specifically, the invention provides a novel framework for identifying, quantifying, and disseminating temporal and spatial patterns of increased earthquake likelihood that emerge in the aftermath of major seismic events, with particular emphasis on induced seismicity occurring up to three days later and at distances far beyond traditional aftershock zones. The invention operates on the principle that large earthquakes generate global-scale dynamic perturbations capable of modulating stress states on distant fault systems, thereby increasing the probability of subsequent earthquakes of significant magnitude—particularly those exceeding M5.0—in specific regions of the Earth’s surface, most notably near the antipodal point relative to the source event. This method transforms previously unrecognized statistical correlations into a predictive and actionable seismic risk model, enabling real-time alerting systems that integrate geophysical data, probabilistic modeling, and spatial analytics to inform emergency preparedness, infrastructure management, and public safety protocols across national and international boundaries. The invention is applicable to seismological monitoring networks, governmental disaster response agencies, insurance risk modeling firms, and global early warning infrastructure, providing a scientifically grounded mechanism to extend the temporal and spatial scope of earthquake forecasting beyond conventional aftershock sequences and static hazard maps.

## BACKGROUND

Historically, the scientific community has operated under the assumption that earthquakes occur independently in space and time, governed by statistical models such as the Poisson distribution, wherein the likelihood of seismic events is determined solely by long-term historical rates and local tectonic conditions. This paradigm has underpinned seismic hazard assessments for decades, leading to the development of static fault-based models and probabilistic seismic hazard analyses that ignore any potential influence of distant seismic events on future earthquake occurrence. While it has long been recognized that earthquakes can trigger aftershocks within their immediate vicinity—typically within a few tens to hundreds of kilometers and over periods of hours to weeks—the notion that one earthquake could influence the timing or location of another thousands of kilometers away, particularly with a delay of several days, has been met with skepticism and largely dismissed as statistically insignificant or coincidental. Early studies attempted to detect such teleseismic triggering effects but were constrained by limited data resolution, inadequate declustering techniques, and narrow temporal windows, often restricting analysis to the first 24 hours following a mainshock. These limitations led to the conclusion that no systematic, reproducible relationship existed between large earthquakes and subsequent seismicity at remote distances.

More recent investigations have identified isolated instances where seismic waves from major earthquakes appeared to correlate with increased microseismic activity, slow slip events, or minor tremors at distances exceeding 1,000 kilometers. However, these observations were typically limited to events of low magnitude—generally below M4.0—and were often attributed to transient dynamic stresses or resonant responses in already critically stressed fault zones. The prevailing consensus held that the energy imparted by seismic waves attenuates rapidly with distance and is insufficient to overcome the static frictional barriers of tectonic faults unless those faults were already on the verge of failure. Consequently, the idea that a single large earthquake could systematically elevate the probability of other large earthquakes—M5.0 and above—across the globe, with a delay of up to 72 hours, was considered speculative and unsupported by rigorous statistical evidence. Furthermore, the absence of a widely accepted physical mechanism capable of explaining how such long-range, delayed triggering could occur contributed to the marginalization of this hypothesis within mainstream seismology.

Existing earthquake early warning systems are designed to detect the initial seismic waves of an event and issue alerts only to regions immediately adjacent to the rupture zone, typically providing seconds to minutes of warning before the arrival of destructive surface waves. These systems rely on real-time ground motion sensors and rapid hypocenter determination but do not incorporate any predictive element based on prior global seismic activity. Similarly, long-term seismic hazard maps, used for building codes and infrastructure planning, are derived from historical earthquake catalogs and fault slip rates, and are inherently static, offering no capacity to adapt to transient changes in global stress conditions. As a result, regions that are not located near known fault lines or tectonic boundaries remain unmonitored for potential increases in seismic risk, even when those regions are demonstrably influenced by the dynamic aftermath of distant, high-magnitude earthquakes.

Moreover, prior attempts to analyze global seismic correlations have been hampered by methodological shortcomings. Many studies failed to adequately remove aftershock sequences from their datasets, leading to inflated baseline rates and obscuring subtle but significant patterns of remote triggering. Others employed overly broad magnitude groupings, conflating distinct seismic behaviors across different magnitude ranges, or restricted their temporal analysis to windows too short to capture delayed responses. The statistical methods used were often not optimized for rare, spatially non-uniform events, and failed to account for the cumulative probability of multiple correlated observations across different geographic bins. As a consequence, even when patterns suggestive of triggering were observed, they were frequently dismissed as statistical noise or artifacts of data selection bias.

This invention overcomes these longstanding limitations by introducing a novel analytical framework that rigorously isolates global seismic responses following large earthquakes, employs a statistically robust control group derived from over four decades of seismic data, and applies precise binomial probability modeling to quantify the likelihood that observed increases in seismicity are non-random. The invention further introduces a spatially resolved risk model that identifies specific geographic zones—particularly those near the antipodal point relative to a source earthquake—where the probability of subsequent seismic events is significantly elevated. By integrating these findings into a real-time alerting system, the invention enables the first-ever global, dynamic, and predictive seismic risk assessment tool capable of identifying regions at heightened risk not due to their proximity to active faults, but due to their position relative to recent, large-magnitude seismic events occurring anywhere on the planet. This represents a paradigm shift in earthquake forecasting, moving from a purely local and static model to a global and dynamic one, grounded in empirical observation and validated through extensive statistical testing.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a method and system for determining and disseminating an elevated risk of earthquake occurrence following a large-magnitude seismic event, based on statistically significant global patterns of seismic triggering that manifest up to three days after the initiating event. The invention is based on the discovery that large earthquakes—particularly those with magnitudes of M7.0 and greater—produce measurable, non-random increases in the likelihood of subsequent earthquakes of magnitude M5.0 and above at distances far exceeding traditional aftershock zones, with the highest probability of triggering occurring in specific antipodal regions of the Earth’s surface. The method involves the systematic collection of global seismic data, the removal of aftershock sequences using a magnitude-dependent declustering algorithm, the construction of a control group comprising thousands of non-triggering time windows, and the calculation of relative rates and statistical p-values to determine whether observed seismic activity following a large event deviates significantly from background expectations. The invention further provides a spatial model that identifies the antipodal region as the zone of maximum triggering potential, with a secondary peak near 45 degrees from the source and a zone of quiescence near 90 degrees, forming a distinct, repeatable global pattern that is independent of local tectonic setting.

The invention further encompasses a system for automatically detecting the occurrence of a qualifying large earthquake, computing the geographic distribution of elevated seismic risk across the globe using the established model, and disseminating real-time alerts to designated recipients, including emergency management agencies, infrastructure operators, and public safety authorities. The system integrates seismic monitoring networks, automated event detection algorithms, probabilistic risk mapping engines, and multi-channel alert dissemination protocols to deliver actionable intelligence within minutes of a qualifying event. The alerts are not based on local ground motion detection but on global seismic context, enabling advance warning to regions that may otherwise be considered seismically stable. The system is capable of processing multiple triggering events within a single three-day window, identifying overlapping risk zones, and adjusting alert priorities based on the magnitude and location of each source event. Furthermore, the invention includes a mechanism for calibrating the risk model over time by continuously updating the baseline seismicity distribution and refining the spatial response function using newly acquired data, ensuring that the system remains accurate and adaptive to long-term changes in global seismic behavior.

The invention also provides a method for determining the likelihood that a given earthquake was itself triggered by a prior large event, enabling retrospective analysis of seismic sequences and the identification of earthquake chains that span continents and ocean basins. This capability allows for improved understanding of seismic cascades and supports the development of more accurate probabilistic forecasts for regions experiencing unusual seismic clustering. The system is designed to operate independently of any assumed physical mechanism for triggering, relying instead on empirical statistical relationships derived from over 40 years of global seismic data. The invention thus provides a practical, scalable, and legally defensible framework for incorporating global seismic interactions into modern earthquake risk management, fundamentally altering the way seismic hazards are assessed, communicated, and mitigated on a planetary scale.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### I. Terms

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms are defined with precision to ensure clarity and legal enforceability. The term “large-magnitude earthquake” refers to any seismic event with a moment magnitude (Mw) of 6.5 or greater, as determined by authoritative seismic monitoring agencies such as the United States Geological Survey (USGS), the Global Centroid Moment Tensor (GCMT) project, or equivalent international seismic networks. The term “triggered earthquake” refers to any seismic event of magnitude 5.0 or greater that occurs within a temporal window of up to 72 hours following the occurrence of a large-magnitude earthquake, and that is located at a distance exceeding the conventional aftershock zone as defined by the rupture length of the source event multiplied by a factor of three. The term “antipodal region” refers to the geographic area on the Earth’s surface that lies approximately 180 degrees of arc distance from the epicenter of a source earthquake, encompassing a circular zone with a radius of 30 degrees centered on the antipode, within which the probability of triggered seismicity is statistically elevated above background levels. The term “relative rate” refers to the ratio of the observed frequency of triggered earthquakes in a specified geographic bin to the expected frequency derived from a long-term control group of non-triggering time windows, normalized for spatial and temporal sampling density. The term “control group” refers to a collection of 5,355 non-overlapping three-day time windows, each associated with a specific geographic origin point, selected from a historical seismic catalog spanning the years 1973 through 2016, wherein no large-magnitude earthquake occurred during the window, and which are used to establish the baseline distribution of seismicity under the null hypothesis of independent, random occurrence. The term “declustered seismic catalog” refers to a modified version of the global earthquake catalog from which aftershocks and foreshocks have been systematically removed using a magnitude-dependent spatial and temporal windowing algorithm, ensuring that the remaining seismic events conform to a Poisson distribution in space and time. The term “binomial probability model” refers to the statistical framework used to compute the likelihood that the observed number of triggered earthquakes in a given spatial bin could have occurred by chance, given the baseline rate derived from the control group and the total number of possible events in the observation window. The term “p-value” refers to the probability, under the null hypothesis, of observing a number of triggered events equal to or greater than that which was actually observed, with p-values less than 0.05 indicating statistical significance. The term “quiescence zone” refers to the geographic region approximately 80 to 100 degrees from the source earthquake where the relative rate of triggered seismicity is systematically lower than the baseline rate, indicating a temporary suppression of seismic activity following large events. The term “earthquake chain” refers to a sequence of two or more large-magnitude earthquakes in which the second or subsequent event is determined to have been triggered by the first, with the triggering occurring within the 72-hour window and at a distance consistent with the spatial patterns described herein. The term “global seismic risk map” refers to a dynamic, real-time visualization of the Earth’s surface divided into ten-degree arc-distance bins relative to the source earthquake, with each bin assigned a relative rate value and a corresponding alert level based on its deviation from the baseline. The term “alert dissemination system” refers to the integrated hardware and software architecture that receives seismic event data, computes risk levels using the model described herein, and transmits alerts via secure, multi-modal communication channels to authorized recipients. The term “normalized relative rate” refers to a relative rate adjusted to a common sample size to enable comparison across different magnitude classes of source or triggered events, ensuring that differences in statistical power do not bias the interpretation of spatial patterns. The term “fourth-order polynomial fit” refers to a mathematical curve fitted to the average relative rate data across all magnitude classes, used to define the global average response function and to establish upper and lower bounds of triggering potential. The term “systematic deviation” refers to a non-random, reproducible pattern in seismicity that persists across multiple triggering events and is inconsistent with the predictions of a Poisson distribution, serving as the primary evidence for the validity of the triggering model. The term “real-time seismic monitoring network” refers to a globally distributed array of seismic sensors and data transmission systems capable of detecting, locating, and characterizing earthquakes with sufficient speed and accuracy to support the operational requirements of the alerting system. The term “qualified seismic event” refers to any earthquake that meets the magnitude threshold of M6.5 or greater and is detected and cataloged by an authoritative seismic agency within a time frame sufficient to initiate the alerting protocol.

### II. Method for Determining Enhancement of Earthquake Risk

The method for determining enhancement of earthquake risk begins with the continuous acquisition of seismic event data from a global network of seismic monitoring stations, wherein each event is characterized by its origin time, geographic coordinates, and moment magnitude, as determined by established seismic analysis protocols. Upon detection of a seismic event that meets or exceeds the threshold of M6.5, the system initiates a prospective analysis to determine whether the event is likely to induce subsequent seismicity at remote locations. The first step in this process involves the extraction of all seismic events recorded in the global catalog from the year 1973 through the present, with a minimum magnitude threshold of M5.0 to ensure global detectability and completeness of the dataset. The catalog is then subjected to a declustering procedure designed to remove aftershocks and foreshocks that would otherwise bias the baseline seismicity rate. This declustering is performed using a magnitude-dependent spatial and temporal windowing algorithm, wherein the spatial extent of the window is calculated as 10^(0.1238M + 0.983) kilometers and the temporal extent is calculated as either 10^(0.032M + 2.7389) days for events of magnitude M6.5 and above, or 10^(0.5409M − 0.547) days for events below M6.5. Any seismic event falling within the combined spatial and temporal window of a larger event is flagged as an aftershock or foreshock and excluded from subsequent analysis. This process ensures that the remaining seismic events in the catalog conform to a Poisson distribution, thereby satisfying the statistical assumptions required for the binomial probability model.

Following declustering, the system constructs a control group consisting of 5,355 non-overlapping three-day time windows, each associated with a unique geographic origin point derived from the locations of all qualifying large-magnitude earthquakes in the historical catalog. For each of these control windows, the system computes the spatial distribution of all M5.0+ seismic events that occurred during the window, using the origin point of the control event as the reference center. The arc distance from the origin to each event is calculated using the great-circle distance formula, and the events are binned into 180 one-degree angular bins, extending from 0 to 180 degrees from the origin. These binned counts are aggregated across all control windows to produce a baseline distribution of seismicity as a function of arc distance, representing the expected frequency of earthquakes at each angular interval under the assumption of independent, random occurrence. The baseline distribution is then normalized to account for variations in seismic monitoring sensitivity over time and across regions, ensuring that the resulting probability model is geographically and temporally consistent.

The system then processes the qualifying large-magnitude earthquake event to determine the spatial distribution of seismic events that occurred within the subsequent 72-hour period. All M5.0+ events occurring during this window are identified, and their arc distances from the source epicenter are calculated using the same methodology applied to the control group. The observed counts in each of the 180 one-degree bins are then compared to the baseline counts derived from the control group. To enhance statistical robustness and reduce noise, the one-degree bins are aggregated into ten-degree bins, offset by five degrees, resulting in 30 overlapping bins covering the full 180-degree range. The relative rate for each bin is computed as the ratio of the observed count to the expected count, with values greater than one indicating an increase in seismicity and values less than one indicating a decrease. The system then computes a binomial p-value for each bin, using the total number of observed events in the bin as the number of successes, the total number of events in the bin (observed plus baseline) as the number of trials, and a probability of success equal to one divided by the size of the control group plus one (i.e., 1/5,356). A mid-p-value correction is applied to account for the discrete nature of earthquake counts, ensuring that the p-values are not biased by the integer constraints of the data.

The system further computes collective p-values for groups of adjacent bins, using the product of individual p-values and applying a Bonferroni-type adjustment to account for multiple comparisons. The results are visualized as a global risk profile, with each ten-degree bin assigned a color-coded alert level based on its p-value and relative rate. The system identifies statistically significant deviations from the null hypothesis, with p-values below 0.05 indicating a significant enhancement of seismic risk. The system also identifies zones of quiescence, where relative rates fall below one and collective p-values indicate a statistically significant suppression of seismicity, particularly in the 80- to 100-degree range for large-magnitude source events. The output of this method is a comprehensive, spatially resolved assessment of earthquake risk enhancement, which is stored in a secure database and made available for use by the alert dissemination system.

### III. Method for Generating and Disseminating an Earthquake Alert

The method for generating and disseminating an earthquake alert begins upon the detection and confirmation of a qualifying large-magnitude earthquake, as determined by the method described in Section II. Upon initiation, the system automatically retrieves the geographic coordinates and magnitude of the source event and computes the global seismic risk profile using the baseline distribution and statistical model established in the prior section. The system then generates a dynamic risk map that overlays the relative rates and p-values onto a global projection, highlighting regions of elevated risk, particularly those within the antipodal region (150 to 180 degrees from the source), as well as the secondary peak near 45 degrees and the quiescence zone near 90 degrees. The risk map is divided into alert levels: low risk for bins with p-values greater than 0.1, moderate risk for bins with p-values between 0.05 and 0.1, high risk for bins with p-values between 0.01 and 0.05, and extreme risk for bins with p-values less than 0.01. Each alert level is associated with a specific communication protocol and recipient group.

The system then identifies all regions that fall within the high-risk or extreme-risk zones and determines whether those regions contain critical infrastructure, population centers, or seismic monitoring stations. For each identified region, the system generates a tailored alert message that includes the source event details, the predicted time window of elevated risk (72 hours), the geographic coordinates of the affected region, the relative rate and p-value for the relevant bin, and recommended actions such as the suspension of non-essential construction, activation of emergency response protocols, or public advisories regarding potential seismic activity. The alert message is formatted according to international emergency communication standards and is transmitted via multiple redundant channels, including secure satellite links, encrypted terrestrial networks, government emergency alert systems, and public notification platforms such as mobile broadcast systems and web-based dashboards.

The system is further configured to handle multiple triggering events occurring within the same 72-hour window. If a second qualifying earthquake occurs before the first alert window expires, the system recalculates the global risk profile by incorporating the new source event and computes the overlapping risk zones between the two events. If the antipodal regions of the two events intersect, the system assigns a compounded risk level to the overlapping zone, indicating a higher probability of triggering than either event alone. The system also identifies potential earthquake chains by examining whether any M5.0+ event occurring within the 72-hour window following the first source event is itself located within the antipodal region of a prior large-magnitude earthquake, thereby suggesting that it may have been triggered by that prior event. In such cases, the system generates a chain alert, notifying recipients of the potential for sequential triggering and recommending extended monitoring beyond the initial 72-hour window.

The dissemination system is designed to prioritize alerts based on the magnitude of the source event, the proximity of the affected region to population centers, and the historical seismic vulnerability of the region. For example, an M8.0 event triggering a high-risk zone in a densely populated coastal city will generate a higher-priority alert than an M7.0 event triggering a remote, sparsely inhabited region. The system also includes a feedback mechanism that records the outcome of each alert—whether a triggered earthquake occurred within the predicted zone and time window—and uses this data to refine the risk model over time, adjusting the relative rate thresholds and p-value cutoffs to improve predictive accuracy. The entire process, from detection to dissemination, is completed within a maximum of 15 minutes after the source event is confirmed, ensuring that alerts are issued with sufficient lead time to enable meaningful protective actions.

### IV. System for Providing Earthquake Alerts

The system for providing earthquake alerts comprises a distributed network of hardware and software components designed to operate continuously and autonomously, with minimal human intervention. The core of the system is a global seismic data ingestion module, which receives real-time seismic event data from multiple authoritative sources, including the United States Geological Survey (USGS), the European-Mediterranean Seismological Centre (EMSC), the Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA), the China Earthquake Administration (CEA), and other national and international seismic networks. The data ingestion module is equipped with redundancy protocols to ensure continuous operation even if one or more data sources become unavailable, and it applies quality control filters to reject erroneous or duplicate event reports.

The system further includes a triggering event detection engine, which continuously monitors incoming seismic data for events meeting the magnitude threshold of M6.5 or greater. Upon detection, the engine triggers a sequence of automated processes, including the initiation of the risk determination method described in Section II, the retrieval of the historical control group data, and the computation of the global risk profile. The risk profile is generated by a probabilistic modeling engine that executes the binomial probability calculations, applies the mid-p-value correction, and aggregates the results into the ten-degree spatial bins. The modeling engine is implemented as a high-performance computational module capable of processing multiple triggering events in parallel, with the ability to handle up to five concurrent triggering events without degradation in performance.

The system includes a dynamic risk mapping module that generates visual representations of the global risk profile in real time, using standardized geographic information system (GIS) formats compatible with emergency response platforms. The mapping module overlays the risk levels onto a globe or Mercator projection, with color-coded zones, numerical indicators, and interactive tooltips that display the relative rate, p-value, and confidence interval for each bin. The mapping module is accessible via web interface and can be integrated into command and control centers used by national disaster management agencies.

The alert dissemination module is responsible for generating and transmitting alerts to authorized recipients. This module includes a recipient registry that contains contact information for government agencies, emergency responders, infrastructure operators, and public notification systems, each with configurable alert thresholds and communication preferences. The module supports multiple delivery protocols, including Secure Messaging Protocol (SMP), Emergency Alert System (EAS) integration, SMS broadcast, email, API endpoints for third-party applications, and satellite-based messaging for remote or off-grid locations. The module also includes a logging and audit trail function that records every alert sent, the recipient, the time of transmission, and the content of the message, ensuring compliance with legal and regulatory requirements.

The system further includes a calibration and learning module that periodically updates the baseline seismicity distribution and refines the relative rate model using newly acquired data. This module automatically incorporates all new M5.0+ events into the historical catalog, re-computes the declustered dataset, and re-estimates the control group distribution on a monthly basis. The module also evaluates the predictive accuracy of past alerts by comparing predicted risk zones with actual triggered events and adjusts the statistical thresholds to minimize false positives and false negatives. The calibration module operates independently of the operational alerting system, ensuring that updates to the model do not interfere with real-time alert generation.

The entire system is hosted on a secure, redundant computing infrastructure with failover capabilities, including geographically distributed data centers, encrypted data storage, and continuous backup protocols. The system is designed to operate 24 hours per day, 365 days per year, with automated health monitoring and self-repair functions that detect and correct software anomalies without requiring manual intervention. The system is compliant with international data privacy and cybersecurity standards, and access to the system is restricted to authorized personnel with multi-factor authentication and role-based permissions.

### V. A Generalized Computer Environment

The generalized computer environment in which the system operates comprises a suite of interconnected computing devices, data storage systems, and communication networks designed to support the high-throughput, low-latency processing required for real-time seismic risk assessment and alert dissemination. The environment includes one or more high-performance computing clusters, each equipped with multi-core central processing units, high-speed memory, and parallel processing capabilities to execute the binomial probability calculations and spatial modeling tasks. The computing clusters are connected to a centralized data repository that stores the historical seismic catalog, the declustered event database, the control group statistics, and the current risk model parameters. The data repository is implemented as a distributed database system with replication across multiple geographic locations to ensure data integrity and availability.

The environment further includes a network interface module that facilitates secure, encrypted communication with external seismic monitoring networks, emergency response systems, and public alert platforms. The network interface module supports multiple communication protocols, including TCP/IP, HTTPS, MQTT, and satellite-based data links, and is configured to operate in both high-bandwidth and low-bandwidth environments to accommodate global deployment. The environment also includes a user interface server that provides access to the risk mapping and alert configuration tools for authorized personnel, including web-based dashboards, mobile applications, and desktop software clients.

The system is operated under a real-time operating system optimized for deterministic processing, with priority scheduling for seismic event detection and alert generation tasks. The operating system is configured to isolate critical processes from non-essential background tasks, ensuring that alert generation is never delayed due to system load. The environment includes comprehensive logging and monitoring tools that track system performance, resource utilization, and alert delivery success rates, with automated alerts generated if any component deviates from expected operational parameters.

The environment is secured through a multi-layered cybersecurity architecture that includes firewalls, intrusion detection systems, data encryption at rest and in transit, and regular penetration testing. Access to the system is governed by strict authentication and authorization protocols, with all user actions logged and audited. The system is designed to comply with international standards for critical infrastructure protection, including ISO/IEC 27001, NIST SP 800-53, and the International Telecommunication Union’s guidelines for emergency communication systems.

The generalized computer environment is scalable and modular, allowing for the addition of new data sources, alert recipients, and geographic regions without requiring system-wide reconfiguration. The system can be deployed in standalone mode for national use or integrated into multinational emergency response frameworks, with data sharing agreements and interoperability protocols in place to ensure seamless coordination across borders. The environment is designed for long-term operational sustainability, with provisions for hardware upgrades, software updates, and model recalibrations that do not disrupt ongoing alerting operations.

### VI. General Considerations

The invention described herein is not predicated on any specific physical mechanism for the long-range triggering of earthquakes, nor does it require the acceptance of any particular theory regarding the propagation of stress perturbations through the Earth’s crust. The method and system are based entirely on empirical statistical relationships derived from over four decades of global seismic data, and their validity is established through rigorous probabilistic testing against a large, independently constructed control group. The invention does not claim to explain how large earthquakes induce seismicity at remote distances, but rather demonstrates that such an induction occurs with sufficient statistical significance to warrant operational use in risk mitigation. The invention is therefore agnostic to the underlying physics, whether that physics involves dynamic stress transfer via seismic waves, delayed aseismic slip, fluid migration, or any other mechanism, and its utility is not diminished by uncertainty regarding the causal pathway.

The invention is designed to operate independently of local geological conditions, meaning that it can provide actionable alerts to regions that are not located near known fault lines or tectonic boundaries, thereby expanding the scope of earthquake preparedness beyond traditional hazard zones. The invention does not replace existing early warning systems but complements them by providing a global, context-aware layer of risk assessment that operates on a different timescale and spatial framework. The invention is also not intended to predict the exact time, location, or magnitude of a future earthquake, but rather to indicate an elevated probability of seismic activity within a defined window and geographic region, thereby enabling precautionary measures that reduce vulnerability without requiring precise forecasting.

The system is designed to be transparent and auditable, with all data inputs, processing steps, and output parameters documented and available for peer review. The statistical methods employed are standard in the field of probability and statistics, and the implementation is open to independent verification by any qualified researcher with access to the global seismic catalog and the control group data. The invention is not limited to any particular geographic region, language, or political jurisdiction, and is intended for global application, with the potential to save lives and reduce economic losses in both developed and developing nations.

The invention may be implemented in whole or in part by governmental agencies, private corporations, academic institutions, or international organizations, and may be licensed, sublicensed, or otherwise distributed under terms that ensure its continued operation for the public benefit. The invention may be integrated into existing seismic monitoring infrastructures, emergency response protocols, and insurance risk models, and may be adapted for use in other geophysical contexts where remote triggering effects may be present, such as volcanic activity, induced seismicity from fluid injection, or glacial rebound. The invention represents a fundamental advancement in the science of seismic risk assessment and constitutes a novel, non-obvious, and industrially applicable solution to a long-standing problem in global disaster preparedness.
