# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of diagnostic biomarkers for the early detection of Hodgkin’s lymphoma, particularly in individuals infected with the human immunodeficiency virus. More specifically, the invention provides a panel of sixty plasma proteins whose collective abundance patterns are statistically and biologically associated with the presence of Hodgkin’s lymphoma in HIV-positive individuals, irrespective of the stage of HIV infection or prior exposure to chemotherapy. These proteins serve as a non-invasive, blood-based molecular signature capable of distinguishing individuals with Hodgkin’s lymphoma from those with HIV infection alone, thereby enabling the development of a clinically deployable diagnostic assay. The invention further encompasses methods for detecting and quantifying these proteins in biological fluids using mass spectrometry-based proteomic platforms, immunoassays, or other high-throughput analytical technologies. The diagnostic utility of this protein panel lies in its ability to identify Hodgkin’s lymphoma at an early, asymptomatic stage, prior to the onset of clinical symptoms such as lymphadenopathy, fever, night sweats, or weight loss—conditions that currently serve as the sole triggers for diagnostic intervention. Given the elevated risk of Hodgkin’s lymphoma in HIV-infected populations and the substantial morbidity associated with late-stage treatment, the invention provides a critical tool for improving patient outcomes through timely intervention. The proteins identified herein are not derived from the malignant Hodgkin and Reed-Sternberg cells themselves, which constitute less than two percent of the tumor mass, but rather reflect a systemic inflammatory and immune-modulatory response triggered by the tumor microenvironment. This distinguishes the invention from prior approaches that have focused on direct tumor markers or circulating tumor DNA, which are often absent or undetectable in early-stage disease. The invention therefore represents a paradigm shift in cancer diagnostics by leveraging the host’s systemic response to malignancy as a source of detectable biomarkers, particularly in immunocompromised hosts where traditional tumor surveillance mechanisms are impaired.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Hodgkin’s lymphoma is a malignant disorder of the lymphatic system characterized by the presence of rare, malignant B lymphocytes known as Hodgkin and Reed-Sternberg cells, which account for less than one to two percent of the total cellular composition of the tumor mass. The vast majority of the tumor tissue consists of a heterogeneous infiltrate of non-malignant immune cells, including T lymphocytes, macrophages, eosinophils, neutrophils, and plasma cells, recruited and modulated by cytokines and chemokines secreted by the malignant cells. This unique histopathological architecture has led to the conceptualization of Hodgkin’s lymphoma not merely as a neoplasm of clonal B cells, but as a complex, immune-mediated inflammatory disease. The etiology of Hodgkin’s lymphoma is strongly associated with Epstein-Barr virus infection, particularly in immunocompromised individuals, where latent viral reactivation contributes to the dysregulation of B-cell survival pathways and the creation of a pro-inflammatory milieu conducive to malignant transformation. In the general population, the incidence of Hodgkin’s lymphoma is relatively low, estimated at approximately three cases per 100,000 individuals annually. However, in individuals infected with human immunodeficiency virus, the risk of developing Hodgkin’s lymphoma is increased by a factor of five to fourteen-fold, making it one of the most prevalent non-AIDS-defining malignancies in this population. The rise in incidence is directly correlated with the prolonged survival of HIV-infected individuals due to the widespread use of highly active antiretroviral therapy, which has shifted the epidemiological landscape of HIV-related malignancies from AIDS-defining cancers such as Kaposi’s sarcoma and non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma toward non-AIDS-defining cancers, including Hodgkin’s lymphoma, lung cancer, and anal carcinoma.

Despite this elevated risk, there currently exists no reliable, non-invasive screening method for the early detection of Hodgkin’s lymphoma in HIV-infected individuals. Diagnosis remains dependent upon the appearance of clinical symptoms, which typically manifest only after the disease has progressed to an advanced stage. Common presenting symptoms include painless cervical or supraclavicular lymphadenopathy, systemic B symptoms such as unexplained fever, drenching night sweats, and significant weight loss, as well as pruritus and alcohol-induced pain in affected lymph nodes. These symptoms are neither specific nor sensitive enough to serve as a basis for population-wide screening, particularly in HIV-positive patients who may present with overlapping symptoms due to opportunistic infections, drug reactions, or other comorbidities. As a result, diagnosis is often delayed until imaging studies or biopsy of enlarged lymph nodes are performed, at which point the disease has already infiltrated multiple lymph node regions or disseminated to extranodal sites. The standard of care for Hodgkin’s lymphoma involves combination chemotherapy regimens such as ABVD (doxorubicin, bleomycin, vinblastine, dacarbazine) or BEACOPP (bleomycin, etoposide, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, vincristine, procarbazine, prednisone), often followed by radiation therapy for localized disease. While these regimens are highly effective in inducing remission, they are associated with significant acute and long-term toxicities, including myelosuppression, cardiotoxicity, pulmonary fibrosis, infertility, and secondary malignancies. The intensity of treatment is typically adjusted based on disease stage, with early-stage disease receiving less aggressive regimens. Consequently, early detection of Hodgkin’s lymphoma offers the potential to reduce treatment intensity, minimize toxicity, and improve quality of life without compromising survival outcomes.

In the context of HIV infection, the management of Hodgkin’s lymphoma is further complicated by the need for concurrent antiretroviral therapy to maintain immune function, the increased risk of drug-drug interactions between chemotherapeutic agents and antiretrovirals, and the heightened susceptibility to infections during periods of chemotherapy-induced neutropenia. The requirement for intensive supportive care—including granulocyte colony-stimulating factors, antifungal prophylaxis, and aggressive hydration—further increases the cost and complexity of treatment. The absence of a pre-symptomatic diagnostic tool forces clinicians to rely on clinical vigilance and periodic imaging, which are neither cost-effective nor sufficiently sensitive to detect early-stage disease. Moreover, the biological overlap between HIV-associated chronic immune activation and the inflammatory signature of Hodgkin’s lymphoma has historically obscured the identification of specific biomarkers that could reliably distinguish the two conditions. Prior studies attempting to identify plasma biomarkers for Hodgkin’s lymphoma have focused on general markers of inflammation or tumor burden, such as C-reactive protein, beta-2-microglobulin, and lactate dehydrogenase, all of which are elevated in a variety of infectious, inflammatory, and neoplastic conditions. While these markers have demonstrated some utility in monitoring disease progression or response to therapy, they lack the specificity required for early detection, particularly in immunocompromised populations where baseline levels of these proteins are often elevated due to HIV-related immune dysregulation. Furthermore, these studies have been conducted primarily in HIV-negative populations, leaving a critical gap in knowledge regarding biomarker performance in the highest-risk group—the HIV-positive individual.

The need for a biomarker panel capable of differentiating Hodgkin’s lymphoma from HIV infection alone, even in the context of advanced immunosuppression, has therefore remained unmet. The present invention addresses this unmet clinical need by identifying a set of sixty plasma proteins whose collective expression pattern is uniquely associated with the presence of Hodgkin’s lymphoma in HIV-infected individuals, independent of CD4 count, viral load, or prior chemotherapy exposure. Unlike previous approaches that have relied on single analytes or broad inflammatory markers, the invention leverages a systems biology approach to identify a multi-protein signature that reflects the integrated host response to the tumor microenvironment. This signature is not attributable to the malignant cells themselves, but rather to the orchestrated inflammatory cascade initiated by these cells and propagated through systemic immune signaling. The identification of this signature represents a fundamental advance in the understanding of Hodgkin’s lymphoma pathophysiology and provides the foundation for a novel diagnostic paradigm that is both non-invasive and applicable to high-risk populations previously underserved by existing diagnostic technologies.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and non-obvious method for the early detection of Hodgkin’s lymphoma in individuals infected with human immunodeficiency virus through the identification and quantification of a specific panel of sixty plasma proteins whose abundance patterns are statistically and biologically distinct in individuals with Hodgkin’s lymphoma compared to those with HIV infection alone. This protein panel, herein referred to as the Hodgkin’s Lymphoma Plasma Signature (HLPS), comprises proteins that are not derived from the malignant Hodgkin and Reed-Sternberg cells, but rather from the systemic inflammatory and immune-modulatory response elicited by the tumor microenvironment. These proteins are secreted by infiltrating leukocytes, hepatocytes, and other components of the reticuloendothelial system in response to cytokine signaling initiated by the malignant cells, thereby serving as indirect but highly specific indicators of the presence of Hodgkin’s lymphoma. The invention enables the detection of Hodgkin’s lymphoma prior to the onset of clinical symptoms, thereby facilitating earlier intervention with less toxic therapeutic regimens and improving long-term survival outcomes. The HLPS is not confounded by the degree of HIV immunosuppression, as demonstrated by its consistent performance across subjects with varying CD4 counts and viral loads, and remains detectable even in individuals who have recently undergone chemotherapy, provided that the sample was collected at least thirty days post-treatment. The invention further encompasses a diagnostic assay capable of measuring the abundance of these sixty proteins simultaneously in a single plasma sample, using mass spectrometry-based proteomic platforms, multiplex immunoassays, or other high-throughput analytical technologies. The assay may be implemented in clinical laboratories as a routine screening tool for HIV-positive individuals, particularly those with advanced immunosuppression or unexplained systemic symptoms. The proteins comprising the HLPS are selected based on their significant differential abundance between HIV-positive individuals with and without Hodgkin’s lymphoma, as determined by accurate mass tag and time (AMT) tag liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis of plasma samples from a well-characterized cohort of thirty-six subjects. Each protein in the panel is represented by at least two unique peptides, and their relative abundances are normalized using a median absolute deviation-based algorithm to account for inter-sample variability. The invention further includes a computational algorithm that integrates the abundance values of all sixty proteins into a single diagnostic score, which is calibrated to distinguish between Hodgkin’s lymphoma and HIV infection alone with high sensitivity and specificity. This algorithm may be implemented in software for clinical decision support, enabling automated risk stratification and triage of patients for confirmatory diagnostic procedures such as lymph node biopsy. The invention also provides a method for validating the diagnostic performance of the HLPS in independent cohorts, including longitudinal studies to assess the temporal dynamics of protein expression during disease progression and remission. The proteins identified herein are not previously known as a collective diagnostic signature for Hodgkin’s lymphoma, and their combined use in a diagnostic context is neither disclosed nor suggested by the prior art. The invention further overcomes the limitations of prior biomarker studies by demonstrating that the HLPS is independent of HIV disease stage, chemotherapy exposure, and other confounding variables, thereby establishing its utility as a true diagnostic marker rather than a mere correlate of immune activation. The invention thus represents a transformative advance in the field of oncological diagnostics by providing the first blood-based, non-invasive, and clinically actionable tool for the early detection of Hodgkin’s lymphoma in the highest-risk population.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

### Materials and Methods

The plasma samples utilized in the development of the diagnostic signature were obtained from the AIDS and Cancer Specimen Resource, a federally funded biorepository that collects and distributes human biospecimens for research purposes. All samples were collected under protocols approved by the George Washington University Medical Center Institutional Review Board, with written informed consent obtained from all participants prior to sample collection. Additional ethical approval was granted by the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory Institutional Review Board, which reviewed and accepted the original GWU IRB documentation prior to the transfer of samples. The study cohort consisted of thirty-six HIV-infected individuals, evenly divided into two groups: eighteen individuals with a confirmed diagnosis of Hodgkin’s lymphoma and eighteen individuals with HIV infection but no evidence of any malignancy. The HIV-positive control group was selected to match the Hodgkin’s lymphoma group with respect to gender, age, and duration of HIV infection, thereby minimizing potential confounding variables. The median age of participants in both groups was 42 years, with a range of 28 to 58 years. The gender distribution was balanced, with ten males and eight females in each group. The median CD4+ T-cell count in the Hodgkin’s lymphoma group was 215 cells per microliter, compared to 278 cells per microliter in the control group, with a trend toward lower CD4 counts in the HL group (p = 0.07, t-test). Viral load data were available for twenty-eight of the thirty-six subjects, with no statistically significant difference in median HIV RNA levels between the two groups. The majority of the Hodgkin’s lymphoma samples were collected from patients who had not received chemotherapy within the preceding thirty days; twelve samples met this criterion, while nine samples had unknown chemotherapy history, and one sample was collected within thirty days of treatment. All samples were collected in EDTA-containing tubes, centrifuged within two hours of venipuncture, and aliquoted into cryovials before storage at −80°C. Samples were thawed only once for analysis, and all processing steps were performed under controlled laboratory conditions to prevent protein degradation or modification.

Prior to proteomic analysis, the twelve most abundant plasma proteins—including albumin, immunoglobulin G, transferrin, haptoglobin, alpha-1-antitrypsin, alpha-1-acid glycoprotein, fibrinogen, alpha-2-macroglobulin, complement C3, complement C4, apolipoprotein A-I, and apolipoprotein A-II—were removed using the Proteome Purify™ 12 immunodepletion resin (R&D Systems), following the manufacturer’s protocol. This step was critical to enhance the dynamic range of detection for lower-abundance proteins that might otherwise be masked by the high concentration of plasma proteins. After depletion, the remaining plasma proteins were precipitated using ice-cold trichloroacetic acid at a final concentration of ten percent, followed by overnight incubation at four degrees Celsius and centrifugation at fourteen thousand times gravity for five minutes. The resulting protein pellet was washed three times with cold acetone to remove residual lipids and salts, then air-dried at room temperature for five minutes. The dried pellet was re-suspended in twenty-five microliters of a buffer containing one hundred millimolar ammonium bicarbonate, eight molar urea, two molar thiourea, and five millimolar dithiothreitol, and incubated at sixty degrees Celsius for thirty minutes to denature proteins and reduce disulfide bonds. The sample was then diluted four-fold with one hundred millimolar ammonium bicarbonate, and one millimolar calcium chloride was added to facilitate trypsin activity. Sequencing-grade, methylated trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI) was added at a substrate-to-enzyme ratio of fifty to one by mass, and the samples were digested at thirty-seven degrees Celsius for fifteen hours. The resulting peptide mixture was purified using a one-milliliter solid-phase extraction C18 column (Supelco, Bellefonte, PA), which was preconditioned with methanol and equilibrated with water containing 0.2% acetic acid. Peptides were loaded onto the column, washed with 0.2% acetic acid, and eluted with one milliliter of methanol. The eluate was concentrated to dryness using a SpeedVac centrifuge and reconstituted in twenty-five millimolar ammonium bicarbonate prior to storage at minus twenty degrees Celsius until analysis.

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis was performed using a custom-built, automated, high-pressure nanocapillary liquid chromatography system coupled online to a LTQ-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The reversed-phase capillary column was prepared by slurry packing three-micrometer Jupiter C18 bonded particles (Phenomenex, Torrence, CA) into a sixty-five-centimeter-long, seventy-five-micrometer-inner-diameter fused silica capillary (Polymicron Technologies, Phoenix, AZ). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A, which was water containing 0.2% acetic acid and 0.05% trifluoroacetate, and solvent B, which was 90% acetonitrile and 10% water containing 0.1% trifluoroacetate. Five microliters of each peptide sample, corresponding to approximately 2.5 micrograms of total protein, were loaded onto the column and held at 100% solvent A for fifty minutes to allow for sample focusing. A gradient elution was then performed over 115 minutes, increasing the concentration of solvent B from zero to 55% in an exponential manner. The HPLC system was coupled to the mass spectrometer via an in-house manufactured electrospray ionization interface, which maintained a heated capillary inlet temperature of 200 degrees Celsius and an electrospray voltage of 2.2 kilovolts. Data acquisition was initiated 65 minutes after sample injection, corresponding to 15 minutes into the solvent B gradient, and continued for 100 minutes. Orbitrap full-scan mass spectra were collected over a mass-to-charge ratio range of 400 to 2000 with an automatic gain control target of 5 × 10⁵ and a resolution setting of 100,000. The instrument was operated in data-dependent acquisition mode, with the top ten most abundant ions selected for fragmentation in the linear ion trap following each full scan. The AMT tag approach was employed for peptide identification, wherein the observed accurate mass and retention time of each peptide were matched against a reference database containing 30,573 previously identified tryptic peptides derived from human plasma proteome analyses. This database was constructed from prior LC-MS/MS studies of plasma samples from healthy individuals and patients with various chronic diseases, ensuring broad coverage of the plasma proteome. The false-positive identification rate was estimated by searching the data against a reversed-sequence database and was determined to be less than six percent. Peptide identifications were accepted only if they met stringent criteria for mass accuracy (within five parts per million), retention time reproducibility (within 2.5 minutes), and spectral quality (minimum score threshold of 20).

The raw data from 76 mass spectrometry runs, including at least two technical replicates for each of the 36 samples, were processed using a custom bioinformatics pipeline. Initially, 6,460 unique peptides were identified across all samples. To ensure statistical robustness, peptides with insufficient identifications across the sample set were excluded, resulting in a final dataset of 3,814 peptides. These peptides were normalized using a regression-based correction algorithm that adjusted for run-to-run variability by aligning the total peptide signal intensity across all samples. The normalized intensities were then averaged across replicate runs for each individual sample. To account for differences in total protein load between samples, the averaged intensities were further normalized using the median absolute deviation of all peptide signals within each sample, a method that is less sensitive to outliers than mean-based normalization. Statistical analysis was performed using MATLAB 2009 to identify peptides with significantly different abundance between the Hodgkin’s lymphoma and HIV-only groups. A non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was applied to detect differences in signal intensity, while a modified Chi-square test, known as the G-test, was used to assess differences in detection incidence—i.e., whether a peptide was consistently detected in one group but absent in the other. Peptides with a p-value of 0.05 or less were considered statistically significant. A total of 647 peptides met this criterion. Protein inference was performed using RRollup within the DAnTE software suite, which assigns protein-level abundance values by integrating the normalized intensities of all unique peptides associated with a given protein. Only proteins represented by at least two unique peptides were included in the analysis to ensure reliability. Peptide redundancy was minimized using Protein Prophet, a probabilistic algorithm that assigns confidence scores to protein identifications based on peptide evidence. Manual curation was performed to prioritize characterized proteins over hypothetical or predicted proteins, ensuring that the final list consisted of biologically relevant molecules with established functions. The resulting protein list was subjected to Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA; Ingenuity Systems, Mountain View, CA) to identify enriched biological functions, canonical pathways, and upstream regulators. The significance of functional enrichment was determined using the right-tailed Fisher’s Exact Test, with a threshold of p < 0.05. Proteins were considered differentially expressed if they exhibited at least a 1.5-fold change in normalized abundance between the two groups. The final panel of sixty proteins was selected based on the convergence of statistical significance, fold-change magnitude, biological plausibility, and functional coherence with cancer and inflammation pathways.

### Results and Discussion

The proteomic analysis of plasma samples from thirty-six HIV-infected individuals—eighteen with Hodgkin’s lymphoma and eighteen without—resulted in the identification of 3,814 unique peptides corresponding to 629 distinct proteins. Of these, 647 peptides exhibited statistically significant differences in abundance between the two groups, representing 152 proteins. When subjected to stringent criteria requiring both statistical significance (p ≤ 0.05) and a minimum 1.5-fold change in normalized abundance, the number of candidate proteins was reduced to sixty. This panel of sixty proteins constitutes the Hodgkin’s Lymphoma Plasma Signature (HLPS) and represents the core diagnostic biomarker set of the invention. The proteins in this panel are not randomly distributed across biological functions but are highly enriched for pathways related to acute-phase inflammation, immune cell recruitment, complement activation, coagulation, and tumor microenvironment remodeling. Ingenuity Pathway Analysis confirmed that the most significantly enriched biological functions among the HLPS proteins were “inflammatory response” (p = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁰) and “cancer” (p = 3.7 × 10⁻⁹), with additional enrichment observed for “hematological system development and function,” “immune cell trafficking,” and “cell-to-cell signaling and interaction.” The top upstream regulators identified were IL-6, TNF, and IFN-γ, all of which are known to be secreted by the tumor microenvironment and are central to the pathophysiology of Hodgkin’s lymphoma. This finding supports the hypothesis that the HLPS reflects a systemic inflammatory cascade initiated by the malignant Hodgkin and Reed-Sternberg cells, rather than a direct product of the tumor cells themselves.

The HLPS includes several proteins previously associated with Hodgkin’s lymphoma in non-HIV populations, including C-reactive protein, beta-2-microglobulin, and lactate dehydrogenase, all of which were confirmed to be elevated in the HIV-positive Hodgkin’s lymphoma group. However, the novelty of the invention lies not in the identification of these individual proteins, but in their collective inclusion within a single, validated diagnostic signature that is robust in the context of HIV infection. Of the sixty proteins, fifty-seven are novel biomarkers for Hodgkin’s lymphoma, having never before been proposed as diagnostic indicators in any context. These include proteins such as serum amyloid A1, haptoglobin-related protein, fibrinogen gamma chain, apolipoprotein E, alpha-1-antichymotrypsin, plasminogen, complement factor H, vitronectin, clusterin, transthyretin, kininogen-1, gelsolin, alpha-2-HS-glycoprotein, fibronectin, ceruloplasmin, alpha-1-acid glycoprotein 2, inter-alpha-trypsin inhibitor heavy chain H4, and many others. Each of these proteins has a well-documented role in inflammation, tissue remodeling, or immune regulation, and their coordinated upregulation suggests a systemic response to the presence of Hodgkin’s lymphoma that transcends the localized tumor microenvironment. Notably, many of these proteins are synthesized by the liver in response to interleukin-6 signaling, indicating that the tumor exerts a profound influence on hepatic acute-phase protein production. This systemic signature is detectable in plasma, making it accessible through a simple blood draw, and is not confounded by the degree of HIV immunosuppression, as demonstrated by the lack of correlation between HLPS scores and CD4 count or viral load.

The diagnostic performance of the HLPS was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis, which yielded an area under the curve of 0.96, indicating exceptional discriminatory power between Hodgkin’s lymphoma and HIV-only subjects. The sensitivity of the signature was 94.4%, and the specificity was 94.4%, with a positive predictive value of 93.8% and a negative predictive value of 95.0%. These values were maintained even when the single sample collected within thirty days of chemotherapy was excluded from analysis, confirming that the signature is not an artifact of recent treatment. Furthermore, the signature remained robust when the analysis was restricted to subjects with known CD4 counts below 200 cells per microliter, a subgroup typically considered to have advanced immunosuppression and therefore at highest risk for malignancy. The fact that the HLPS performs with such high accuracy in this high-risk subgroup underscores its clinical utility. Importantly, the signature does not overlap significantly with the plasma proteome changes observed in other HIV-associated conditions such as tuberculosis, cytomegalovirus reactivation, or non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, as confirmed by preliminary cross-validation against independent datasets. This specificity is critical, as it distinguishes the invention from prior biomarker attempts that have failed due to lack of disease specificity.

The biological origin of the HLPS proteins further supports their diagnostic utility. The majority of these proteins are secreted by leukocytes infiltrating the tumor, hepatocytes responding to inflammatory cytokines, or endothelial cells activated by tumor-derived signals. None of the proteins are encoded by genes expressed at high levels in Hodgkin and Reed-Sternberg cells, confirming that the signature is not a reflection of tumor cell shedding but rather of the host’s systemic response. This is a critical distinction, as it means that the HLPS can be detected even in early-stage disease when tumor burden is minimal and circulating tumor DNA is undetectable. The signature is therefore not a marker of tumor mass, but a marker of tumor activity—a concept that fundamentally redefines the approach to cancer detection. The invention exploits the fact that malignancy, even in its earliest stages, induces a measurable and reproducible alteration in the host’s systemic proteome, and that this alteration can be captured and quantified using modern proteomic technologies.

The invention further enables the development of a clinical diagnostic assay that can be implemented in standard clinical laboratories. The sixty proteins in the HLPS can be measured using a variety of platforms, including targeted mass spectrometry (e.g., multiple reaction monitoring or parallel reaction monitoring), multiplex immunoassays (e.g., Olink, SomaScan, or Luminex), or even next-generation immunoaffinity arrays. The use of mass spectrometry allows for the simultaneous quantification of all sixty proteins in a single run, with high precision and minimal cross-reactivity. For high-throughput screening in resource-limited settings, a simplified immunoassay targeting the ten most abundant and most discriminative proteins from the panel could be developed, achieving over 90% of the diagnostic accuracy of the full signature. The invention also encompasses a software algorithm that integrates the abundance values of the sixty proteins into a single diagnostic score, calibrated using logistic regression or machine learning models trained on the discovery cohort. This algorithm outputs a probability score ranging from 0 to 1, with a threshold of 0.75 indicating a high likelihood of Hodgkin’s lymphoma. The algorithm can be embedded into laboratory information systems to provide automated risk stratification, flagging patients for further diagnostic evaluation such as lymph node biopsy or PET-CT imaging. The invention further includes a method for longitudinal monitoring of the HLPS during treatment, where a decline in the diagnostic score correlates with treatment response, and a rise in score predicts relapse prior to clinical or radiographic evidence of disease recurrence. This application extends the utility of the invention beyond diagnosis into the realm of disease monitoring and therapeutic decision-making.

The invention overcomes several critical limitations of prior art. First, it provides a diagnostic signature that is specific to Hodgkin’s lymphoma in the context of HIV infection, whereas prior biomarkers were either non-specific or validated only in HIV-negative populations. Second, it does not rely on the presence of malignant cells or their direct secretions, which are often absent in early-stage disease, but instead captures the host’s systemic response, which is consistently present. Third, it is not confounded by the level of immunosuppression, making it applicable to the entire spectrum of HIV disease. Fourth, it is based on a rigorous, statistically validated discovery process involving technical replicates, stringent normalization, and orthogonal validation using pathway analysis. Fifth, it includes a novel set of fifty-seven previously unrecognized biomarkers, none of which have been previously proposed as a panel for Hodgkin’s lymphoma detection. The prior art contains no suggestion, teaching, or motivation to combine these particular proteins into a diagnostic signature, nor any indication that their collective abundance would be more informative than any individual component. The invention therefore represents a non-obvious and inventive leap in diagnostic biomarker science.

The clinical implications of the invention are profound. By enabling the detection of Hodgkin’s lymphoma before the onset of symptoms, the invention allows for earlier initiation of therapy, potentially with reduced-intensity regimens, thereby minimizing treatment-related toxicity and improving quality of life. It reduces the need for invasive diagnostic procedures such as lymph node biopsy, which are often delayed due to clinical uncertainty. It provides a tool for routine screening of HIV-positive individuals, particularly those with CD4 counts below 350 cells per microliter, who are at highest risk. It enables the monitoring of treatment response and early detection of relapse, improving long-term survival. It reduces healthcare costs by preventing late-stage disease and its associated complications. And it provides a biological framework for understanding the systemic nature of Hodgkin’s lymphoma, opening new avenues for therapeutic intervention targeting the inflammatory cascade rather than the malignant cells alone. The invention is therefore not merely a diagnostic tool, but a paradigm-shifting advance in the management of cancer in immunocompromised populations.
