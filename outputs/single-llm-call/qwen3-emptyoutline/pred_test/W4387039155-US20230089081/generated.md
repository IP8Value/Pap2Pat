# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to systems and methods for the predictive detection of electrical faults in power distribution and transmission networks, particularly those operating at medium voltage levels, using only existing current and voltage measurements without the need for additional sensors or infrastructure. More specifically, the invention encompasses a machine learning-based framework that processes waveform disturbance recordings captured by standard substation instrumentation—such as instrument transformers and IEC 61850-compliant communication interfaces—to forecast the occurrence of electrical faults up to one week in advance. The invention is particularly applicable to electrical grids that include both overhead lines and underground or underwater cables, where traditional fault prediction techniques relying on partial discharge monitoring, temperature sensing, or aerial inspection are either infeasible, cost-prohibitive, or insufficiently comprehensive. The system operates by extracting high-dimensional time-series features from raw voltage and current waveforms, filtering out irrelevant or noisy recordings through a learned classification mechanism, and feeding the filtered sequence of feature vectors into a long short-term memory neural network architecture designed to model temporal dependencies over extended horizons. The output of the network is a probabilistic estimate indicating the likelihood of a fault occurring within a predefined prediction window, enabling proactive maintenance and operational interventions prior to actual failure. This approach is uniquely suited for deployment in legacy power systems where retrofitting with new sensor networks is impractical, and where the economic and operational imperative to minimize unplanned outages demands a highly reliable, low-cost, and scalable predictive solution. The invention further extends to multi-location configurations wherein data from interconnected feeders or substations are processed independently and then fused to enhance predictive accuracy through contextual awareness across spatially distributed network segments.

## BACKGROUND ART

Electrical power systems have grown increasingly complex and interconnected over the past several decades, driven by the integration of renewable energy sources, electrification of transportation, and the expansion of industrial and urban load demands. As a consequence, the frequency and severity of faults—such as short circuits, ground faults, insulation breakdowns, and conductor failures—have risen significantly, leading to substantial financial losses, service interruptions, and safety hazards for both utilities and end users. Traditional fault detection methodologies have historically relied on real-time protection relays that respond to abnormal conditions after they have already occurred, often triggering circuit breakers to isolate the faulted section. While effective at mitigating damage post-failure, these systems provide no advance warning and thus offer no opportunity for preventive action. The need for predictive capabilities that can anticipate faults before they manifest has therefore become a critical objective in modern grid management.

Several approaches have been proposed in the literature to address this challenge. One prominent class of methods involves the detection and analysis of partial discharges within insulation systems. These micro-discharges, which occur as a precursor to dielectric failure, can be measured using high-frequency sensors and analyzed through spectral or temporal pattern recognition. However, this technique suffers from several fundamental limitations. First, it requires specialized and expensive sensor hardware that is not typically installed in standard substations, making widespread deployment economically unviable. Second, partial discharge monitoring is largely ineffective for underground and underwater cables, where the physical environment suppresses or distorts the electromagnetic signatures of discharges, rendering the method inapplicable to a significant portion of modern grid infrastructure. Third, partial discharge-based methods are inherently limited to faults originating from insulation degradation and are incapable of detecting faults caused by mechanical damage, vegetation contact, animal interference, moisture ingress, or thermal overload—factors that account for a substantial proportion of real-world failures.

Alternative approaches have sought to leverage temperature measurements along power lines using fiber-optic sensors or infrared imaging systems. While these methods can detect thermal anomalies associated with increased resistance or overloading, they are similarly constrained by the requirement for additional sensor deployment, which introduces significant capital and maintenance costs. Moreover, temperature-based methods are ineffective for faults that do not generate appreciable heat prior to failure, such as sudden conductor breaks or flashovers caused by lightning or switching surges. Another class of methods employs unmanned aerial vehicles equipped with thermal or visual cameras to inspect overhead lines for signs of damage, vegetation encroachment, or corona discharge. Although useful for visual inspection, these systems are limited to overhead infrastructure, are highly susceptible to weather conditions, cannot operate continuously, and lack the temporal resolution necessary to detect subtle, evolving precursors to failure. Furthermore, the data collected by such systems must be manually interpreted or processed using computer vision algorithms, introducing latency and reducing scalability.

Other research has explored the use of weather data—such as rainfall, wind speed, humidity, and lightning activity—as indirect indicators of fault risk. While meteorological factors can correlate with certain types of faults, particularly those involving vegetation or insulator contamination, these models lack the granularity to distinguish between benign environmental conditions and those that are truly predictive of imminent failure. They also fail to capture the internal electrical dynamics of the system, such as transient overvoltages, harmonic distortions, or impedance shifts, which are often more direct and reliable indicators of impending faults. Consequently, weather-based models exhibit high false positive rates and poor generalization across different geographic regions and grid configurations.

A further limitation common to many existing approaches is their reliance on continuous, high-bandwidth data transmission from distributed sensors to centralized processing units. This necessitates the establishment of robust, low-latency communication networks, which may not be available in remote or rural substations, and imposes substantial computational and storage burdens on cloud or edge infrastructure. The volume of raw waveform data generated by standard 4 kHz sampling rates—often exceeding gigabytes per day per feeder—renders real-time processing infeasible without significant data reduction techniques. Many proposed solutions therefore require either expensive edge computing hardware or extensive data compression, both of which increase system complexity and cost.

In contrast to these methods, the present invention operates exclusively on data already available within standard substation automation systems, utilizing the same instrument transformers, I/O interfaces, and communication protocols—specifically IEC 61850-9-2LE and IEC 61850-8-1—that are employed for protection and control functions. By leveraging disturbance recordings triggered by existing protection algorithms, the invention eliminates the need for any additional hardware, communication infrastructure, or sensor installation. It further introduces a novel data processing pipeline that transforms high-dimensional, irregularly sampled waveform data into a compact, semantically rich feature representation, enabling efficient training and inference on standard computing hardware. The invention also introduces a filtering mechanism that dynamically identifies and discards irrelevant or non-predictive disturbance events, thereby reducing noise and improving model accuracy without increasing computational load. Finally, the use of a long short-term memory network architecture allows the system to model long-term temporal dependencies spanning up to one week, a temporal horizon far exceeding that of conventional short-term forecasting methods. This combination of features—zero additional hardware, high predictive accuracy, extended prediction window, computational efficiency, and applicability to all types of power lines, including underground and underwater cables—constitutes a fundamental advancement over the state of the art.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a system and method for predicting electrical faults in power transmission and distribution networks up to one week in advance, utilizing only existing current and voltage measurements obtained from standard substation instrumentation without requiring the installation of any additional sensors. The system operates by capturing disturbance recordings of voltage and current waveforms at a sampling rate of four kilohertz, as triggered by protection algorithms within the substation’s IEC 61850-compliant communication infrastructure. These raw waveform recordings are processed through a feature engineering pipeline that extracts a set of scalar-derived features, including root mean square values, impedance, active and reactive power, harmonic components, phase angles, and statistical descriptors such as maximum, minimum, and standard deviation, resulting in a feature vector of approximately three hundred dimensions that encapsulates the essential electrical characteristics of each disturbance event. These feature vectors are organized into a time series representing a one-week historical window of disturbance activity preceding each prediction point, with the target variable being a binary indicator of whether a fault occurs within the subsequent one-week period.

The system employs a neural network architecture comprising three sequential components: a filtering module, a long short-term memory layer, and a classification head. The filtering module is a fully connected neural network trained on a manually labeled subset of disturbance recordings to distinguish between those that contain meaningful precursors to fault conditions and those that represent benign or irrelevant events, such as normal switching transients or measurement noise. Only those recordings classified as relevant are passed to the subsequent layer, thereby reducing computational load and improving predictive fidelity by eliminating distracting signals. The long short-term memory layer processes the filtered sequence of feature vectors to capture long-range temporal dependencies and nonlinear patterns in the evolution of electrical conditions leading to fault initiation. The output of the LSTM layer is a single high-dimensional feature representation that encodes the temporal context of the preceding week. This representation is then fed into a classification head consisting of two fully connected layers with nonlinear activation functions, followed by a sigmoid function that outputs a probability value between zero and one, representing the likelihood of a fault occurring within the next seven days.

The system is capable of operating on data from multiple spatially distributed feeders or substations by independently processing each feeder’s time series through its own filtering and LSTM components, then concatenating the resulting feature vectors before applying the classification head. This enables the model to leverage contextual information from neighboring network segments, such as correlated disturbances caused by shared load patterns, cascading events, or common environmental stressors, without requiring centralized fusion of raw data. Data augmentation techniques, including the permutation of phase sequences across three-phase systems, are applied during training to artificially expand the dataset and improve model robustness, effectively multiplying the number of training samples by a factor of six without requiring additional recorded events. The entire system is designed to be computationally efficient, enabling deployment on standard laptop-grade central processing units, thereby eliminating the need for specialized hardware or cloud-based inference infrastructure. The model is trained using a binary cross-entropy loss function and optimized through hyperparameter tuning focused on maximizing specificity to minimize false alarms, while maintaining a high recall rate to ensure that genuine fault precursors are not overlooked. The resulting system provides a scalable, cost-effective, and universally applicable solution for predictive fault detection in modern electrical grids, capable of operating in environments where traditional sensor-based methods are impractical, and where the economic and operational consequences of unplanned outages demand the highest possible reliability.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF SOME EMBODIMENTS

The detailed embodiment of the invention encompasses a fully integrated system architecture for the prediction of electrical faults in medium voltage power distribution and transmission networks, implemented through a sequence of interdependent software modules operating on standardized substation data. The system is designed to function without any modification to existing hardware, relying exclusively on the current and voltage measurements routinely captured by instrument transformers and digitized by protective relays compliant with the IEC 61850 standard. These measurements are transmitted via IEC 61850-9-2LE for sampled values and IEC 61850-8-1 for generic object-oriented substation events, forming the sole data source for the entire predictive framework. The absence of additional sensors, communication infrastructure, or external data feeds distinguishes this invention from all prior approaches, which either require dedicated instrumentation or supplementary data sources such as weather feeds or visual inspections.

The first component of the system is the data acquisition and pre-filtering module, which resides on an edge computing device located within or adjacent to the substation. This device continuously monitors the IEC 61850 data stream for disturbances triggered by protection algorithms, such as overcurrent, differential, or distance relays, which activate upon detecting deviations from normal operating conditions. Upon triggering, the edge device captures a time window of voltage and current waveforms, typically spanning several cycles before and after the event, at a sampling rate of four kilohertz. Each disturbance recording is time-stamped and tagged with metadata indicating the feeder identifier, phase, and type of relay activation. The recordings are then compressed and transmitted to a centralized cloud-based storage system, where they are aggregated across multiple substations and feeders. This architecture ensures that only a fraction of the total data generated by the substation—namely, those events flagged as disturbances—is transmitted, thereby minimizing bandwidth consumption and reducing the computational burden on the cloud infrastructure. The use of edge-based pre-filtering is critical to the system’s scalability, as it prevents the transmission of redundant or non-informative data that would otherwise overwhelm downstream processing systems.

Once stored in the cloud, the disturbance recordings are ingested by the feature engineering module, which performs a structured transformation of each high-dimensional waveform into a compact, semantically meaningful feature vector. This process begins with the extraction of fundamental electrical quantities from the raw voltage and current samples. For each phase and for each recorded disturbance, the root mean square values of voltage and current are computed over the entire duration of the event, providing a measure of the overall magnitude of the disturbance. The impedance is then derived as the ratio of voltage to current, averaged across the waveform, yielding insight into the load or fault impedance characteristics. Active and reactive power are calculated using standard three-phase formulas, allowing the system to detect imbalances or power factor deviations that may indicate deteriorating insulation or unbalanced loading. Harmonic components are extracted via fast Fourier transform analysis, with magnitudes and phase angles of the first fifteen harmonics recorded, as these are known to be sensitive indicators of nonlinear loads, arcing, or insulation degradation. Phase angles between voltage and current, as well as between the three phases themselves, are computed to detect asymmetries that may precede unbalanced faults. In addition to these derived quantities, statistical descriptors are calculated for each feature, including the maximum, minimum, mean, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis, ensuring that both the magnitude and variability of the disturbance are captured. The result is a single feature vector of approximately three hundred scalar values per disturbance recording, representing a dense, low-dimensional encoding of the original waveform that preserves the most predictive information while reducing computational complexity by over two orders of magnitude.

The feature vectors are then organized into a multidimensional time series structure, where each row corresponds to a disturbance event and each column to a specific feature. The time series is constructed such that, for any given prediction time, the input to the model consists of all disturbance recordings occurring within the preceding seven-day window. This temporal framing is critical to the invention’s ability to predict faults up to one week in advance, as it allows the model to learn the evolution of precursor conditions over extended periods. The target variable for each prediction point is a binary label indicating whether a fault occurred within the subsequent seven-day period following the prediction time. Faults are defined as any event that results in the tripping of a circuit breaker or the activation of a protection relay that isolates a section of the network, as recorded in the substation’s event log. This binary labeling scheme ensures that the model is trained to predict the occurrence of actual, actionable faults rather than transient disturbances or nuisance alarms.

The core of the invention lies in the neural network architecture, which is composed of three distinct but interconnected components: a filtering module, a long short-term memory layer, and a classification head. The filtering module is implemented as a fully connected feedforward neural network with multiple hidden layers, trained on a manually labeled dataset of disturbance recordings. This dataset was curated by domain experts who reviewed hundreds of recorded events and classified them as either “relevant” or “irrelevant” based on their association with known fault conditions. Relevant recordings were those that preceded a subsequent fault by a period of hours to days and exhibited consistent electrical signatures such as increasing harmonic distortion, progressive impedance drift, or persistent phase imbalance. Irrelevant recordings were those that occurred during normal switching operations, lightning-induced transients, or other events that did not lead to fault initiation. The filtering network is trained using binary cross-entropy loss to maximize the accuracy of this classification. Once trained, the filtering module operates in real time to screen each incoming disturbance recording, passing only those classified as relevant to the subsequent LSTM layer. This step is essential for reducing noise and preventing the model from being misled by transient or non-predictive events, which are abundant in real-world grid data. Without this filtering mechanism, the model would be overwhelmed by irrelevant signals, leading to degraded performance and increased false alarm rates.

The long short-term memory layer, which follows the filtering module, is a recurrent neural network architecture specifically designed to handle sequential data with long-term dependencies. Unlike conventional feedforward networks, which treat each input independently, the LSTM layer maintains an internal state that evolves over time, allowing it to selectively retain or discard information from previous time steps based on learned gating mechanisms. This capability is crucial for modeling the gradual degradation processes that precede electrical faults, which may unfold over days or even weeks. The LSTM layer processes the sequence of filtered feature vectors, one at a time, updating its hidden state with each new input. The final hidden state, after processing the entire seven-day window, serves as a compressed representation of the temporal evolution of the electrical conditions leading up to the prediction point. This representation encapsulates not only the magnitude of individual disturbances but also their frequency, sequence, and interdependencies, enabling the model to recognize patterns that are invisible to static feature analysis.

The output of the LSTM layer is then passed to the classification head, which consists of two fully connected layers with rectified linear unit activation functions, followed by a final sigmoid layer that outputs a probability value between zero and one. This probability represents the model’s estimate of the likelihood that a fault will occur within the next seven days, given the historical pattern of disturbances. The classification head is trained using binary cross-entropy loss, with the target being the binary fault label associated with each prediction point. During training, the model is optimized to minimize the difference between predicted probabilities and actual fault occurrences, while also incorporating regularization techniques such as dropout and weight decay to prevent overfitting. The model is trained on a dataset comprising thousands of disturbance sequences from multiple substations, with each sequence spanning a seven-day window and labeled according to whether a fault occurred within the subsequent week. The training process is conducted over multiple epochs, with the learning rate and decay parameters carefully tuned to balance recall and specificity, with a strong emphasis on maximizing specificity to avoid costly false alarms.

An important aspect of the training methodology is the application of data augmentation techniques to increase the diversity and size of the training dataset. Given the limited number of actual fault events in real-world power systems, the dataset is inherently imbalanced, with far more non-fault recordings than fault-associated ones. To mitigate this imbalance and improve generalization, the system employs a phase permutation augmentation strategy. In three-phase systems, the relative ordering of phases A, B, and C is arbitrary from a physical standpoint, as long as the phase relationships are preserved. Therefore, the system generates additional training samples by randomly permuting the phase labels of each disturbance recording, effectively creating six unique variations of each original recording (3! = 6 permutations). This technique does not alter the underlying electrical behavior of the system but increases the effective size of the dataset by a factor of six, thereby enhancing the model’s ability to learn invariant patterns across phase configurations. This augmentation is applied only during training and is not used during inference, ensuring that the model’s predictions remain grounded in real-world phase relationships.

The system is further designed to support multi-location prediction by processing data from multiple feeders or substations in parallel. Each feeder’s disturbance sequence is independently processed through its own filtering and LSTM components, producing a unique feature vector that encodes the temporal evolution of conditions specific to that segment of the network. These individual feature vectors are then concatenated into a single input vector for the classification head, allowing the model to consider the combined influence of correlated disturbances across multiple locations. For example, a fault on one feeder may be preceded by a series of disturbances on an adjacent feeder due to shared load patterns, voltage sags, or cascading effects. By incorporating information from neighboring feeders, the model gains a broader contextual understanding of grid behavior, improving its ability to detect early warning signs that may not be apparent when considering a single feeder in isolation. This multi-location capability is particularly valuable in radial distribution networks, where faults on one branch can influence the behavior of downstream feeders, and in meshed networks, where power flows may shift dynamically in response to disturbances elsewhere in the system.

The inference process is designed for continuous, real-time operation. At regular intervals—typically once per hour—the system retrieves the most recent seven-day window of disturbance recordings from the cloud storage, applies the filtering module to remove irrelevant events, feeds the filtered sequence into the LSTM layer, and computes the fault probability via the classification head. The resulting probability is then transmitted back to the monitoring system, where it is displayed to operators alongside contextual information such as weather conditions, load profiles, and maintenance schedules. If the probability exceeds a predefined threshold, an alert is generated, triggering a maintenance workflow that may include visual inspection, thermal scanning, or targeted diagnostic testing. The threshold is not fixed but may be adjusted dynamically based on operational priorities, with higher thresholds used during periods of low grid stress to minimize false alarms, and lower thresholds activated during extreme weather events or periods of high load to maximize fault detection sensitivity.

The computational efficiency of the system is a key innovation. Unlike deep learning models that require high-performance graphics processing units or cloud-based inference servers, the architecture of this invention is deliberately optimized for low-resource environments. The feature engineering process reduces the dimensionality of the input data by over 99%, and the filtering mechanism further reduces the number of inputs to the LSTM layer by up to 70%, depending on the density of relevant disturbances. The LSTM layer itself is designed with a moderate number of hidden units and a single recurrent layer, avoiding the computational overhead of deep or stacked architectures. The classification head is minimal, consisting of only two fully connected layers. As a result, the entire inference pipeline can be executed on a standard laptop-class central processing unit with no specialized hardware, enabling deployment in remote substations, mobile inspection units, or even field service devices. This level of computational efficiency is unprecedented in the field of predictive grid analytics and represents a significant departure from the prevailing paradigm of cloud-dependent, high-performance computing.

The system’s performance has been validated across multiple real-world installations, including medium voltage substations serving urban, suburban, and rural distribution networks. In all cases, the system demonstrated a recall rate exceeding 66% at a specificity of over 91%, meaning that it correctly identified more than two-thirds of all faults occurring within the prediction window while maintaining a false alarm rate of less than nine percent. This performance significantly outperformed baseline methods that relied on simple counting of disturbance events or thresholding of harmonic levels, which achieved recall rates below 10% at comparable specificity levels. The system’s ability to predict faults up to one week in advance enabled utilities to schedule maintenance during planned outages, reducing the number of unplanned outages by over 40% and decreasing average restoration times by more than 50%. Furthermore, because the system requires no additional hardware, the total cost of ownership was reduced by over 90% compared to sensor-based alternatives, making it accessible to utilities of all sizes, including those with limited capital budgets.

The invention also includes a model retraining and adaptation mechanism that allows the system to continuously improve its performance over time. As new fault events occur and are confirmed by maintenance logs, the associated disturbance sequences are added to the training dataset, and the model is periodically retrained to incorporate this new information. This feedback loop ensures that the system adapts to changes in grid configuration, load patterns, environmental conditions, and equipment aging, maintaining high accuracy over its operational lifetime. The retraining process is automated and can be scheduled during off-peak hours to minimize computational load on the cloud infrastructure. Additionally, the system supports model versioning and rollback, allowing operators to revert to a previous version if performance degrades due to data drift or other anomalies.

In summary, the invention provides a comprehensive, end-to-end solution for predictive fault detection in electrical power systems that is unprecedented in its combination of accuracy, scalability, cost-effectiveness, and operational simplicity. By leveraging existing infrastructure, eliminating the need for additional sensors, and employing a novel neural network architecture optimized for long-term temporal prediction, the system transforms the way utilities manage grid reliability. It enables proactive maintenance, reduces operational costs, minimizes revenue loss from outages, and enhances the safety and resilience of the electrical grid—all without requiring any modification to the physical infrastructure. This represents a paradigm shift in power system analytics, moving from reactive protection to predictive assurance, and establishes a new standard for intelligent grid management in the 21st century.
