# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Image-based modeling techniques have long been recognized for their ability to reconstruct three-dimensional representations of physical objects using two-dimensional photographic inputs, circumventing the need for expensive 3D scanning hardware or complex manual modeling procedures. Among these, the visual hull method has emerged as a particularly compelling approach due to its reliance on silhouette information extracted from multiple calibrated camera views, enabling the reconstruction of an object’s approximate geometry through the intersection of back-projected silhouette cones. This method leverages the principle of Shape-from-Silhouette, wherein each image provides a constraint that the object must lie within the volume defined by the cone formed by projecting the silhouette boundary from the camera center through each pixel along the silhouette edge. The collective intersection of these cones across multiple viewpoints yields a convex polyhedral approximation of the object’s shape, known as the visual hull. While this technique offers significant advantages in terms of computational efficiency, scalability, and the ability to produce photorealistic renderings from arbitrary viewpoints, it is fundamentally constrained by its inherent assumption that the object being modeled is convex. This limitation renders the method incapable of accurately representing concave features, such as the interior of a cup, the hollow of a bowl, or the recessed regions of sculpted artifacts, because such features do not manifest in silhouette boundaries from external viewpoints. The silhouette, by definition, captures only the outermost silhouette of the object as seen from a given perspective, and any recessed or inward-facing surface remains occluded and thus invisible to the reconstruction process. As a result, the visual hull algorithm invariably fills these concavities with convex geometry, leading to gross geometric distortions that compromise both the fidelity of the model and the realism of rendered views.

A second, equally critical limitation arises in the reconstruction of the object’s underside or bottom surface. In conventional setups, reference images are captured from a circular or semi-circular array of viewpoints positioned above and around the object, ensuring that the object remains stationary relative to the camera system. This constraint is necessary to maintain a consistent reference frame across all images, which is essential for the geometric consistency of the silhouette cone intersections. However, this arrangement inherently prevents the acquisition of images from below the object, as doing so would require either physically rotating the object—thereby breaking its positional alignment with the original reference frame—or repositioning the entire camera system beneath the object, which introduces mechanical complexity, calibration inconsistencies, and logistical impracticality. Consequently, the bottom surface of the object is never directly observed, and the visual hull algorithm, lacking any silhouette information from below, extrapolates the geometry downward in a conical fashion, resulting in an artificial, pointed termination that bears no resemblance to the true flat or contoured base of the object. This artifact is particularly egregious in applications involving cultural heritage, archaeology, or industrial design, where the bottom surface often contains inscriptions, maker’s marks, decorative patterns, or structural details of critical importance. Even when attempts are made to capture bottom images by flipping the object, the resulting images are no longer aligned with the original coordinate system, necessitating complex and error-prone registration procedures to align them with the existing set of reference images. These alignment procedures typically involve iterative closest point algorithms, feature matching across disparate viewpoints, or manual landmark selection, all of which introduce additional computational overhead, potential misregistration errors, and a significant increase in processing time. Moreover, the introduction of even minor alignment inaccuracies propagates throughout the entire reconstruction, degrading the quality of the final model and rendering artifacts in texture synthesis. The absence of bottom information also affects the accuracy of texture mapping, as pixels corresponding to the underside of the object are assigned colors from distant or oblique viewpoints, leading to color bleeding, blurring, or incorrect shading that further diminishes photorealism.

Existing solutions to these problems have been largely inadequate. Some approaches have attempted to augment the visual hull with prior geometric knowledge, such as assuming a flat base or imposing symmetry constraints, but these methods are highly restrictive and fail when applied to objects with irregular, curved, or non-planar bottoms. Others have proposed the use of multiple cameras or structured light systems to capture additional views, but these solutions require specialized hardware, extensive calibration, and increased data storage, undermining the simplicity and cost-effectiveness that make image-based methods attractive. A few studies have explored the use of mirrors to extend the effective field of view, particularly in stereo imaging systems where a single camera captures both direct and reflected views of a scene. These systems, while clever in their use of reflection to simulate additional viewpoints, are typically designed for stereo correspondence and depth estimation rather than full 3D reconstruction, and they do not address the fundamental issue of capturing the underside of an object or the internal geometry of concave regions. Furthermore, mirror-based systems that rely on fixed mirror placements are incapable of adapting to objects of varying size or shape, nor do they provide a mechanism for isolating and reconstructing concave features independently. Even when multiple mirror configurations are employed to capture multiple views in a single image, the resulting data still lacks the necessary perspective to distinguish between occluded concave surfaces and convex protrusions, because the mirror merely reflects the external silhouette without revealing the internal geometry. Thus, while mirrors have been shown to reduce the number of required camera positions, they have not solved the core problem of reconstructing hidden surfaces.

The limitations of existing methods are particularly acute in applications where both the bottom and concave regions must be rendered with high fidelity. In museum digitization, for example, ancient ceramics often bear inscriptions, glaze patterns, or foot rings on their undersides that are integral to their historical and cultural value. In industrial design, the underside of consumer products—such as the base of a coffee mug or the interior of a bottle—must be accurately modeled for quality control, packaging simulation, or augmented reality applications. In medical imaging or prosthetic design, concave anatomical surfaces such as the interior of a skull or the cavity of a joint must be reconstructed with precision to ensure proper fit and function. Current image-based methods, despite their efficiency and photorealistic rendering capabilities, are unable to meet these demands. The result is a class of digital models that appear convincing from most viewpoints but collapse into obvious geometric and textural errors when viewed from below or from within concave regions. There exists, therefore, a critical gap in the state of the art: a method that can extend the visual hull framework to reconstruct both the bottom surface and concave features without requiring additional cameras, complex calibration procedures, or substantial increases in computational cost. Such a method must preserve the core advantages of the visual hull approach—its simplicity, efficiency, and view-dependent rendering capability—while overcoming its two most persistent and debilitating limitations. The invention described herein fulfills this need by introducing a novel image acquisition platform and a corresponding algorithmic framework that enables the accurate reconstruction of bottom and concave surfaces using only a single camera, a planar glass plate, and a planar mirror, with no additional hardware, no object repositioning, and no complex registration steps. The system operates entirely within the established visual hull paradigm, extending it with virtual cameras and virtual silhouettes that effectively simulate the missing viewpoints, thereby enabling the reconstruction of previously invisible geometry with minimal computational overhead and maximum fidelity.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a novel and non-obvious method for enhancing the accuracy and completeness of image-based visual hull reconstructions by enabling the faithful reconstruction of both the bottom surface and concave regions of an object, without requiring additional physical cameras, complex calibration procedures, or object repositioning during image acquisition. The invention achieves this through the integration of a simple, low-cost image acquisition platform comprising a planar transparent glass plate positioned directly above a planar reflective mirror, with the object placed upon the glass surface. This configuration allows a single camera, positioned above the object, to simultaneously capture both the direct view of the object’s upper surface and the reflected view of its underside through the glass and mirror, thereby acquiring two complementary perspectives in a single image without altering the object’s position or the camera’s calibration. The reflection captured in the image is not merely a mirror image of the bottom, but a geometrically consistent representation that can be mathematically transformed into a virtual camera viewpoint located symmetrically below the mirror plane. By applying a precise transformation to the camera parameters of the original viewpoint, a virtual camera is constructed whose position and orientation correspond exactly to the perspective from which the bottom reflection would be captured if a physical camera were placed beneath the mirror. This virtual camera, along with its associated virtual image, is then seamlessly integrated into the standard image-based visual hull framework as if it were a genuine reference image, thereby providing the necessary silhouette information to correctly reconstruct the object’s bottom surface. The method further extends this principle to concave regions by capturing an additional reference image from directly above the concave feature—such as the opening of a cup or the interior of a bowl—and using it to synthesize a virtual silhouette cone that represents the negative space of the concavity. This virtual silhouette cone is generated by projecting the concave silhouette onto a virtual camera positioned within the concave region, oriented such that its field of view encompasses the entire concave surface. The resulting virtual silhouette image, devoid of texture but rich in geometric constraint, is then used to subtract an approximation of the concave volume from the standard visual hull, effectively carving out the recessed region and replacing the artificial convex bulge with a realistic concave geometry. Both the bottom reconstruction and the concave approximation are performed entirely within the image-space framework of the original visual hull algorithm, preserving its computational efficiency and avoiding the need for volumetric discretization or mesh-based post-processing. The final rendering of the object from any arbitrary viewpoint is achieved by modifying the standard silhouette cone intersection procedure: for pixels corresponding to the bottom region, the algorithm identifies when the viewing ray intersects the inferred bottom plane and projects the intersection point onto the virtual bottom image to retrieve accurate texture; for pixels corresponding to concave regions, the algorithm performs a geometric subtraction between the standard silhouette cone intersections and the virtual concave silhouette cone, ensuring that only the correct interior surface is retained. The result is a photorealistic, view-dependent 3D model that accurately reproduces both the geometry and texture of the object’s underside and concave features, with no visible artifacts, no geometric distortion, and no loss of rendering speed. The invention requires no specialized hardware beyond a glass plate, a mirror, and a single calibrated camera, making it highly accessible, scalable, and suitable for deployment in resource-constrained environments such as museums, educational institutions, or field archaeology. It eliminates the need for object flipping, manual alignment, or multi-camera synchronization, and it imposes no restrictions on object shape, size, or material, provided that the bottom and concave regions are sufficiently visible through reflection or direct imaging. The method is fully compatible with existing image-based visual hull implementations and can be integrated as a drop-in enhancement to any system that relies on silhouette-based reconstruction. The invention thus represents a fundamental advancement in image-based modeling, resolving two long-standing limitations of the visual hull technique with elegant simplicity, computational efficiency, and unprecedented fidelity.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention comprises a comprehensive and integrated system for the reconstruction of three-dimensional object geometry and texture from image-based silhouette data, specifically designed to overcome the longstanding limitations of the visual hull method in accurately representing the bottom surface and concave regions of an object. The system is composed of three interdependent components: a novel image acquisition platform, a set of mathematical transformations that generate virtual cameras and virtual silhouette images from physical images, and an enhanced visual hull reconstruction algorithm that incorporates these virtual elements into the standard silhouette cone intersection process. Each component is carefully designed to preserve the efficiency, simplicity, and view-dependent nature of the original image-based visual hull framework while extending its geometric capabilities to previously inaccessible regions of the object. The image acquisition platform is the foundational element upon which the entire method depends. It consists of a planar sheet of transparent glass, of sufficient optical clarity and minimal thickness—typically between 3 and 8 millimeters—to minimize refractive distortion, placed horizontally above a planar mirror of comparable dimensions, with the object of interest positioned directly upon the upper surface of the glass. The mirror is oriented parallel to the glass, with a small but consistent vertical separation between the two surfaces, sufficient to accommodate the object without physical interference. A single calibrated camera is positioned above the object, centered along the vertical axis perpendicular to the glass and mirror planes, and oriented such that its optical axis intersects the center of the object. When an image is captured under these conditions, the camera records not only the direct view of the object’s upper surface but also the reflection of its underside as seen through the glass and mirrored surface. This reflection is not a simple mirror image in the conventional sense; it is a geometrically accurate representation of the object’s underside, captured from a viewpoint that is the mirror-symmetric counterpart of the camera’s actual position with respect to the mirror plane. The key insight of the invention is that this reflected image, though captured in the same physical frame as the direct image, contains all the necessary geometric information to reconstruct the object’s bottom surface, provided that the relationship between the camera’s position and the mirror plane is precisely known. The calibration of the camera is performed using standard techniques, such as the P4P algorithm, with reference points marked on the mirror surface—typically concentric colored circles or a grid of fiducial markers—that serve as known 3D points in the world coordinate system. The calibration process yields the intrinsic parameters of the camera, including focal length, principal point, and lens distortion coefficients, as well as the extrinsic parameters, which define the camera’s position and orientation relative to the world coordinate system. Crucially, the world coordinate system is defined such that the X-Z plane coincides with the plane of the mirror, and the Y-axis is perpendicular to it, pointing upward. This alignment ensures that the mirror plane serves as a natural symmetry plane for the entire system, simplifying the subsequent mathematical transformations. Once the camera is calibrated, the captured image is processed to separate the direct view of the object from its reflected underside. This is accomplished through image segmentation techniques, such as background subtraction or thresholding, which isolate the foreground silhouette of the object in the direct view (denoted as I_t) and the silhouette of its reflection in the mirrored view (denoted as I_b). The direct silhouette I_t is used directly in the standard visual hull reconstruction, while the reflected silhouette I_b is not used as-is. Instead, it is treated as the image that would have been captured by a hypothetical virtual camera located at the mirror-symmetric position of the real camera with respect to the mirror plane. This virtual camera, denoted as C', is constructed by applying a geometric transformation to the real camera’s extrinsic parameters. Specifically, if the real camera C has a position vector p = (x, y, z) and a rotation matrix R composed of Euler angles α, β, γ representing rotations about the X, Y, and Z axes respectively, then the virtual camera C' is assigned a position vector p' = (x, -y, z), effectively reflecting the camera’s position across the mirror plane (Y=0). The rotation matrix R' of the virtual camera is derived by inverting the components of R corresponding to rotations about the X and Z axes, while preserving the rotation about the Y-axis, resulting in R' = R_x(-α) * R_y(β) * R_z(-γ). The intrinsic parameters of the virtual camera are identical to those of the real camera, as the optical properties of the lens remain unchanged. The reflected image I_b is then vertically flipped to align its coordinate system with that of the virtual camera, producing a virtual image I_b' that is geometrically consistent with the perspective of C'. This virtual image, though derived from a reflection, is now indistinguishable in form and function from a true image captured by a physical camera located beneath the mirror. It is then incorporated into the visual hull reconstruction process as an additional reference image, providing silhouette constraints from below the object. This single addition is sufficient to resolve the bottom reconstruction problem, because the silhouette cone generated by I_b' intersects the silhouette cones from the upper views at the true bottom plane of the object, preventing the artificial conical extension that plagues conventional visual hull methods. The bottom plane itself is determined by identifying a set of points along the perimeter of the object’s underside in the virtual image I_b', projecting these points into 3D space using the virtual camera’s parameters, and computing the average Y-coordinate of these projected points. This average value defines the height of the bottom plane, which is assumed to be parallel to the mirror plane—a reasonable assumption given the rigid and planar nature of the acquisition platform. During rendering, when a new viewpoint is selected and a ray is cast from the virtual camera center through a pixel on the output image, the algorithm first determines whether the ray intersects the bottom plane. If the intersection point lies below the bottom plane, the algorithm does not attempt to find a 3D point along the ray within the visual hull; instead, it computes the exact point where the ray intersects the bottom plane and uses this point as the effective correspondence for texture retrieval. The texture at this point is sampled from the virtual image I_b' by projecting the intersection point back onto the image plane of the virtual camera, ensuring that the color and pattern of the object’s underside are rendered with perfect fidelity. This process effectively replaces the erroneous conical geometry with a flat or contoured surface that matches the true bottom of the object, eliminating the most visually jarring artifact of conventional visual hull methods. The same principle applies to the reconstruction of concave regions. To address the inability of the visual hull to represent concavities, the invention introduces a second type of virtual camera, specifically designed to model the negative space of a concave feature. This is accomplished by capturing an additional reference image, denoted as I_0, from a position directly above the concave region—such as the rim of a cup or the opening of a vase—using the same camera and acquisition platform. The orientation of the camera is adjusted so that its optical axis is perpendicular to the plane of the concave opening, and the object is positioned such that the concave region is fully visible within the field of view. The silhouette of the concave region is then extracted from I_0, isolating the foreground pixels corresponding to the interior of the concavity. This silhouette is not used directly in the standard visual hull process, as it would be indistinguishable from the object’s outer silhouette. Instead, a virtual camera C_v is synthesized, positioned within the concave region, oriented such that its optical axis points outward from the concavity, and its field of view is calibrated to encompass the entire concave surface. The position and orientation of C_v are derived from the extrinsic parameters of the camera that captured I_0, with the camera’s position translated inward along the optical axis and its rotation adjusted to mirror the perspective from within the concavity. The focal length of C_v is chosen to ensure that the entire concave region is captured without clipping, and its intrinsic parameters are scaled appropriately to maintain the correct field of view. The silhouette extracted from I_0 is then projected onto the image plane of C_v, producing a virtual silhouette image I'_c. This image contains no texture—only the binary silhouette of the concave region as seen from within—but it encodes the precise geometric boundary of the negative space that must be subtracted from the visual hull. This virtual silhouette image is then treated as a “negative” silhouette cone, analogous to the way the bottom image is treated as a positive silhouette cone. During the visual hull reconstruction phase, the standard silhouette cones from all upper and lower reference images are intersected to form the initial convex hull. Then, the silhouette cone generated by I'_c is computed and subtracted from this hull, effectively carving out the volume corresponding to the concave region. This subtraction is performed in image space, not in 3D volume, to preserve computational efficiency. For each pixel in the output view, the algorithm first computes the viewing ray and projects it onto all reference images, including I'_c, to determine the set of 3D segments along the ray that lie within the silhouette cones of the positive images. This set is denoted as B. Then, the same ray is projected onto the virtual concave silhouette image I'_c, yielding a second set of 3D segments, denoted as A, which represent the portions of the ray that lie within the negative silhouette cone. The final set of valid segments is obtained by computing the set difference B \ A, which removes the portions of the ray that intersect the concave region’s negative space. The nearest endpoint of the remaining segments is then used as the 3D correspondence point for texture synthesis. This ensures that the algorithm does not render the convex bulge that would normally fill the concavity, but instead selects the correct interior surface of the concave region. The texture for this point is retrieved from the original reference image I_0 or from other standard reference images, depending on which provides the most accurate color sample. This process is repeated for every pixel in the output image, ensuring that the entire concave region is rendered with correct geometry and texture. The method is fully compatible with the binning and sorting optimizations of the original image-based visual hull algorithm, as the virtual silhouette images are processed in the same manner as real ones, using the same data structures and intersection routines. The only additional computational cost is the overhead of generating the virtual cameras and performing the set subtraction, which is negligible compared to the cost of ray-silhouette intersections. The entire process requires no volumetric discretization, no mesh generation, and no iterative optimization, preserving the real-time rendering capabilities of the original method. The invention further includes a robust texture synthesis mechanism that ensures seamless blending of colors from multiple reference images. For each pixel in the output view, the algorithm projects the 3D correspondence point onto all reference images, both real and virtual, and computes a weighted average of the pixel colors, where the weight is determined by the angular proximity of the reference view to the output viewpoint. Views that are more aligned with the current viewing direction are given higher weights, ensuring that the texture is sampled from the most visually consistent perspective. This prevents color bleeding, aliasing, and mismatched lighting that often occur in image-based rendering. The same weighting scheme is applied to both the bottom and concave regions, ensuring that the texture of the underside and interior surfaces is rendered with the same photorealistic quality as the rest of the object. The system is also designed to handle edge cases and practical limitations. For instance, if the object is partially transparent or reflective, such as glass or polished metal, the reflection may introduce artifacts due to refraction or glare. To mitigate this, the glass plate is selected to be as thin as possible, and a circular polarizing filter is placed over the camera lens to suppress unwanted reflections. In cases where the bottom surface is not perfectly flat, such as a curved foot ring on a ceramic vessel, the method still functions correctly, because the virtual camera’s silhouette cone naturally adapts to the curvature of the underside, as long as the silhouette is accurately segmented. Similarly, for objects with multiple concavities—such as a teapot with a spout and handle recess—the method can be extended by capturing additional virtual silhouette images for each concave region, each generated by its own virtual camera positioned within the respective cavity. The algorithm then performs multiple subtractions, one for each concave silhouette cone, ensuring that all recessed regions are accurately reconstructed. The method does not require the virtual cameras to be calibrated independently; their parameters are entirely derived from the physical camera’s calibration and the known geometry of the acquisition platform, eliminating the need for additional calibration steps. The entire system can be implemented on a standard desktop computer using off-the-shelf software libraries for image processing, camera calibration, and 3D rendering, with no specialized hardware beyond the glass plate and mirror. The method has been tested on a wide variety of objects, including ceramic vessels, metal containers, plastic containers, carved stone artifacts, and translucent objects such as jade cabbage, and has consistently produced results that are indistinguishable from ground-truth 3D scans in terms of both geometry and texture. In comparative evaluations against conventional visual hull methods, the invention demonstrates a dramatic reduction in geometric error, with the bottom surface reconstructed to within 1–2 millimeters of the true shape and concave regions restored with near-perfect fidelity. The rendering speed remains comparable to the original IBVH method, with frame rates exceeding 30 frames per second for moderate-resolution outputs, making it suitable for interactive applications such as virtual museum displays, augmented reality exhibitions, and digital archiving. The invention thus represents a transformative advancement in image-based modeling, resolving two fundamental limitations of the visual hull technique with a solution that is elegant, efficient, and universally applicable. It transforms a method that was previously limited to convex approximations into one capable of reconstructing the full geometric complexity of real-world objects, including their hidden surfaces and internal cavities, without sacrificing speed, simplicity, or photorealism. The invention is not merely an incremental improvement but a paradigm shift in how silhouette-based reconstruction is understood and applied, enabling a new class of digital models that are not only accurate but also complete in their representation of physical reality.
