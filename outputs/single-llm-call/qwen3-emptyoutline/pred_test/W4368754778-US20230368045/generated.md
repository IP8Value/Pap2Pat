## FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of machine learning and probabilistic modeling of complex dynamical systems involving multiple interacting agents, particularly in the context of autonomous systems such as autonomous vehicles, robotics, and intelligent transportation systems. More specifically, the invention provides a novel architecture and inference methodology for predicting the future trajectories of multiple interacting agents under uncertainty, using a deterministic approximation to the predictive distribution over time that is both computationally efficient and capable of capturing multi-modal behavior. The invention integrates graph neural networks with deep state-space models to model the latent dynamics of interacting agents, and introduces a Gaussian mixture model over the initial latent state to represent multiple plausible future behaviors. Crucially, the invention replaces stochastic sampling methods traditionally used for inference in such models with a deterministic moment-matching procedure that propagates mean and covariance statistics through graph neural network layers, enabling real-time deployment on embedded systems with constrained computational resources. The invention further introduces structured approximations to the covariance matrices of the Gaussian mixture components to reduce computational complexity from cubic to quadratic scaling with respect to the number of agents, making it feasible for large-scale traffic scenarios involving dozens of interacting vehicles. The resulting system is particularly suited for applications requiring high-fidelity, multi-modal trajectory forecasting under uncertainty, such as motion planning, collision avoidance, and decision-making in autonomous driving systems.

## BACKGROUND INFORMATION

The accurate prediction of future behavior in systems composed of multiple interacting agents remains a fundamental challenge in artificial intelligence and control theory. Such systems arise in numerous real-world contexts, including urban traffic flow, pedestrian dynamics, swarm robotics, and multiplayer game AI. In these settings, the behavior of each agent is not only influenced by its own internal state—such as velocity, acceleration, or intent—but also by the actions and positions of other agents in the environment. Traditional modeling approaches often treat agents independently, ignoring the rich, non-linear, and dynamic interactions that govern real-world behavior. This simplification leads to poor predictive performance, particularly in dense or high-stakes environments such as intersections, roundabouts, or highway merge zones.

Recent advances in deep learning have introduced powerful tools for modeling such systems. Graph neural networks (GNNs) have emerged as a natural framework for representing interactions between agents by encoding them as nodes in a graph, with edges representing spatial or functional relationships. GNNs enable information exchange between agents through message-passing mechanisms, allowing the state of one agent to be updated based on the states of its neighbors. This approach has been successfully applied to deterministic physical systems, such as rigid-body dynamics and fluid simulations, where the goal is to predict a single future trajectory for each agent. However, many real-world systems are inherently stochastic, and a single predicted trajectory is insufficient to capture the range of possible future outcomes. For example, in autonomous driving, a vehicle approaching a roundabout may either exit at the next available exit or continue circulating, and the decision often depends on hidden factors such as driver intent, which cannot be directly observed from position and velocity data alone.

To account for this uncertainty, probabilistic models have been developed that output a distribution over possible future trajectories rather than a single point estimate. State-space models (SSMs) provide a principled framework for modeling such systems by separating the latent, unobserved state of the system from the observed measurements. In deep state-space models (DSSMs), the transition and emission functions are parameterized by neural networks, allowing them to capture highly non-linear dynamics. However, computing the predictive distribution over future time steps in such models requires propagating probability distributions through non-linear functions, a task that generally lacks a closed-form solution. Existing approaches to this problem rely on Monte Carlo sampling, where thousands of trajectories are simulated to approximate the distribution. While these methods can, in principle, capture complex multi-modal behavior, they are computationally expensive and scale poorly with the number of agents, as the dimensionality of the latent state grows linearly with the number of agents. This makes them unsuitable for real-time applications on embedded systems with limited memory and processing power, such as those found in autonomous vehicles.

Alternative approaches attempt to avoid sampling by assuming that the predictive distribution remains approximately Gaussian at each time step. These methods, known as moment-matching techniques, propagate only the first two moments—mean and covariance—through the system dynamics. While effective for single-agent systems with linear or locally linear dynamics, these methods have not been extended to multi-agent systems with interaction-dependent dynamics modeled by GNNs. Moreover, even when such extensions are attempted, the resulting covariance matrices become prohibitively large and dense, as each agent’s state correlates with every other agent’s state through the interaction graph. The computational cost of maintaining and updating a full covariance matrix scales cubically with the number of agents, rendering these methods infeasible for scenarios involving more than a handful of vehicles.

Furthermore, existing probabilistic models for multi-agent systems often fail to distinguish between different behavioral modes. For instance, a vehicle may exhibit one of several distinct behaviors—such as turning left, continuing straight, or yielding—each of which leads to a qualitatively different future trajectory. A unimodal distribution cannot adequately represent such multi-modal behavior, leading to overconfident and inaccurate predictions. While mixture models, such as Gaussian mixture models (GMMs), have been proposed to capture multiple modes, their application to deep state-space models with GNN-based dynamics has been hindered by the lack of efficient, deterministic inference procedures. Current methods either resort to sampling-based approximations, which are slow and unstable, or impose restrictive assumptions on the structure of the latent dynamics that limit their expressiveness.

Finally, existing training objectives for probabilistic models often mismatch the intended use case. Many models are trained to maximize the joint likelihood of observed trajectories over time, which encourages the model to generate plausible-looking sequences of states. However, in autonomous systems, the primary concern is not the plausibility of entire trajectories, but the accuracy of marginal predictions at specific future time horizons—for example, predicting the position of surrounding vehicles at 3 or 5 seconds ahead to enable safe braking or lane changes. Training objectives that do not directly optimize for these marginal predictions often lead to suboptimal performance in real-world deployment. Thus, there exists a critical gap in the state of the art: a method that simultaneously (1) models interactions between agents via graph neural networks, (2) captures multi-modal future behavior via a mixture model over initial latent states, (3) performs efficient, deterministic inference without sampling, (4) scales gracefully with the number of agents through structured covariance approximations, and (5) is trained to optimize the predictive log-likelihood at future time horizons relevant to decision-making.

The present invention addresses all of these limitations by introducing a novel Graph Deep State-Space Model (GDSSM) with deterministic moment-matching inference, structured covariance approximations, and a training objective aligned with real-world autonomous system requirements. The invention enables the first fully deterministic, scalable, and multi-modal predictive framework for interacting dynamical systems that is suitable for deployment in safety-critical, resource-constrained environments.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method and system for predicting the future trajectories of multiple interacting agents in a stochastic dynamical system using a deterministic, computationally efficient, and multi-modal inference framework. The invention comprises a Graph Deep State-Space Model (GDSSM) that represents the latent state of each agent as part of a joint, high-dimensional latent space, where interactions between agents are modeled through graph neural network layers that propagate information across a dynamically constructed interaction graph. The initial latent state of the system is modeled as a Gaussian mixture model, enabling the representation of multiple distinct behavioral modes—such as turning, yielding, or accelerating—each corresponding to a distinct mixture component. The predictive distribution over future trajectories is computed deterministically by propagating the mean and covariance of each mixture component forward in time using a novel moment-matching procedure that operates directly on the outputs of graph neural network layers, eliminating the need for Monte Carlo sampling. The invention further introduces structured approximations to the covariance matrices of the mixture components that reduce computational complexity from cubic to quadratic scaling with respect to the number of agents, making the method suitable for real-time deployment on embedded systems. The model is trained to maximize the predictive log-likelihood of future observations at fixed time horizons, aligning the training objective with the actual decision-making requirements of autonomous systems. The resulting system produces calibrated, multi-modal predictive distributions that accurately reflect the uncertainty and interaction-dependent behavior of agents in complex environments, such as urban traffic scenarios, while maintaining computational efficiency sufficient for use in autonomous vehicles and other real-time control systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EXAMPLE EMBODIMENTS

The present invention is embodied in a system and method for predicting the future trajectories of multiple interacting agents in a stochastic dynamical system using a Graph Deep State-Space Model (GDSSM) with deterministic moment-matching inference and structured covariance approximations. The system operates by first encoding historical observations of agent states into a context variable that includes both the temporal history of each agent’s position and velocity, as well as the spatial relationships between agents at the current time step. This context variable is then processed by an embedding function, implemented as a graph neural network, which maps the historical observations into a probabilistic initial latent state represented as a Gaussian mixture model. Each mixture component in this initial distribution corresponds to a distinct behavioral hypothesis about the future, such as whether an agent intends to turn, merge, or continue straight. The number of mixture components is a configurable hyperparameter that determines the expressiveness of the model’s multimodal capacity.

Following initialization, the system propagates each mixture component forward in time using a deterministic moment-matching procedure that operates on the mean and covariance of the latent state at each time step. The transition dynamics between time steps are modeled by a graph neural network that updates the latent state of each agent based on the latent states of its neighbors in the interaction graph. The interaction graph is dynamically constructed at each time step based on the spatial proximity of agents, with edges connecting agents within a predefined radius. The graph neural network consists of two alternating operations: a message-passing aggregation step, in which each agent collects information from its neighbors via a permutation-invariant aggregation function such as mean pooling, and an update step, in which the agent’s latent state is transformed by a neural network that incorporates both its own state and the aggregated information from its neighbors. The transition model also includes a stochastic component that adds noise to the latent state, enabling the model to account for uncertainty in the dynamics.

The key innovation of the invention lies in the deterministic propagation of the mean and covariance of each mixture component through these graph neural network layers. Rather than sampling trajectories, the system computes the expected value and covariance of the next latent state by applying analytical moment-matching rules derived for each type of layer in the graph neural network. For affine transformations, the output mean and covariance are computed using standard linear transformation rules. For mean aggregation, the output moments are derived by treating the aggregation as a linear operation over the adjacency matrix of the graph, which is normalized to ensure numerical stability. For non-linear activation functions such as ReLU, the moments are computed using closed-form approximations derived from the properties of the activation function and the input distribution. The cross-covariance between the input and output of the transition function is computed using Stein’s lemma, which relates the expected Jacobian of the neural network to the cross-covariance term required for accurate moment propagation. This procedure is applied independently to each mixture component, ensuring that the multi-modal structure of the initial distribution is preserved throughout the prediction horizon.

The emission model, which maps the latent state to the observed space, is implemented as a separate neural network that decodes the latent state of each agent into its predicted position and velocity. This model is assumed to be agent-independent, meaning that the emission function for each agent does not depend on the states of other agents, although the latent state itself is coupled through the transition model. The emission model outputs a Gaussian distribution over the observed variables, with mean and covariance parameterized by neural networks. The final predictive distribution over future observations is obtained by marginalizing over the latent state distribution, which, due to the linearity of the emission model and the Gaussian nature of the latent distributions, results in a Gaussian mixture model over the observed space. The mixture weights remain unchanged from the initial distribution, while the mean and covariance of each component are updated according to the deterministic propagation rules.

To address the computational burden of maintaining full covariance matrices for large numbers of agents, the invention introduces a family of structured covariance approximations. The full covariance matrix, which captures all pairwise correlations between agents, scales cubically with the number of agents and becomes intractable beyond a small number of agents. The invention proposes four alternative approximations: (1) the main diagonal approximation, which retains only the diagonal elements of the covariance matrix, effectively assuming independence between all agents; (2) the main blocks approximation, which retains only the block-diagonal elements corresponding to each agent’s own state, preserving intra-agent correlations but assuming inter-agent independence; (3) the all diagonals approximation, which retains the diagonal elements of each agent-agent sub-block, allowing for correlations between agents but assuming independence between different latent features of the same agent; and (4) the full covariance approximation, which retains all elements. These approximations are selected based on the trade-off between computational efficiency and predictive accuracy, with the all diagonals approximation providing the best compromise, offering nearly the same predictive performance as the full covariance matrix while reducing computational complexity from O(M³) to O(M²), where M is the number of agents. The choice of approximation is made during system design and can be adapted to the computational constraints of the target deployment platform, such as an onboard vehicle computer with limited memory and processing power.

The model is trained using a predictive log-likelihood objective that directly optimizes the likelihood of future observations at fixed time horizons, without requiring feedback from intermediate observations. This objective is aligned with the operational requirements of autonomous systems, where decisions must be made based on predictions of future states at specific time points—such as 3 or 5 seconds ahead—rather than on the plausibility of entire trajectories. The training process involves computing the predictive log-likelihood for each mixture component using the deterministic moment-matching procedure, summing over all mixture components weighted by their initial probabilities, and maximizing the resulting objective using gradient-based optimization. This approach eliminates the need for variational inference, particle filtering, or Monte Carlo sampling during training, resulting in a stable, reproducible, and computationally efficient training procedure. The gradients are computed using backpropagation through the deterministic moment-matching operations, which are differentiable and can be implemented using standard deep learning frameworks.

The system is deployed in real-time by first receiving a sequence of historical observations of agent positions and velocities over a fixed time window, such as three seconds. The system then constructs the interaction graph based on the spatial proximity of agents, applies the embedding function to generate the initial Gaussian mixture model over the latent state, and propagates each mixture component forward in time using the deterministic moment-matching rules. At each time step, the system outputs a Gaussian mixture model over the predicted positions and velocities of all agents, which can be used directly for downstream decision-making tasks such as motion planning, collision avoidance, or risk assessment. The mixture weights provide a measure of belief in each behavioral mode, allowing the system to reason about uncertainty and ambiguity in agent intentions. The structured covariance approximations ensure that the system can operate in real-time even in dense traffic scenarios with dozens of agents, achieving inference latencies on the order of milliseconds on embedded hardware.

The invention has been validated on two large-scale autonomous driving datasets: the rounD dataset, which contains vehicle trajectories recorded at multiple roundabouts in Germany, and the NGSIM dataset, which contains vehicle trajectories recorded on U.S. highways. In both datasets, the proposed model outperforms state-of-the-art alternatives in terms of predictive accuracy, as measured by negative log-likelihood and root mean square error, while achieving orders of magnitude faster inference times than Monte Carlo-based methods. Ablation studies confirm that each component of the invention—namely, the GNN-based transition model, the Gaussian mixture prior over the initial latent state, the deterministic moment-matching inference, and the structured covariance approximations—contributes significantly to the overall performance. Furthermore, experiments on out-of-distribution traffic environments demonstrate that the model generalizes robustly to unseen intersection geometries and traffic patterns, indicating its potential for deployment in novel and unstructured environments.

The invention enables a paradigm shift in the modeling of interacting dynamical systems by replacing stochastic sampling with deterministic moment propagation, thereby achieving the expressiveness of multi-modal, interaction-aware models while retaining the computational efficiency required for real-time, safety-critical applications. The resulting system is not merely an incremental improvement over existing methods but represents a fundamentally new class of probabilistic models that are both scalable and interpretable, making them uniquely suited for deployment in autonomous vehicles, intelligent transportation systems, robotic navigation, and other domains where accurate, efficient, and uncertainty-aware prediction is essential.
