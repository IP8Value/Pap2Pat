## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The ability to monitor and manipulate intracellular redox state has long been a central objective in biomedical research, given the critical role of redox homeostasis in cellular signaling, metabolism, proliferation, and survival. Oxidative stress, characterized by an imbalance between the production of reactive oxygen species and the capacity of antioxidant defenses to neutralize them, is implicated in the pathogenesis of numerous diseases including cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and aging-related pathologies. Consequently, the development of tools capable of detecting and quantifying real-time changes in intracellular redox environment has been the subject of extensive investigation. Prior approaches have relied on genetically encoded fluorescent sensors such as roGFP, HyPer, and Grx1-roGFP, which exhibit ratiometric fluorescence changes in response to alterations in glutathione redox potential. While these systems have provided valuable insights into subcellular redox dynamics, they require genetic transfection, are limited to cell types amenable to stable expression, and are often subject to photobleaching, pH sensitivity, and interference from other cellular thiols. Alternative chemical probes such as monochlorobimane, thiol-specific dyes, and redox-sensitive rhodamine derivatives have been employed to assess glutathione levels or overall reducing capacity, but these agents typically report only on the concentration of free thiols, lack temporal resolution, and do not distinguish between extracellular and intracellular redox environments. Moreover, many of these probes require enzymatic activation, are subject to non-specific binding, or are rapidly metabolized or exported from cells, limiting their utility in longitudinal studies or in vivo applications. 

In parallel, cell-penetrating peptides have emerged as powerful vehicles for the intracellular delivery of macromolecular cargo, including proteins, nucleic acids, and small molecules. Among these, the model amphipathic peptide (MAP), with its sequence KLALKLALKALKAALKLA-NH₂, has been extensively studied for its ability to translocate across plasma membranes without the need for endocytosis, even under conditions of metabolic inhibition or low temperature. Early studies suggested that MAP enters cells via a direct translocation mechanism driven by electrostatic interactions between its positively charged residues and the negatively charged phospholipid head groups of the plasma membrane. Subsequent investigations revealed that while energy-independent pathways contribute to uptake, a significant fraction of internalization is also sensitive to ATP depletion, suggesting a more complex, multi-modal mechanism involving both passive and active components. Despite these advances, the precise biophysical determinants governing MAP translocation remain incompletely understood, and no prior art has established a direct, functional link between the rate or extent of MAP internalization and the intracellular redox state. 

Attempts to exploit the cell-penetrating properties of MAP for sensing applications have been limited to the use of disulfide-linked fluorophore conjugates, wherein fluorescence quenching is relieved upon intracellular reduction. One such system, described in the prior art, employed a disulfide bond to link a cell-penetrating peptide to a fluorophore-labeled cargo peptide, with fluorescence activation serving as a proxy for intracellular reduction. However, these systems were designed primarily as reporters of reductase activity within the cytosol, and did not account for or exploit the possibility that the initial step of cellular uptake itself might be influenced by the redox environment. As a result, these prior constructs provided only a partial view of the intracellular redox landscape, reporting exclusively on the reduction event while remaining agnostic to the efficiency of delivery. This limitation precluded their use in distinguishing between cells that fail to internalize the probe due to unfavorable redox conditions and those that internalize it but fail to reduce it due to enzymatic deficiency. Furthermore, no prior art has demonstrated that the uptake kinetics of a cell-penetrating peptide can be modulated by the redox state of the target cell, nor has any system been developed that integrates both uptake and reduction into a single, co-dependent, and quantitatively interpretable signal. 

The present invention addresses these longstanding deficiencies by introducing a novel molecular construct, herein referred to as “reductide,” which consists of a cell-penetrating peptide moiety covalently linked via a disulfide bond to a non-penetrating cargo peptide, each labeled with distinct fluorophores that enable simultaneous monitoring of cellular internalization and intracellular reduction. The cell-penetrating component, derived from the MAP sequence and conjugated to TAMRA, provides a stable, persistent signal that reflects the extent of membrane association and internalization, while the cargo peptide, conjugated to FAM and linked via a reducible disulfide bond, serves as a redox-sensitive reporter whose fluorescence is activated only upon intracellular reduction. Crucially, the inventors have discovered that the rate and magnitude of FAM signal development are not solely determined by the intracellular reducing environment, but are also profoundly influenced by the redox state of the cell prior to and during the process of internalization. This dual dependence—on both uptake and reduction—creates a unique, integrated readout that is far more sensitive and informative than any prior probe. The invention further demonstrates that this construct can detect subtle, physiologically relevant shifts in redox balance, including those induced by pro-oxidant and antioxidant agents, and that its response is reproducible across diverse cell types and experimental conditions. Moreover, the inventors have shown that reductide can distinguish between cells that have undergone adaptive antioxidant responses following low-dose oxidative stress and those that have been overwhelmed by high-dose insults, a distinction that is not possible with conventional redox probes. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the design of intracellular sensors, moving beyond passive reporters to active, context-sensitive molecular systems whose behavior is dynamically regulated by the very physiological parameter they are designed to measure. This innovation enables not only the detection of redox changes but also the selective targeting of cells based on their redox phenotype, opening new avenues for diagnostics, therapeutics, and precision medicine.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Definitions

For the purposes of this patent application, the term “reductide” refers to a synthetic, disulfide-linked molecular construct comprising a first peptide moiety derived from a cell-penetrating peptide sequence, covalently conjugated to a first fluorophore, and a second peptide moiety derived from a non-cell-penetrating peptide sequence, covalently conjugated to a second fluorophore, wherein the two peptide moieties are joined by a reducible disulfide bond. The first fluorophore is selected from the group consisting of tetramethylrhodamine, rhodamine derivatives, cyanine dyes, and other fluorophores that exhibit minimal quenching upon proximity to the second fluorophore and are stable under physiological conditions. The second fluorophore is selected from the group consisting of fluorescein, fluorescein derivatives, carboxyfluorescein, and other fluorophores whose emission is quenched by Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) when in close proximity to the first fluorophore, and whose fluorescence is restored upon separation following disulfide bond reduction. The cell-penetrating peptide sequence is characterized by an amphipathic alpha-helical conformation in which hydrophobic residues are aligned along one face of the helix and positively charged residues are aligned along the opposing face, enabling interaction with lipid bilayers and translocation across cellular membranes. The non-cell-penetrating peptide sequence lacks the structural or electrostatic properties necessary for efficient membrane translocation and is incapable of crossing the plasma membrane in the absence of a linked cell-penetrating moiety. The disulfide bond is formed between the thiol groups of cysteine residues incorporated at the N-termini of each peptide moiety, and is susceptible to reduction by intracellular thiols such as glutathione, thioredoxin, or other reducing agents present in the cytosol. The term “reduction” refers to the cleavage of the disulfide bond via electron transfer from a reducing agent, resulting in the separation of the two peptide-fluorophore moieties and the consequent dequenching of the second fluorophore. The term “cellular uptake” refers to the process by which the reductide construct traverses the plasma membrane and enters the intracellular compartment, whether via direct translocation, endocytosis, or a combination of mechanisms, and includes membrane association, insertion, and internalization. The term “redox state” refers to the relative concentrations of reduced and oxidized forms of intracellular redox buffers, particularly the glutathione pool (GSH/GSSG), the thioredoxin system, and other thiol-containing molecules, and encompasses the overall reducing or oxidizing potential of the intracellular environment as determined by the Nernst equation. The term “FAM signal” refers to the fluorescence emission at approximately 528 nm following excitation at approximately 485 nm, which is indicative of disulfide bond reduction and thus intracellular reduction potential. The term “TAMRA signal” refers to the fluorescence emission at approximately 590 nm following excitation at approximately 530 nm, which is indicative of the presence and internalization of the reductide construct, independent of disulfide reduction. The term “redox-modifying agent” refers to any chemical compound or biological agent that alters the intracellular redox state, either by increasing the concentration of reducing equivalents (e.g., N-acetylcysteine, dithiothreitol, reduced glutathione) or by depleting reducing equivalents or increasing oxidizing equivalents (e.g., hydrogen peroxide, diamide, 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene, buthionine sulfoximine). The term “plate reader assay” refers to a high-throughput fluorescence detection method performed using a microplate reader equipped with appropriate excitation and emission filters to quantify FAM and TAMRA signals in cell populations grown in multi-well plates. The term “flow cytometry” refers to the quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity in individual cells suspended in a fluid stream, using lasers and photomultiplier tubes to detect TAMRA and FAM signals, enabling the discrimination of subpopulations based on uptake and reduction kinetics. The term “live cell imaging” refers to the real-time visualization of fluorescence signals in living cells maintained under physiological conditions, using fluorescence microscopy systems with environmental control for temperature, CO₂, and humidity. The term “stable expression” refers to the long-term, heritable incorporation and expression of a transgene in a cell line, typically achieved through viral transduction and selection with antibiotics such as puromycin. The term “ratiometric measurement” refers to the calculation of the ratio of fluorescence intensities at two different excitation or emission wavelengths to normalize for variations in probe concentration, cell thickness, or instrument sensitivity. The term “intracellular reduction potential” refers to the thermodynamic tendency of the intracellular environment to donate electrons, as determined by the GSH/GSSG ratio and the Nernst equation, and is expressed in millivolts (mV). The term “pro-oxidant” refers to a substance that promotes the generation of reactive oxygen species or depletes cellular reducing equivalents, leading to a shift in redox state toward oxidation. The term “antioxidant” refers to a substance that scavenges reactive oxygen species or increases the concentration of reducing equivalents, leading to a shift in redox state toward reduction. The term “cell death” refers to the irreversible loss of cell viability, as determined by morphological changes, membrane integrity loss, or metabolic inactivity, and is distinguishable from transient stress responses or adaptive signaling events. The term “selective delivery” refers to the preferential internalization and activation of the reductide construct in cells exhibiting a specific redox phenotype, such as elevated reducing potential, thereby enabling targeted detection or therapeutic intervention. The term “physiological redox gradient” refers to the natural variation in intracellular redox state across different cell types, tissues, developmental stages, or disease states, which can be exploited by the reductide construct for diagnostic or therapeutic purposes.

### Invention

The invention comprises a novel molecular construct, designated reductide, designed to detect and report on the intracellular redox state through a mechanism that integrates both cellular uptake and intracellular reduction into a single, quantifiable fluorescent signal. The reductide construct is a synthetic, covalently linked molecule comprising two distinct peptide moieties connected by a disulfide bond. The first peptide moiety is derived from a cell-penetrating peptide sequence, specifically a modified version of the model amphipathic peptide (MAP), which has been engineered to include an N-terminal cysteine residue for disulfide conjugation. This peptide moiety is covalently attached to a first fluorophore, tetramethylrhodamine (TAMRA), which emits fluorescence at approximately 590 nm upon excitation at approximately 530 nm. The TAMRA fluorophore is chosen for its photostability, low sensitivity to pH, and minimal quenching by proximity to other fluorophores, allowing its signal to serve as a reliable indicator of the extent of cellular internalization of the reductide construct, regardless of the redox status of the cell. The second peptide moiety is derived from a non-cell-penetrating peptide sequence, CLKANL, which lacks the amphipathic structure and positive charge density necessary for membrane translocation. This peptide moiety is covalently attached to a second fluorophore, fluorescein amidite (FAM), which emits fluorescence at approximately 528 nm upon excitation at approximately 485 nm. In the intact reductide construct, the FAM fluorophore is held in close proximity to the TAMRA fluorophore, resulting in Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) that quenches FAM fluorescence. Upon reduction of the disulfide bond linking the two peptide moieties, the FAM-labeled peptide is released, leading to a significant increase in FAM fluorescence due to the loss of FRET quenching. This increase in FAM fluorescence is therefore a direct and quantitative indicator of intracellular reduction. 

Crucially, the invention is not merely a disulfide-linked fluorescent probe, but a system in which the rate and magnitude of FAM signal development are governed not only by the intracellular reducing environment, but also by the redox state of the cell prior to and during the process of cellular uptake. The inventors have demonstrated that cells with a more reduced intracellular environment exhibit faster and more robust internalization of the reductide construct, as evidenced by earlier and stronger TAMRA signal, and correspondingly earlier and more pronounced FAM signal activation. Conversely, cells with an oxidized intracellular environment exhibit delayed and diminished uptake, resulting in slower FAM signal development. This dual dependence on both uptake and reduction creates a unique, integrated readout that is far more sensitive and informative than any prior art system. For example, in a cell with low glutathione levels, the reductide construct may be internalized slowly, and even if reduction occurs, the overall FAM signal will be weak due to limited delivery. In contrast, in a cell with high glutathione levels, the construct is rapidly internalized and efficiently reduced, resulting in a strong and rapid FAM signal. This mechanism enables the reductide construct to distinguish between cells that are merely reducing-capable and those that are both reducing-capable and actively taking up the probe—a distinction that is impossible with prior probes that report only on reduction. 

The reductide construct is synthesized using standard solid-phase peptide synthesis techniques, with the MAP-derived peptide and CLKANL peptide synthesized separately, each incorporating an N-terminal cysteine residue. The cysteine residues are then oxidized to form a disulfide bond between the two peptides, followed by conjugation of TAMRA to the N-terminus of the MAP peptide and FAM to the N-terminus of the CLKANL peptide. The final product is purified by reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography and characterized by mass spectrometry to ensure homogeneity and correct molecular weight. The reductide construct is stable in aqueous buffer at physiological pH and remains intact in extracellular environments, including serum-containing media, until it encounters a reducing intracellular environment. Once internalized, the disulfide bond is reduced by intracellular thiols, primarily glutathione, leading to the separation of the two peptide-fluorophore moieties. The TAMRA-labeled peptide remains associated with the cell, providing a persistent signal that reflects the total amount of construct internalized, while the FAM-labeled peptide diffuses freely within the cytosol and nucleus, producing a transient but highly specific signal of reduction. The inventors have confirmed that the TAMRA signal is not significantly affected by reduction, as the fluorophore is not quenched by proximity to FAM in the intact construct, and its emission intensity remains constant regardless of the redox state of the cell. This allows for the independent quantification of uptake and reduction, enabling the calculation of a redox-dependent uptake efficiency index, defined as the ratio of FAM signal to TAMRA signal, which provides a normalized measure of intracellular reduction potential relative to the extent of delivery. 

The reductide construct has been tested across multiple mammalian cell types, including human fibroblasts (BJ and IMR90), rat neonatal cardiomyocytes (H9c2), and other cell lines, and has demonstrated consistent behavior across all systems. The construct is non-toxic at concentrations up to 10 μM, does not induce apoptosis or necrosis, and does not alter baseline cellular metabolism or redox state. It is stable in culture media for at least 24 hours and can be used in both short-term (minutes to hours) and long-term (up to 24 hours) assays. The construct can be applied to cells in suspension or adherent culture, and is compatible with standard fluorescence detection platforms, including microplate readers, flow cytometers, and fluorescence microscopes. The invention further encompasses methods of using the reductide construct to detect changes in intracellular redox state in response to pharmacological agents, environmental stressors, disease states, or genetic modifications. For example, pretreatment of cells with N-acetylcysteine, a precursor to glutathione synthesis, results in a dose-dependent increase in FAM signal, reflecting enhanced uptake and reduction. Conversely, pretreatment with hydrogen peroxide, a pro-oxidant, results in a dose-dependent decrease in FAM signal at high concentrations, but an increase at low concentrations, reflecting the activation of adaptive antioxidant responses. These responses are not observed with conventional redox probes, which report only on the final reduction event and not on the preceding uptake dynamics. The invention thus provides a novel, non-genetic, non-invasive, and highly sensitive method for interrogating the redox state of living cells, with applications in drug screening, disease diagnostics, personalized medicine, and targeted delivery.

### Materials and Methods

The reductide construct is synthesized using standard solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) employing Fmoc chemistry on Rink amide resin. The first peptide moiety, corresponding to the sequence Cys-Lys-Leu-Ala-Leu-Lys-Leu-Ala-Leu-Lys-Ala-Leu-Lys-Ala-Ala-Leu-Lys-Leu-Ala-amide, is assembled on the resin with the N-terminal cysteine residue protected with a trityl group. Upon completion of the peptide chain, the trityl group is selectively removed, and the free thiol is oxidized to form a disulfide bond with a second, pre-synthesized peptide moiety, Cys-Leu-Lys-Ala-Asn-Leu, which has been synthesized separately on a different resin and purified by HPLC. The two peptide fragments are then mixed in a buffered solution at pH 7.4 and allowed to react for 12 hours at room temperature to form the disulfide-linked intermediate. The resulting construct is purified by preparative reverse-phase HPLC using a C18 column and eluted with a gradient of acetonitrile in water containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid. Fractions containing the desired product are collected, lyophilized, and analyzed by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry to confirm the molecular weight of the disulfide-linked dimer. The purified disulfide-linked construct is then conjugated to 5(6)-carboxytetramethylrhodamine (TAMRA) at the N-terminus of the MAP-derived peptide and to fluorescein amidite (FAM) at the N-terminus of the CLKANL-derived peptide using standard NHS ester coupling chemistry in dimethylformamide (DMF) at pH 8.5. The reaction is quenched with excess glycine, and the final reductide product is purified again by HPLC to remove unreacted fluorophores and side products. The purity of the final product is confirmed by analytical HPLC and mass spectrometry, with a purity threshold of ≥95% required for all experiments. The reductide construct is stored in lyophilized form at -20°C and reconstituted in 3% acetic acid immediately prior to use, followed by dilution into physiological buffers such as tris-buffered saline (TBS) at pH 7.4.

For cellular experiments, human fibroblasts (BJ and IMR90) and rat neonatal cardiomyocytes (H9c2) are cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂. Cells are seeded into 96-well black-walled, clear-bottom plates at densities ranging from 4,000 to 5,000 cells per well for plate reader assays, or onto glass coverslips for live cell imaging. For experiments involving redox-modifying agents, cells are pretreated with compounds such as N-acetylcysteine (1–10 mM), hydrogen peroxide (50–800 μM), 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (10–50 μM), l-buthionine sulfoximine (10–100 μM), or diamide (10–100 μM) for 30 minutes to 24 hours, depending on the experimental objective. Following pretreatment, cells are washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual compounds, and reductide is added to the culture medium at a final concentration of 1 μM. Cells are then incubated for 15 minutes to 4 hours at 37°C in 5% CO₂, after which fluorescence is measured using a Synergy HT microplate reader (BioTek) with excitation/emission wavelengths of 485 nm/528 nm for FAM and 530 nm/590 nm for TAMRA. For live cell imaging, cells are seeded onto four-chamber glass coverslips and imaged using an Olympus FV1000 confocal microscope equipped with a stage-top incubator maintaining 37°C and 5% CO₂. Images are acquired every 15 minutes for up to 4.5 hours using 488 nm and 543 nm laser lines for FAM and TAMRA excitation, respectively, with emission collected through 510–540 nm and 570–620 nm bandpass filters. For flow cytometry, cells are seeded in 10 cm dishes, pretreated with redox-modifying agents, washed, and incubated with reductide for 3, 15, or 30 minutes. Cells are then trypsinized, resuspended in PBS without phenol red, and analyzed using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer. TAMRA and FAM fluorescence are detected using 561 nm and 488 nm laser lines, with emission collected through 585/42 nm and 530/30 nm filters, respectively. Data are analyzed using FlowJo software, and fluorescence intensities are normalized to cell count and side scatter to exclude debris. For comparison with other redox probes, cells are treated in parallel with monochlorobimane (100 μM) or Alamar Blue™ (1:10 dilution) under identical conditions, and fluorescence is measured using excitation/emission wavelengths of 390 nm/478 nm and 540 nm/590 nm, respectively. For experiments involving stable expression of redox-sensitive green fluorescent protein (roGFP), H9c2 cells are transduced with retroviral particles encoding Grx1-roGFP under the CMV promoter, selected with puromycin (4 μg/mL) for 15 population doublings, and confirmed for expression by fluorescence microscopy and Western blot. Cells are then pretreated with redox-modifying agents, washed, and imaged using a BD Pathway High Content Bioimager 855 with 405 nm and 488 nm excitation, and ratiometric images are generated by dividing the 405 nm emission by the 488 nm emission using ImageJ software. Immediately after imaging, reductide is added at 1 μM, and cells are incubated for 30 minutes before fluorescence is measured in a plate reader. All experiments are performed in triplicate, and each experiment is repeated at least twice to ensure reproducibility.

### Statistical Analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of at least three independent biological replicates, with each replicate representing a separate cell culture preparation and assay run. For comparisons between two groups, statistical significance is assessed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test assuming unequal variances. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) is performed, followed by post-hoc Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test for pairwise comparisons. When data violate assumptions of normality or homogeneity of variance, non-parametric tests such as the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test are employed. Correlation between reductide FAM signal and roGFP emission ratios is assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient, with significance defined as p < 0.05. For dose-response experiments, data are fitted to a four-parameter logistic curve using GraphPad Prism software to determine EC₅₀ and IC₅₀ values. The redox-dependent uptake efficiency index is calculated as the ratio of FAM fluorescence intensity to TAMRA fluorescence intensity for each condition, and differences in this index between experimental groups are analyzed using ANOVA with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Flow cytometry data are analyzed using median fluorescence intensity (MFI) values, and shifts in population distributions are assessed using Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests. All statistical analyses are performed using R version 4.3.1 or GraphPad Prism version 9.5.0, and p-values less than 0.05 are considered statistically significant, while p-values less than 0.01 are considered highly significant. Graphical representations of data include bar graphs, scatter plots, line graphs, and heat maps, with error bars representing SD. For plate reader assays, background fluorescence from cell-free wells containing reductide in media without cells is subtracted from all sample readings. For flow cytometry, unstained cells and cells treated with vehicle alone are used as negative controls to set gating thresholds. For live cell imaging, background fluorescence from regions devoid of cells is subtracted from each frame, and fluorescence intensity is normalized to the initial time point (t=0) to account for photobleaching. All statistical tests are performed blinded to experimental condition to avoid bias, and data are archived in a secure digital repository with metadata including date, cell line, reagent batch, instrument settings, and operator ID.

## Results

### Effects of GSH/GSSG on Reductide Redox-Dependent Fluorescence

When reductide is introduced into a buffered solution containing reduced glutathione (GSH), a rapid and concentration-dependent increase in FAM fluorescence is observed, indicating the reduction of the disulfide bond and the consequent separation of the FAM-labeled peptide from the TAMRA-labeled peptide. This increase in fluorescence occurs within minutes and reaches a plateau within 20 minutes, demonstrating that the disulfide bond is efficiently cleaved under reducing conditions. The magnitude of the FAM signal is directly proportional to the concentration of GSH in the buffer, with higher concentrations of GSH producing greater fluorescence intensities. In contrast, when oxidized glutathione (GSSG) is added to the buffer in equimolar or greater proportions relative to GSH, the rate of FAM signal development is significantly attenuated, and the maximum fluorescence intensity achieved is reduced. This effect is dose-dependent, with increasing concentrations of GSSG leading to progressively slower kinetics and lower peak fluorescence. The total glutathione pool, defined as the sum of GSH and twice the concentration of GSSG, is held constant at 5 mM in all experiments to ensure that changes in fluorescence are attributable to alterations in the GSH/GSSG redox potential rather than total thiol concentration. The data demonstrate that the reduction of reductide is governed by the thermodynamic driving force provided by the GSH/GSSG ratio, as predicted by the Nernst equation, with a reduction potential of approximately -200 mV in the presence of 5 mM GSH and approximately -164 mV in the presence of 2 mM GSH and 1.5 mM GSSG. In the absence of GSH, even in the presence of GSSG, no increase in FAM fluorescence is observed above baseline levels, confirming that reduction is absolutely dependent on the availability of reducing equivalents. TAMRA fluorescence, in contrast, remains constant over time and is unaffected by the GSH/GSSG ratio, indicating that the TAMRA-labeled peptide moiety does not undergo any conformational change or chemical modification during the reduction process. This stability confirms that TAMRA serves as a reliable internal control for the amount of reductide present in the system, independent of redox state. The lack of reciprocity between TAMRA and FAM signals—wherein TAMRA intensity does not decrease as FAM increases—further supports the conclusion that the FAM signal arises from the physical separation of the two fluorophores following disulfide reduction, rather than from a change in the local chemical environment of the fluorophores. These findings establish that reductide functions as a sensitive and quantitative reporter of redox potential in a cell-free system, and provide the foundational evidence that the same principles apply in living cells.

### Flow Cytometry

Flow cytometry analysis of IMR90 fibroblasts exposed to reductide reveals a time-dependent increase in both TAMRA and FAM fluorescence, with TAMRA signal rising more rapidly and to a higher magnitude than FAM signal. This pattern is consistent with the hypothesis that cellular uptake precedes intracellular reduction, and that the TAMRA-labeled peptide remains associated with the cell following reduction, while the FAM-labeled peptide diffuses away from the site of internalization. Cells pretreated with the reducing agent N-acetylcysteine (4 mM) exhibit significantly higher TAMRA fluorescence at all time points compared to untreated controls, indicating enhanced uptake of the reductide construct. FAM fluorescence is also elevated in NAC-pretreated cells, but to a lesser extent, suggesting that while uptake is increased, the rate of reduction is not proportionally enhanced. In contrast, cells pretreated with hydrogen peroxide (600 μM) exhibit markedly lower TAMRA and FAM signals, consistent with reduced internalization and impaired reduction. The temporal resolution of TAMRA signal is superior to that of FAM, with TAMRA reaching a plateau within 30 minutes, whereas FAM continues to increase gradually over the same period. This difference in kinetics reflects the distinct biological processes underlying each signal: TAMRA reflects the cumulative internalization of the construct, while FAM reflects the cumulative reduction of the disulfide bond, which is a slower, enzymatically mediated process. Importantly, no increase in cell death, as assessed by side scatter or DAPI staining, is observed in any condition, confirming that reductide is non-toxic at the concentrations and time points used. The distribution of fluorescence intensities across the cell population is heterogeneous, with a subset of cells exhibiting high TAMRA and FAM signals, while others show minimal fluorescence. This heterogeneity is consistent with known variations in redox state across individual cells within a population, and suggests that reductide can be used to identify and isolate subpopulations of cells based on their redox phenotype. The ability to detect this heterogeneity at the single-cell level provides a powerful advantage over plate reader assays, which report only on population averages. Furthermore, the correlation between TAMRA and FAM signals across individual cells is variable, with some cells exhibiting high TAMRA but low FAM (suggesting uptake without reduction), and others exhibiting low TAMRA but high FAM (suggesting inefficient uptake but efficient reduction), although the latter is rare. This variability underscores the importance of measuring both signals simultaneously to accurately interpret the redox status of a cell. The data from flow cytometry thus validate the dual-component nature of reductide and confirm its utility as a high-resolution, single-cell redox sensor.

### Reductide Response to a Small Library of Redox Modifying Compounds

A high-throughput screening assay was performed using the Screen-Well™ REDOX library, comprising 84 chemically diverse compounds known to modulate cellular redox state. BJ fibroblasts were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with each compound at a fixed concentration of 50 μM for 24 hours, followed by washing and incubation with reductide at 1 μM for four hours. FAM fluorescence was then measured to assess the redox-dependent response of each compound. Of the 84 compounds tested, 65 (77.4%) elicited a statistically significant increase in FAM signal compared to vehicle-treated controls, indicating that these compounds induced a more reduced intracellular environment. Nine compounds (10.7%) caused a statistically significant decrease in FAM signal, suggesting an oxidizing effect. The remaining 10 compounds (11.9%) produced no significant change. Notably, many of the compounds classified as antioxidants in the literature, such as GERI-BP002A and carvedilol, initially appeared to act as pro-oxidants at 50 μM, reducing FAM signal. However, when retested at lower concentrations, these same compounds produced the expected increase in FAM signal, indicating that their apparent pro-oxidant effect at high concentration was due to cytotoxicity or off-target effects rather than true redox modulation. This finding highlights the importance of dose-response characterization in redox screening and demonstrates that reductide can distinguish between genuine redox modulation and non-specific toxicity. Furthermore, several compounds that caused visible signs of cell death—including cell rounding, detachment, and shrinkage—still produced a significant increase in FAM signal, indicating that the reductide response is not a consequence of cell death or membrane permeabilization. For example, promethazine HCl and retinyl palmitate, both of which induced cell death at 50 μM, resulted in 76% and 123% increases in FAM signal, respectively, compared to controls. These results confirm that reductide is not a viability indicator and that its signal reflects specific redox changes rather than general cellular stress. In contrast, compounds that did not induce cell death but still increased FAM signal, such as selenomethionine, demonstrated a dose-dependent increase in signal across a wide concentration range, confirming their role as true redox modulators. The screening results demonstrate that reductide is a robust, high-throughput tool for identifying novel redox-modifying agents and for distinguishing between compounds that act as antioxidants, pro-oxidants, or cytotoxins. The ability to screen hundreds of compounds in parallel, with a single readout that integrates both uptake and reduction, represents a significant advancement over conventional redox assays, which require multiple measurements and are often confounded by cytotoxicity.

## Discussion

### Reductide Uptake as Well as Reduction Depends on Cellular Redox State

The invention demonstrates that the intracellular detection of reductide is not a simple two-step process of uptake followed by reduction, but rather a tightly coupled, redox-dependent phenomenon in which the efficiency of cellular internalization is itself modulated by the intracellular redox state. This finding fundamentally alters the understanding of how cell-penetrating peptides interact with living cells. Prior models assumed that uptake was governed primarily by electrostatic interactions, membrane fluidity, or endocytic machinery, with reduction being a separate, downstream event. The data presented here show that the rate and extent of uptake are directly influenced by the concentration of reduced glutathione and the GSH/GSSG redox potential. This is evidenced by the fact that TAMRA signal, which reflects total internalization, appears earlier and more intensely in cells pretreated with reducing agents such as N-acetylcysteine, and later and more weakly in cells pretreated with oxidizing agents such as hydrogen peroxide or diamide. This effect is not merely a consequence of altered membrane permeability or fluidity, as the same cells, when first internalized with reductide and then oxidized, show significantly attenuated FAM signal development compared to cells that are oxidized first and then exposed to reductide. This asymmetry indicates that the redox state must be favorable at the time of contact with the plasma membrane for efficient internalization to occur. The mechanism underlying this phenomenon may involve redox-sensitive regulation of ion channels, membrane potential, or lipid composition. For instance, reduction of disulfide bonds in potassium channels has been shown to hyperpolarize the membrane, increasing the electrochemical gradient that drives the translocation of positively charged peptides such as MAP. Similarly, oxidative stress has been shown to alter membrane lipid composition, increasing rigidity and reducing the ability of amphipathic peptides to insert into the bilayer. The reductide construct thus functions as a molecular sensor that not only reports on the intracellular redox state but also actively responds to it by modulating its own delivery. This feedback loop creates a self-regulating system in which the probe is preferentially internalized and activated in cells that are already in a reduced state, such as healthy or antioxidant-primed cells, while being excluded from oxidized or stressed cells. This property has profound implications for targeted delivery, as it suggests that reductide or its derivatives could be used to selectively deliver therapeutic cargo to cells with a favorable redox environment, such as regenerating tissues, neurons, or cardiomyocytes, while sparing cells in a state of oxidative stress, such as those in tumors or inflamed tissues. The invention therefore represents a paradigm shift in the design of cell-penetrating systems, moving from passive carriers to active, context-sensitive vectors whose behavior is dynamically regulated by the physiological state of the target cell.

### Pro-Oxidants Activate an Antioxidative Response

The observation that low doses of hydrogen peroxide (200–400 μM) lead to an increase in reductide FAM signal, while higher doses (≥600 μM) lead to a decrease, reveals a sophisticated, biphasic response of cells to oxidative stress that is not captured by conventional redox probes. This phenomenon is not an artifact of probe instability or cytotoxicity, as confirmed by the lack of correlation with cell viability assays such as Alamar Blue™. Instead, it reflects the activation of endogenous antioxidant defense mechanisms in response to mild oxidative challenge. At low concentrations, hydrogen peroxide acts as a signaling molecule, triggering the nuclear translocation of transcription factors such as Nrf2, which upregulates the expression of genes encoding glutathione synthesis enzymes, thioredoxin, catalase, and superoxide dismutase. This adaptive response leads to an increase in intracellular reducing capacity, which is detected by reductide as an increase in FAM signal. At higher concentrations, however, the oxidative insult overwhelms these defenses, leading to depletion of glutathione, oxidation of critical thiols, and disruption of redox homeostasis, resulting in decreased FAM signal. The reductide construct thus provides a unique, integrated readout of cellular adaptation to oxidative stress, distinguishing between acute damage and adaptive resilience. This capability is unprecedented in the field of redox sensing and has significant implications for disease modeling, drug discovery, and personalized medicine. For example, in cancer, tumor cells often exhibit elevated basal levels of oxidative stress and rely on upregulated antioxidant systems for survival. The reductide construct would be expected to show high FAM signal in these cells due to their enhanced reducing capacity, even in the presence of high ROS levels. In contrast, normal cells exposed to chronic oxidative stress may show reduced FAM signal due to exhaustion of antioxidant defenses. This differential response could be exploited to identify tumor cells for targeted therapy or to monitor the efficacy of antioxidant therapies in degenerative diseases. The invention thus provides not only a sensor of redox state, but a dynamic biomarker of cellular adaptation, enabling the discrimination between pathological and compensatory redox changes. This level of biological insight was previously unattainable with existing probes, which report only on the net redox balance and not on the underlying adaptive mechanisms. The reductide construct therefore represents a transformative tool for understanding the complex, context-dependent nature of redox biology in health and disease.

BJ fibroblasts were pretreated with N-acetylcysteine (A), 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (B), or hydrogen peroxide (C, D) for 30 minutes or 24 hours, respectively, followed by washing and incubation with reductide (1 μM) for one hour (A–C) or four hours (D). Median fluorescence intensity (MFI) of TAMRA (A) and FAM (B) was measured by flow cytometry. TAMRA signal increased rapidly and peaked at 30 minutes, while FAM signal increased more slowly and showed less temporal resolution, consistent with the sequential nature of uptake and reduction.

Figure 9. Dose-response of reductide signal to redox-modifying compounds.
