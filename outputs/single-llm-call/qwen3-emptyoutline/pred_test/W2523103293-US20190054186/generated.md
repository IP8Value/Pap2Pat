The invention is not merely an extension of prior art in magnetic hyperthermia or magnetically guided drug delivery, but constitutes a fundamentally new paradigm in cancer therapeutics based on mechano-biological disruption rather than thermal ablation or pharmacological intervention.

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention resides in the field of biomedical engineering and oncological therapeutics, specifically in the development of non-invasive, non-thermal, and biologically selective cancer treatment modalities that exploit the mechanical properties of malignant cells. More particularly, the invention relates to a system and method for the remote, spatially controlled, and cell-type-specific disruption of the cytoskeletal integrity of cancerous cells through the application of super-low frequency alternating magnetic fields to intracellularly localized superparamagnetic nanoparticles coated with amphiphilic block copolymers. The invention further pertains to the design of polymer-coated magnetic nanoparticle complexes that are engineered to accumulate preferentially within lysosomes of malignant cells and to transmit mechanical torque and shear stress to the surrounding cytoskeletal network upon magnetic actuation. The field encompasses the intersection of nanotechnology, biophysics, cell mechanics, and oncology, wherein the physical interaction between magnetic nanoparticles and cellular structures is harnessed to induce selective cytotoxicity without systemic toxicity, thermal damage, or genetic modification. The invention is distinct from prior art in magnetic hyperthermia, which relies on resistive heating from high-frequency alternating fields, and from magnetic drug targeting, which depends on external field gradients for spatial localization. Instead, the invention operates at frequencies below 100 Hz and field strengths below 150 kA/m, where thermal effects are negligible, and the primary biological effect is mechanical perturbation of the cytoskeleton. The invention is applicable to solid tumors of epithelial origin, particularly those resistant to conventional therapies such as triple-negative breast cancer, and is adaptable to other malignancies characterized by cytoskeletal softening, including pancreatic, ovarian, and metastatic carcinomas. The technology may also be extended to diagnostic applications, including mechano-imaging of tumor stiffness and real-time monitoring of cytoskeletal integrity during treatment.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The treatment of cancer remains one of the most formidable challenges in modern medicine, with conventional therapies such as chemotherapy, radiation, and surgical resection often accompanied by significant systemic toxicity, incomplete tumor eradication, and the development of drug resistance. While targeted therapies and immunotherapies have improved outcomes for certain subtypes of cancer, many aggressive malignancies, particularly those lacking defined molecular targets such as triple-negative breast cancer, continue to exhibit poor prognoses and limited therapeutic options. In recent years, magnetic nanoparticles have emerged as promising agents for cancer therapy due to their ability to be remotely actuated, their biocompatibility, and their potential for multifunctional design. A dominant paradigm in this field has been magnetic hyperthermia, wherein superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles are exposed to high-frequency alternating magnetic fields (typically above 100 kHz) to generate localized heat through Néel and Brownian relaxation mechanisms, resulting in thermal ablation of tumor tissue. However, this approach is fundamentally limited by the requirement for high field frequencies and amplitudes, which can lead to non-specific heating of healthy tissues, inefficient energy transfer in deep-seated tumors, and the need for precise temperature control to avoid collateral damage. Moreover, hyperthermia is ineffective against hypoxic and necrotic regions of tumors, which are often the most resistant to treatment. Alternative approaches have explored the use of magnetic nanoparticles for drug delivery, gene silencing, or as contrast agents for imaging, but these strategies still rely on passive accumulation, chemical release mechanisms, or external guidance, none of which provide intrinsic selectivity for malignant cells over healthy ones. 

Prior art has also investigated the mechanical effects of magnetic fields on cellular structures. Some studies have demonstrated that magnetic nanoparticles can be manipulated using rotating or gradient magnetic fields to exert torque on cell membranes or organelles, leading to membrane disruption or altered signaling. However, these methods typically require high-frequency fields, large nanoparticle sizes (>50 nm), or direct surface conjugation to membrane proteins, which limits their applicability to intracellular targets and increases the risk of off-target effects. Furthermore, these approaches have not demonstrated the ability to distinguish between malignant and non-malignant cells based on intrinsic biomechanical properties. The notion that cancer cells possess a distinct mechanical phenotype—namely, increased softness due to cytoskeletal reorganization during epithelial-mesenchymal transition—has been well documented in the literature, and this property has been exploited in diagnostic techniques such as atomic force microscopy and microfluidic deformation assays. However, no prior art has successfully leveraged this mechanical vulnerability as a therapeutic target. The present invention bridges this critical gap by demonstrating that when superparamagnetic nanoparticles are internalized by cancer cells and subjected to a super-low frequency alternating magnetic field, they generate localized mechanical forces sufficient to disrupt the cytoskeleton of malignant cells, which are mechanically compliant, while leaving the stiffer, more resilient cytoskeleton of non-malignant cells intact. This selectivity is not dependent on differential uptake, metabolic activity, or surface receptor expression, but rather on the fundamental difference in mechanical stiffness between transformed and normal cells. The invention further introduces a novel class of pentablock copolymer coatings that enhance nanoparticle stability, promote lysosomal accumulation, and enable efficient force transmission to the cytoskeleton through physical entanglement and membrane anchoring mechanisms. The use of low-frequency fields (e.g., 30–100 Hz) and moderate field strengths (e.g., 50–100 kA/m) ensures that thermal effects are negligible, and the biological response is purely mechano-transductive. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in cancer therapy, moving away from chemical or thermal ablation toward a physics-based, mechanically selective approach that exploits the intrinsic biomechanical signature of malignancy.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel system and method for the selective, non-thermal, and non-invasive destruction of cancerous cells through the remote actuation of intracellularly localized superparamagnetic nanoparticles using super-low frequency alternating magnetic fields. The invention is based on the discovery that cancerous cells, due to their inherently softer cytoskeletal architecture, are susceptible to mechanical disruption induced by the torque and shear forces generated by magnetically actuated nanoparticles, while non-malignant cells, which possess a stiffer and more organized cytoskeleton, remain unaffected despite equivalent nanoparticle internalization. The system comprises a plurality of superparamagnetic nanoparticles coated with a pentablock copolymer comprising two polyanionic blocks and a central hydrophobic block, wherein the polyanionic blocks confer colloidal stability and the central hydrophobic block promotes lysosomal accumulation and physical anchoring to the lysosomal membrane. Upon internalization into cells, the nanoparticles aggregate within lysosomes, forming dense physical nanogels that act as mechanical transducers. When exposed to a super-low frequency alternating magnetic field, the aggregated nanoparticles rotate and oscillate, generating localized mechanical stresses that are transmitted to the surrounding cytoskeletal network, particularly actin filaments. In cancerous cells, these forces exceed the mechanical resilience of the cytoskeleton, leading to filament rupture, loss of structural integrity, and subsequent cell death via mechanical failure. In non-malignant cells, the same forces are dissipated without causing structural damage due to the higher stiffness and greater tensile strength of the cytoskeleton. The invention further provides that the application of the magnetic field in a pulsed regime—alternating between periods of activation and rest—enhances the mechanical damage by promoting repeated assembly and disassembly of nanoparticle aggregates, thereby inducing fatigue-like failure in the cytoskeletal network. The invention demonstrates that this mechanism is independent of lysosomal membrane permeabilization, thermal effects, or chemical toxicity, and that the selectivity for cancerous cells is absolute under defined conditions. The invention further provides that the mechanical sensitivity of non-malignant cells can be experimentally mimicked by pharmacological disruption of actin polymerization, thereby sensitizing them to the same treatment, confirming that the differential response is purely biomechanical in origin. The invention thus provides a platform technology for cancer therapy that is broadly applicable to solid tumors characterized by cytoskeletal softening, is non-toxic to healthy tissues, requires no genetic modification or pharmacological payload, and can be remotely controlled with high spatial precision using external magnetic field generators. The invention further enables real-time monitoring of treatment efficacy through imaging of cytoskeletal integrity and may be combined with diagnostic modalities to guide therapeutic intervention. The invention represents a fundamental departure from conventional cancer therapies and provides a new class of treatment that exploits the physical properties of cancer cells rather than their biochemical signatures.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive system and method for the selective, non-thermal, and non-invasive destruction of cancerous cells through the remote actuation of intracellularly localized superparamagnetic nanoparticles using super-low frequency alternating magnetic fields. The invention is predicated on a novel mechanistic understanding that the differential mechanical compliance of the cytoskeleton between malignant and non-malignant cells serves as a biophysical biomarker that can be exploited for therapeutic selectivity. The system comprises three essential components: (1) superparamagnetic nanoparticles coated with a pentablock copolymer designed to promote lysosomal accumulation and mechanical coupling to the cytoskeleton; (2) a super-low frequency alternating magnetic field generator capable of producing fields in the range of 30 to 3000 Hz with amplitudes between 10 and 150 kA/m; and (3) a treatment protocol that leverages the pulsed application of the magnetic field to induce fatigue-like mechanical failure in the cytoskeleton of cancerous cells. The superparamagnetic nanoparticles used in the invention are composed of magnetite (Fe₃O₄) or maghemite (γ-Fe₂O₃) with a core diameter ranging from 5 to 15 nanometers, selected to ensure superparamagnetic behavior at room temperature and to facilitate efficient aggregation within lysosomes. These nanoparticles are coated with a pentablock copolymer of the general structure A-B-C-B-A, wherein A represents a polyanionic block such as polyacrylic acid or polymethacrylic acid, and C represents a central hydrophobic block composed of poly(propylene oxide) derived from Pluronic® P85. The polyanionic blocks provide colloidal stability in physiological environments, prevent nonspecific protein adsorption, and enable functionalization through carboxylate groups, while the central hydrophobic block promotes interaction with the lipid bilayer of lysosomal membranes and facilitates the formation of a dense physical nanogel upon nanoparticle aggregation. The copolymer coating is synthesized via sequential living polymerization techniques, ensuring precise control over block lengths and molecular weight distribution, with the total molecular weight of the pentablock copolymer ranging from 15,000 to 30,000 Da. The resulting polymer-coated nanoparticles exhibit a hydrodynamic diameter of 30 to 70 nanometers, a zeta potential between −35 and −50 mV, and a polymer-to-iron mass ratio of approximately 60:40, as confirmed by thermogravimetric analysis and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry. The nanoparticles are stable in aqueous suspension for over 48 hours in physiological buffers, cell culture media, and serum-containing environments, and do not aggregate or precipitate under conditions of physiological ionic strength. Following internalization into cells, the nanoparticles are trafficked to lysosomes via endocytosis, where they accumulate in high numbers—on the order of 10⁵ to 10⁶ particles per cell—forming dense, physically entangled networks due to the hydrophobic interactions between the poly(propylene oxide) segments of adjacent copolymer chains. This aggregation is critical to the invention, as it amplifies the net magnetic moment of the nanoparticle ensemble, enabling the generation of sufficient mechanical torque to disrupt the cytoskeleton upon magnetic actuation. The magnetic field generator used in the invention is a custom-designed device capable of producing a sinusoidal alternating magnetic field with precise control over frequency, amplitude, and temporal profile. The field is generated by a water-cooled inductor with a ferromagnetic core, which is positioned adjacent to a temperature-controlled cell culture chamber maintained at 37°C to ensure physiological conditions during treatment. The field frequency is set between 30 and 100 Hz, with optimal efficacy observed at 50 Hz, and the field amplitude is maintained between 50 and 100 kA/m, with 50 kA/m being sufficient to induce cytotoxic effects in cancerous cells while remaining below thresholds for thermal heating. The magnetic field is applied in either a continuous mode (30 minutes of uninterrupted exposure) or, more preferably, in a pulsed mode comprising alternating 10-minute intervals of field application and 5-minute intervals of field cessation, for a total of 30 minutes of field exposure. The pulsed regime is found to be significantly more effective than the continuous regime, as it allows for repeated assembly and disassembly of the nanoparticle aggregates within lysosomes, thereby inducing cyclic mechanical stress on the cytoskeleton and promoting fatigue-like failure. The invention further provides that the cytotoxic effect is entirely dependent on the presence of the polymer-coated nanoparticles within the cell; exposure of cells to the magnetic field in the absence of nanoparticles results in no measurable loss of viability. Similarly, cells treated with nanoparticles but not exposed to the magnetic field exhibit no cytotoxicity, confirming that the effect is not due to nanoparticle toxicity or chemical leaching. The invention further demonstrates that the cytotoxic effect is selective for cancerous cells, as non-malignant cells such as MCF10A human mammary epithelial cells, which exhibit a stiffer cytoskeleton and higher Young’s modulus, remain viable despite equivalent nanoparticle uptake and identical field exposure conditions. This selectivity is not attributable to differences in endocytic uptake, lysosomal pH, or metabolic activity, as quantitative measurements of intracellular iron content confirm that nanoparticle accumulation is comparable between malignant and non-malignant cells. The mechanism of cell death is not apoptotic, necrotic, or autophagic in the classical sense, but rather results from mechanical failure of the actin cytoskeleton, as evidenced by confocal microscopy showing fragmentation and disorganization of F-actin filaments in cancerous cells following magnetic exposure, while the actin architecture in non-malignant cells remains intact. The invention further provides that the mechanical disruption of the cytoskeleton is not accompanied by lysosomal membrane permeabilization, as confirmed by multiple assays including Lysotracker Green colocalization, acridine orange staining, and transmission electron microscopy, all of which demonstrate retention of lysosomal integrity and punctate fluorescence patterns before and after magnetic exposure. The invention further provides that the mechanical forces generated by the actuated nanoparticles are sufficient to exceed the dynamic tensile strength of actin filaments under cyclic loading, as the repeated oscillation of nanoparticle aggregates at 50 Hz results in approximately 30,000 loading cycles during a 10-minute exposure period, which is sufficient to induce fatigue failure in cytoskeletal structures whose static rupture strength exceeds 600 pN but whose fatigue strength is reduced by a factor of three to four under dynamic conditions. The invention further provides that the central hydrophobic block of the pentablock copolymer plays a critical role in anchoring the nanoparticle aggregates to the lysosomal membrane, thereby ensuring efficient force transmission to the cytoskeleton rather than dissipation into the lysosomal lumen. This anchoring is mediated by the insertion of poly(propylene oxide) chains into the lipid bilayer of the lysosomal membrane, a phenomenon previously documented for Pluronic surfactants, which results in the formation of a mechanically coupled complex wherein the entire nanoparticle aggregate moves as a single unit with the lysosome. The invention further provides that the cytoskeletal disruption is localized to the perinuclear region, where actin filaments are most densely organized and where lysosomes are preferentially trafficked, leading to loss of cell polarity, impaired motility, and eventual collapse of cellular architecture. The invention further provides that the mechanical sensitivity of non-malignant cells can be experimentally modulated to mimic that of cancerous cells through pharmacological disruption of actin polymerization using sub-lethal doses of cytochalasin D, a compound that depolymerizes actin filaments and reduces cellular stiffness to levels comparable to malignant cells. When non-malignant cells are pretreated with cytochalasin D and then exposed to the nanoparticle-magnetic field system, they exhibit cytoskeletal disruption and cell death indistinguishable from that observed in cancerous cells, thereby confirming that the differential response is purely biomechanical and not due to any inherent biochemical or genetic difference between the cell types. The invention further provides that the therapeutic effect is scalable and can be applied to multicellular tumor spheroids, organoids, and in vivo tumor models, with magnetic field penetration sufficient to reach deep-seated tumors without the need for invasive delivery. The invention further provides that the system may be combined with imaging modalities such as magnetic resonance imaging or fluorescence microscopy to monitor nanoparticle localization and cytoskeletal integrity in real time, enabling personalized treatment protocols based on real-time biomechanical feedback. The invention further provides that the polymer coating may be further functionalized with targeting ligands, fluorescent dyes, or therapeutic agents without compromising the mechano-actuation mechanism, thereby enabling multimodal therapeutic platforms. The invention further provides that the magnetic field parameters may be optimized for different tumor types based on their known mechanical properties, with softer tumors requiring lower field amplitudes and shorter exposure times, while stiffer tumors may require higher amplitudes or longer exposure durations. The invention thus provides a fundamentally new class of cancer therapy that is non-toxic, non-invasive, selectively cytotoxic to malignant cells, and independent of molecular markers, making it broadly applicable across cancer subtypes and resistant to conventional mechanisms of drug resistance.

### Experimental Methods

The experimental methods employed to validate the invention were designed to rigorously characterize the physicochemical properties of the polymer-coated superparamagnetic nanoparticles, quantify their intracellular uptake and distribution, assess their cytotoxic effects under magnetic field exposure, and elucidate the underlying biomechanical mechanism of selective cell death. All cell lines used in the experiments were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection and maintained under standard culture conditions in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, with the exception of MCF10A cells, which were cultured in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 10 μg/mL human insulin, and 10 ng/mL human epidermal growth factor. Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ and were passaged at 80–90% confluence using trypsin-EDTA. For nanoparticle synthesis, superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles were prepared by co-precipitation of ferrous and ferric chloride salts in an alkaline aqueous solution under nitrogen atmosphere, followed by washing and resuspension in deionized water. The nanoparticles were then coated with a pentablock copolymer composed of polyacrylic acid-poly(propylene oxide)-polyacrylic acid (PAA-P85-PAA) via ligand exchange, wherein the carboxylate groups of the polyacrylic acid blocks coordinate with the iron oxide surface, while the poly(propylene oxide) block extends outward into the aqueous medium. The resulting polymer-coated nanoparticles were purified by dialysis against deionized water and characterized for size, charge, and composition using dynamic light scattering, zeta potential measurements, thermogravimetric analysis, and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry. The hydrodynamic diameter was measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument at 25°C, with each sample analyzed in triplicate to determine mean values and standard deviations. The polymer content was determined by thermogravimetric analysis using a Q50 instrument, wherein samples were heated from 25°C to 1000°C at a rate of 10°C/min under nitrogen atmosphere, and the weight loss corresponding to polymer decomposition was quantified. Iron content was determined by digesting nanoparticle suspensions in trace-grade nitric acid at 70°C for 12 hours, diluting to a known volume, and analyzing via ICP-MS using a NexION 300D instrument. For fluorescent labeling, the nanoparticles were conjugated with Alexa Fluor®647 hydrazine using EDC/NHS chemistry, wherein the carboxylate groups of the polyacrylic acid blocks were activated with EDC and S-NHS, followed by reaction with the hydrazine-functionalized dye. The labeled nanoparticles were purified by size exclusion chromatography on Sephadex G-50 columns and concentrated using 100 kDa cutoff centrifugal filters. For intracellular uptake studies, cells were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well and allowed to adhere for 72 hours. Cells were then treated with nanoparticle suspensions at concentrations ranging from 0.005 to 0.5 mg/mL for either 1 hour or 24 hours, followed by three washes with acidic saline (pH 3) to remove non-internalized nanoparticles. Cells were harvested using trypsin-EDTA, pelleted, resuspended in deionized water, and sonicated to disrupt cellular membranes. The resulting suspensions were digested in nitric acid and analyzed by ICP-MS to quantify intracellular iron content. For intracellular distribution studies, cells were seeded in eight-well Lab-Tek chamber slides and treated with fluorescently labeled nanoparticles for 24 hours. Cells were then stained with Lysotracker Green to label acidic organelles and Hoechst 33342 to label nuclei, and imaged using a Zeiss CLSM 710 confocal laser scanning microscope equipped with a 63×/1.4 oil Plan Apo objective. Colocalization between nanoparticle fluorescence and Lysotracker signal was quantified using the Colocalization Threshold plugin in ImageJ/Fiji. For transmission electron microscopy, cells were grown on Thermanox coverslips, treated with nanoparticles, fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde and 2.5% glutaraldehyde, post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, and embedded in Polybed 812 epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were cut using a diamond knife, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and imaged using a LEO EM910 transmission electron microscope at 80 kV. For cytotoxicity assays, cells were seeded in 96-well high-binding plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well and allowed to adhere for 72 hours. Cells were treated with nanoparticles at concentrations ranging from 0.05 to 0.5 mg/mL for 24 hours, washed with acidic saline, and exposed to the alternating magnetic field using either a continuous (30 minutes) or pulsed (10 minutes on, 5 minutes off, totaling 30 minutes on) exposure regime. Following exposure, cells were incubated for an additional 24 hours, after which cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay. Briefly, 25 μL of MTT reagent (5 mg/mL) was added to each well and incubated for 4 hours at 37°C. The formazan crystals were solubilized with dimethyl sulfoxide, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a SpectraMax M5 plate reader. Cell viability was calculated as the ratio of absorbance in treated wells relative to untreated controls. For lysosomal membrane permeabilization assays, cells were treated with nanoparticles and exposed to the magnetic field as described above, followed by staining with acridine orange (10 μg/mL) for 15 minutes. Cells were washed and imaged using the confocal microscope, with red fluorescence indicating intact lysosomes and green fluorescence indicating cytosolic leakage. Positive controls were treated with 150 μM hydrogen peroxide for 3 hours. For cytoskeletal modulation experiments, cells were pretreated with 100 nM cytochalasin D for 1 hour prior to nanoparticle treatment and magnetic field exposure. Cells were then fixed, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, and stained with ActinGreen 488 and Hoechst 33342 to visualize actin filaments and nuclei, respectively. Images were acquired using the same confocal microscope and analyzed for actin filament integrity. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA with Ryan-Einot-Gabriel-Welsch post-hoc correction for multiple comparisons, with p-values less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

### Example 2

In this example, the intracellular uptake and distribution of the pentablock copolymer-coated superparamagnetic nanoparticles were quantified across three distinct human cell lines: MDA-MB-231, a triple-negative breast cancer line; BT474, a HER2-positive breast ductal carcinoma line; and MCF10A, a non-tumorigenic mammary epithelial cell line. Cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well in six-well plates and allowed to adhere for 72 hours prior to treatment with nanoparticle suspensions at concentrations ranging from 0.005 to 0.5 mg/mL. After 1 hour or 24 hours of incubation, cells were washed extensively with acidic saline (pH 3) to remove non-internalized nanoparticles, harvested using trypsin-EDTA, and subjected to cell lysis and acid digestion for iron quantification via inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry. The results demonstrated that all three cell lines internalized the nanoparticles in a concentration- and time-dependent manner, with no statistically significant differences in uptake between the cancerous and non-cancerous lines at any concentration or time point. At the lowest concentration of 0.005 mg/mL, intracellular iron content ranged from 0.8 to 1.2 ng Fe per μg of cellular protein after 24 hours, while at the highest concentration of 0.5 mg/mL, iron content reached 12 to 15 ng Fe per μg of protein, with no significant variation among the three cell types. These findings conclusively demonstrate that the pentablock copolymer coating enables efficient and uniform internalization across both malignant and non-malignant cell lines, thereby eliminating differential uptake as a potential source of selectivity. Confocal microscopy of cells treated with Alexa Fluor®647-labeled nanoparticles further confirmed that the nanoparticles were predominantly localized within lysosomes, as evidenced by strong colocalization with Lysotracker Green fluorescence. At low concentrations (0.05 and 0.1 mg/mL), greater than 80% of the nanoparticle signal overlapped with lysosomal markers in all three cell lines. However, at higher concentrations (0.5 mg/mL), the colocalization decreased to approximately 30%, indicating that excess nanoparticles overwhelmed the lysosomal capacity and began to disperse into the cytoplasm. Transmission electron microscopy revealed that the nanoparticles were not present as individual entities within lysosomes but instead formed dense aggregates, with each lysosome containing hundreds to thousands of nanoparticles packed into a compact, irregularly shaped cluster. These aggregates were surrounded by a continuous polymer matrix, consistent with the physical entanglement of the hydrophobic poly(propylene oxide) blocks of adjacent copolymer chains. The formation of these nanogels was critical to the invention, as it amplified the magnetic moment of the nanoparticle ensemble and enabled the generation of sufficient mechanical torque to disrupt the cytoskeleton upon magnetic actuation. The uniformity of uptake and distribution across all cell lines further supported the conclusion that the selective cytotoxicity observed in subsequent experiments was not due to differential nanoparticle internalization but rather to differences in the mechanical resilience of the cytoskeleton.

### Example 3

In this example, the cytotoxic effects of the nanoparticle-magnetic field system were evaluated in MDA-MB-231, BT474, and MCF10A cells under controlled exposure conditions. Cells were seeded in 96-well high-binding plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well and allowed to adhere for 72 hours. Cells were then treated with nanoparticle suspensions at concentrations ranging from 0.05 to 0.5 mg/mL for 24 hours, followed by washing with acidic saline to remove non-internalized nanoparticles. The cells were then exposed to a super-low frequency alternating magnetic field (50 Hz, 50 kA/m) using either a continuous (30 minutes) or pulsed (10 minutes on, 5 minutes off, totaling 30 minutes on) exposure regime. Following exposure, cells were incubated for an additional 24 hours and subjected to MTT viability assays. The results revealed a striking and highly selective cytotoxic effect in the cancerous cell lines, while non-malignant cells remained viable. In MDA-MB-231 cells, exposure to the pulsed field regime resulted in a dose-dependent reduction in viability, with 0.05 mg/mL nanoparticles reducing viability to 50%, 0.1 mg/mL reducing viability to 30%, and 0.25 mg/mL reducing viability to 15%. At 0.5 mg/mL, viability plateaued at approximately 10%, suggesting saturation of the cytotoxic effect. In contrast, exposure of MCF10A cells to the same concentrations and field parameters resulted in no measurable reduction in viability, with cell survival remaining above 95% across all conditions. The continuous field regime was significantly less effective, with MDA-MB-231 cells retaining 80% viability at 0.25 mg/mL and only 50% viability at 0.5 mg/mL. In BT474 cells, the response was even more pronounced, with 0.05 mg/mL nanoparticles and pulsed field exposure reducing viability to 25%, and 0.1 mg/mL reducing viability to 10%. The continuous field regime caused only a modest reduction in BT474 viability, with 50% survival at 0.1 mg/mL. Importantly, exposure of any cell line to the magnetic field in the absence of nanoparticles resulted in no reduction in viability, and exposure of nanoparticles to the magnetic field in the absence of cells produced no detectable heating, as confirmed by temperature monitoring with a fiber-optic probe placed directly in the culture medium. These findings demonstrate that the cytotoxic effect is strictly dependent on the combination of intracellular nanoparticles and magnetic field exposure, and that the selectivity for cancerous cells is absolute under the defined conditions. The pulsed regime was consistently more effective than the continuous regime, suggesting that the cyclic nature of the exposure promotes mechanical fatigue in the cytoskeleton. The lack of dose-dependent enhancement beyond 0.25 mg/mL indicates that the cytotoxic threshold is reached at a critical nanoparticle density within lysosomes, beyond which additional nanoparticles do not increase the mechanical stress. The differential response between MDA-MB-231 and BT474 cells, despite similar nanoparticle uptake, suggests that the cytoskeletal architecture of BT474 cells, which grow in dense multilayered colonies, may be more susceptible to mechanical disruption, possibly due to greater intercellular tension or higher actin density in basal cells.

### Example 4

In this example, the mechanism of cell death was investigated to determine whether the observed cytotoxicity was due to lysosomal membrane permeabilization, thermal effects, or other known pathways of cell death. To assess lysosomal integrity, cells were treated with Alexa Fluor®647-labeled nanoparticles and exposed to the pulsed magnetic field regime (50 Hz, 50 kA/m, 30 minutes on). After 24 hours, cells were stained with acridine orange, a lysosomotropic dye that emits red fluorescence when concentrated in acidic lysosomes and green fluorescence when leaked into the cytosol. Cells treated with hydrogen peroxide, a known inducer of lysosomal membrane permeabilization, exhibited complete loss of red fluorescence and diffuse green staining, confirming the assay’s sensitivity. In contrast, cells treated with nanoparticles and magnetic field exposure retained punctate red fluorescence, with no detectable increase in green cytosolic signal, indicating that the lysosomal membrane remained intact. Similar results were observed with Lysotracker Green staining, wherein the punctate pattern of lysosomal fluorescence was preserved after magnetic exposure, with no loss of intensity or dispersion into the cytoplasm. Transmission electron microscopy further confirmed the structural integrity of lysosomes, revealing no evidence of membrane rupture, swelling, or content leakage. To assess thermal effects, the temperature of the cell culture medium was monitored continuously during magnetic field exposure using a fiber-optic thermometer with sub-0.1°C resolution. No measurable temperature increase was detected, even after 30 minutes of continuous exposure at 100 kA/m, confirming that the observed cytotoxicity was not due to heating. To rule out apoptosis, necrosis, or autophagy, cells were stained with Annexin V and propidium iodide following magnetic exposure. No significant increase in Annexin V-positive cells was observed, and propidium iodide uptake remained below 5%, indicating that the cells were not undergoing classical apoptotic or necrotic death. Furthermore, no activation of caspase-3 or cleavage of PARP was detected by Western blot analysis. These findings conclusively demonstrate that the mechanism of cell death is neither apoptotic, necrotic, nor autophagic, and is not mediated by lysosomal rupture or thermal ablation. Instead, the data support a novel mechanism of mechanical failure of the cytoskeleton, wherein the actuated nanoparticle aggregates generate localized shear forces sufficient to rupture actin filaments, leading to catastrophic loss of cellular architecture and viability.

### Example 5

In this example, the role of the cytoskeleton in mediating the selective cytotoxic effect was directly investigated through pharmacological modulation of actin polymerization. Cells were pretreated with 100 nM cytochalasin D, a sub-lethal concentration known to depolymerize actin filaments and reduce cellular stiffness without inducing cell death, for 1 hour prior to nanoparticle treatment and magnetic field exposure. Following treatment, cells were fixed, stained with ActinGreen 488 to visualize F-actin, and imaged using confocal microscopy. In untreated MCF10A cells, the actin cytoskeleton was organized into thick, parallel bundles extending from the cell periphery, characteristic of healthy epithelial cells. After cytochalasin D treatment, these bundles were fragmented, and the actin network became diffuse and disorganized, resembling the cytoskeletal architecture of MDA-MB-231 cells. When these cytochalasin D-pretreated MCF10A cells were exposed to the nanoparticle-magnetic field system, they exhibited severe disruption of the actin cytoskeleton, with complete loss of filamentous structure and collapse of cell shape, indistinguishable from the damage observed in untreated cancerous cells. In contrast, cancerous cells pretreated with cytochalasin D showed no additional cytoskeletal damage beyond that caused by the nanoparticle-magnetic field system alone, indicating that their cytoskeleton was already maximally compromised. Cell viability assays confirmed these observations: MCF10A cells pretreated with cytochalasin D and exposed to the magnetic field exhibited a dramatic reduction in viability to 25%, comparable to the 20–30% viability observed in untreated cancerous cells under the same conditions. In contrast, MCF10A cells exposed to the magnetic field without cytochalasin D pretreatment retained 95% viability. These results provide definitive evidence that the differential response between malignant and non-malignant cells is not due to genetic, metabolic, or biochemical differences, but is purely a function of cytoskeletal mechanical stiffness. The sensitization of non-malignant cells to the treatment by pharmacological softening of the cytoskeleton confirms that the invention exploits a biomechanical vulnerability inherent to cancer cells, rather than a molecular target. This finding further suggests that the invention could be extended to other cell types with mechanically soft cytoskeletons, such as metastatic cells or cells undergoing epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and that the treatment could be tailored to individual tumors based on their biomechanical profile.

### Example 6

In this example, the influence of magnetic field exposure regime—continuous versus pulsed—on the efficacy of the treatment was systematically evaluated. Cells were treated with 0.1 mg/mL nanoparticles and exposed to the magnetic field (50 Hz, 50 kA/m) under three conditions: continuous exposure for 30 minutes, pulsed exposure with 10 minutes on and 5 minutes off repeated three times (total 30 minutes on), and no exposure. Cell viability was assessed 24 hours after exposure. In MDA-MB-231 cells, continuous exposure resulted in 50% viability, while pulsed exposure reduced viability to 25%. In BT474 cells, continuous exposure yielded 50% viability, whereas pulsed exposure reduced viability to 15%. In MCF10A cells, both exposure regimes resulted in >95% viability. The pulsed regime was consistently more effective, with a 2-fold increase in cytotoxicity across both cancerous lines. To investigate the mechanistic basis for this difference, cells were treated with nanoparticles and exposed to the magnetic field under the same conditions, but with the magnetic field applied for only 10 minutes followed by 20 minutes of rest, and then a second 10-minute exposure, with a final 10-minute exposure after another 20-minute rest. The results showed that the cytotoxic effect was cumulative, with each exposure cycle contributing incrementally to cell death. This suggests that the pulsed regime promotes repeated assembly and disassembly of the nanoparticle aggregates within lysosomes, leading to cyclic mechanical stress on the cytoskeleton. Transmission electron microscopy of cells exposed to the pulsed regime revealed that nanoparticle aggregates appeared more loosely packed after each off-cycle, with evidence of partial disassembly and re-aggregation during subsequent on-cycles. This dynamic behavior is consistent with the hypothesis that the magnetic field induces alignment of the nanoparticles along the field direction during the on-phase, followed by randomization during the off-phase, resulting in repeated mechanical strain on the cytoskeletal anchors. The continuous field, in contrast, maintains a steady-state alignment, which may allow for stress relaxation in the cytoskeleton, thereby reducing the cumulative damage. The pulsed regime thus mimics a fatigue-loading protocol, wherein repeated stress cycles below the static failure threshold induce material failure over time. This mechanism is analogous to the fatigue failure of polymers or metals under cyclic loading, and is consistent with the observation that the dynamic tensile strength of actin filaments is significantly lower than their static strength. The invention thus provides a novel therapeutic strategy that exploits the principles of materials fatigue to induce selective cell death, a concept previously unexplored in biomedical applications.

### Example 7

In this example, the spatial and temporal dynamics of nanoparticle aggregation and cytoskeletal disruption were visualized in real time using live-cell confocal microscopy. Cells were seeded in eight-well chamber slides and treated with Alexa Fluor®647-labeled nanoparticles for 24 hours. The magnetic field was then applied in real time using a custom-built magnetic field chamber integrated with the confocal microscope. Time-lapse imaging was performed at 5-minute intervals for 60 minutes, capturing both nanoparticle fluorescence and actin filament structure labeled with LifeAct-RFP. In the absence of magnetic field exposure, nanoparticle aggregates remained stationary within lysosomes, and the actin cytoskeleton remained intact. Upon initiation of the pulsed magnetic field (50 Hz, 50 kA/m), the nanoparticle aggregates exhibited rapid, jerky movements within the lysosomal compartment, with visible oscillations and rotational displacements occurring synchronously with the field frequency. These movements were not observed in cells treated with non-magnetic control nanoparticles. Concurrently, the actin filaments in cancerous cells began to show localized buckling, fragmentation, and detachment from focal adhesions, with the damage propagating from the perinuclear region outward. The damage occurred in discrete bursts, coinciding with the onset of each 10-minute field exposure. In non-malignant cells, no such movements or disruptions were observed, even after prolonged exposure. The magnitude of nanoparticle displacement was estimated to be on the order of 100–200 nanometers per oscillation cycle, sufficient to generate shear stresses exceeding 100 Pa at the nanoparticle-lysosome-cytoskeleton interface. The data further revealed that the cytoskeletal damage was irreversible; even after cessation of the magnetic field, the fragmented actin filaments did not reassemble, and the cells underwent progressive rounding and detachment over the next 24 hours. These real-time observations confirm that the mechanical disruption is a direct consequence of nanoparticle actuation and not a secondary effect of biochemical signaling or delayed apoptosis. The invention thus provides a direct, physics-based mechanism of cell death that can be visualized, quantified, and controlled in real time, enabling unprecedented precision in therapeutic delivery.

### Example 8

In this example, the therapeutic potential of the invention was extended to a three-dimensional tumor model to assess its efficacy in a more physiologically relevant context. MDA-MB-231 cells were cultured in Matrigel-coated wells to form multicellular spheroids with diameters of 150–200 micrometers, mimicking the architecture of small solid tumors. Spheroids were treated with 0.1 mg/mL nanoparticles for 48 hours to ensure deep penetration, followed by exposure to the pulsed magnetic field (50 Hz, 50 kA/m, 30 minutes on) using a custom-designed coil system that ensured uniform field penetration throughout the spheroid. Spheroids were then cultured for an additional 72 hours, after which viability was assessed using calcein AM and propidium iodide staining, followed by confocal z-stack imaging. Control spheroids treated with nanoparticles but no magnetic field exposure remained intact, with >90% viable cells throughout the core and periphery. Spheroids exposed to the magnetic field exhibited extensive cell death, with a 70% reduction in viable cell volume and complete collapse of spheroid architecture. The damage was most pronounced in the inner core, where nanoparticle accumulation was highest, and propagated outward, indicating that the magnetic field penetrated the spheroid uniformly and that the cytotoxic effect was not limited to the periphery. Histological analysis confirmed the absence of thermal damage, with no signs of protein denaturation or tissue charring. These results demonstrate that the invention is effective in a three-dimensional tumor model, where diffusion barriers and hypoxic regions typically limit the efficacy of conventional therapies. The ability of the magnetic field to penetrate deeply into tissue without attenuation, combined with the autonomous targeting of nanoparticles to lysosomes and the biomechanical selectivity for malignant cells, positions this invention as a transformative platform for the treatment of solid tumors. The system requires no invasive delivery, no genetic modification, and no pharmacological payload, making it a safe, scalable, and broadly applicable therapeutic modality for cancer.
