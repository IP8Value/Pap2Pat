# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of structural biology has long been constrained by the limitations of conventional X-ray crystallography, particularly in its inability to capture the native conformations of proteins that are sensitive to cryogenic conditions or require room-temperature dynamics for functional integrity. The advent of X-ray free-electron lasers (XFELs) has revolutionized this domain by enabling the collection of diffraction data from micro- and nanoscale protein crystals before the onset of radiation damage, a phenomenon known as “diffraction before destruction.” This principle, first theoretically proposed in the early 2000s and later experimentally validated, has opened new avenues for determining high-resolution structures of proteins that were previously intractable due to their inability to form large, well-ordered crystals suitable for synchrotron-based studies. Serial femtosecond crystallography (SFX), the experimental methodology that leverages XFEL pulses for this purpose, has since become indispensable for studying membrane proteins, large macromolecular complexes, and transient enzymatic intermediates. However, despite the technological triumphs of XFEL sources, the efficiency and practicality of SFX remain critically dependent on the method by which protein crystals are delivered into the ultra-intense X-ray beam. The most widely adopted delivery systems—such as the gas dynamic virtual nozzle (GDVN) and the lipidic cubic phase (LCP) injector—each carry significant drawbacks that limit their applicability across the broad spectrum of biological targets.

The GDVN, which propels protein crystals suspended in aqueous mother liquor through a narrow capillary using a high-velocity gas sheath, has been instrumental in the early successes of SFX. It enables rapid delivery of crystals at flow rates on the order of microliters per minute, ensuring a constant supply of fresh material to the interaction region. However, this high flow rate comes at an enormous cost: the vast majority of crystals—often more than 99.99%—are wasted as they pass through the beam without being hit by an X-ray pulse. This inefficiency translates into protein consumption levels that can reach tens of milligrams per data set, a prohibitive barrier for proteins that are difficult to express, purify, or crystallize, such as many membrane proteins, multi-subunit complexes, and human therapeutic targets. The economic and logistical burden of producing such quantities of protein is not merely a practical inconvenience; it fundamentally restricts the scope of biological questions that can be addressed using SFX. Furthermore, the high shear forces and rapid evaporation associated with GDVN operation can induce crystal degradation, particularly for delicate systems such as those stabilized by detergents or low-ionic-strength buffers.

In response to these challenges, alternative delivery methods have been developed, with the LCP injector emerging as the most successful for membrane proteins. LCP, a viscous, liquid-crystalline matrix formed by the spontaneous self-assembly of monoacylglycerols and water, provides a biomimetic environment that preserves the structural integrity of membrane proteins during crystallization and delivery. Its high viscosity—orders of magnitude greater than that of aqueous solutions—reduces the flow rate to nanoliters per minute, thereby decreasing protein consumption by several orders of magnitude. This innovation enabled the first SFX structures of G-protein-coupled receptors and other challenging membrane targets, marking a paradigm shift in structural biology. However, LCP is not a universal solution. Its formulation is inherently incompatible with many crystallization conditions, particularly those involving high salt concentrations or non-lipidic precipitants such as polyethylene glycols. Moreover, LCP introduces significant background scattering in X-ray diffraction patterns, particularly in the 3–5 Å resolution range, due to the diffuse scattering from lipid bilayers and the presence of long-range structural order in the cubic phase. This background obscures weak diffraction signals from large unit cells, limiting the achievable resolution and complicating data processing. Additionally, LCP requires specialized handling techniques, including precise temperature control and complex mixing protocols, making it unsuitable for high-throughput or routine applications. The preparation of LCP-injector samples is labor-intensive, and the medium itself is expensive and not universally compatible with all protein systems.

Other viscous media have been explored as potential alternatives, including mineral oil-based greases, petroleum jelly, and synthetic polymers such as polyvinyl alcohol and polyethylene oxide. While some of these materials have demonstrated the ability to form stable streams and reduce protein consumption, they suffer from severe limitations. Mineral oil and petroleum jelly, for instance, produce intense Debye–Scherrer rings in the 3.7–5 Å region, which interfere with the detection of protein Bragg peaks and compromise data quality. These media also lack aqueous compatibility, rendering them unsuitable for proteins that require hydration or are destabilized by hydrophobic environments. Furthermore, none of these alternatives have been successfully applied to the delivery of large, multi-domain membrane complexes such as photosystem I or photosystem II, which are among the most structurally intricate and functionally vital biological machines known. The inability to deliver such complexes has left a critical gap in the structural understanding of photosynthesis, cellular respiration, and other fundamental biological processes. Even more recently proposed media, such as Mebiol gel, are restricted by narrow temperature windows for viscosity, limiting their utility across diverse crystallization conditions. Thus, despite significant progress, there remains no general-purpose, robust, low-background, and broadly compatible viscous medium capable of delivering protein crystals of all types—from small soluble proteins to massive membrane complexes—under a wide range of environmental conditions, including both vacuum and ambient pressure, with minimal protein consumption and maximal structural fidelity.

The need for such a medium is not merely technical; it is biological. Membrane proteins constitute over 60% of all current drug targets, yet fewer than 550 unique structures have been solved to date, representing a minuscule fraction of the total known membrane protein families. The structural biology community faces a persistent bottleneck: the inability to obtain high-resolution structures of proteins that are scarce, unstable, or crystallize under non-standard conditions. Conventional crystallization methods often fail for these targets, and when they do succeed, the resulting crystals are too small or too fragile for synchrotron analysis. SFX offers a solution, but only if the delivery medium can match the versatility of the crystallization conditions themselves. A truly general delivery system must be chemically inert, thermally stable across a broad range, compatible with diverse precipitants and cryoprotectants, and capable of maintaining crystal integrity during extrusion without introducing confounding background signals. It must be inexpensive, easy to prepare, and scalable for routine use. Most importantly, it must not alter the native state of the protein or interfere with the diffraction signal. The absence of such a medium has hindered the expansion of SFX into new biological domains, leaving many of the most medically and scientifically significant proteins structurally uncharacterized. The invention described herein addresses this fundamental limitation by introducing agarose, a naturally derived polysaccharide, as a novel, high-performance viscous medium for serial femtosecond crystallography that overcomes the shortcomings of all prior delivery systems.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention discloses a novel method and composition for the delivery of protein crystals in serial femtosecond crystallography using agarose as a viscous, non-Newtonian medium, enabling the efficient, low-consumption, and high-fidelity structural characterization of both soluble and membrane protein complexes under a wide range of experimental conditions. This invention fundamentally transforms the practicality and scope of SFX by providing a delivery system that is broadly compatible with existing crystallization protocols, produces minimal X-ray background scattering, requires negligible quantities of protein, and operates stably across a wide temperature range and under both vacuum and ambient pressure environments. Unlike prior viscous media, agarose does not interfere with the crystallization process, does not denature proteins upon embedding, and does not introduce structural artifacts into the diffraction data. The method involves the post-crystallization embedding of pre-formed protein crystals into a solution of agarose dissolved in a buffer that matches the crystallization conditions, followed by the addition of a cryoprotectant such as glycerol or sucrose to stabilize the crystals during extrusion in vacuum. The resulting agarose-crystal suspension is then extruded through a capillary at low flow rates, typically less than 200 nanoliters per minute, using a standard LCP injector setup, ensuring that the crystals are delivered to the XFEL beam in a continuous, stable stream with high spatial alignment and minimal waste.

The key innovation lies in the selection of agarose as the viscous medium, a natural polymer derived from red algae that forms a thermoreversible gel upon cooling. Agarose is composed of approximately 93% water and 7% polysaccharide chains, resulting in a matrix that is chemically inert, biocompatible, and structurally disordered on the length scales relevant to X-ray diffraction. This composition minimizes background scattering, particularly at low resolution, where lipid-based media such as LCP exhibit strong diffuse signals due to their ordered bilayer architecture. In contrast, agarose produces a background that is less than one-third that of LCP in the 3–5 Å region and exhibits negligible scattering above 30 Å, making it uniquely suited for the study of proteins with large unit cells. The method further enables the use of a wide variety of crystallization conditions, including high concentrations of salts such as ammonium sulfate and magnesium chloride, polyethylene glycols of molecular weights ranging from 400 to 8000, organic precipitants such as 2-methyl-2,4-pentanediol, and even detergent-only buffers that are incompatible with other viscous media. Agarose is also compatible with cryoprotectants such as glycerol, sucrose, and trehalose, allowing for the stabilization of crystals in vacuum without inducing ice formation or structural collapse. The temperature range over which agarose maintains its viscous properties spans from 4°C to 30°C, accommodating crystallization conditions that span physiological, cryogenic, and elevated temperatures, unlike other media that are only functional within narrow thermal windows.

The invention further includes a novel protocol for the embedding of crystals into agarose using a syringe-based mixing system adapted from lipidic cubic phase crystallization techniques. This protocol ensures homogenous distribution of crystals within the agarose matrix without inducing mechanical damage, dehydration, or precipitation of crystallization agents. The agarose solution is prepared at an elevated concentration (typically 7% w/v) and dissolved in the crystallization buffer along with the chosen cryoprotectant. After thorough heating and homogenization, the hot agarose solution is drawn into a syringe and allowed to cool to ambient temperature, at which point it enters the gel phase. The crystal suspension, concentrated to approximately 10¹¹ crystals per milliliter, is then introduced into a second syringe and connected via a coupler to the agarose syringe. Through repeated back-and-forth mixing—typically at least 40 cycles—the crystals are uniformly dispersed within the agarose matrix, achieving a final concentration of approximately 2 × 10¹⁰ crystals per milliliter. This process dilutes the agarose to a final concentration of 5.6% w/v, which, in combination with glycerol or sucrose, produces a stream with optimal viscoelastic properties for stable extrusion. The resulting sample is then loaded into a standard LCP injector and delivered to the XFEL beam at flow rates as low as 160 nanoliters per minute, reducing protein consumption by two orders of magnitude compared to conventional GDVN systems.

The invention has been successfully demonstrated with a diverse array of protein systems, including the soluble phycocyanin complex, the membrane-bound photosystem I trimer, and the membrane-bound photosystem II dimer, each representing a different class of biological target with distinct crystallization requirements. Phycocyanin, a 200 kDa trimeric light-harvesting complex, was crystallized using vapor diffusion in high-salt conditions and successfully embedded in agarose without dissolution or degradation. Photosystem II, a 700 kDa dimeric complex containing over 100 cofactors, was delivered in agarose prepared with PEG and calcium-containing buffers, yielding high-quality diffraction patterns despite the absence of lipidic components. Photosystem I, a 1080 kDa trimeric complex that crystallizes in detergent-only, low-ionic-strength buffers and is notoriously sensitive to glycerol and salt, was stabilized in agarose by incorporating sucrose as a cryoprotectant, demonstrating that even the most fragile and unconventional crystallization conditions are compatible with this medium. In all cases, the resulting diffraction data exhibited superior signal-to-noise ratios compared to those obtained using LCP, with significantly reduced background scattering and no observable Debye–Scherrer rings. The structure of phycocyanin was solved to 2.5 Å resolution using molecular replacement, with an R-work of 18.7% and an R-free of 25.5%, confirming the structural integrity of the protein within the agarose matrix. These results establish agarose as a general-purpose delivery medium capable of supporting SFX for the full spectrum of protein targets, from small soluble proteins to the largest known membrane complexes.

The invention further provides a method for data collection under ambient pressure using a helium environment, eliminating the need for cryoprotectants in certain cases and reducing the risk of dehydration-induced crystal damage. The agarose stream remains stable under helium, although vacuum operation is preferred for optimal stream alignment and reduced background. The low cost, commercial availability, and ease of preparation of agarose make this method accessible to any laboratory equipped with an XFEL, eliminating the need for specialized reagents or complex formulations. The invention thus represents a transformative advance in structural biology, enabling the routine structural determination of proteins that were previously inaccessible due to scarcity, instability, or incompatible crystallization conditions. By decoupling the delivery medium from the crystallization conditions, this invention removes a critical bottleneck in SFX and opens the door to the structural characterization of previously intractable biological systems, including drug targets, viral envelope proteins, and multi-subunit cellular machines.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention pertains to a novel method and composition for the delivery of protein crystals in serial femtosecond crystallography, wherein agarose, a naturally derived polysaccharide, is employed as a viscous, non-Newtonian medium that enables the efficient, low-consumption, and high-fidelity delivery of protein crystals to the interaction region of an X-ray free-electron laser. The method is broadly applicable to both soluble and membrane protein complexes, regardless of their size, structural complexity, or the specific conditions under which they were crystallized. The invention overcomes the fundamental limitations of prior delivery systems by providing a medium that is chemically inert, structurally disordered, thermally stable across a wide temperature range, and compatible with virtually all common crystallization conditions, including high salt concentrations, polyethylene glycols, detergents, and organic precipitants. The agarose medium does not denature proteins, does not dissolve crystals, and does not introduce significant background scattering into the diffraction patterns, thereby preserving the structural integrity of the protein and maximizing the quality of the collected data.

The core of the invention is the formulation of a viscous agarose-based suspension that is prepared post-crystallization and prior to injection into the XFEL beam. This formulation is not a pre-mixed commercial product but a custom-prepared solution that is tailored to the specific crystallization conditions of the target protein. Agarose, a linear polysaccharide composed of repeating units of agarobiose, is dissolved in a solution that contains the crystallization buffer, a cryoprotectant, and optionally, additional stabilizing agents. The agarose is heated to a temperature exceeding 85°C to ensure complete dissolution, after which it is allowed to cool to ambient temperature, where it undergoes a phase transition from a viscous liquid to a gel-like solid. This gelation process is reversible and reproducible, allowing for the preparation of consistent batches of the medium. The concentration of agarose is carefully optimized to ensure that, after mixing with the crystal suspension, the final concentration falls within a range that provides sufficient viscosity for stable extrusion while maintaining the homogeneity of crystal distribution. In preferred embodiments, the initial agarose concentration is 7% (w/v), which, upon mixing with a 5 µl volume of concentrated crystal suspension, yields a final concentration of approximately 5.6% (w/v). This dilution is necessary to prevent excessive viscosity that would hinder flow through the injector capillary, while still maintaining the mechanical stability required to prevent crystal sedimentation or aggregation during extrusion.

The cryoprotectant component of the medium is selected based on the sensitivity of the protein crystals to dehydration and freezing. In vacuum environments, where evaporative cooling can lead to ice formation and crystal damage, cryoprotectants such as glycerol, sucrose, or trehalose are essential. Glycerol is particularly effective in increasing the viscosity of the agarose matrix while simultaneously suppressing ice nucleation. For proteins that are sensitive to glycerol, such as photosystem I, which crystallizes in low-ionic-strength detergent-only buffers, sucrose is used as an alternative cryoprotectant. Sucrose provides equivalent cryoprotection without inducing crystal dissolution or structural perturbation. The concentration of cryoprotectant is adjusted to achieve optimal stream stability and crystal preservation, with glycerol concentrations typically ranging from 20% to 30% (v/v) and sucrose concentrations ranging from 0.5 M to 2.0 M, depending on the protein system. The choice of cryoprotectant is not arbitrary but is dictated by the chemical compatibility of the protein with the surrounding medium. For example, proteins crystallized in high-salt conditions may tolerate glycerol but not sucrose, while proteins crystallized in detergent micelles may be destabilized by glycerol but stabilized by sucrose. The invention encompasses a systematic approach to selecting and optimizing cryoprotectants for each protein system, ensuring that the medium does not compromise the native state of the protein.

The crystallization buffer used to dissolve the agarose is identical to the buffer in which the crystals were grown. This is a critical aspect of the invention, as it ensures that the chemical environment surrounding the crystals remains unchanged during embedding and delivery. The buffer may contain salts such as ammonium sulfate, magnesium chloride, or calcium chloride, precipitants such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) of molecular weights ranging from 400 to 8000, or detergents such as β-dodecylmaltoside. The agarose medium is fully compatible with these components, and no precipitation or phase separation occurs upon mixing. In cases where the addition of precipitant reduces the viscosity of the agarose solution, the initial agarose concentration is increased to 9% (w/v) to compensate for dilution and maintain extrusion stability. This flexibility allows the method to be applied to a wide variety of crystallization protocols without modification, a feature that distinguishes it from all prior delivery systems, which require the adaptation of crystallization conditions to fit the constraints of the medium.

The method of embedding crystals into the agarose matrix is a key innovation of the invention. Rather than attempting to grow crystals within the agarose gel—a process that is hindered by the low diffusion coefficients of large proteins and the gel’s restrictive pore structure—the invention employs a post-crystallization embedding technique. Pre-formed crystals, typically ranging in size from 1 to 5 micrometers, are first concentrated by low-speed centrifugation or sedimentation to achieve a density of approximately 10¹¹ crystals per milliliter. The crystals are then filtered through a 10 µm stainless-steel filter to remove aggregates and debris. The agarose solution, prepared as described above, is drawn into a syringe that has been pre-warmed by repeated aspiration and ejection of boiling water to prevent premature gelation during handling. A second syringe is filled with the concentrated crystal suspension. The two syringes are connected via a syringe coupler, a device originally developed for lipidic cubic phase crystallization, which allows for the controlled transfer of fluids between the two chambers. Through a series of at least 40 back-and-forth movements of the syringe plungers, the crystals are uniformly dispersed throughout the agarose matrix. This mixing protocol is gentle enough to prevent mechanical damage to the crystals while ensuring complete homogeneity. The resulting suspension contains approximately 2 × 10¹⁰ crystals per milliliter, a density sufficient to yield a high hit rate during SFX data collection.

The agarose-crystal suspension is then loaded into a standard LCP injector, which consists of a 50 µm capillary through which the medium is extruded under the guidance of a high-velocity nitrogen sheath gas. The flow rate is typically maintained at 160 nanoliters per minute, a rate that is two orders of magnitude lower than that of conventional GDVN systems. This low flow rate ensures that only a minimal volume of protein-containing medium is consumed during data collection, reducing protein requirements from milligrams to micrograms per data set. The extrusion is stable and continuous, with no observable breakage, oscillation, or turbulence in the stream, even over extended periods of data collection. The stream is centered within the X-ray beam using the sheath gas, and the crystals are delivered in random orientations, enabling the collection of a complete diffraction data set from a population of randomly oriented microcrystals. The entire process, from crystal embedding to data collection, can be performed at temperatures ranging from 4°C to 30°C, accommodating crystallization conditions that span physiological, cryogenic, and elevated temperatures. This thermal flexibility is a significant advantage over other viscous media, such as Mebiol gel, which is only viscous above 25°C, or petroleum jelly, which becomes too brittle at low temperatures.

The invention further encompasses the use of the agarose medium under ambient pressure conditions, particularly in a helium atmosphere. In this configuration, the risk of dehydration-induced crystal damage is eliminated, and cryoprotectants are not strictly required. The agarose stream remains stable under helium, although the background scattering is slightly higher than in vacuum due to the presence of ambient gas molecules. Nevertheless, the background remains significantly lower than that produced by LCP, mineral oil-based grease, or petroleum jelly. The ambient-pressure setup is particularly advantageous for proteins that are sensitive to cryoprotectants or for experiments requiring the preservation of dynamic states that may be perturbed by the addition of glycerol or sucrose. The agarose medium’s compatibility with both vacuum and ambient-pressure environments makes it uniquely versatile and suitable for a wide range of XFEL beamlines and experimental configurations.

The X-ray diffraction properties of the agarose medium are a defining feature of the invention. Unlike LCP, which produces strong diffuse scattering from lipid bilayers at 4.5 Å resolution, or mineral oil-based greases, which generate Debye–Scherrer rings at 3.77–5 Å, agarose exhibits minimal background scattering across the entire diffraction range. The scattering profile of agarose is dominated by a weak, broad feature centered at approximately 3.3 Å, which arises from the disordered hydration shell of the polysaccharide chains and does not interfere with protein Bragg peaks. At low resolution (>30 Å), agarose produces negligible scattering, in stark contrast to LCP, which exhibits strong low-angle scattering due to its long-range ordered cubic structure. This low background is particularly advantageous for proteins with large unit cells, such as photosystem I and photosystem II, where weak diffraction signals from large, low-symmetry lattices are easily obscured by background noise. The reduction in background scattering translates directly into improved signal-to-noise ratios, higher data completeness, and more accurate structure determination. The invention has been validated through the collection of high-quality diffraction data from multiple protein systems, including phycocyanin, photosystem I, and photosystem II, all of which yielded interpretable electron density maps and high-resolution structures.

The structural integrity of the protein within the agarose matrix has been confirmed through the successful determination of the phycocyanin structure to 2.5 Å resolution. The electron density map clearly resolved side chains, loops, and the phycocyanobilin chromophore, with no evidence of conformational distortion or ligand displacement attributable to the agarose medium. The refinement statistics, including an R-work of 18.7% and an R-free of 25.5%, are comparable to those obtained using conventional crystallographic methods and superior to those obtained using LCP for similar systems. The ability to solve structures of such complexity using agarose as a delivery medium demonstrates that the medium does not interfere with the native structure of the protein and that the data collected are of sufficient quality for high-resolution structure determination. The invention thus provides not only a means of delivering crystals but also a means of preserving their structural fidelity throughout the entire process.

The invention further includes the use of standard laboratory equipment and commercially available reagents, making it accessible to any research group with access to an XFEL. Agarose is inexpensive, readily available from multiple suppliers, and does not require specialized handling or storage. The syringe mixing protocol requires only standard laboratory syringes, a syringe coupler, and a water bath, all of which are commonly found in structural biology laboratories. The LCP injector, which is used for extrusion, is already in widespread use at XFEL facilities, eliminating the need for new hardware. The method is scalable, reproducible, and suitable for high-throughput applications. It can be applied to any protein system that can be crystallized, regardless of its size, complexity, or sensitivity to environmental conditions. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the field of serial femtosecond crystallography, transforming it from a technique accessible only to a few specialized laboratories into a broadly applicable tool for structural biology.

### EXAMPLE 1

The invention is exemplified by the successful application of agarose as a crystal delivery medium for serial femtosecond crystallography using the phycocyanin complex, a large, soluble, multi-subunit light-harvesting protein from the cyanobacterium Thermosynechococcus elongatus. Phycocyanin was selected as a model system due to its size, structural complexity, and the fact that it crystallizes under conditions that are incompatible with many existing viscous delivery media. The protein forms a disc-like trimer, with each monomer composed of two subunits, α and β, and contains multiple phycocyanobilin chromophores that are essential for its function in energy transfer. The crystals of phycocyanin were grown using free interface diffusion, a method that produces microcrystals of 1 to 5 micrometers in size, which are too small for conventional synchrotron-based data collection but ideal for SFX. The crystallization buffer consisted of 1.0 M ammonium sulfate and 40 mM MES at pH 6.4, conditions that are highly saline and incompatible with lipidic media such as LCP, which tend to dissolve under such high ionic strength.

To prepare the agarose medium, 7% (w/v) ultralow-gelling-temperature agarose (Sigma-Aldrich, catalog No. A5030) was dissolved in a solution containing 600 µl of glycerol and 1.4 ml of the crystallization buffer. The mixture was placed in a 15 ml centrifuge tube and submerged in a water bath at 100°C for 30 minutes to ensure complete dissolution of the agarose powder. A 100 µl Hamilton syringe was pre-warmed by drawing up and ejecting boiling water 10 to 15 times to prevent premature gelation during handling. The hot agarose solution was then drawn into the syringe while the tube remained submerged in the boiling water bath. The syringe was removed from the bath and allowed to equilibrate at room temperature for approximately 20 minutes, during which time the agarose underwent gelation, forming a semi-solid, viscous matrix. Simultaneously, a second syringe was filled with 5 µl of a highly concentrated suspension of phycocyanin crystals, prepared by low-speed centrifugation of the crystal-containing solution to achieve a density of approximately 10¹¹ crystals per milliliter. The two syringes were connected using a syringe coupler, and the crystals were embedded into the agarose matrix through 40 alternating movements of the syringe plungers, ensuring complete homogenization without mechanical disruption of the crystals. The final concentration of agarose in the mixture was approximately 5.6% (w/v), and the crystal density was estimated at 2 × 10¹⁰ crystals per milliliter.

The agarose-crystal suspension was loaded into a standard LCP injector equipped with a 50 µm capillary and extruded into the X-ray interaction region of the Coherent X-ray Imaging (CXI) instrument at the Linac Coherent Light Source (LCLS) at SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory. The extrusion was performed under vacuum conditions, with a nitrogen sheath gas used to stabilize and center the stream. The flow rate was maintained at 160 nanoliters per minute, and data collection was performed over a period of 72 minutes, during which 513,848 diffraction patterns were recorded. Using Cheetah software, the patterns were analyzed for hit detection, and those containing at least 25 Bragg spots were retained for further processing. Of the 513,848 patterns, 41,100 were identified as hits, yielding an overall hit rate of 8%. Of these, 14,143 patterns were successfully indexed using CrystFEL, with a clear hexagonal lattice identified having unit-cell parameters a = b = 153.4 Å and c = 39.6 Å. The space group was determined to be P6₃, and an indexing ambiguity was resolved using the ambigator algorithm within CrystFEL. The data were truncated at a resolution of 2.5 Å based on the CC* value and multiplicity, with an overall multiplicity of 250.6 and a multiplicity of 12.5 in the highest resolution shell (2.63–2.5 Å).

The indexed and integrated diffraction data were then merged and used for molecular replacement using phenix.phaser, with the structure of phycocyanin from PDB entry 4gy3 serving as the search model. Following molecular replacement, the model was refined using phenix.refine through ten cycles of positional and individual B-factor refinement, including two cycles of simulated annealing. Water molecules were added and refined iteratively, and the final model exhibited an R-work of 18.7% and an R-free of 25.5%. The electron density map was of high quality, clearly resolving the side chains of the α and β subunits, the loops connecting secondary structural elements, and the phycocyanobilin chromophores, with no evidence of disorder or distortion attributable to the agarose medium. The refined structure was consistent with previously determined structures of phycocyanin, confirming that the agarose delivery method preserved the native conformation of the protein.

The success of this experiment demonstrates the efficacy of agarose as a delivery medium for SFX. The protein consumption was reduced by two orders of magnitude compared to conventional GDVN systems, with only a few micrograms of protein used to collect a complete data set. The background scattering from agarose was significantly lower than that observed in LCP, with no Debye–Scherrer rings or strong diffuse features interfering with the Bragg peaks. The method was compatible with high-salt crystallization conditions, which are typically incompatible with lipidic media. The agarose medium remained stable throughout the data collection period, with no observable stream breakage, oscillation, or crystal sedimentation. The entire process—from crystal embedding to structure determination—was completed using standard laboratory equipment and commercially available reagents, with no need for specialized formulations or custom hardware. The results establish agarose as a robust, general-purpose medium for SFX that is applicable to a wide range of protein systems, including those that are difficult to crystallize, sensitive to environmental conditions, or available in limited quantities. This example serves as a definitive proof of concept for the invention, demonstrating that agarose is not merely a viable alternative to existing delivery media but a superior medium that overcomes their fundamental limitations and expands the scope of structural biology to previously inaccessible targets.
