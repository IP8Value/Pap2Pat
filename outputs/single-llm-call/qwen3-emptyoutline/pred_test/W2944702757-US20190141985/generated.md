# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE DISCLOSED SUBJECT MATTER

The disclosed subject matter relates to systems, methods, and apparatuses for the extracorporeal recovery and regeneration of severely damaged organs, particularly lungs, following acute and severe injury such as that caused by gastric aspiration. More specifically, the invention provides a novel platform for prolonged normothermic extracorporeal organ support that integrates systemic physiological regulation with repeated, clinically relevant therapeutic interventions, enabling not only the maintenance of organ viability but also the active regeneration of tissue architecture, cellular function, and physiological performance. The disclosed invention is particularly directed to the use of cross-circulation with a living host to sustain injured lungs outside the body for extended durations—up to and exceeding 36 hours—during which time the lungs undergo progressive structural and functional restoration, including reconstitution of the alveolar-capillary barrier, restoration of surfactant production and secretion, resolution of inflammation, and recovery of mechanical compliance and gas exchange capacity. The invention further encompasses diagnostic modalities for non-invasive, real-time assessment of organ recovery, including dynamic surface thermography and exosome profiling in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, which serve as quantitative biomarkers of injury severity and regenerative trajectory. The disclosed subject matter finds application in the field of organ transplantation, critical care medicine, and regenerative bioengineering, where the ability to salvage previously deemed unsuitable donor organs significantly expands the donor pool and addresses the critical shortage of transplantable lungs. The invention represents a paradigm shift from passive organ preservation to active, therapeutically guided organ regeneration, enabling the transformation of severely injured lungs into viable transplantable organs through a systemically regulated, physiologically faithful extracorporeal environment.

## BACKGROUND OF THE DISCLOSED SUBJECT MATTER

The clinical success of lung transplantation as a life-saving intervention for end-stage pulmonary disease has been consistently limited by the severe scarcity of suitable donor organs. Despite advances in donor identification, organ procurement, and preservation techniques, a substantial proportion of donated lungs—up to 80% in some reports—remain unused due to injury sustained at the time of death or during the peri-mortem period. Common causes of such injury include trauma, aspiration of gastric contents, ventilator-associated lung injury, and pulmonary contusion. Among these, gastric aspiration stands out as a particularly devastating and prevalent cause of acute lung injury, characterized by the instillation of acidic, enzymatic, and particulate gastric contents into the airways and alveoli. This event triggers an immediate chemical burn to the respiratory epithelium, followed by a robust inflammatory cascade involving neutrophil infiltration, cytokine release, microvascular leakage, and surfactant dysfunction. The resulting pathophysiology—marked by severe hypoxemia, decreased lung compliance, bilateral consolidation, and diffuse alveolar damage—renders these lungs unsuitable for transplantation under current clinical criteria. Traditional ex vivo lung perfusion (EVLP) systems, while effective in evaluating and improving marginal donor lungs, have proven inadequate in the setting of severe aspiration injury. Prior attempts to utilize EVLP for such cases have consistently resulted in progressive edema, failure to reduce inflammatory mediators, and irreversible structural damage, leading to organ discard. The fundamental limitation of existing EVLP platforms lies in their inability to replicate the systemic homeostatic feedback mechanisms of a living organism. These systems rely on artificial perfusates, static or intermittent interventions, and limited physiological monitoring, which fail to support the complex, multi-cellular, and dynamic processes required for true tissue regeneration. Consequently, the clinical pipeline for lung transplantation remains constrained by an inability to recover organs that, while severely injured, may still possess latent regenerative potential. Furthermore, the absence of non-invasive, real-time diagnostic tools capable of quantifying the trajectory of recovery has hindered the objective assessment of therapeutic efficacy during extracorporeal support. There exists, therefore, a profound and unmet need for a system capable of not only sustaining severely damaged lungs outside the body for prolonged periods but also actively promoting their regeneration through continuous physiological regulation and repeated, clinically validated interventions. The disclosed subject matter addresses this need by introducing a cross-circulation-based platform that leverages the full regulatory capacity of a living recipient to provide normothermic, systemic, and dynamic support, enabling the recovery of lungs previously considered irreparable. This innovation fundamentally redefines the boundaries of organ viability and transforms the paradigm of donor organ assessment from one of exclusion to one of therapeutic opportunity.

## SUMMARY

The disclosed subject matter provides a novel and non-obvious system and method for the extracorporeal regeneration of severely damaged lungs through prolonged normothermic cross-circulation with a living host, wherein the injured organ is maintained outside the body for durations exceeding 36 hours while undergoing continuous therapeutic interventions and systemic physiological regulation. The invention comprises a cross-circulation circuit that connects the pulmonary vasculature and airway of a severely injured donor lung to the systemic circulation of a living recipient animal, thereby enabling the transfer of blood, oxygen, nutrients, hormones, immune modulators, and metabolic waste products between the recipient and the extracorporeal lung in a physiologically authentic manner. This configuration permits the maintenance of normothermic perfusion, continuous ventilation, and precise control of hemodynamic, metabolic, and inflammatory parameters, creating an environment conducive to tissue repair and cellular regeneration. The system further integrates a sequence of clinically translatable therapeutic interventions—including bronchoalveolar lavage, exogenous surfactant replacement, and alveolar recruitment maneuvers—performed at regular intervals throughout the support period, each timed and dosed according to established clinical protocols. These interventions are administered under real-time physiological monitoring and are facilitated by a specialized cannulation strategy that enables independent assessment of gas exchange and compliance in the injured lung versus a contralateral control lung. The invention further includes novel diagnostic modalities for non-invasive, longitudinal assessment of organ recovery, including dynamic surface thermography to detect regional ventilation-perfusion mismatch and quantify rewarming kinetics, and quantitative analysis of exosome concentration and size distribution in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid as a biomarker of injury severity and regenerative progression. The disclosed method results in the restoration of key structural and functional attributes of the lung, including the reconstitution of alveolar epithelial tight junctions, recovery of aquaporin-5 and surfactant protein C expression, regeneration of type II pneumocyte lamellar bodies, restoration of endothelial integrity and vascular permeability, resolution of neutrophilic infiltration and apoptosis, and normalization of mechanical compliance and gas exchange capacity. By the conclusion of 36 hours of cross-circulation support, injured lungs demonstrate histological, biochemical, and functional parameters indistinguishable from those of healthy control lungs, meeting all established criteria for clinical transplantation. The invention thus enables the recovery of lungs previously considered unsalvageable, thereby substantially expanding the donor pool and offering a transformative solution to the global organ shortage. The disclosed system and method represent a significant advancement over prior art by integrating systemic physiological regulation, repeated therapeutic interventions, and non-invasive diagnostics into a single, unified platform capable of achieving true organ regeneration rather than mere preservation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE DISCLOSED SUBJECT MATTER

The disclosed subject matter comprises a comprehensive system and method for the extracorporeal regeneration of severely damaged lungs through prolonged normothermic cross-circulation with a living host, wherein the injured organ is sustained outside the body for extended durations while undergoing active therapeutic interventions and systemic physiological regulation. The invention is predicated on the discovery that the complex, multi-cellular, and dynamic processes required for the regeneration of severely injured lung tissue cannot be replicated by artificial perfusion systems but can be effectively supported through the integration of a living recipient’s circulatory and homeostatic systems. The system is designed to maintain the injured lung in a normothermic, perfused, and ventilated state for a minimum of 36 hours, during which time the lung undergoes progressive structural and functional restoration, including the reconstitution of the alveolar-capillary barrier, the regeneration of surfactant-producing type II pneumocytes, the resolution of inflammation, and the recovery of mechanical compliance and gas exchange capacity. The core of the invention lies in the cross-circulation circuit, which establishes a direct, bi-directional connection between the pulmonary vasculature and airway of the donor lung and the systemic circulation of a living recipient animal. This connection is achieved through a specialized cannulation technique that utilizes the aortic arch as a biological bridge, or bio-bridge, between the left atrial cuff of the explanted lung and the pulmonary venous drainage system. This bio-bridge preserves the native endothelial lining and provides a seamless conduit for venous outflow from the lung into the recipient’s systemic circulation, thereby ensuring that the lung receives blood with the full complement of circulating factors, including hormones, cytokines, growth factors, and immune cells, in a manner that mirrors in vivo physiology. The pulmonary artery of the donor lung is cannulated separately to enable arterial inflow from a closed, recirculating circuit that is primed with autologous blood collected during donor procurement. This configuration ensures that the lung is perfused with blood that is fully oxygenated, normothermic, and physiologically compatible, while simultaneously allowing for precise control of flow rates, pressures, and resistance. The airway is maintained via a cuffed endotracheal tube secured in the trachea, permitting continuous, controlled ventilation throughout the entire support period. The recipient animal is maintained under general anesthesia with continuous monitoring of vital signs, including heart rate, mean arterial pressure, oxygen saturation, end-tidal carbon dioxide, and core temperature, ensuring that the systemic environment remains stable and supportive. The recipient receives a bolus of methylprednisolone and calcium chloride prior to initiation of cross-circulation to mitigate inflammatory responses and support cardiovascular stability, and is maintained on a continuous heparin infusion to prevent circuit thrombosis, with activated clotting time monitored hourly to maintain a target range of 250 to 350 seconds. The entire procedure is conducted in a sterile operating room environment using clinically approved equipment, including a centrifugal pump console, softshell reservoir, and medical-grade tubing, ensuring that the system is translatable to human clinical settings. The injured lung is subjected to a series of standardized therapeutic interventions at predetermined intervals—specifically at 0, 2, 4, 6, 12, and 18 hours of cross-circulation—each consisting of three sequential components: bronchoalveolar lavage, surfactant replacement, and alveolar recruitment maneuvers. Bronchoalveolar lavage is performed using a flexible bronchoscope to instill and aspirate sterile normal saline in a segmental fashion, with approximately 50 milliliters of fluid delivered per lobe, for a total volume of approximately 300 milliliters per intervention. This process removes particulate debris, gastric contents, inflammatory exudates, and cellular debris, while simultaneously neutralizing the acidic pH of the airway lumen and reducing the concentration of proteolytic enzymes such as pepsin. Following lavage, exogenous surfactant in the form of bovine lipid extract is delivered bronchoscopically into each lobe of the injured lung at a dose of 12.5 milligrams of phospholipids per kilogram of donor body weight, consistent with established clinical regimens. The surfactant is warmed to room temperature prior to administration and is visualized distally advancing through the airway tree during positive pressure ventilation, confirming its uniform distribution. Alveolar recruitment maneuvers are then performed by transiently increasing the tidal volume by 15 to 20 percent, elevating the positive end-expiratory pressure to a maximum of 12.5 centimeters of water, and applying sustained inspiratory holds at pressures up to 25 centimeters of water, with manual compression applied to areas of atelectasis by an experienced surgeon to direct ventilation toward non-aerated regions. These maneuvers are performed for a total of 10 minutes per intervention and are carefully monitored to ensure that peak inspiratory pressures remain below 30 centimeters of water, thereby avoiding barotrauma. The sequence of interventions is repeated every six hours for a total of six cycles over the 36-hour support period, allowing for progressive and cumulative therapeutic benefit. Throughout the entire duration of cross-circulation, the functional status of the injured lung is continuously monitored through a combination of invasive and non-invasive diagnostics. Hemodynamic parameters, including pulmonary artery and venous pressures, flows, and temperatures, are recorded continuously using a clinical interfacing software system. Blood samples are drawn hourly from the recipient’s arterial line and every six hours from the main pulmonary artery, main pulmonary vein, and single lung venous cannulas to assess gas exchange, electrolytes, inflammatory markers, and hemolytic indices. Dynamic compliance is calculated every six hours using the formula tidal volume divided by the difference between peak inspiratory pressure and positive end-expiratory pressure, and pressure–volume loops are generated using custom data acquisition hardware and software to evaluate changes in lung mechanics. Lung weight is measured every six hours using a top-loading balance within the organ chamber, with the basin and contents tared at each time point to ensure accuracy. Radiographic imaging is performed periodically to assess changes in lung opacity, consolidation, and aerated volume. Critically, the invention introduces two novel non-invasive diagnostic modalities for real-time assessment of lung recovery: dynamic surface thermography and exosome profiling in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. Surface thermography is performed using an infrared camera capable of capturing thermal images at 60 frames per second, with lung surface temperatures averaged over isomorphic regions of interest in both the injured and contralateral control lungs. The system detects regions of elevated surface temperature that correlate with areas of consolidation and perfused but non-ventilated parenchyma, and tracks the normalization of temperature heterogeneity over time as ventilation-perfusion matching improves. A hypothermic probe test is also performed at regular intervals, wherein a cooled aluminum probe is brought into brief contact with the pleural surface, and the rate of rewarming is quantified to determine the efficiency of local thermal conduction, which serves as a surrogate marker of microvascular perfusion and tissue integrity. Exosome concentration in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid is measured using nanoparticle tracking analysis, revealing a transient elevation in exosome particles during the first 12 hours of support, followed by a progressive decline toward control levels, indicating a dynamic resolution of cellular stress and inflammation. The invention further enables the longitudinal assessment of cellular regeneration through histological, immunohistochemical, and ultrastructural analyses performed at multiple time points. These analyses demonstrate the restoration of aquaporin-5 expression in type I pneumocytes, the reconstitution of tight junction proteins such as zonula occludens-1 and zonula occludens-3, the recovery of surfactant protein C expression and lamellar body formation in type II pneumocytes, the normalization of macrophage and neutrophil populations, the resolution of apoptosis as measured by caspase-3 and TUNEL staining, and the regeneration of the pulmonary microvasculature as evidenced by CD31 staining, acetylated LDL uptake, and carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester uptake. Transmission electron microscopy confirms the restoration of the alveolar-capillary barrier, with intact type I and type II pneumocyte membranes, restored basement membrane integrity, and the absence of extravascular erythrocytes or inflammatory cells. Scanning electron microscopy reveals the re-establishment of the ciliated epithelial brush border and the presence of mucus-filled granules in goblet cells, indicating functional regeneration of the conducting airways. The cumulative effect of these interventions and the systemic support provided by cross-circulation is the transformation of a lung that, at the time of explant, exhibits severe diffuse alveolar damage, hemorrhage, edema, and inflammation into a lung that, after 36 hours of support, demonstrates histological, biochemical, and functional characteristics indistinguishable from those of a healthy control lung. The injured lung achieves a PaO2/FiO2 ratio comparable to that of the control lung, demonstrates normalized pressure–volume loop hysteresis, exhibits dynamic compliance values approaching those of healthy lungs, and shows no evidence of persistent inflammation, apoptosis, or structural derangement. The invention thus provides a complete and integrated system for the regeneration of severely damaged lungs, combining systemic physiological regulation, repeated therapeutic interventions, and non-invasive diagnostics into a single, clinically translatable platform that enables the recovery of organs previously deemed unsuitable for transplantation.

## EXAMPLES

### Methods

The methods employed to implement the disclosed subject matter are detailed herein in a manner sufficient to enable a person of ordinary skill in the art to reproduce the invention without undue experimentation. The invention was demonstrated using a large animal model involving sixteen miniature swine, organized into eight donor–recipient pairs, all selected from a herd of partially inbred animals with well-defined major histocompatibility complex loci to minimize immunologic cross-reactivity. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Columbia University. Donor swine underwent general anesthesia via intramuscular induction with Telazol and buprenorphine hydrochloride, followed by continuous infusion of midazolam, fentanyl citrate, and inhaled isoflurane. A standardized gastric aspiration injury model was induced by bronchoscopic delivery of 2 milliliters per kilogram of gastric contents, adjusted to a pH of 2.00 ± 0.05, into a single lung over a period of five minutes, with the bronchoscope tip positioned approximately one centimeter distal to the carina to ensure unilateral injury. The donor was maintained under anesthesia for six hours to allow for the development of severe acute lung injury, as confirmed by radiographic, bronchoscopic, and biochemical criteria, including increased radiopacity, airway inflammation, elevated inflammatory cytokines in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, and histopathological evidence of alveolar hemorrhage, edema, and neutrophil infiltration. Upon explant, the lungs were procured en bloc with the heart, and the heart was removed to expose the left atrial cuff. The aortic arch was dissected and preserved as a bio-bridge, with the left subclavian and brachiocephalic branches ligated, and a circumferential cuff of 3 to 5 millimeters was left on the left atrium to facilitate venous cannulation. A 36 Fr crenellated drainage cannula was secured to the aortic arch using 2–0 Ti-Cron ties, and two 20-gauge, 12-centimeter cannulas were inserted through the wall of the bio-bridge into the left and right pulmonary veins to enable independent venous drainage and hemogas analysis of each lung. An 18–20 Fr cannula was placed in the main pulmonary artery for arterial inflow, and a 7.5–8.0 millimeter cuffed endotracheal tube was secured in the trachea to permit continuous ventilation. The lungs were flushed with cold Perfadex and placed in a sterile organ preservation chamber maintained at 37 degrees Celsius, with the pulmonary artery, pulmonary vein, and endotracheal tube connected to a closed, recirculating circuit primed with autologous blood. The recipient swine underwent bilateral internal jugular vein cannulation using the Seldinger technique, with heparin administered prior to cannulation. The recipient was pre-treated with methylprednisolone and calcium chloride and maintained on a continuous heparin infusion to achieve an activated clotting time of 250 to 350 seconds. Cross-circulation was initiated by splicing the recipient’s venous cannulas into the extracorporeal circuit, thereby establishing a continuous flow of blood between the recipient and the extracorporeal lung. Ventilation was initiated within the first ten minutes of cross-circulation using volume control mode with a respiratory rate of 6 to 8 breaths per minute, a tidal volume of 6 to 8 milliliters per kilogram, a positive end-expiratory pressure of 5 centimeters of water, and a fraction of inspired oxygen of 40 percent. Therapeutic interventions were performed at 0, 2, 4, 6, 12, and 18 hours of cross-circulation, each consisting of bronchoalveolar lavage using 5 milliliters of sterile saline per lung segment, followed by bronchoscopic delivery of one vial of bovine lipid extract surfactant (3 milliliters containing 27 milligrams of phospholipids per milliliter), and concluding with 10 minutes of alveolar recruitment maneuvers involving increased tidal volume, elevated positive end-expiratory pressure, and inspiratory holds. Single lung ventilation was achieved using a 7 Fr endobronchial blocker inserted into the main stem bronchus of the contralateral lung under bronchoscopic guidance, allowing for independent assessment of injured and control lung function. Blood samples were drawn hourly from the recipient’s auricular arterial line and every six hours from the pulmonary artery, pulmonary vein, and single lung venous cannulas for analysis of hemogases, inflammatory markers, and metabolic parameters. Dynamic compliance was calculated every six hours using the formula tidal volume divided by the difference between peak inspiratory pressure and positive end-expiratory pressure. Pressure–volume loops were generated using custom sensors for pressure and airflow connected to an Arduino Uno data acquisition device, with data processed using MATLAB. Lung weight was measured every six hours using a top-loading balance within the organ chamber, with the basin and contents tared at each time point. Radiographic imaging was performed using a portable X-ray unit at 2.2 mAs and 90 kVp. Surface thermography was performed using a FLIR T430sc infrared camera, with thermal images captured at selected time points and analyzed using ResearchIR software. A hypothermic probe test was conducted at each time point by bringing a 4-degree Celsius aluminum probe of 8-millimeter diameter into contact with the pleural surface for one second, with thermal videography recorded at 60 frames per second and rewarming kinetics quantified using ImageJ software. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid was collected at each intervention by wedging the bronchoscope into a subsegmental bronchus and instilling and aspirating 5 milliliters of sterile saline, with samples analyzed for pH, total protein, pepsin concentration, exosome concentration, and cytokine levels using commercially available ELISA kits and nanoparticle tracking analysis. Histopathological analysis was performed on lung tissue samples collected at 0, 18, and 36 hours of cross-circulation, with specimens fixed in 4 percent paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 3 to 5 micrometers, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin, pentachrome, Alcian blue, periodic acid-Schiff, silver reticulin, and immunohistochemical markers for aquaporin-5, surfactant protein C, zonula occludens-1, zonula occludens-3, CD31, CD163, connexin 43, and caspase-3. Transmission electron microscopy was performed on samples fixed in glutaraldehyde and post-fixed in osmium tetroxide, embedded in Lx-112, sectioned at 60 nanometers, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and imaged using a JEOL JEM-1200 EXII electron microscope. Scanning electron microscopy was performed on lyophilized samples imaged using a Zeiss GeminiSEM 300. Metabolic activity was assessed using the Alamar Blue assay, with tissue homogenates incubated with reagent for two hours and absorbance measured at 570 and 600 nanometers. Myeloperoxidase activity was measured using a commercial kit, and exosome concentration was quantified using NanoSight Tracking Analysis. All analyses were performed in triplicate, and statistical significance was determined using one-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s post hoc test and Student’s t-test, with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered significant. The results demonstrated that, following 36 hours of cross-circulation support, the injured lungs exhibited complete restoration of structural and functional parameters, including normalization of PaO2/FiO2 ratio, dynamic compliance, lung weight, and histological appearance, with no residual evidence of inflammation, apoptosis, or vascular leakage, thereby fulfilling all criteria for clinical transplantation.
