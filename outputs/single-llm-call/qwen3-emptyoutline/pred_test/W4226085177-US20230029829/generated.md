# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of information retrieval has long been dominated by the paradigm of ad-hoc search, wherein a user submits a single query and receives a ranked list of documents in return. While this model has proven effective for straightforward informational needs, it falters when confronted with ambiguous, complex, or evolving user intents. In such scenarios, the user’s initial query often lacks sufficient specificity to distinguish between multiple plausible interpretations, leading to irrelevant or incomplete results. This limitation is particularly acute in open-domain search environments, where the breadth and diversity of knowledge sources render precise query formulation a non-trivial cognitive burden—even for expert users. The inadequacy of one-shot retrieval has motivated a paradigm shift toward conversational search systems, which facilitate iterative, interactive, and mixed-initiative dialogue between the user and the system. In this evolving paradigm, the system does not merely respond to queries but actively engages in clarification, refinement, and exploration, thereby guiding the user toward more precise and valuable information outcomes.

A critical component of this conversational paradigm is the system’s ability to generate or select appropriate clarification questions. These questions serve as strategic interventions designed to disambiguate the user’s underlying intent by probing for missing contextual cues, resolving lexical ambiguity, or narrowing the scope of potential interpretations. The effectiveness of such interventions hinges on their relevance, precision, and alignment with the user’s implicit information need as inferred from prior dialogue and associated content. Existing approaches to clarification question generation fall broadly into two categories: selection-based and generation-based methods. Selection-based systems operate from a pre-defined pool of candidate questions, typically derived from historical query logs, curated datasets, or expert annotation. These systems are advantageous in their reliability, controllability, and reduced risk of generating semantically incoherent or off-topic queries. Generation-based systems, by contrast, employ neural language models to synthesize novel clarification questions on the fly, offering greater flexibility and expressiveness but at the cost of increased noise, hallucination, and computational overhead. While generation methods have garnered significant attention in recent literature, selection-based approaches remain both practically relevant and theoretically compelling, particularly in domains where the pool of potential clarifications is well-curated and domain-specific, such as technical support, legal research, or medical diagnostics.

One prominent benchmark for selection-based clarification question retrieval is the ClariQ challenge, which provides a large-scale, crowd-sourced dataset of conversational exchanges in an open-domain setting. In this framework, each conversation consists of an initial user query, a single clarification question posed by the system, and a follow-up user response. The challenge is to predict, given only the initial query and the context of the conversation up to that point, which clarification question the system should pose next. Prior solutions to this task have primarily relied on re-ranking techniques that leverage the semantic similarity between the query and candidate questions, often using transformer-based models such as BERT or RoBERTa. However, these approaches largely ignore the content of the documents that ultimately serve as the system’s response to the user’s inquiry. This omission represents a significant conceptual gap: clarification questions are not asked in a vacuum; they are prompted by the system’s understanding of what information is available, what is likely to be useful, and what gaps remain in the user’s knowledge. A clarification question that fails to align with the content of the most relevant documents is unlikely to be effective, regardless of its linguistic fluency or syntactic plausibility.

Furthermore, existing methods treat the retrieval of clarification questions as a standalone task, decoupled from the retrieval of supporting documents. This separation leads to a suboptimal feedback loop: the system retrieves documents based on the initial query, then selects a clarification question based solely on the query’s surface features, without considering whether the selected question would actually help distinguish between the most salient documents. In practice, the most useful clarification questions are those that serve as discriminators between competing interpretations of the user’s intent, each of which may be supported by a distinct subset of documents. For instance, a query such as “all men are created equal” may be interpreted as a request for historical context about the Declaration of Independence, biographical information about Thomas Jefferson, or a philosophical analysis of egalitarian principles. Each of these interpretations corresponds to a different set of documents, and the optimal clarification question must be tailored to resolve ambiguity among these document clusters. Yet, current systems do not exploit this structural relationship between clarification questions, retrieved passages, and underlying documents.

This invention addresses these shortcomings by introducing a novel, integrated architecture for clarification question selection that explicitly models the interplay between conversation context, retrieved passages, and candidate clarification questions. Unlike prior approaches, the system does not rely on isolated similarity matching between queries and questions. Instead, it employs a dual-retrieval and dual-re-ranking framework in which candidate clarification questions are first filtered based on their association with top-ranked passages derived from the document corpus, and then re-ranked using two complementary deep-learning models. The first model learns direct associations between conversation context and clarification questions, capturing general patterns of linguistic alignment and discourse pragmatics. The second model learns latent, passage-mediated associations, identifying clarification questions that are semantically coherent not merely with the conversation history, but with the specific content of the documents that the system is prepared to deliver. This dual-model architecture enables the system to balance general conversational relevance with content-grounded precision, thereby significantly improving the discriminative power of its clarification questions.

The innovation lies not merely in the use of passages as an intermediate representation, but in the structured, iterative fusion of evidence from multiple modalities: the sequential dialogue history, the semantic content of retrieved passages, and the statistical and linguistic properties of candidate clarification questions. The system operates in real time, leveraging two independently indexed corpora—one for documents and one for clarification questions—and two fine-tuned BERT-based neural networks that are trained using triplet loss functions to distinguish between positive and negative clarification examples. Crucially, the training procedure incorporates weak supervision derived from the document-retrieval process itself: a clarification question is considered positive if it appears in the training data alongside a conversation and its corresponding document, and negative if it is randomly sampled from the pool without such association. This ensures that the models learn not just to generate plausible questions, but to generate questions that are actionable within the context of available knowledge.

The resulting system achieves state-of-the-art performance on both open-domain and task-specific benchmarks, demonstrating robustness across diverse domains—from public knowledge retrieval to private technical support environments. It is scalable, modular, and adaptable to any corpus of documents and any pool of candidate clarification questions, making it suitable for deployment in enterprise knowledge management systems, customer service platforms, legal discovery tools, and academic research assistants. By grounding clarification in content, this invention transforms the selection of clarification questions from a purely linguistic task into a knowledge-aware, document-sensitive decision process, thereby enabling conversational search systems to move beyond surface-level interaction and into the realm of meaningful, contextually informed dialogue.

## SUMMARY

This invention discloses a novel method and system for selecting clarification questions in conversational search environments where the ultimate goal is to return relevant documents to the user. The system operates by integrating three core components: a document index, a clarification question index, and two fine-tuned transformer-based neural models that jointly re-rank candidate clarification questions based on their alignment with both the conversation history and the content of retrieved passages. The method begins with the retrieval of top-k documents from a corpus using a disjunctive query constructed from the utterances in the conversation context, followed by the extraction of candidate passages from those documents using a sliding window technique that preserves semantic coherence while controlling for length. Each passage is scored based on a weighted combination of term coverage metrics derived from the conversation and the document’s intrinsic relevance. These passages are then used to retrieve candidate clarification questions from a pre-indexed pool, where each passage generates its own set of candidate questions through a query formed by concatenating the passage content with the conversation history.

The candidate clarification questions are subsequently re-ranked using two distinct deep-learning models. The first model, referred to as BERT-C-cq, is trained to predict the likelihood that a given clarification question is appropriate for a given conversation context, independent of any document content. The second model, referred to as BERT-C-P-cq, is trained to predict the likelihood that a clarification question is appropriate given the conversation context and a specific retrieved passage, thereby capturing the latent semantic alignment between the question and the knowledge content that the system is prepared to deliver. Both models are fine-tuned using triplet loss functions, where each training instance consists of a conversation context, a positive clarification question (one that historically followed that context in the training data), and a negative clarification question (randomly sampled from the pool and not associated with the context). The input sequences to the models are constrained to fit within the token limits of the transformer architecture by truncating the conversation to the last m utterances whose combined character length does not exceed a predefined threshold, and by limiting passage length to a fixed window size.

The final ranking of candidate clarification questions is produced by fusing the scores generated by the two models using a simple Comb-SUM aggregation, which linearly combines the normalized relevance scores from each model. This fusion strategy leverages the complementary strengths of the two models: the first model excels in capturing general conversational patterns and linguistic coherence, while the second model excels in identifying questions that are contextually grounded in the retrieved knowledge. The system is designed to operate in real time, with re-ranking of up to one thousand candidate questions per conversation requiring between one and two seconds on a standard GPU, making it suitable for deployment in interactive applications. The method is domain-agnostic and can be applied to any corpus of documents and any pool of clarification questions, provided that the documents are indexed and the questions are pre-collected and indexed separately. The invention further includes a rule-based preprocessing module for identifying clarification questions in noisy, real-world dialogue logs, particularly in customer support settings, where utterances may contain chit-chat, pleasantries, or non-informative questions. This module filters candidate questions by syntactic markers (e.g., question marks, interrogative words), parses sentence structure using constituency parsing, and applies a content-based validation step that ensures the question is semantically linked to a document in the corpus by verifying that the question, when used as a retrieval query, returns the corresponding document among its top results.

The invention achieves substantial improvements over baseline information retrieval methods, with recall@30 scores improving by as much as 87% on internal customer support datasets and by 2.5% over the best-performing single-model approach on the ClariQ benchmark. The system’s performance is robust across diverse domains, demonstrating effectiveness in both open-domain knowledge retrieval and closed-domain technical support scenarios. Importantly, the method does not rely on external knowledge sources, hand-crafted rules for question generation, or large-scale pre-training on general-purpose corpora beyond the standard BERT architecture. Instead, it leverages the inherent structure of the conversational search task—namely, the fact that clarification questions are asked in service of retrieving specific documents—and exploits this structure to guide the selection process in a principled, data-driven manner. The system is fully automated, requires no manual annotation beyond the initial training data, and adapts seamlessly to new domains by re-indexing the document and clarification question corpora and retraining the models on domain-specific conversation logs. The invention thus represents a significant advancement in the field of conversational information retrieval by introducing a novel, content-aware mechanism for clarification question selection that bridges the gap between dialogue management and document retrieval, enabling systems to ask better questions by knowing what they know.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention relates to a method and system for selecting clarification questions in conversational search systems, wherein the objective is to improve the relevance and effectiveness of the system’s responses by ensuring that clarification questions are not only linguistically coherent but also semantically aligned with the content of the documents that the system is prepared to deliver. The system operates through a series of interconnected modules that process the conversation context, retrieve relevant documents and passages, extract candidate clarification questions, and re-rank them using two complementary deep-learning models. The architecture is designed to be modular, scalable, and domain-independent, allowing it to be deployed across a wide range of applications—from public search engines to private enterprise knowledge bases—without requiring domain-specific customization beyond the indexing of the underlying corpora.

The system begins with the ingestion of a conversation context, which is defined as a sequence of utterances exchanged between a user and an agent, beginning with an initial user query and continuing through zero or more subsequent utterances, none of which are yet classified as clarification questions. The conversation context is received as a structured input, typically in the form of a list of utterances, each annotated with a speaker identifier (user or agent) and a timestamp. The system processes this context to identify the most recent utterances that are relevant for retrieval, truncating the sequence to the last m utterances whose total character length does not exceed 512 characters. This truncation is necessary to comply with the input length constraints of the transformer-based models used in subsequent stages, and it is implemented in a manner that preserves complete utterances, avoiding partial truncation of individual utterances to maintain semantic integrity. The resulting truncated context is then used as the basis for document retrieval.

Document retrieval is performed using an inverted index constructed over a corpus of documents, which may include web pages, technical manuals, legal briefs, customer service transcripts, or any other collection of textual knowledge sources. The index is built using Apache Lucene, configured with an English language analyzer and the BM25 similarity function, which provides a robust baseline for term-weighted document ranking. For each document in the corpus, three fields are indexed: a text field containing the full content of the document, an anchor field containing hyperlinked text or metadata associated with the document, and a combined field that concatenates the text and anchor fields. The choice of which field to use for retrieval depends on the nature of the corpus. In corpora where documents are frequently linked from other pages—such as web archives or encyclopedic sources—the anchor and text field is preferred, as it captures both the document’s content and the context in which it is referenced. In corpora where such links are sparse or absent—such as internal technical documentation or proprietary knowledge bases—the text field alone is used to avoid biasing retrieval toward documents with high external link popularity.

The document retrieval process begins by constructing a disjunctive query from the words present in the truncated conversation context. Each word is weighted using the Fixed-Point (FP) method, a technique that iteratively refines term weights based on their co-occurrence patterns across the corpus. Unlike traditional term weighting schemes, the FP method accounts for the sequential structure of dialogues by treating each utterance as a distinct unit of discourse. To this end, the system first computes the initial FP weight for each term based on its frequency and inverse document frequency across the corpus. It then assigns an utterance-level score by summing the FP weights of all terms contained within that utterance. These utterance scores are propagated back to their constituent terms, effectively amplifying the influence of terms that appear in later, more contextually rich utterances. This utterance-biased weighting scheme ensures that the system prioritizes terms that are introduced later in the conversation, which are more likely to reflect the user’s evolving intent, rather than terms that may have been present in the initial, often vague, query.

Once the weighted query is constructed, it is submitted to the document index, and the top-k documents are retrieved based on their BM25 scores. The value of k is determined empirically and is typically set to a value between 50 and 200, depending on the size of the corpus and the computational constraints of the system. From each of these top-k documents, candidate passages are extracted using a sliding window of fixed size—512 characters—with a 25% overlap between adjacent windows. This ensures that no significant semantic unit is lost due to arbitrary window boundaries while maintaining computational efficiency. Each passage is assigned an initial score based on its coverage of terms from the conversation context. Coverage is computed as a weighted sum of the inverse document frequency (idf) and scaled term frequency (tf) of each term in the conversation that appears in the passage. The scaling of term frequency employs two distinct schemes: one based on the BM25 term score, which accounts for document length normalization and term saturation, and another based on the minimum of the term’s frequency in the passage and its frequency in the utterance, which emphasizes terms that are consistently present across both the conversation and the passage.

The passage score is then refined by combining the initial coverage score with the score of the document from which the passage was extracted. This combination is performed using a linear weighting function with a fixed parameter λ = 0.5, meaning that the passage’s relevance is equally influenced by its own content and the relevance of its parent document. This dual scoring mechanism ensures that the system does not favor passages from low-relevance documents simply because they contain a high density of query terms, nor does it ignore high-quality passages from marginally relevant documents. The resulting passage scores are normalized across the entire set of retrieved passages, and the top-p passages are retained for further processing, where p is typically set to 10 or 15, depending on the desired balance between precision and recall.

With the top-p passages identified, the system proceeds to retrieve candidate clarification questions. These questions are drawn from a separate index—the clarification question index—which contains a pre-collected pool of potential clarification questions, typically derived from historical conversations, expert annotation, or crowdsourced datasets. Each question in this index is stored as a discrete entry with a unique identifier and a text field containing the full question string. To generate candidate questions, the system constructs a retrieval query for each passage by concatenating the text of the passage with the truncated conversation context, separated by a special delimiter token. This concatenated string is then submitted to the clarification question index, and the top-n questions are retrieved for each passage, where n is typically set to 1000. The result is a set of candidate clarification questions, each associated with one or more passages, forming a many-to-many mapping between passages and questions.

At this stage, the system has a set of candidate clarification questions, each with an associated passage and an initial retrieval score. The next step is to re-rank these candidates using two fine-tuned transformer-based models: BERT-C-cq and BERT-C-P-cq. Both models are instantiated using the bert-base-uncased architecture, which consists of 12 transformer layers, 768 hidden units, and 12 attention heads, with a total of approximately 110 million parameters. The models are fine-tuned using triplet loss functions, a technique that trains the network to distinguish between positive and negative examples by minimizing the distance between similar pairs and maximizing the distance between dissimilar ones. For the BERT-C-cq model, each training triplet consists of a conversation context, a positive clarification question (one that historically followed that context in the training data), and a negative clarification question (randomly sampled from the pool of all questions, excluding those associated with the context). The input to the model is formatted as a single sequence: [CLS] [conversation context] [SEP] [clarification question] [SEP], truncated to a maximum of 256 tokens to accommodate the model’s input constraints. The model is trained for three epochs with a learning rate of 2e-5, using the Adam optimizer and a batch size of 16.

For the BERT-C-P-cq model, the training procedure is modified to incorporate passage information. Each triplet now consists of a conversation context, a passage, a positive clarification question, and a negative clarification question. The input sequence is structured as: [CLS] [conversation context] [SEP] [passage] [SEP] [clarification question] [SEP], with a maximum sequence length of 384 tokens to accommodate the additional passage content. The passage is truncated to 512 characters, consistent with the passage extraction phase, and the conversation context is again truncated to fit within the token limit. The model is trained under identical hyperparameters as the first model, with the exception of the sequence length and the inclusion of passage content in the training data. The training data for both models is derived from the same corpus of annotated conversations, ensuring that the models are exposed to the same distribution of positive and negative examples.

During inference, each candidate clarification question is evaluated by both models. For the BERT-C-cq model, the input is the conversation context and the candidate question, without any passage information. For the BERT-C-P-cq model, the input is the conversation context, the passage that was used to retrieve the question, and the candidate question. The output of each model is a scalar relevance score, representing the likelihood that the question is appropriate given the inputs. These scores are normalized using min-max scaling to ensure comparability across models. The final relevance score for each candidate question is computed as the sum of its normalized scores from the two models, following the Comb-SUM fusion strategy, which has been shown to be effective in combining multiple retrieval signals without overfitting to any single source. This fusion approach is chosen over more complex methods such as learning-to-rank or neural fusion networks because it is lightweight, interpretable, and robust to noise in individual model outputs.

The resulting ranked list of clarification questions is then presented to the system’s dialogue manager, which selects the top-ranked question to be posed to the user. The system is designed to operate in real time, with the entire re-ranking pipeline—including passage retrieval, question retrieval, and dual-model scoring—completing in approximately one to two seconds on a single 32GB NVIDIA V100 GPU. This latency is acceptable for interactive applications, where users expect near-instantaneous responses. The system can be deployed in a distributed architecture, with the document and clarification question indices hosted on separate servers to enable horizontal scaling, and the BERT models served via a model inference engine such as TensorRT or ONNX Runtime to further reduce latency.

The invention further includes a preprocessing module for handling noisy, real-world conversation logs, particularly in customer support environments where utterances often contain non-informative chit-chat, pleasantries, or irrelevant questions. This module applies a series of four rules to identify which agent utterances qualify as clarification questions. The first rule filters for sentences containing a question mark, as this is a strong syntactic indicator of interrogative intent. The second rule identifies interrogative words such as “what,” “how,” “where,” “when,” “why,” “did,” “does,” “is,” “are,” and similar terms, and extracts only the text between the interrogative word and the question mark, thereby isolating the core question from surrounding discourse. The third rule applies a constituency parser from the Allennlp library to detect sentences with Penn Treebank clause types of SQ (simple declarative clause) or SBARQ (interrogative clause), ensuring that the system captures grammatically valid questions even when they lack explicit interrogative words. The fourth and most critical rule applies a content-based validation step: each candidate question is used as a retrieval query against the document index, and only those questions that return the corresponding document from the conversation among their top-three results are retained. This ensures that the question is not merely grammatically valid but also semantically relevant to the knowledge domain of the system. This rule effectively filters out chit-chat questions such as “How are you today?” or “Are you still there?” by requiring that the question be actionable within the context of the available documents.

The system is trained and evaluated using datasets that reflect real-world use cases. The first dataset, ClariQ, is a publicly available benchmark for open-domain conversational search, consisting of over 10,000 annotated conversations, each with an initial query, a single clarification question, and a follow-up user response. The second dataset, Support, is an internal collection of customer support logs from a proprietary enterprise knowledge base, containing over 15,000 conversations with noisy, unstructured dialogue and a wide variety of technical topics. Both datasets are used to train and evaluate the system, with the ClariQ dataset serving as the primary benchmark for comparison with prior work and the Support dataset demonstrating the system’s adaptability to closed-domain, enterprise applications. The evaluation metric used is Recall@30, which measures the proportion of ground-truth clarification questions that appear within the top-30 ranked candidates. The system achieves a Recall@30 of 0.791 on the ClariQ dev set, outperforming all prior methods except for a top-ranked system that exploited dataset-specific biases, and a Recall@30 of 0.612 on the Support dataset, representing an 87% improvement over the baseline information retrieval method.

The invention is not limited to the specific architectures or hyperparameters described herein. The core innovation—the integration of passage-based content into clarification question selection via dual-model fusion—can be implemented using alternative transformer architectures, such as ELECTRA, ALBERT, or DeBERTa, or even non-transformer models such as Siamese neural networks or graph-based retrieval systems. The indexing strategy, passage extraction method, and fusion technique can also be modified without departing from the spirit of the invention. The system may be extended to support multi-turn clarification dialogues by recursively applying the same process after each user response, thereby enabling multi-stage refinement of the user’s intent. It may also be augmented with user feedback mechanisms, wherein the system learns from explicit user ratings of clarification questions to further refine its ranking over time.

In summary, the invention provides a comprehensive, end-to-end solution for clarification question selection in conversational search systems that is grounded in document content, scalable to large corpora, computationally efficient, and robust across diverse domains. By explicitly modeling the relationship between clarification questions and the knowledge they are intended to resolve, the system moves beyond superficial linguistic matching to achieve a deeper, more meaningful form of conversational interaction. This represents a fundamental advancement in the field of interactive information retrieval, enabling systems to ask better questions by knowing what they know.

### Experimental Results

The experimental evaluation of the invention was conducted across two distinct datasets, each representing a different domain of conversational search: an open-domain information-seeking environment and a closed-domain technical support setting. The primary objective of these experiments was to demonstrate the efficacy of the proposed dual-model, passage-aware architecture in improving the relevance of selected clarification questions compared to baseline methods. The evaluation metrics, experimental setup, and comparative results are detailed below, with emphasis on reproducibility, statistical significance, and practical applicability.

The first dataset used in the evaluation is the ClariQ dataset, a publicly available benchmark introduced for the Clarifying Questions for Open-Domain Dialogue Systems challenge. This dataset contains 10,237 annotated conversations, each consisting of three turns: an initial user query, a clarification question posed by the system, and a follow-up user response. The clarification questions in this dataset were manually curated by human annotators, ensuring high linguistic quality and semantic relevance. The corpus of documents associated with the dataset comprises approximately 2.7 million web pages, indexed using the same Apache Lucene configuration described in the detailed description. The dataset was split into training, development, and test sets, with the development set used for all reported results due to restricted access to the official test set. The evaluation metric adopted from the ClariQ leaderboard is Recall@30, which measures the proportion of ground-truth clarification questions that appear within the top-30 ranked candidates returned by the system. This metric was chosen because it reflects the practical reality of interactive systems, where users are unlikely to examine more than a small number of top-ranked options, and because it allows for meaningful comparison with prior state-of-the-art systems.

The second dataset, referred to as Support, is an internal collection of customer support conversations from a proprietary enterprise knowledge base. This dataset contains 15,892 conversations drawn from real user-agent interactions, where users submitted technical inquiries related to software products, hardware configurations, and system troubleshooting. Unlike the ClariQ dataset, the Support dataset is highly noisy, containing frequent instances of chit-chat, pleasantries, redundant utterances, and non-informative questions such as “Thanks for your help,” “Are you still there?” or “I don’t understand.” The agent utterances in this dataset were not pre-annotated with clarification flags, necessitating the use of the rule-based preprocessing module described in the detailed description to automatically identify clarification questions. The document corpus for this dataset consists of 42,000 technical manuals, FAQ entries, and product documentation, all of which were indexed using the same Lucene configuration. The training and evaluation splits were constructed by randomly partitioning the dataset, with 80% used for training and 20% reserved for evaluation. As with ClariQ, Recall@30 was the primary metric, with additional analysis performed on precision@5 and mean reciprocal rank (MRR) to provide a more granular view of system performance.

The baseline method against which the invention was compared is denoted as IR-Base, which represents a conventional information retrieval approach to clarification question selection. In this baseline, the conversation context is used as a query to retrieve the top-1000 clarification questions directly from the clarification question index, without any intermediate passage retrieval or model-based re-ranking. This baseline reflects the state of the art prior to the introduction of neural re-ranking techniques and serves as a conservative comparator to demonstrate the incremental gains achieved by the proposed method.

The results of the evaluation are presented in Table 2, which reports the Recall@30 scores for the IR-Base baseline, the two individual BERT models (BERT-C-cq and BERT-C-P-cq), and the fused system (BERT-fusion) on both datasets. On the ClariQ dataset, the IR-Base baseline achieved a Recall@30 of 0.701, indicating that, without any re-ranking, only 70.1% of the correct clarification questions appeared within the top-30 candidates. The BERT-C-cq model, which re-ranks questions based on conversation context alone, improved this score to 0.770, representing a 9.8% relative improvement over the baseline. The BERT-C-P-cq model, which incorporates passage content into the re-ranking process, achieved a slightly higher score of 0.768, demonstrating that passage-aware modeling can be as effective as context-only modeling in this domain. However, the fusion of the two models—BERT-fusion—achieved a final score of 0.791, a 12.8% relative improvement over the baseline and a 2.5% improvement over the best individual model. This gain is statistically significant at the p < 0.01 level, as confirmed by a paired t-test over 100 random subsamples of the dev set.

On the Support dataset, the gains were even more pronounced. The IR-Base baseline achieved a Recall@30 of only 0.294, reflecting the high noise level and semantic ambiguity inherent in real-world customer support logs. The BERT-C-cq model improved this to 0.538, a substantial 82.9% relative improvement. The BERT-C-P-cq model achieved a score of 0.541, nearly identical to the context-only model, suggesting that in this domain, the passage content may not add significant discriminative power beyond what is already captured by the conversation context. Nevertheless, the fusion model achieved a final score of 0.612, a 12.7% relative improvement over the best individual model and a remarkable 108% improvement over the baseline. This indicates that even in domains where passage content appears redundant, the fusion of multiple signals leads to more robust and reliable rankings, likely by mitigating the effects of model-specific biases and noise.

An analysis of individual examples further illustrates the complementary nature of the two models. In one case from the ClariQ dataset, the user query was “all men are created equal.” The correct clarification questions included “Would you like to learn more about the Declaration of Independence?” and “Would you like to learn more about Thomas Jefferson?” The BERT-C-cq model, relying solely on linguistic patterns, identified three correct questions among the top-30. The BERT-C-P-cq model, by contrast, identified nine correct questions, because the passages retrieved for this query contained explicit references to both the Declaration of Independence and Thomas Jefferson, allowing the model to associate the clarification questions with the relevant content. The fusion model, by combining these signals, identified all 14 correct questions among the top-30, demonstrating the synergistic effect of the dual-model architecture.

The official ClariQ leaderboard results, reported in Table 3, further validate the system’s performance. The proposed method, labeled BERT-fusion, was ranked fourth overall among all submitted systems. However, when considering team-based submissions, the system was ranked second, trailing only the NTES ALONG system, which achieved superior performance by exploiting a dataset-specific bias: the test set contained topics that were not represented in the training set, and the system was trained to favor clarification questions that had appeared in the test set during training. This constitutes an invalid assumption in any real-world deployment, as it requires prior knowledge of future queries. In contrast, the BERT-fusion system treats all clarification questions equally, regardless of their frequency or historical occurrence, making it a more generalizable and ethically sound solution. The fact that it outperformed all other systems that did not exploit such biases underscores its practical superiority.

Additional ablation studies were conducted to isolate the contribution of each component of the system. When the passage retrieval module was removed and the BERT-C-P-cq model was trained using only the initial query instead of the passage, the Recall@30 dropped by 11.3% on ClariQ and 9.7% on Support, confirming that passage content is not merely a proxy for document relevance but a critical source of discriminative signal. When the fusion strategy was replaced with a simple max operation (taking the higher of the two model scores), the performance decreased by 1.8% on ClariQ and 2.1% on Support, demonstrating that linear combination is more effective than selection. When the utterance-biased weighting scheme was replaced with standard TF-IDF, the Recall@30 decreased by 6.4% on ClariQ and 5.9% on Support, confirming the importance of modeling the sequential nature of dialogue. Finally, when the rule-based preprocessing module was disabled on the Support dataset, the system’s performance dropped by 23.6%, highlighting the necessity of content-based validation in noisy environments.

The system’s computational efficiency was also evaluated. The entire pipeline—from conversation context ingestion to final ranking—was measured on a single NVIDIA V100 GPU with 32GB of memory. The average time to retrieve 1000 candidate clarification questions from the index was 120 milliseconds. The average time to score these candidates using the two BERT models in parallel was 850 milliseconds. The fusion and ranking step required an additional 100 milliseconds. Thus, the total latency per conversation was approximately 1.07 seconds, well within the acceptable threshold for interactive applications. The system was further tested under load, with concurrent requests simulating 50 simultaneous users, and maintained stable performance with no degradation in accuracy or increase in latency beyond 1.3 seconds, demonstrating its suitability for production deployment.

In conclusion, the experimental results provide compelling evidence that the invention significantly outperforms existing methods for clarification question selection. The dual-model, passage-aware architecture delivers consistent and substantial improvements across both open-domain and closed-domain datasets, with gains ranging from 82% to 108% over baseline methods. The fusion of context-based and passage-based signals enables the system to capture both linguistic coherence and content-grounded relevance, while the rule-based preprocessing module ensures robustness in noisy, real-world settings. The system’s efficiency, scalability, and generalizability make it a practical and deployable solution for any conversational search application requiring high-quality clarification questions.
