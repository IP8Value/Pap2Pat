# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The complement system is a fundamental component of the innate immune response, playing a critical role in the recognition and elimination of pathogens, the clearance of immune complexes and apoptotic cells, and the modulation of adaptive immunity. Complement activation occurs through three distinct pathways—the classical, lectin, and alternative pathways—each converging at the central component C3, whose cleavage into C3a and C3b is the pivotal step in amplifying the immune response. C3b, the activated fragment of C3, serves as the central opsonin and catalytic subunit of the C3 and C5 convertases, which drive the downstream cascade leading to pathogen opsonization, inflammation, and membrane attack complex formation. The regulation of C3b activity is tightly controlled by a network of soluble and membrane-bound regulators, including factor H, complement receptor 1 (CR1), and factor I, which together ensure that complement activation is confined to pathogen surfaces and does not cause collateral damage to host tissues.

Dysregulation of the complement system, particularly the alternative pathway, has been implicated in a growing number of human diseases, including age-related macular degeneration, atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis, ischemia-reperfusion injury, autoimmune disorders such as systemic lupus erythematosus, and complications arising from cardiopulmonary bypass and transplantation. In these conditions, uncontrolled C3b deposition on host cells leads to chronic inflammation, tissue destruction, and organ dysfunction. Current therapeutic strategies targeting complement have largely focused on inhibiting upstream components such as factor D or factor B, blocking the terminal pathway with anti-C5 antibodies, or administering recombinant regulatory proteins such as soluble CR1 or compstatin analogs. While these approaches have demonstrated clinical efficacy in certain contexts, they often suffer from limitations including broad immunosuppression, poor pharmacokinetics, lack of pathway specificity, or inability to target the active convertase complex directly.

A major challenge in complement therapeutics has been the development of agents that selectively inhibit the alternative pathway without compromising the classical or lectin pathways, which remain essential for host defense against encapsulated bacteria and immune complex clearance. Targeting C3 itself has proven problematic due to its extraordinary abundance in plasma—approximately 120 µg/mL—which necessitates high drug doses and increases the risk of systemic complement suppression. Furthermore, inhibitors that bind native C3 may interfere with its physiological functions, including its role in immune surveillance and homeostasis. An alternative and more precise strategy involves targeting C3b, the activated form of C3, which is transiently generated only upon complement activation and exists at significantly lower concentrations in circulation. However, the structural similarity between C3 and C3b, coupled with the dynamic conformational changes that occur during C3 activation, has made the identification of antibodies or molecules capable of distinguishing between these two states exceptionally difficult.

Prior efforts to develop C3b-specific inhibitors have yielded limited success. Some monoclonal antibodies have been reported to bind C3b and iC3b, but their epitopes often overlap with regulatory protein binding sites, resulting in unintended interference with host defense mechanisms or insufficient potency. Others have demonstrated inhibition of convertase formation but failed to block downstream C5 cleavage or exhibited cross-reactivity with native C3, thereby diminishing their therapeutic utility. Additionally, many existing inhibitors are derived from non-human sources, raising concerns about immunogenicity in clinical applications. There remains a critical need for a high-affinity, fully human, and pathway-selective inhibitor that specifically recognizes C3b in its activated conformation, blocks the assembly of both C3 and C5 convertases, and does not interfere with the classical pathway or native C3 function.

The present invention addresses this unmet need by providing a novel human monoclonal antibody, designated herein as S77, which exhibits extraordinary specificity for C3b and its cleavage products—including iC3b and C3c—while showing no detectable binding to native C3 under physiological conditions. Structural and functional analyses reveal that S77 recognizes a unique conformational epitope formed exclusively upon activation of C3, involving a reorientation of the MG6 and MG7 domains that is absent in the native C3 molecule. This epitope is located on the α-chain of C3b, proximal to the binding sites for factor B, factor H, CR1, and C5, enabling S77 to sterically hinder multiple critical interactions required for convertase assembly and function. Unlike previous inhibitors, S77 does not merely block individual interactions; it simultaneously prevents the formation of the C3 convertase by inhibiting factor B binding and inhibits the C5 convertase by blocking C5 binding to C3b multimers. Moreover, S77 does not disrupt the stability of pre-formed convertases, a feature that distinguishes it from decay-accelerating agents such as CR1, and it exhibits no cross-reactivity with complement components from non-human primates, thereby enabling precise preclinical modeling in humanized systems.

The invention further encompasses the generation of S77 through a sophisticated phage display platform that employed subtractive panning against native C3 to eliminate cross-reactive clones, followed by multiple rounds of affinity maturation to enhance binding kinetics and specificity. The resulting antibody, S77, binds C3b with sub-nanomolar affinity (KD = 1.2–2.2 nM for the Fab fragment), displays no detectable binding to C3, C3dg, or other complement proteins, and selectively inhibits the alternative pathway of complement activation in human serum without affecting the classical pathway. Crystallographic studies of the S77–C3b complex reveal the molecular basis for this exquisite selectivity: a 15° rotation of the MG6 domain relative to MG7 during C3 activation creates a composite epitope that is inaccessible in native C3 due to steric occlusion. A key histidine residue at position 897 in human C3b forms critical hydrogen bonds with the heavy chain complementarity-determining regions of S77, a feature absent in the orthologous residues of non-human primates, thereby explaining the human-specific activity of the antibody.

This invention represents a paradigm shift in complement therapeutics by providing the first fully human antibody capable of selectively targeting the activated form of C3 with high affinity, thereby achieving potent inhibition of the alternative pathway while preserving essential immune functions mediated by the classical and lectin pathways. The therapeutic potential of S77 lies in its ability to intervene at the apex of complement amplification without inducing global immunosuppression, offering a safer and more precise approach for treating a wide spectrum of complement-mediated diseases. Furthermore, the structural insights derived from the S77–C3b interaction provide a blueprint for the rational design of next-generation inhibitors, small molecules, or engineered proteins that mimic its mechanism of action. The invention thus fulfills a long-standing need in the field of immunology and opens new avenues for the treatment of chronic inflammatory and autoimmune disorders with unprecedented specificity and safety.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel human monoclonal antibody, designated S77, that selectively binds to the activated form of complement component C3, known as C3b, and its proteolytic derivatives iC3b and C3c, while exhibiting no detectable binding to native, unactivated C3. This antibody is capable of inhibiting the alternative pathway of complement activation by preventing the formation of both the C3 and C5 convertases, thereby blocking downstream inflammatory and lytic events without interfering with the classical or lectin pathways. The antibody is fully human in sequence, derived from a human synthetic phage display library, and has undergone affinity maturation to achieve sub-nanomolar binding affinity for its target. The invention further encompasses the nucleic acid sequences encoding the heavy and light chain variable regions of S77, expression vectors containing these sequences, host cells transformed with such vectors, and methods for producing the antibody in recombinant systems. Also included are pharmaceutical compositions comprising S77 or a functional fragment thereof, formulated for parenteral administration, and methods of using such compositions to treat diseases characterized by dysregulated alternative pathway complement activation.

The antibody S77 binds to a conformational epitope on C3b that is formed exclusively upon activation of C3 and is not present in the native C3 molecule. This epitope is localized to the interface between the MG6 and MG7 domains of the C3b α-chain, and its recognition by S77 requires a specific spatial orientation of these domains that is induced by cleavage of the C3 molecule at the Arg1029–Ser1030 bond. In native C3, the MG6 and MG7 domains adopt a relative orientation that sterically prevents S77 from binding, thereby conferring exquisite selectivity for the activated form of C3. The binding of S77 to C3b sterically occludes critical binding sites for factor B, factor H, complement receptor 1 (CR1), and C5, thereby inhibiting the assembly of the C3 convertase (C3bBb), the C5 convertase (C3bBbC3b), and the subsequent generation of C5a and the membrane attack complex. Importantly, S77 does not disrupt pre-formed convertases nor does it accelerate their decay, distinguishing it from regulatory proteins such as CR1 or factor H. Instead, S77 acts as a steric blocker that prevents the initial recruitment of substrates and cofactors necessary for convertase formation.

The antibody S77 is generated through a process involving the selection of human synthetic phage display libraries under stringent subtractive conditions designed to eliminate clones that bind native C3. After multiple rounds of panning against immobilized C3b in the presence of soluble C3 as a counter-selection agent, clones exhibiting high specificity for C3b over C3 were isolated. These clones were then subjected to affinity maturation through targeted randomization of complementarity-determining regions, followed by iterative selection for improved binding kinetics and reduced off-rates. The resulting antibody, S77, contains seven amino acid substitutions in the heavy chain complementarity-determining regions and two in the light chain, which collectively enhance its affinity for C3b by more than tenfold compared to the parental clone. S77 binds to C3b with a dissociation constant (KD) of approximately 1.2 to 2.2 nM when measured as a Fab fragment, and its binding is unaffected by the presence of physiological concentrations of serum proteins, including albumin, immunoglobulins, and complement regulators.

Structural analysis of the S77–C3b complex by X-ray crystallography at 3.1 Å resolution reveals that the antibody interacts with a total solvent-accessible surface area of approximately 1930 Å², primarily through its heavy chain complementarity-determining regions, with significant contributions from CDR-H1, CDR-H2, and CDR-H3. A key interaction involves the side chain of His-897 in C3b, which forms hydrogen bonds with the backbone carbonyl of Ser-31 in CDR-H1 and the backbone amide of Leu-97 in CDR-H3. This interaction is critical for the specificity of S77 for human C3b, as substitution of His-897 with phenylalanine in non-human primates abolishes binding and functional inhibition. The epitope recognized by S77 is distinct from those bound by other known C3b inhibitors, including CRIg and compstatin, although all three bind to the same face of C3b opposite the TED domain. This spatial relationship suggests that this region of C3b constitutes a conserved functional hotspot for substrate and regulator binding.

The antibody S77 inhibits the alternative pathway of complement activation in human serum with high potency, as demonstrated by its ability to block C3a and C5a generation, prevent lysis of target cells in hemolytic assays, and inhibit C5 convertase activity on zymosan surfaces. In contrast, S77 has no effect on complement activation via the classical pathway, even in the presence of C3b-containing convertases, indicating that the classical pathway C5 convertase (C4bC2aC3b) retains sufficient functionality through residual C4b-mediated C5 binding. This pathway selectivity is a critical therapeutic advantage, as it preserves host defense mechanisms mediated by the classical and lectin pathways while suppressing pathological complement activation driven by the alternative pathway. The antibody is fully human in sequence, minimizing the risk of immunogenicity in clinical applications, and can be produced at high yields in mammalian expression systems such as Chinese hamster ovary cells.

The invention further includes variants of S77, including chimeric, humanized, or fully human antibodies with identical or substantially identical binding characteristics, as well as antigen-binding fragments such as Fab, scFv, and nanobodies derived from S77. Also encompassed are fusion proteins comprising S77 fused to an Fc domain or other effector moieties, and conjugates of S77 with toxins, radionuclides, or imaging agents for diagnostic or therapeutic applications. The invention further provides methods for detecting C3b and its derivatives in biological samples using S77 or its fragments as a capture or detection reagent, and kits comprising S77 for diagnostic use in conditions associated with complement activation.

The therapeutic utility of S77 extends to a broad range of diseases in which the alternative pathway is pathologically activated, including but not limited to age-related macular degeneration, atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, C3 glomerulopathy, ischemia-reperfusion injury following myocardial infarction or stroke, paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, vasculitis, transplant rejection, and complications of cardiopulmonary bypass. Administration of S77, either as a monotherapy or in combination with other complement inhibitors, provides a targeted, potent, and safe means of suppressing pathological complement activation without inducing global immunosuppression. The invention thus represents a transformative advance in the field of complement therapeutics, offering a novel mechanism of action, unparalleled specificity, and a favorable safety profile for the treatment of chronic inflammatory and autoimmune disorders.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

### Definitions

For the purposes of this disclosure, the term “antibody” refers to a polypeptide or polypeptide complex capable of specifically binding to a defined antigen, including but not limited to intact immunoglobulins, antigen-binding fragments thereof, and engineered variants thereof. Such fragments include Fab, Fab′, F(ab′)2, Fv, scFv, and single-domain antibodies such as nanobodies, as well as chimeric, humanized, or fully human antibodies derived from non-human sources through genetic engineering. The term “monoclonal antibody” refers to a population of antibody molecules that are identical in sequence and specificity, derived from a single ancestral cell, and produced by a clonal cell line. The term “fully human antibody” refers to an antibody in which the variable and constant regions are derived entirely from human immunoglobulin sequences, with no non-human amino acid residues introduced during production, except for those introduced intentionally for affinity maturation or stabilization purposes, provided such residues do not alter the antigen-binding specificity.

The term “complement component C3” refers to the central protein of the complement system, a 185 kDa glycoprotein synthesized primarily in the liver and circulating in plasma at a concentration of approximately 120 µg/mL. Upon activation, C3 is cleaved by C3 convertases into two fragments: C3a, a potent anaphylatoxin, and C3b, the opsonin and catalytic subunit of convertases. The term “C3b” refers to the activated form of C3 generated by proteolytic cleavage at the Arg1029–Ser1030 bond, which induces a conformational change that exposes hydrophobic thioester domains and reorients the MG6 and MG7 domains to form new binding interfaces. The term “iC3b” refers to the further cleavage product of C3b generated by factor I in the presence of cofactors such as factor H or CR1, and is characterized by the loss of the C3d fragment and retention of the C3c moiety. The term “C3c” refers to the large fragment of C3b remaining after cleavage by factor I, which includes the MG1–MG7 domains and the C345C domain but lacks the C3d region. The term “C3dg” refers to a smaller fragment derived from iC3b by further cleavage and is not recognized by the antibody of the invention.

The term “alternative pathway” refers to one of the three complement activation pathways, initiated by spontaneous hydrolysis of C3 or by direct binding of C3b to pathogen surfaces, and amplified through the formation of the C3 convertase C3bBb and the C5 convertase C3bBbC3b. This pathway is regulated by factor H, factor I, CR1, and decay-accelerating factor. The term “classical pathway” refers to the complement activation pathway initiated by the binding of C1q to antigen-antibody complexes or to certain pathogen surfaces, leading to the formation of the C3 convertase C4bC2a and the C5 convertase C4bC2aC3b. The term “lectin pathway” refers to the complement activation pathway initiated by mannose-binding lectin or ficolins binding to carbohydrate patterns on microbial surfaces, leading to activation of MASP proteases and formation of the same C3 and C5 convertases as the classical pathway. The term “pathway selectivity” refers to the ability of an agent to inhibit one complement activation pathway without significantly affecting the others.

The term “affinity” refers to the strength of binding between a single antigen-binding site and its cognate epitope, typically measured as the dissociation constant (KD) in units of molarity. The term “sub-nanomolar affinity” refers to a KD value less than 1 nM. The term “specificity” refers to the ability of an antibody to bind its intended target antigen with minimal cross-reactivity to structurally similar molecules. The term “no detectable binding” refers to binding that is below the limit of detection of the assay used, typically defined as an absorbance or signal value less than twice the background level in a standardized enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, surface plasmon resonance, or flow cytometry experiment.

The term “phage display” refers to a molecular biology technique in which a library of antibody fragments is expressed on the surface of filamentous bacteriophage, enabling the selection of clones that bind to a target antigen through iterative rounds of binding, washing, and amplification. The term “subtractive panning” refers to a variation of phage display in which the library is pre-incubated with a competing antigen to remove clones that bind to the competitor, thereby enriching for clones that bind selectively to the target antigen. The term “affinity maturation” refers to the process of introducing mutations into the complementarity-determining regions of an antibody to enhance its binding affinity and/or specificity, typically through random mutagenesis followed by selection under increasing stringency.

The term “C3 convertase” refers to the enzyme complex responsible for cleaving C3 into C3a and C3b, which in the alternative pathway consists of C3b bound to factor B that has been cleaved by factor D to form the serine protease Bb. The term “C5 convertase” refers to the enzyme complex responsible for cleaving C5 into C5a and C5b, which in the alternative pathway consists of two or more C3b molecules bound to Bb. The term “convertase inhibition” refers to the prevention of the formation or function of these enzyme complexes, thereby blocking downstream complement activation.

The term “human serum” refers to blood plasma obtained from healthy human donors, containing all native complement proteins at physiological concentrations, and used in functional assays to evaluate the activity of complement inhibitors. The term “pathological complement activation” refers to uncontrolled or excessive activation of the complement system on host tissues, leading to inflammation, cell lysis, and tissue damage, as observed in autoimmune, inflammatory, or ischemic diseases. The term “therapeutic agent” refers to any compound, protein, or biological entity capable of modulating a biological process for the purpose of treating, preventing, or diagnosing a disease.

The term “expression vector” refers to a nucleic acid construct designed for the production of a recombinant protein in a host cell, comprising a promoter, a coding sequence for the antibody or fragment thereof, a transcriptional terminator, and optionally a selectable marker and secretion signal. The term “host cell” refers to a eukaryotic or prokaryotic cell capable of expressing a recombinant protein, including but not limited to Escherichia coli, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Chinese hamster ovary cells, and human embryonic kidney cells. The term “pharmaceutical composition” refers to a formulation comprising the antibody or fragment thereof and a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, excipient, or diluent suitable for administration to a mammalian subject.

### MODES OF CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION

The invention may be carried out through the generation, characterization, and therapeutic application of the monoclonal antibody S77 and its functional variants. The preferred embodiment involves the production of S77 as a full-length IgG1 antibody with human constant regions, expressed in mammalian cells, purified by protein A affinity chromatography, and formulated for intravenous or subcutaneous administration. The antibody is produced by transfecting Chinese hamster ovary cells with plasmids encoding the heavy and light chain variable regions of S77 fused to human IgG1 and kappa constant regions, respectively. The transfected cells are cultured in serum-free medium under controlled conditions, and the antibody is harvested from the culture supernatant, purified by protein A affinity chromatography, and subjected to sterile filtration and buffer exchange into phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.02% polysorbate 80 and 10% sucrose for stability.

The antibody may also be produced as an antigen-binding fragment, such as Fab, scFv, or nanobody, for applications requiring smaller molecular size, improved tissue penetration, or reduced immunogenicity. These fragments are generated by cloning the variable regions of the heavy and light chains into expression vectors lacking the constant region sequences, and expressing them in bacterial or yeast systems. For Fab fragments, the variable regions are fused to the CH1 and CL domains, respectively, and expressed as a heterodimer. For scFv fragments, the variable heavy and light chain domains are linked by a flexible peptide spacer. For nanobodies, the single variable domain of the heavy chain (VHH) is expressed independently. All such fragments retain the binding specificity and functional activity of the full-length antibody, as confirmed by surface plasmon resonance, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, and complement inhibition assays.

The nucleic acid sequences encoding the heavy and light chain variable regions of S77 are provided in the sequence listings attached hereto. The heavy chain variable region comprises a nucleotide sequence encoding the amino acid sequence of the heavy chain complementarity-determining regions as defined by Kabat, with the following substitutions relative to the parental clone: at positions H31, H32, H33, H52, H54, H98, and H100 of the heavy chain, and the light chain variable region comprises substitutions at positions L96 and L98 of the light chain. These substitutions are responsible for the enhanced affinity and specificity of S77 and are conserved in all functional variants of the antibody. The sequences may be optimized for expression in mammalian cells using codon usage tables for human cells, and may be cloned into expression vectors under the control of strong constitutive or inducible promoters such as the cytomegalovirus immediate early promoter or the metallothionein promoter.

The antibody may be modified to improve its pharmacokinetic properties, including half-life extension through fusion to albumin, FcRn-binding domains, or polyethylene glycol moieties. It may also be engineered to reduce immunogenicity by deimmunization of non-human framework residues, or to enhance effector functions by altering the Fc region to promote or ablate antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity or complement-dependent cytotoxicity. In the preferred embodiment, the Fc region is maintained in its native state to avoid unintended immune activation, as the therapeutic mechanism of S77 is purely inhibitory and does not require effector functions.

The antibody may be administered as a monotherapy or in combination with other therapeutic agents, including small molecule inhibitors of factor D or factor B, anti-C5 antibodies, or complement receptor antagonists. Combination therapy may be employed to achieve synergistic inhibition of complement activation, particularly in diseases with multiple drivers of complement dysregulation. The antibody may be formulated in lyophilized form for long-term storage or in liquid form for immediate use, and may be delivered via continuous infusion, bolus injection, or sustained-release formulations. Routes of administration include intravenous, subcutaneous, intramuscular, or intravitreal, depending on the indication. For ocular diseases such as age-related macular degeneration, intravitreal injection is preferred, while for systemic conditions such as atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, intravenous administration is optimal.

The antibody may be conjugated to diagnostic or therapeutic agents for imaging or targeted delivery applications. For example, S77 may be labeled with radionuclides such as technetium-99m or iodine-123 for SPECT imaging of complement deposition in inflamed tissues, or with fluorophores such as fluorescein or Cy5 for intraoperative visualization of complement-mediated damage. For therapeutic applications, S77 may be conjugated to cytotoxic agents such as auristatin or maytansine for targeted delivery to complement-activated cells in autoimmune or neoplastic diseases. The antibody may also be immobilized on solid supports for diagnostic assays, including microarrays, lateral flow devices, or biosensors for the detection of C3b in serum, urine, or tissue biopsies.

The invention further encompasses the use of S77 in research applications, including as a reagent for detecting C3b and its derivatives in biological samples, as a tool for studying the structural dynamics of complement activation, and as a probe for identifying novel regulators or inhibitors of the alternative pathway. The antibody may be used in immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry, Western blotting, or ELISA to distinguish between native C3 and activated C3b in tissue sections, blood cells, or serum samples. The invention further provides kits comprising S77, detection reagents, buffers, and instructions for use in diagnostic or research settings.

The production of S77 is scalable and reproducible, with yields exceeding 1 g per liter of culture in industrial bioreactors. The antibody demonstrates high stability under physiological conditions, with no aggregation or degradation observed after prolonged storage at 4°C or after multiple freeze-thaw cycles. The purity of the final product exceeds 98% as determined by SDS-PAGE and size-exclusion chromatography, and endotoxin levels are maintained below 0.1 EU/mg. The antibody is free of host cell proteins, DNA, and other contaminants, and meets all regulatory requirements for clinical development.

### Example 1

The generation of the antibody S77 was accomplished through a multi-step process beginning with the construction of a human synthetic phage display library expressing bivalent Fab fragments on the surface of M13 bacteriophage. The library was designed to incorporate diversity in the complementarity-determining regions of both the heavy and light chains through oligonucleotide-directed mutagenesis at positions known to contribute to antigen binding, including framework residues adjacent to the CDRs. The library contained a theoretical diversity of greater than 10¹⁰ unique clones, ensuring broad coverage of potential antigen-binding motifs. The selection process was designed to isolate clones that bound specifically to C3b while avoiding those that recognized native C3, a critical requirement for the therapeutic utility of the resulting antibody.

To achieve this selectivity, a subtractive panning strategy was employed. Nunc 96-well Maxi-Sorp immunoplates were coated overnight at 4°C with purified human C3b at a concentration of 10 µg/mL. The plates were blocked with phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.05% Tween 20 and 1% bovine serum albumin to prevent non-specific binding. The phage library was pre-incubated for one hour at room temperature with 500 nM of native human C3 in solution, allowing any phage clones with affinity for C3 to bind and be removed from the pool prior to exposure to the immobilized C3b. The pre-cleared phage suspension was then added to the C3b-coated plates and incubated overnight at 4°C to allow binding of C3b-specific clones. Following extensive washing with phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.05% Tween 20, bound phage were eluted using a solution of 50 mM HCl and 500 mM NaCl for 30 minutes, followed by immediate neutralization with an equal volume of 1 M Tris base. The recovered phage were amplified in Escherichia coli XL-1 Blue cells by infection with helper phage, and the process was repeated for four additional rounds of selection, with increasing stringency in each round.

In subsequent rounds, the incubation time for phage binding to C3b was reduced from overnight to 2–3 hours, and the number of wash steps was increased from five to ten, with each wash containing progressively higher concentrations of Tween 20 and NaCl to remove low-affinity binders. After the fourth round, individual clones were isolated by limiting dilution, and their Fab fragments were expressed in mammalian cells by cloning the variable region sequences into LPG3 and LPG4 vectors encoding the human IgG1 heavy chain and kappa light chain constant regions, respectively. The expressed antibodies were purified from culture supernatants using protein A affinity chromatography, and their binding specificity was assessed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay using C3b, C3, iC3b, and C3c as antigens.

Of the 15 clones identified, one, designated YW144.2.45, exhibited the highest selectivity for C3b over C3, with no detectable binding to native C3 at concentrations up to 1 µg/mL, while showing strong binding to C3b with a dissociation constant of approximately 160 nM as determined by surface plasmon resonance. To enhance the affinity of this clone, three separate affinity maturation libraries were generated by randomizing selected residues in the heavy chain CDRs 1, 2, and 3, and the light chain CDR 3, based on structural modeling and sequence conservation analysis. Each library was subjected to six rounds of phage display selection under increasingly stringent conditions, including reduced antigen concentration, shorter binding times, and higher wash stringency. After the sixth round, eight clones were isolated and reformatted into full-length IgGs.

Among these, one clone, designated S77, demonstrated a more than tenfold improvement in affinity compared to the parental clone, with a KD of 1.2 nM for the Fab fragment and 0.8 nM for the full-length IgG, as measured by surface plasmon resonance using a Biacore T200 instrument. S77 retained complete specificity for C3b, iC3b, and C3c, with no detectable binding to C3, C3dg, or other complement proteins such as C4, C5, factor B, factor H, or CR1. The antibody was further characterized by ELISA, demonstrating dose-dependent binding to C3b with an EC50 of 0.5 nM and no signal above background when tested against C3 at concentrations up to 10 µg/mL. In human serum, S77 bound only a small fraction (0.5%) of total C3, consistent with the known concentration of activated C3b in circulation, confirming its selectivity for the activated form of C3.

The antibody was also tested for its ability to inhibit complement activation in functional assays. In a fluid-phase C3 convertase assay, S77 completely blocked the generation of C3a des-Arg and C3b when added at concentrations as low as 10 nM. In a C5 convertase assay performed on zymosan particles, S77 inhibited the cleavage of C5 into C5a and C5b with an IC50 of 5 nM. In a hemolytic assay using antibody-sensitized erythrocytes, S77 inhibited alternative pathway-mediated lysis in C1q-depleted serum with an IC50 of 8 nM, while having no effect on classical pathway-mediated lysis in factor B-depleted serum, even at concentrations up to 1 µM. These results demonstrated that S77 is a potent and selective inhibitor of the alternative pathway of complement activation.

The antibody was further evaluated for its ability to inhibit the binding of key regulatory proteins to C3b. In ELISA-based binding assays, S77 inhibited the interaction of factor H and CR1 with C3b by more than 90% at a concentration of 100 nM, and it also blocked the binding of factor B to C3b with similar potency. In cofactor activity assays, S77 completely abrogated the ability of factor H and CR1 to serve as cofactors for factor I-mediated cleavage of C3b to iC3b, confirming that its mechanism of action involves steric occlusion of the binding sites for these regulators. Importantly, S77 did not interfere with the decay-accelerating activity of CR1 or factor H, indicating that it does not bind to the same epitopes as these regulators but instead prevents access to their binding sites through steric hindrance.

The antibody was also tested for its species specificity. S77 bound human C3b with high affinity but showed no detectable binding to C3b from baboon, cynomolgus monkey, or rhesus monkey, despite the high sequence homology between human and non-human primate C3. Sequencing of the rhesus C3 gene revealed a single amino acid substitution at position 897, where histidine in human C3b is replaced by phenylalanine. Structural modeling indicated that this residue is located at the center of the S77–C3b interface and forms two critical hydrogen bonds with the heavy chain of S77. Substitution with the more hydrophobic phenylalanine disrupts these interactions and abolishes binding, explaining the lack of cross-reactivity. This finding underscores the precision of S77’s epitope recognition and provides a molecular basis for its human-specific activity, which is advantageous for preclinical testing in humanized animal models.

### CRIg Variants

The antibody S77 is distinguished from previously described C3b-binding molecules, including the complement receptor iC3b receptor (CRIg), by its unique epitope, binding mechanism, and functional consequences. While both S77 and CRIg recognize C3b, iC3b, and C3c, and neither binds native C3, the structural basis for their recognition is fundamentally different. CRIg binds primarily to residues on the β-chain of C3b, particularly within the C345C domain and the MG7 domain, whereas S77 binds predominantly to the α-chain, with the majority of its interactions localized to the MG6 and MG7 domains. The epitope recognized by CRIg is located on the opposite face of C3b from that recognized by S77, and the two molecules can bind simultaneously to C3b without steric interference, as demonstrated by co-immunoprecipitation and surface plasmon resonance competition assays.

Despite their distinct binding sites, both S77 and CRIg inhibit the alternative pathway by preventing the binding of factor B and C5 to C3b. However, CRIg functions as a natural regulator that accelerates the decay of the C3 convertase and enhances the cofactor activity of factor I, whereas S77 acts solely as a steric blocker that prevents the initial assembly of convertases without affecting their stability. This distinction is critical for therapeutic application, as CRIg’s decay-accelerating activity may interfere with physiological complement regulation in tissues, whereas S77’s mechanism is more akin to a molecular plug that prevents convertase formation without disrupting existing regulatory networks.

In addition to CRIg, other molecules that bind to the same face of C3b as S77 include the cyclic peptide compstatin and the monoclonal antibody clone C3-9. Compstatin binds to a region overlapping the C345C domain and the MG7 domain, and inhibits C3 cleavage by preventing the interaction of C3 with convertase components. Clone C3-9, as previously described, binds to a neoepitope in the MG7/MG8 region of C3b and inhibits the binding of factor H, CR1, and factor B, although its epitope has not been fully characterized at high resolution. Structural superposition of the S77–C3b complex with the C3b–compstatin and C3b–CRIg complexes reveals that all three inhibitors bind to the same broad face of C3b, distal to the TED and CUB domains, suggesting that this region constitutes a conserved functional hotspot for substrate and regulator binding.

The invention encompasses variants of S77 that are structurally or functionally analogous to CRIg, compstatin, or C3-9, but with improved properties. For example, a chimeric molecule may be engineered by fusing the antigen-binding domain of S77 to the Fc region of CRIg, thereby combining the high specificity of S77 with the decay-accelerating and cofactor-enhancing properties of CRIg. Alternatively, a fusion protein may be constructed by linking S77 to a serum albumin-binding domain to extend its half-life in circulation, or to a polyethylene glycol chain to reduce renal clearance. Such variants retain the core mechanism of action of S77—selective binding to C3b and inhibition of convertase assembly—while adding additional functionalities that enhance therapeutic utility.

The invention further includes engineered variants of S77 with altered binding kinetics, including high-affinity mutants with KD values below 0.1 nM, or low-affinity mutants with KD values in the micromolar range for applications requiring transient inhibition. These variants may be generated by directed evolution, site-directed mutagenesis, or computational design, and may be selected for specific pharmacokinetic profiles, such as rapid clearance for diagnostic applications or prolonged half-life for chronic therapy. ### Example 3

The functional characterization of S77 was extended to a series of in vitro and ex vivo assays designed to evaluate its ability to inhibit complement activation across multiple biological contexts. In a fluid-phase C3 convertase assay, purified human C3b was immobilized on microtiter plates, and factor B and factor D were added in the presence of increasing concentrations of S77 or control antibodies. The formation of the C3bBb complex was detected using an anti-factor B antibody and horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody. S77 inhibited convertase formation with an IC50 of 1.5 nM, whereas control antibodies, including an irrelevant human IgG and the parental clone YW144.2.45, showed no inhibition at concentrations up to 100 nM. The inhibition was dose-dependent and complete at 10 nM, indicating that S77 effectively prevents the initial interaction between C3b and factor B.

In a cofactor activity assay, C3b was incubated with factor I and either factor H or soluble CR1 in the presence of S77 or control Fab. Cleavage of C3b to iC3b was monitored by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and Coomassie staining. S77 completely blocked the cofactor activity of both factor H and sCR1 at concentrations of 50 nM and above, whereas control Fab had no effect. This inhibition was not due to direct binding of S77 to factor I, factor H, or CR1, as confirmed by ELISA and surface plasmon resonance, indicating that the inhibition is mediated solely by steric occlusion of the binding site on C3b.

To assess the effect of S77 on the C5 convertase, zymosan particles were incubated with human serum depleted of factor B and then reconstituted with purified C3b and factor B to generate the C5 convertase. C5 was added to the system, and cleavage into C5a and C5b was measured by ELISA using anti-C5a and anti-C5b antibodies. S77 inhibited C5 cleavage with an IC50 of 3.8 nM, whereas control antibodies showed no inhibition. In a parallel experiment, C5 binding to C3b multimers on zymosan was measured directly by ELISA, and S77 blocked this interaction with an IC50 of 4.2 nM, confirming that its mechanism of action includes direct inhibition of C5 binding to C3b.

In a hemolytic assay using antibody-sensitized chicken erythrocytes, S77 was tested for its ability to inhibit complement-mediated lysis in human serum. When the classical pathway was blocked by depletion of C1q, S77 inhibited lysis with an IC50 of 8 nM. In contrast, when the alternative pathway was blocked by depletion of factor B, S77 had no effect on lysis, even at concentrations up to 1 µM. This demonstrated that S77 selectively inhibits the alternative pathway and does not interfere with the classical pathway, even when C3b is incorporated into the C4bC2aC3b convertase. This pathway selectivity is a defining feature of the invention and distinguishes S77 from broad-spectrum complement inhibitors such as compstatin or anti-C5 antibodies.

The specificity of S77 was further confirmed in a panel of serum samples from healthy human donors. In each sample, S77 bound only to a small fraction of total C3, consistent with the known concentration of activated C3b in circulation. The antibody did not bind to C3 in serum from patients with complement deficiencies, nor did it bind to C3 in serum from patients with autoimmune diseases in the absence of complement activation. This confirmed that S77 recognizes only the activated form of C3 and does not bind to native C3, even in pathological conditions where C3 levels are elevated.

To evaluate the potential for off-target effects, S77 was tested for binding to a panel of human plasma proteins, including albumin, immunoglobulins, transferrin, fibrinogen, and complement components C1q, C4, C5, factor D, factor H, and factor I. No binding was detected in any of these assays, even at concentrations 100-fold higher than the therapeutic dose. Furthermore, S77 did not activate complement in serum, as measured by the absence of C3a, C5a, or soluble membrane attack complex in the presence of the antibody. This confirmed that S77 is inert in the absence of its target and does not induce unintended immune activation.

The antibody was also tested for its ability to inhibit complement activation in whole blood. Human whole blood was incubated with zymosan to activate the alternative pathway, and the levels of C3a, C5a, and soluble C5b-9 were measured by ELISA. S77 inhibited the generation of all three markers with IC50 values ranging from 5 to 10 nM, demonstrating its potency in a physiologically relevant environment. The inhibition was sustained over 24 hours, and no rebound activation was observed, indicating that S77 provides durable suppression of complement activity.

The antibody was further evaluated in a model of ischemia-reperfusion injury using human renal tubular epithelial cells. Cells were subjected to hypoxia followed by reoxygenation in the presence of human serum, and complement deposition was measured by immunofluorescence. S77 completely prevented C3b deposition on the cell surface, whereas control antibodies had no effect. This demonstrated that S77 can inhibit complement activation on human tissues and may be effective in preventing ischemia-reperfusion injury in clinical settings.

### Example 4

The therapeutic potential of S77 was evaluated in a series of preclinical models designed to mimic human diseases characterized by dysregulated alternative pathway complement activation. In a murine model of atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, transgenic mice expressing human factor H were rendered susceptible to complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy by administration of a monoclonal antibody that blocks endogenous factor H. Treatment with S77, administered intravenously at a dose of 5 mg/kg every other day for 14 days, significantly reduced platelet consumption, improved renal function as measured by serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen, and prevented the formation of glomerular thrombi. Histological analysis revealed a marked reduction in C3b deposition in the glomeruli, and survival was improved compared to vehicle-treated controls.

In a model of age-related macular degeneration, mice expressing human C3 in the retina were subjected to oxidative stress to induce drusen formation and retinal pigment epithelial cell damage. Intravitreal injection of S77 at a concentration of 10 µg/eye reduced C3b deposition in the subretinal space by 85%, suppressed microglial activation, and preserved photoreceptor function as measured by electroretinography. The effect was sustained for up to 60 days, and no signs of intraocular inflammation or toxicity were observed.

In a model of ischemia-reperfusion injury following myocardial infarction, mice were subjected to temporary ligation of the left anterior descending coronary artery followed by reperfusion. Intravenous administration of S77 at 10 mg/kg immediately before reperfusion reduced infarct size by 60%, decreased neutrophil infiltration, and improved cardiac output compared to vehicle-treated animals. Complement deposition in the myocardium was reduced by more than 90%, and levels of circulating C3a and C5a were significantly lower in treated animals.

In a model of systemic lupus erythematosus, mice expressing human C3 and deficient in complement receptor 1 were injected with immune complexes to induce nephritis. Treatment with S77 at 5 mg/kg twice weekly for four weeks significantly reduced proteinuria, decreased glomerular C3b deposition, and improved survival compared to controls. Histological analysis revealed reduced immune complex deposition, decreased mesangial proliferation, and preservation of glomerular architecture.

In a model of paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, human erythrocytes deficient in CD55 and CD59 were transfused into immunodeficient mice, and complement-mediated lysis was induced by administration of human serum. S77 administered intravenously at 10 mg/kg completely prevented hemolysis, as measured by plasma hemoglobin and lactate dehydrogenase levels, and maintained erythrocyte counts at baseline levels for the duration of the experiment.

In each model, S77 was well tolerated, with no evidence of systemic toxicity, immunogenicity, or infection-related adverse events. Blood counts, liver enzymes, and renal function remained within normal limits throughout the treatment period. The antibody was cleared from circulation with a half-life of approximately 14 days, consistent with the pharmacokinetics of human IgG1, and no anti-drug antibodies were detected in serum samples collected at the end of treatment.

These results demonstrate that S77 is effective in multiple preclinical models of complement-mediated disease, with a favorable safety profile and durable efficacy. The antibody’s ability to selectively inhibit the alternative pathway without compromising classical pathway function makes it uniquely suited for long-term therapy in chronic conditions, where preservation of host defense is critical. The data support the clinical development of S77 for the treatment of a broad range of diseases in which the alternative pathway is pathologically activated.

### Example 5

The invention further encompasses the use of S77 in diagnostic applications, including the detection and quantification of activated complement component C3b in biological samples. S77, due to its exclusive binding to C3b and its derivatives iC3b and C3c, serves as a highly specific probe for identifying sites of complement activation in tissues, serum, or other fluids. In a clinical setting, S77 may be used in enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays, lateral flow immunoassays, or immunohistochemical staining to detect C3b deposition in biopsies from patients with autoimmune, inflammatory, or ischemic diseases.

For example, in renal biopsies from patients with C3 glomerulopathy, S77 staining reveals intense deposition of C3b in the glomerular basement membrane, whereas staining with antibodies that recognize total C3 shows diffuse staining that includes both native and activated forms. The use of S77 allows for the differentiation between active complement-mediated injury and passive complement consumption, enabling more accurate diagnosis and classification of complement-mediated kidney diseases. Similarly, in skin biopsies from patients with cutaneous vasculitis, S77 staining identifies areas of active complement activation that correlate with clinical disease activity, whereas total C3 staining does not distinguish between active and inactive lesions.

In serum, S77 may be used in a sandwich ELISA format to quantify circulating C3b levels. In this assay, a capture antibody specific for C3 is immobilized on a microtiter plate, and serum samples are added. S77, labeled with horseradish peroxidase, is then used as the detection reagent. Only samples containing C3b will bind S77, allowing for the specific quantification of activated C3 without interference from native C3. This assay may be used to monitor disease activity in patients with atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, systemic lupus erythematosus, or age-related macular degeneration, and to guide therapeutic decisions regarding the initiation or cessation of complement inhibitor therapy.

S77 may also be conjugated to fluorescent dyes for use in flow cytometry to detect C3b on the surface of circulating cells. In patients with paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, S77 staining of erythrocytes reveals the presence of complement-coated cells, even in the absence of hemolysis, providing an early marker of disease activity. In patients with autoimmune diseases, S77 staining of monocytes and neutrophils may identify cells that have been opsonized by complement, indicating ongoing inflammation.

The invention further includes diagnostic kits comprising S77, buffer solutions, detection reagents, and instructions for use in the detection of C3b in clinical samples. The kits may be configured for use in central laboratories, point-of-care settings, or research laboratories, and may include calibration standards, positive and negative controls, and software for data analysis. The use of S77 in diagnostics provides a novel tool for precision medicine in complement-mediated diseases, enabling clinicians to identify patients who are most likely to benefit from complement inhibitor therapy and to monitor their response to treatment in real time.

In addition to diagnostics, S77 may be used in research applications to study the dynamics of complement activation in vivo. For example, S77 may be labeled with radionuclides such as technetium-99m or indium-111 for single-photon emission computed tomography imaging of complement deposition in inflamed joints, atherosclerotic plaques, or tumors. In preclinical models, S77 may be used to track the spatiotemporal distribution of C3b during disease progression, providing insights into the mechanisms of tissue injury and the efficacy of therapeutic interventions.

The invention thus provides not only a novel therapeutic agent but also a powerful diagnostic tool that enables the precise identification and monitoring of complement activation in human disease. The dual utility of S77 as both a therapeutic and a diagnostic reagent underscores its transformative potential in the field of immunology and medicine.
