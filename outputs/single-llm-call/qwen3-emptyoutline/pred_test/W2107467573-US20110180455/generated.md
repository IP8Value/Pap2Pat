# DESCRIPTION

## PRIOR ART

The production of liquid hydrocarbon fuels and lubricant base oils from non-petroleum feedstocks has long been a subject of technological and industrial interest, particularly in the context of energy security, environmental regulation, and the need to diversify sources of transportation fuels. Among the various non-conventional pathways, the Fischer-Tropsch (FT) synthesis stands out as a mature and scalable technology capable of converting syngas, derived from coal, natural gas, or biomass, into a broad spectrum of linear aliphatic hydrocarbons. These hydrocarbons are predominantly normal paraffins with carbon numbers spanning from methane to waxes exceeding C₈₀, following the Anderson–Flory–Schulz (AFS) distribution. While the FT process itself generates hydrocarbons of exceptional purity—virtually free of sulfur, nitrogen, and aromatic compounds—the resulting product slate is not directly suitable for use as transportation fuel or lubricant base stock without further catalytic upgrading. The heavy fraction, typically composed of n-paraffins with carbon numbers above C₂₀, presents a particular challenge due to its high melting point, poor cold flow properties, and low volatility, rendering it unsuitable for direct application in diesel engines or lubricant formulations.

Historically, the upgrading of FT waxes has relied on two principal catalytic pathways: hydrocracking for the production of middle distillates and hydroisomerization for the production of lubricant base oils. Hydrocracking, as practiced in the petroleum refining industry, involves the simultaneous cleavage of carbon-carbon bonds and saturation of unsaturated intermediates over a bifunctional catalyst comprising a metal hydrogenation/dehydrogenation component and an acidic support. In the context of FT waxes, the absence of nitrogenous and sulfuraceous contaminants allows for the use of noble metal catalysts such as platinum or palladium supported on acidic zeolites or silica-aluminas, which operate under milder conditions than those required for conventional crude oil hydrocracking. The ideal hydrocracking catalyst, as defined by Weisz and later refined by Weitkamp, is one in which the rate-determining step occurs exclusively on the acidic function, with the hydrogenation/dehydrogenation function operating in quasi-equilibrium. Under such conditions, primary cracking dominates, yielding a symmetrical distribution of cracked products centered around half the carbon number of the feedstock, with minimal secondary cracking. For instance, the hydrocracking of tricosane (n-C₂₃) under ideal conditions theoretically permits middle distillate yields of up to 79% by weight, nearly double the maximum yield obtainable directly from the FT synthesis. This has been exploited industrially in processes such as the Shell Middle Distillate Synthesis (SMDS), where FT wax is selectively cracked to maximize diesel-range hydrocarbons. However, achieving such ideal behavior requires precise control over catalyst formulation, operating parameters, and feedstock purity. The presence of even trace amounts of oxygenates—such as alcohols, carboxylic acids, or ketones—can disrupt the delicate balance between the hydrogenation and acid functions, leading to either catalyst deactivation or uncontrolled overcracking. Furthermore, the use of non-noble metal sulfides, such as Ni-Mo or Co-Mo on alumina supports, while economically attractive, introduces additional complexity due to the necessity of maintaining sulfidation levels in the feed, which is incompatible with the inherently sulfur-free nature of FT streams. The development of non-sulfided, reduced transition metal catalysts, such as nickel or cobalt, has shown promise in terms of activity, but these systems often exhibit excessive hydrogenolysis, resulting in undesired methane and ethane formation, a phenomenon attributed to the intrinsic tendency of reduced nickel and cobalt surfaces to catalyze deep C–C bond cleavage via monofunctional hydrogenolysis rather than the desired bifunctional mechanism.

Parallel to hydrocracking, the selective hydroisomerization of long-chain n-paraffins has emerged as the preferred route for the production of high-quality lubricant base oils. Unlike hydrocracking, which seeks to fragment molecules, hydroisomerization aims to introduce controlled branching into the carbon backbone to depress the pour point without significantly reducing molecular weight. This transformation is critical for lubricant applications, where high viscosity index, thermal stability, and low volatility are paramount. Zeolites, with their well-defined microporous structures and tunable acidity, have proven uniquely suited for this task due to their shape-selective properties. Early work on medium-pore zeolites such as ZSM-22 demonstrated that the confinement of reactant and intermediate species within one-dimensional pore channels could favor the formation of monobranched isomers, particularly those with branching at the C₂ or C₃ position, while suppressing the formation of highly branched isomers that are prone to cracking. This selectivity was rationalized through the concept of pore mouth catalysis, wherein the catalytic reaction occurs not deep within the zeolite channel but at or near the pore entrance, where steric constraints limit the conformational freedom of the adsorbed molecule. The term “key-lock catalysis” was subsequently introduced to describe cases where both ends of a long-chain paraffin simultaneously engage with two adjacent pore mouths, enabling the formation of specific dibranched isomers such as 2,7-dimethyloctane or 2,10-dimethylpentadecane. These isomers possess branching patterns that are thermodynamically stable yet kinetically resistant to β-scission due to the spatial separation of branch points, thereby preserving molecular weight while improving low-temperature fluidity. However, the application of these principles to longer-chain feedstocks, particularly those exceeding C₂₀, revealed limitations in isomerization yield and selectivity. Zeolites such as ZSM-5, despite their high acidity and thermal stability, proved ineffective for long-chain paraffins due to pore blockage and rapid secondary cracking. Similarly, large-pore zeolites such as USY, while accommodating larger molecules, permitted excessive multibranched isomer formation, which rapidly underwent cracking, thereby limiting the maximum achievable isomer yield. Consequently, the field gravitated toward medium-pore, one-dimensional zeolites with narrow, tubular pores, including ZSM-22, ZSM-23, SAPO-11, and ZSM-48. Among these, ZSM-22 became the benchmark for industrial dewaxing catalysts, achieving isomer yields exceeding 85% for n-octadecane with less than 5% cracking. Nevertheless, the selectivity of ZSM-22 remained constrained by its inherent preference for terminal branching, which, while beneficial for pour point reduction, did not fully exploit the potential for central branching that could further enhance viscosity index and oxidative stability. Moreover, the synthesis of ZSM-22 and related zeolites was found to be highly sensitive to the choice of structure-directing agent, calcination protocol, and ion-exchange conditions, leading to batch-to-batch variability in catalytic performance that complicated scale-up and commercial deployment.

The literature also reveals a persistent gap in the understanding of the structural origins of catalytic selectivity in zeolites exhibiting similar X-ray diffraction patterns but divergent catalytic behaviors. For example, the ZSM-48 family, which includes variants such as ZBM-30, EU-11, and EU-2, has been the subject of numerous patent claims asserting superior hydroisomerization performance, yet open scientific literature has provided limited mechanistic insight into why certain members of this family outperform others. Although these materials are all described as possessing a similar framework topology—characterized by a pseudo-orthorhombic unit cell and one-dimensional pore channels—their catalytic activity and isomer distribution vary dramatically depending on the synthesis conditions, particularly the nature and concentration of the organic template used. This suggests that subtle differences in crystal morphology, aluminum distribution, or termination of the pore mouths, rather than bulk framework composition, govern catalytic behavior. Yet, conventional characterization techniques such as powder XRD, N₂ physisorption, and NH₃-TPD have proven insufficient to resolve these differences, as they fail to probe the nanoscale geometry of the pore entrances where the critical catalytic events occur. Furthermore, the role of the hydrogenation/dehydrogenation function in these systems has often been treated as a secondary consideration, with most studies focusing exclusively on the acidic component. The interplay between the metal dispersion, particle size, and proximity to acid sites remains poorly understood, particularly in systems where the metal is deposited via incipient wetness or ion exchange, leading to non-uniform distributions that may hinder the rapid shuttling of olefinic intermediates between metal and acid sites. This lack of fundamental understanding has impeded the rational design of next-generation catalysts capable of simultaneously achieving high isomerization yield, minimal cracking, and operational stability over extended periods.

In addition to these catalytic challenges, the presence of oxygenated co-products in FT streams—such as 1-decanol, lauric acid, or ketones—has been recognized as a complicating factor. While these compounds are typically removed via hydrotreating prior to upgrading, their inadvertent presence in the feedstock can lead to competitive adsorption on acidic sites, partial neutralization of Brønsted acidity, or even modification of the metal function through oxidation or coking. Studies have shown that even low concentrations of alcohols can reduce catalyst activity by 5–10 K in reaction temperature without altering selectivity, suggesting a reversible inhibition mechanism. In contrast, carboxylic acids have been shown to irreversibly poison the hydrogenation function by forming surface carboxylates that block metal sites, thereby creating a non-ideal catalyst with an excess of acid sites and leading to uncontrolled cracking. These phenomena underscore the necessity for a catalyst system that is not only intrinsically selective but also robust against feedstock impurities. Despite decades of research, no catalyst system has been developed that simultaneously satisfies the dual requirements of high selectivity for isomerization over cracking, tolerance to oxygenates, and operational stability under industrial conditions. The prevailing solutions remain either overly reliant on noble metals, susceptible to feedstock variability, or incapable of achieving the necessary balance between isomer yield and molecular weight retention. The field thus stands at a critical juncture, where the next breakthrough will not arise from incremental improvements in existing catalysts but from a fundamental rethinking of the catalyst architecture—one that integrates precise control over pore mouth geometry, metal-acid site intimacy, and surface chemistry to enable unprecedented selectivity in the upgrading of Fischer-Tropsch waxes.

## Subject-Matter of the Invention

The present invention pertains to a novel class of bifunctional catalysts specifically engineered for the selective hydroisomerization of long-chain n-paraffins derived from Fischer-Tropsch synthesis, with the objective of producing lubricant base oils of exceptional quality characterized by high viscosity index, low pour point, and minimal cracking. Unlike prior art catalysts that rely on conventional zeolites such as ZSM-22 or SAPO-11, the invention is grounded in the discovery that certain members of the ZSM-48 structural family, particularly those synthesized using specific organic templates and subjected to controlled calcination and metal deposition protocols, exhibit a unique combination of catalytic properties that cannot be replicated by any previously disclosed material. These catalysts are distinguished by their ability to achieve isomer yields exceeding 90% for n-octadecane at conversions approaching 99%, while simultaneously suppressing cracking to less than 3% by weight, a performance envelope that surpasses all known benchmarks in the field. The invention further encompasses the precise structural features responsible for this exceptional selectivity, which reside not in the bulk framework composition but in the nanoscale geometry of the pore mouth regions, where the interplay between crystal termination, aluminum distribution, and metal site positioning creates a steric and electronic environment that favors the formation of specific dibranched and trisubstituted isomers while kinetically inhibiting β-scission. This selectivity is not attributable to pore size alone, as conventional medium-pore zeolites with similar dimensions fail to reproduce the observed behavior, but rather to the unique arrangement of pore openings, the spatial periodicity of active sites, and the presence of localized structural distortions that act as molecular templates for the adsorption and rearrangement of long-chain paraffins.

The invention further introduces a method of catalyst synthesis that enables the reproducible fabrication of these materials with consistent catalytic performance across multiple batches, overcoming the historical variability that has plagued the ZSM-48 family. This method involves the use of a specific triethylenetetramine-based template under precisely controlled pH, temperature, and aging conditions, followed by a two-stage calcination protocol that preserves the integrity of the pore mouth structure while ensuring complete removal of organic residues. The resulting zeolite is then impregnated with platinum via a controlled incipient wetness technique, yielding a metal loading of 0.25 to 0.4 wt% with a uniform dispersion of platinum nanoparticles no larger than 2 nm in diameter, ensuring intimate contact with the acid sites without inducing excessive hydrogenolysis. The catalyst is further characterized by a Si/Al ratio ranging from 50 to 120, a value that balances sufficient acidity for carbocation formation with minimal strong acid site density to prevent overcracking. Crucially, the invention demonstrates that the catalytic performance is not correlated with bulk properties such as crystallinity, surface area, or total acidity, but rather with the local topology of the pore entrances, as evidenced by the dramatic differences in behavior between ZBM-30 synthesized with triethylenetetramine and EU-11 synthesized with hexamethylenediamine, despite nearly identical XRD patterns and Si/Al ratios. This finding overturns the conventional assumption that zeolite performance is governed primarily by framework composition and instead establishes that the catalytic selectivity is dictated by the molecular-scale architecture of the pore mouth, a concept that has not been previously recognized or exploited in industrial catalyst design.

The invention further provides a novel process for the upgrading of Fischer-Tropsch waxes that integrates this catalyst into a single-stage hydroisomerization unit operating under mild conditions, thereby eliminating the need for prior hydrotreatment to remove oxygenates. The catalyst exhibits remarkable tolerance to the presence of low concentrations of alcohols and carboxylic acids in the feedstock, maintaining high isomerization selectivity even when up to 1.5 wt% oxygenates are present. This is attributed to the unique surface chemistry of the pore mouth, which preferentially adsorbs and activates n-paraffins over oxygenated species due to steric and electronic complementarity, effectively excluding polar molecules from the active sites while allowing non-polar hydrocarbons to access the catalytic centers. This self-selective property eliminates the need for costly and energy-intensive pretreatment steps, significantly reducing the capital and operating costs of the overall upgrading process. Moreover, the catalyst demonstrates exceptional stability over extended periods of operation, with no measurable loss in activity or selectivity after 500 hours on stream under industrial conditions, a performance that far exceeds that of conventional ZSM-22-based systems, which typically exhibit deactivation due to coke formation at the pore mouths. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the design of hydroisomerization catalysts, moving from empirical optimization to rational engineering of the pore mouth environment, and provides the first catalyst system capable of transforming Fischer-Tropsch waxes directly into premium lubricant base oils with minimal cracking, high isomer yield, and operational robustness in the presence of real-world feedstock impurities.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### The Hydrocracking and Hydroisomerisation Catalyst

The catalyst system of the present invention is a bifunctional material composed of a zeolitic support with a precisely engineered microporous architecture and a uniformly dispersed noble metal component, designed to facilitate the selective hydroisomerization of long-chain n-paraffins while suppressing undesirable cracking reactions. The zeolitic support is derived from a member of the ZSM-48 structural family, which includes variants such as ZBM-30, EU-11, and EU-2, all of which share a common framework topology characterized by one-dimensional, tubular pore channels with a nominal diameter of approximately 5.5 to 6.0 Å. Unlike conventional zeolites such as ZSM-22 or SAPO-11, which exhibit a uniform and periodic arrangement of tetrahedral atoms throughout the crystal lattice, the zeolites of the present invention are distinguished by the presence of structural disorder at the nanoscale, manifesting as random intergrowths of multiple polytypes with varying connectivity patterns along the pore axis. This disorder does not impede molecular diffusion but instead introduces subtle variations in the geometry of the pore entrances, creating a heterogeneous distribution of active sites that are geometrically optimized for the adsorption and rearrangement of long-chain paraffins. The framework composition of the zeolite is defined by a silicon-to-aluminum atomic ratio ranging from 50 to 120, a range that ensures sufficient Brønsted acidity to protonate olefinic intermediates while minimizing the density of strong acid sites that would otherwise promote β-scission and secondary cracking. The aluminum atoms are preferentially distributed in positions that correspond to the most sterically constrained regions of the pore mouth, thereby enhancing the shape-selective properties of the catalyst without increasing the overall acidity. This selective localization of aluminum is achieved through a controlled synthesis protocol that employs a specific organic template, triethylenetetramine, under carefully regulated pH and aging conditions, which directs the incorporation of aluminum into the framework in a manner that maximizes the number of acid sites located within the first nanometer of the pore entrance.

The hydrogenation/dehydrogenation function of the catalyst is provided by platinum, deposited via an incipient wetness technique to achieve a loading of 0.25 to 0.4 wt%. The platinum is present in the form of highly dispersed nanoparticles, with an average diameter not exceeding 2 nanometers, as confirmed by transmission electron microscopy and CO chemisorption measurements. This level of dispersion ensures that the metal sites are in close proximity to the Brønsted acid sites, satisfying the Weisz intimacy criterion and enabling the rapid shuttling of olefinic intermediates between the metal and acid functions. The platinum is maintained in its metallic state under reaction conditions, as verified by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy and hydrogen temperature-programmed reduction, and exhibits no evidence of oxidation or sintering during prolonged operation. The catalyst is further characterized by a high degree of crystallinity, as evidenced by sharp and well-defined X-ray diffraction peaks, and a specific surface area ranging from 350 to 450 m²/g, with a pore volume of 0.25 to 0.35 cm³/g, indicating a well-developed microporous structure without significant mesoporosity. The absence of mesopores is critical, as their presence would facilitate the diffusion of bulky isomerized products out of the catalyst before they could undergo further rearrangement, thereby reducing the yield of multibranched isomers. The catalyst is subjected to a two-stage calcination protocol, wherein the first stage involves heating in a stream of dry air at 500°C for four hours to remove the organic template, followed by a second stage at 550°C for two hours under a controlled oxygen partial pressure to ensure complete combustion of residual carbonaceous deposits without inducing structural collapse or dealumination. This protocol preserves the integrity of the pore mouth geometry, which is essential for the observed selectivity.

The catalytic performance of the invention is fundamentally different from that of prior art catalysts in several key respects. First, the isomerization selectivity is not governed by the bulk pore size or the total number of acid sites, but rather by the precise spatial arrangement of the pore entrances, which act as molecular templates that favor the formation of specific dibranched and trisubstituted isomers. This is evidenced by the fact that catalysts with identical Si/Al ratios and similar XRD patterns but synthesized using different templates—such as hexamethylenediamine or ethylenediamine—exhibit dramatically lower isomer yields and higher cracking rates, despite possessing comparable acidity and surface area. Second, the catalyst exhibits a unique ability to promote central branching over terminal branching, a feature that is not observed in ZSM-22 or SAPO-11 systems, where branching is overwhelmingly concentrated at the C₂ and C₃ positions. In the present invention, the distribution of monobranched isomers shows a significant proportion of branching at the C₄, C₅, and C₆ positions, which contributes to a higher viscosity index and improved low-temperature fluidity. Third, the catalyst demonstrates an unprecedented resistance to deactivation by oxygenates, maintaining high isomerization selectivity even in the presence of up to 1.5 wt% alcohols or carboxylic acids in the feedstock. This is attributed to the hydrophobic nature of the pore mouth region, which preferentially adsorbs non-polar hydrocarbons while excluding polar molecules through steric and electrostatic repulsion. The result is a catalyst that operates as a self-selective system, capable of upgrading real Fischer-Tropsch waxes without the need for prior hydrotreatment, thereby simplifying the process flow and reducing capital expenditure.

The catalyst is further distinguished by its operational stability. Under continuous flow conditions at 480 to 520°C and a hydrogen-to-hydrocarbon molar ratio of 10 to 15, the catalyst exhibits no measurable loss in activity or selectivity over 500 hours of operation, a performance that exceeds that of conventional ZSM-22-based systems, which typically deactivate within 200 to 300 hours due to coke deposition at the pore mouths. The resistance to coking is attributed to the narrow geometry of the pore entrances, which limits the formation of polycyclic aromatic species that are the precursors to coke, and to the rapid desorption of isomerized products before they can undergo further condensation reactions. Furthermore, the uniform dispersion of platinum ensures that any coke precursors that do form are hydrogenated and removed before they can polymerize into graphitic deposits. The catalyst is also mechanically robust, with pellet crush strengths exceeding 120 N/mm, making it suitable for use in fixed-bed reactors under industrial operating conditions. The combination of high isomerization selectivity, resistance to oxygenates, and operational stability renders this catalyst uniquely suited for the production of premium lubricant base oils from Fischer-Tropsch waxes, offering a level of performance that has not been previously attainable with any known catalytic system.

### The Embodiments According to the Invention

The embodiments of the invention encompass a range of catalyst formulations and synthesis protocols that collectively define the scope of the invention, each optimized for specific operational conditions and feedstock characteristics. The primary embodiment is a catalyst comprising a zeolitic support derived from ZBM-30 synthesized using triethylenetetramine as the structure-directing agent, with a Si/Al atomic ratio of 75, impregnated with platinum at a loading of 0.3 wt%. This catalyst exhibits the highest isomer yield and lowest cracking rate among all tested variants, achieving an isomerization yield of 92.4% at 99.1% conversion of n-octadecane under standard reaction conditions of 506 K, 0.45 MPa total pressure, and a hydrogen-to-hydrocarbon molar ratio of 13.1. The synthesis of this embodiment begins with the preparation of a gel containing colloidal silica, aluminum isopropoxide, triethylenetetramine, and water in a molar ratio of SiO₂:Al₂O₃:template:H₂O = 100:1:1.2:2000. The gel is aged at 95°C for 72 hours under autogenous pressure in a Teflon-lined autoclave, followed by filtration, washing with deionized water, and drying at 110°C overnight. The dried material is then calcined in two stages: first at 500°C for four hours in a stream of dry air, and then at 550°C for two hours under a controlled oxygen partial pressure of 0.1 atm to ensure complete removal of organic residues without inducing dealumination. The calcined zeolite is ion-exchanged with ammonium nitrate solution at 80°C for six hours to convert the framework to the NH₄⁺ form, followed by a second calcination at 500°C for three hours to generate the H⁺ form. Platinum is then deposited via incipient wetness using an aqueous solution of tetraammineplatinum(II) chloride, with the amount of solution carefully adjusted to achieve a final platinum loading of 0.3 wt%. The impregnated material is dried at 120°C for six hours and then reduced in a stream of pure hydrogen at 400°C for two hours to activate the metal component. The resulting catalyst is pelletized with a binder comprising 15 wt% alumina and extruded into cylindrical pellets of 1.6 mm diameter, which are then crushed and sieved to a particle size range of 0.25 to 0.50 mm for use in fixed-bed reactors.

A second embodiment involves the use of ZSM-48 synthesized with octylamine as the structure-directing agent, with a Si/Al ratio of 65 and a platinum loading of 0.35 wt%. This catalyst exhibits slightly lower isomerization yield (89.7%) but higher activity, achieving 99% conversion of n-octadecane at a shorter contact time than the primary embodiment. The synthesis protocol for this embodiment follows the same steps as above, with the exception that octylamine is substituted for triethylenetetramine, and the aging time is reduced to 48 hours to accommodate the higher volatility of the template. The calcination protocol is modified to include a ramp rate of 1°C per minute during the first stage to prevent thermal shock to the framework. This embodiment is particularly suited for applications requiring high throughput and moderate isomer yield, such as in the production of diesel-range isomers for blending into premium diesel fuels.

A third embodiment utilizes EU-2 synthesized with hexamethonium bromide as the structure-directing agent, with a Si/Al ratio of 90 and a platinum loading of 0.25 wt%. Although this catalyst exhibits lower overall activity and isomer yield compared to the primary embodiment, it demonstrates superior resistance to feedstock impurities, maintaining an isomer yield of 87.2% even when the feed contains 1.2 wt% lauric acid. The synthesis protocol for this embodiment employs a lower template-to-silica ratio and a longer aging period of 96 hours to promote the formation of larger, more ordered crystallites. The calcination protocol includes a final hold at 570°C for one hour to enhance the thermal stability of the framework. This embodiment is intended for use in situations where the feedstock is not fully hydrotreated and contains significant levels of oxygenated compounds, such as in biomass-derived Fischer-Tropsch streams or in regions where hydrotreating infrastructure is limited.

A fourth embodiment involves the use of a mixed-bed catalyst system comprising a physical mixture of the primary embodiment and a zeolite with a modified pore mouth geometry, such as a modified SAPO-11 with a Si/Al ratio of 40 and a platinum loading of 0.2 wt%. This embodiment is designed to broaden the isomer distribution and increase the yield of trisubstituted isomers, which are particularly valuable for high-viscosity-index lubricant applications. The physical mixture is prepared by blending the two catalysts in a weight ratio of 3:1, with the primary embodiment constituting the majority component. The mixture is then pelletized and used in a single reactor, where the SAPO-11 component promotes the formation of central branching while the ZBM-30 component ensures high overall isomer yield and low cracking. This embodiment is particularly effective for upgrading feedstocks with a broad carbon number distribution, such as those derived from coal-to-liquids processes.

A fifth embodiment incorporates a post-synthesis treatment involving the controlled introduction of trace amounts of phosphorus into the zeolite framework via impregnation with ammonium dihydrogen phosphate, followed by calcination at 500°C. This treatment reduces the density of the strongest acid sites by approximately 20%, as measured by pyridine adsorption and FTIR spectroscopy, without altering the pore mouth geometry or the metal dispersion. The resulting catalyst exhibits a slight reduction in activity but a marked improvement in selectivity, with cracking reduced to less than 2% at 99% conversion. This embodiment is intended for use in applications where the feedstock is exceptionally pure and the goal is to maximize the yield of high-value multibranched isomers.

Each of these embodiments is characterized by a unique combination of synthesis parameters, metal loading, and structural features that collectively define the catalytic performance envelope of the invention. The invention is not limited to any single embodiment but encompasses the entire range of catalysts that exhibit the key characteristics of high isomerization selectivity, low cracking, resistance to oxygenates, and operational stability, as defined by the specific pore mouth architecture and the precise control over aluminum distribution and metal dispersion. The invention further encompasses the use of these catalysts in any reactor configuration, including fixed-bed, moving-bed, or fluidized-bed systems, and under any combination of temperature, pressure, and hydrogen-to-hydrocarbon ratio that enables the selective hydroisomerization of Fischer-Tropsch waxes to produce lubricant base oils with a pour point below -15°C and a viscosity index above 130.

## The Products Obtained

The hydroisomerization of Fischer-Tropsch waxes using the catalysts of the present invention yields a product slate that is fundamentally distinct from those obtained by any previously disclosed catalytic system. The primary product is a highly branched, high-molecular-weight hydrocarbon stream composed predominantly of monobranched, dibranched, and trisubstituted isooctadecanes, with a negligible fraction of cracked products. The overall isomer yield exceeds 90% at conversions approaching 99%, with cracking suppressed to less than 3% by weight, a performance that represents a significant advancement over conventional ZSM-22-based systems, which typically achieve isomer yields of 80–85% with cracking rates of 8–12%. The product stream is characterized by a narrow carbon number distribution centered around C₁₈, with less than 0.5% of the product falling outside the C₁₆–C₂₀ range, indicating that the catalyst effectively preserves molecular weight while introducing branching. The isomer distribution is dominated by monobranched isomers, which constitute approximately 55–60% of the total isomer fraction, with dibranched isomers accounting for 30–35% and trisubstituted isomers making up the remaining 5–10%. This distribution is markedly different from that obtained using ZSM-22, where monobranched isomers dominate to the exclusion of multibranched species, and from that obtained using large-pore zeolites, where multibranched isomers are formed in excess but rapidly cracked. The unique feature of the present invention is the high proportion of central branching, with a significant fraction of the dibranched isomers exhibiting methyl groups at the C₄, C₅, and C₆ positions, rather than the terminal C₂ and C₃ positions favored by conventional catalysts. This central branching pattern is critical for the production of high-quality lubricant base oils, as it results in a higher viscosity index, improved oxidative stability, and lower volatility compared to terminal-branched isomers.

The pour point of the hydroisomerized product is consistently below -15°C, even when the feedstock has a pour point as high as +40°C, demonstrating the exceptional efficiency of the catalyst in modifying the molecular structure of the wax to achieve low-temperature fluidity. The viscosity index, a key indicator of lubricant performance, exceeds 130, with many samples reaching values as high as 145, surpassing the requirements for Group III base oils as defined by the American Petroleum Institute. The product also exhibits a high saturation level, with less than 0.1% olefin content and negligible aromatic content, as determined by gas chromatography and nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, ensuring excellent thermal and oxidative stability. The product stream is virtually free of sulfur, nitrogen, and oxygen compounds, with total heteroatom content below 1 ppm, a direct consequence of the catalyst’s tolerance to oxygenates and its ability to operate without prior hydrotreatment. This purity level is unprecedented in the field of Fischer-Tropsch upgrading and eliminates the need for downstream polishing steps such as hydrotreating or adsorption, further reducing process complexity and cost.

The product distribution is also notable for its lack of light ends. The C₅–C₁₅ fraction constitutes less than 1.5% of the total product, and the C₁–C₄ fraction is below 0.3%, indicating that the catalyst effectively suppresses hydrogenolysis and β-scission. The absence of methane and ethane, which are commonly produced by non-ideal catalysts due to excessive hydrogenolysis on reduced metal sites, confirms that the platinum component operates exclusively in the bifunctional mode, with no contribution from monofunctional hydrogenolysis. The product is also characterized by a high proportion of branched isomers with multiple methyl groups spaced at least four carbon atoms apart, a structural feature that renders them kinetically resistant to cracking while enhancing their lubricity and low-temperature performance. This is in stark contrast to the product obtained from ZSM-22, where the majority of dibranched isomers have branch points in close proximity, making them susceptible to β-scission under prolonged reaction conditions. The product of the present invention is thus not only a high-yield isomerization product but also a structurally optimized lubricant base stock, with a molecular architecture that is uniquely suited for high-performance applications in automotive, industrial, and aviation lubricants.

The product stream is amenable to direct use as a base oil without further refining, or it may be blended with other base stocks to produce formulations with tailored viscosity grades. The product exhibits excellent compatibility with common additives such as viscosity index improvers, anti-wear agents, and antioxidants, and demonstrates superior performance in standard lubricant tests, including the Four-Ball Wear Test, the Timken OK Load Test, and the High-Temperature High-Shear viscosity test. The low volatility and high thermal stability of the product also make it suitable for use in high-temperature environments, such as jet engine lubricants and heavy-duty diesel engine oils, where conventional mineral oils and even synthetic esters exhibit degradation. The absence of aromatics and sulfur ensures compliance with stringent environmental regulations, and the high viscosity index reduces the need for viscosity modifiers, thereby improving fuel economy and reducing emissions. The product thus represents a new class of synthetic lubricant base oils that combine the purity of Fischer-Tropsch feedstocks with the molecular architecture of high-performance synthetic hydrocarbons, offering a sustainable and high-value alternative to conventional base oils derived from petroleum.

### Example 1

The catalyst of the invention was prepared by synthesizing ZBM-30 using triethylenetetramine as the structure-directing agent. A gel was prepared by mixing 100 g of colloidal silica (Ludox AS-40), 1.0 g of aluminum isopropoxide, 1.2 g of triethylenetetramine, and 2000 g of deionized water in a Teflon-lined autoclave. The mixture was aged at 95°C for 72 hours under autogenous pressure, after which the solid product was recovered by filtration, washed with deionized water until the filtrate was free of chloride ions, and dried at 110°C overnight. The dried material was calcined in two stages: first at 500°C for four hours in a stream of dry air, followed by a second calcination at 550°C for two hours under a controlled oxygen partial pressure of 0.1 atm. The calcined zeolite was then ion-exchanged with a 1.0 M aqueous solution of ammonium nitrate at 80°C for six hours, followed by a second calcination at 500°C for three hours to generate the H⁺ form. Platinum was deposited via incipient wetness using an aqueous solution of tetraammineplatinum(II) chloride, with the volume of solution adjusted to achieve a final platinum loading of 0.3 wt%. The impregnated material was dried at 120°C for six hours and then reduced in a stream of pure hydrogen at 400°C for two hours. The resulting catalyst was pelletized with a binder comprising 15 wt% alumina, extruded into cylindrical pellets of 1.6 mm diameter, and crushed to a particle size range of 0.25 to 0.50 mm.

The catalyst was tested in a fixed-bed reactor under the following conditions: temperature of 506 K, total pressure of 0.45 MPa, hydrogen-to-hydrocarbon molar ratio of 13.1, and a weight hourly space velocity of 1.2 h⁻¹. The feedstock was a commercially purified Fischer-Tropsch wax containing 98.7 wt% n-octadecane, with trace amounts of n-heptadecane and n-eicosane. The reactor was operated continuously for 500 hours, with samples collected every 50 hours for analysis by gas chromatography equipped with a capillary column (DB-1) and flame ionization detection. The conversion of n-octadecane reached 99.1% after 10 hours and remained constant throughout the duration of the test. The isomer yield was 92.4%, with dibranched isomers accounting for 33.2% of the total isomer fraction and trisubstituted isomers for 7.1%. The cracking yield was 2.8%, consisting primarily of C₁₄ and C₁₆ hydrocarbons, with no detectable methane or ethane. The pour point of the product was -16.5°C, and the viscosity index was 142. The product contained less than 0.05 wt% olefins and no detectable oxygenates. The catalyst showed no measurable loss in activity or selectivity over the 500-hour test period, with no evidence of coke formation or metal sintering upon post-reaction characterization by XRD, TEM, and CO chemisorption. The results demonstrate the exceptional performance of the catalyst in achieving high isomer yield, low cracking, and operational stability under industrial conditions.

### Example 2

A second catalyst was prepared according to the same protocol as in Example 1, with the exception that octylamine was used as the structure-directing agent instead of triethylenetetramine, and the Si/Al ratio of the zeolite was adjusted to 65 by varying the amount of aluminum isopropoxide. The catalyst was tested under identical conditions as in Example 1, with the feedstock being the same Fischer-Tropsch wax. The conversion of n-octadecane reached 99.3% after 8 hours, with an isomer yield of 89.7% and a cracking yield of 3.1%. The dibranched isomer fraction was 30.5%, and the trisubstituted isomer fraction was 6.8%. The pour point of the product was -15.8°C, and the viscosity index was 138. The product contained less than 0.07 wt% olefins and no detectable oxygenates. The catalyst exhibited a higher initial activity than the catalyst of Example 1, achieving 90% conversion in 4 hours compared to 6 hours for the triethylenetetramine-based catalyst. However, the isomer yield was slightly lower, and the cracking yield was marginally higher, indicating a trade-off between activity and selectivity. The product distribution showed a higher proportion of terminal-branched isomers, with 2-methyloctadecane accounting for 42% of the monobranched fraction, compared to 35% in Example 1. The presence of 4,7-dimethyloctadecane was still significant, but at a lower relative abundance than in Example 1. The catalyst maintained stable performance over 500 hours, with no measurable deactivation. The results indicate that while the octylamine-based catalyst is more active, the triethylenetetramine-based catalyst offers superior selectivity for central branching and multibranched isomers, making it the preferred embodiment for high-value lubricant production.

### Example 3

A third catalyst was prepared using EU-2 synthesized with hexamethonium bromide as the structure-directing agent, with a Si/Al ratio of 90 and a platinum loading of 0.25 wt%. The catalyst was tested under the same conditions as in Example 1, but with a feedstock containing 1.2 wt% lauric acid and 0.8 wt% 1-decanol, simulating a real Fischer-Tropsch effluent that had not been hydrotreated. The conversion of n-octadecane reached 98.6% after 12 hours, with an isomer yield of 87.2% and a cracking yield of 3.5%. The dibranched isomer fraction was 29.1%, and the trisubstituted isomer fraction was 6.2%. The pour point of the product was -14.9°C, and the viscosity index was 135. The product contained less than 0.1 wt% olefins and no detectable oxygenates, indicating that the catalyst effectively excluded the oxygenated impurities from the active sites. The product distribution was similar to that of Example 1, with a high proportion of 4,7-dimethyloctadecane and other centrally branched isomers. The catalyst showed no measurable loss in activity or selectivity over 500 hours, even in the presence of the oxygenates, and no evidence of poisoning or coke formation was observed upon post-reaction characterization. The results demonstrate that the catalyst of the invention is uniquely capable of upgrading unhydrotreated Fischer-Tropsch waxes without the need for pretreatment, a capability that has not been demonstrated by any previously disclosed catalyst system. The ability to tolerate oxygenates while maintaining high isomer yield and low cracking represents a major technological advancement in the field of Fischer-Tropsch upgrading.
