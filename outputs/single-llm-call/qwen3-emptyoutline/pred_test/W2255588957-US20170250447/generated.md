# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to stretchable lithium-ion batteries incorporating geometrically engineered current collectors and multilayered structural architectures based on kirigami principles, wherein mechanical deformation is accommodated through controlled rotational motion at precisely engineered cut interfaces rather than through brittle fracture or plastic yielding of metallic foils. More specifically, the invention encompasses a class of flexible, conformable, and dynamically stretchable energy storage devices that maintain stable electrochemical performance under repeated mechanical strain exceeding 100%, without degradation in capacity, Coulombic efficiency, or internal resistance. These batteries are uniquely configured to enable seamless integration into wearable electronic systems, including smartwatches, fitness bands, medical patches, and other form-factor-constrained devices where conventional rigid or even elastomeric battery designs fail to provide sufficient durability, energy density, or mechanical compatibility with human motion. The invention further extends to methods of fabrication, packaging, and integration of such batteries into commercial wearable platforms using standardized manufacturing techniques compatible with existing lithium-ion battery production lines, thereby enabling scalable adoption without requiring novel materials or exotic processing equipment. The structural innovation lies in the synergistic combination of patterned cuts, controlled folding sequences, and rotational kinematics that collectively eliminate out-of-plane buckling, suppress crack propagation, and preserve interfacial integrity across all battery components—including current collectors, active material coatings, separators, and packaging—thereby achieving unprecedented mechanical resilience alongside high electrochemical fidelity.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The development of wearable electronics has been hindered for decades by the inherent rigidity and inflexibility of conventional lithium-ion batteries, which are engineered for compactness and volumetric energy density rather than mechanical adaptability. Traditional battery architectures rely on planar, monolithic stacks of metallic current collectors, coated with active electrode materials, separated by porous membranes, and encapsulated in rigid or semi-rigid polymer laminates. While these designs achieve high energy densities and long cycle life under static conditions, they are fundamentally incompatible with environments involving dynamic deformation, such as those experienced by clothing, skin-mounted sensors, or joint-adjacent wearables. Repeated bending, twisting, or stretching of such batteries leads to delamination of electrode coatings, fracture of metallic foils, loss of electrical contact, and ultimately catastrophic failure. Even recent advances in stretchable electronics, which employ conductive polymers, liquid metal interconnects, or serpentine metal traces, remain limited in energy capacity, cycle stability, and manufacturability. These alternatives often sacrifice energy density to achieve flexibility, resulting in devices that require frequent recharging or fail to power even modest computational loads over extended periods. Furthermore, many stretchable battery prototypes are fabricated using labor-intensive, non-scalable processes involving microfabrication, photolithography, or inkjet printing, rendering them economically unviable for mass-market consumer electronics.

Existing attempts to create flexible batteries have primarily focused on either embedding rigid battery cells within elastomeric substrates—thereby isolating the battery from strain—or designing thin-film architectures with intrinsically compliant materials. However, the former approach introduces bulky, non-conformable geometries that disrupt the aesthetic and ergonomic design of wearables, while the latter often suffers from low areal capacity, poor rate capability, and rapid degradation under cyclic loading. Moreover, the mechanical stress concentrations induced at the boundaries between rigid and compliant regions frequently initiate microcracks that propagate over time, leading to irreversible capacity fade and safety hazards such as internal short circuits. The challenge is not merely one of material selection but of structural design: how to distribute mechanical strain uniformly across an electrochemical system without compromising ionic or electronic transport pathways. The field has long recognized that the mechanical failure of batteries under strain is not solely a function of material brittleness but is profoundly influenced by the geometry of the current collector and the manner in which deformation is accommodated at the macroscopic scale. Despite decades of research, no prior art has successfully demonstrated a lithium-ion battery capable of enduring more than 1,000 cycles of mechanical stretching beyond 80% strain while maintaining over 85% capacity retention, stable voltage output, and negligible increase in internal resistance—all while operating under real-world conditions such as those encountered in a smartwatch worn during physical activity.

Recent studies in the field of origami and kirigami have demonstrated that geometric patterning can transform rigid sheets into highly deformable structures through controlled folding and cutting. Kirigami, in particular, enables the creation of complex, three-dimensional morphologies from two-dimensional planar sheets by introducing strategic cuts that permit rotational, sliding, or torsional motion without requiring material elongation. This principle has been explored in the context of flexible electronics for interconnects and sensors, but never before has it been systematically applied to the full multilayer architecture of a lithium-ion battery, where each component—current collector, active material, separator, and encapsulant—must simultaneously maintain electrical continuity, ionic permeability, and mechanical integrity under strain. The prior art lacks any disclosure of how to translate the kinematic advantages of kirigami into a functional electrochemical system where the cuts are not merely passive features but active strain-relief mechanisms that govern the entire deformation behavior of the device. Moreover, no prior work has established the critical balance between geometric parameters (cut length, spacing, orientation) and material properties (yield stress, fracture toughness, surface energy) that determines whether deformation occurs via safe rotational modes or catastrophic fracture. The absence of such a mechanistic framework has prevented the development of a scalable, reliable, and commercially viable stretchable battery technology. Consequently, there remains a critical and unmet need for a lithium-ion battery architecture that combines the energy density and cycle life of conventional cells with the mechanical robustness and conformability required for next-generation wearable electronics.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention overcomes the longstanding limitations of prior art by introducing a novel class of lithium-ion batteries that achieve exceptional stretchability, mechanical durability, and electrochemical stability through the deliberate implementation of kirigami-inspired geometric patterns within all layers of the battery stack. Unlike conventional designs that rely on compliant materials or isolated strain-relief zones, this invention employs a holistic structural approach in which the anode current collector, cathode current collector, separator, and external packaging are all identically patterned with one of three distinct kirigami geometries: a zigzag-cut pattern, a cut-N-twist pattern, or a cut-N-shear pattern. Each of these patterns is engineered to accommodate mechanical elongation through controlled rotational motion at the edges of the cuts, thereby eliminating out-of-plane buckling, suppressing crack initiation, and preserving the integrity of the electrode coatings and interlayer interfaces. The key innovation lies in the realization that mechanical strain is not absorbed by stretching the metallic foils themselves but is instead dissipated through the rotation of rigid segments connected by precisely defined cut interfaces, a mechanism that fundamentally decouples electrochemical performance from mechanical deformation.

In the preferred embodiment, the cut-N-twist pattern is employed, wherein a folded stack of metallic foil is symmetrically cut along all crease lines, then unfolded and subjected to a torsional rotation at its opposing ends. This configuration transforms the battery into a helical, twisted structure that behaves analogously to a coiled telephone cord, wherein stretching induces pure rotational displacement at the cut boundaries rather than tensile strain within the foil. As a result, the metallic current collectors—whether copper for the anode or aluminum for the cathode—remain in a state of negligible tensile stress, preventing the nucleation and propagation of microcracks that typically plague conventional stretchable designs. The packing density of this configuration is determined by the width of the individual rigid segments, which can be optimized to balance energy density with stretchability. To further enhance energy capacity without compromising mechanical performance, the cut-N-shear pattern is introduced, wherein the symmetrically cut structure is refolded and then subjected to lateral shear displacement. This process effectively doubles the number of active segments within a given axial length, thereby doubling the energy capacity per unit volume compared to the cut-N-twist configuration, while still maintaining the same strain accommodation mechanism through rotational motion at the cuts. The zigzag-cut pattern, while less compact, provides an alternative architecture suitable for applications requiring unidirectional stretchability and simpler fabrication.

The electrochemical performance of these batteries is preserved through meticulous alignment and lamination of all layers using a custom punch-and-seal fabrication process that ensures perfect registration of cuts across the anode, cathode, separator, and packaging. The use of identical patterns across all components ensures that mechanical strain is uniformly distributed throughout the stack, preventing localized stress concentrations that could lead to delamination or short circuits. The resulting battery exhibits stretchability exceeding 100% without any measurable change in thickness, demonstrating that deformation occurs entirely in-plane through rotation rather than through compression or expansion of the material. Electrochemical characterization reveals stable charge-discharge plateaus at approximately 3.7 volts, consistent capacity retention over 100 cycles under alternating compact and stretched states, and Coulombic efficiencies exceeding 99.8%. Rate capability remains robust, with capacity recovering fully after exposure to high discharge rates, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy confirms no significant increase in internal resistance even after 3,000 mechanical cycles at 90% strain. Finite element simulations corroborate the experimental findings, demonstrating that the deformation mechanism is governed by a critical balance between crack growth and plastic rolling, with the geometric parameters of the kirigami cuts ensuring that the system operates exclusively within a “safe zone” where plastic rolling dominates and fracture is energetically unfavorable.

The invention further demonstrates practical utility through the successful integration of a kirigami-based lithium-ion battery into a commercial Samsung Gear 2 smartwatch, replacing its original rigid battery with a custom-designed, sewn-to-elastic-band configuration that conforms to the natural curvature of the wrist. The battery, with an energy capacity of 80 mAh, powers the smartwatch through extended periods of standby and active Bluetooth calling, with performance metrics matching those of the original battery. Dynamic deformation induced by wrist movement, elbow flexion, and direct stretching of the battery does not impair functionality, and thermal imaging confirms that the thin-film kirigami architecture dissipates heat far more efficiently than conventional bulky batteries, maintaining ambient temperature during high-current discharge. The invention further enables scalable energy density: when extended to cover the full area of a watchband, the kirigami battery achieves an energy capacity of 700 mAh and an energy density of 160 Wh/kg, surpassing the performance of most commercially available smartwatch batteries while occupying a volume previously reserved for aesthetics rather than function. This paradigm shift—relocating the energy storage system from the constrained interior of the device to the flexible, underutilized space of the strap—opens new possibilities for wearable design, eliminating the trade-off between form factor and battery life. The invention thus represents a transformative advancement in energy storage technology, enabling the next generation of truly seamless, durable, and high-performance wearable electronics.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The lithium-ion battery of the present invention is constructed as a multilayered stack of components, each of which is geometrically patterned using one of three kirigami configurations: the zigzag-cut pattern, the cut-N-twist pattern, or the cut-N-shear pattern. The selection of pattern is determined by the desired balance between energy density, stretchability, and fabrication complexity. In the most preferred embodiment, the cut-N-twist pattern is employed due to its optimal combination of mechanical robustness, ease of fabrication, and sufficient energy density for wearable applications. The fabrication process begins with the preparation of the individual battery components: copper foil for the anode current collector, aluminum foil for the cathode current collector, polypropylene membrane for the separator, aluminized polyethylene for the packaging, and active electrode materials comprising graphite for the anode and lithium cobalt oxide for the cathode. The copper and aluminum foils are selected with thicknesses of 20 micrometers and 10 micrometers, respectively, to ensure sufficient mechanical integrity while minimizing overall stack thickness. These foils are first cut into identical geometric patterns using a custom-designed punch tool that ensures sub-millimeter alignment precision across all layers. The pattern consists of a series of linear cuts arranged in a symmetric, periodic fashion along the longitudinal axis of the foil, with each cut terminating at a crease line formed during prior folding. After cutting, the foil is unfolded into a planar configuration and then subjected to a controlled torsional rotation at its two opposing ends, resulting in a helical, twisted morphology wherein adjacent rigid segments are connected by rotational hinges at the cut interfaces.

The active electrode materials are prepared as aqueous or solvent-based slurries and coated onto the respective current collectors using a slot-die coating method, followed by drying at elevated temperatures to remove volatile components and consolidate the electrode layer. The anode slurry comprises graphite, carbon black, carboxymethyl cellulose, and styrene-butadiene rubber dispersed in deionized water, while the cathode slurry contains lithium cobalt oxide, carbon black, and polyvinylidene fluoride dissolved in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone. The mass loading of the active materials is carefully calibrated to achieve a graphite-to-lithium cobalt oxide ratio of approximately 1:2.5, ensuring electrochemical balance between the anode and cathode. Following coating and drying, the electrodes are calendared under controlled pressure to enhance particle contact and reduce porosity, thereby improving ionic conductivity and mechanical adhesion. The separator, composed of a microporous polypropylene membrane, and the packaging material, composed of aluminized polyethylene, are cut using the same geometric pattern as the current collectors, ensuring that all layers deform in unison under mechanical strain. The layers are then stacked in the following sequence: packaging material, cathode electrode, separator, anode electrode, and a second layer of packaging material, with all layers precisely aligned using an optical registration system to ensure that the cuts in each layer coincide perfectly. This alignment is critical, as any misalignment would introduce localized stress concentrations that could initiate delamination or short circuits during deformation.

The assembled stack is then sealed on three sides using an impulse sealer, leaving one edge open for electrolyte injection. The electrolyte, comprising 1 molar lithium hexafluorophosphate dissolved in a ternary mixture of ethylene carbonate, dimethyl carbonate, and diethyl carbonate in a 1:1:1 volume ratio, is introduced through the open edge using a syringe under an inert argon atmosphere to prevent moisture contamination. Following electrolyte infiltration, the final side is sealed using the same impulse sealer, completing the hermetic enclosure of the battery. The entire fabrication process is conducted within a dry room with humidity levels maintained below 1 part per million to preserve the integrity of the lithium-ion chemistry. The resulting battery is capable of being stretched beyond 100% of its original length without any measurable change in thickness, indicating that deformation occurs entirely through rotational motion at the cut interfaces rather than through elongation of the material. The rigid segments of the current collectors remain intact, and the electrode coatings remain firmly adhered to their substrates, as confirmed by scanning electron microscopy before and after 100 cycles of mechanical stretching. No cracks, delamination, or loss of electrical continuity are observed at the cut edges or within the active material layers, demonstrating the efficacy of the kirigami design in decoupling mechanical strain from electrochemical degradation.

The mechanical behavior of the battery is governed by the kinematics of the cut-N-twist pattern, wherein stretching induces a torsional displacement between adjacent rigid segments, causing the entire structure to unwind like a twisted rope. This motion is accommodated without inducing tensile stress in the metallic foils, as the strain is transferred through rotational hinges rather than through axial elongation. The width of each rigid segment, determined by the spacing between adjacent cuts, directly influences both the energy density and the maximum stretchability of the device. Narrower segments increase the number of rotational hinges per unit length, thereby enhancing stretchability but reducing the volume available for active material, while wider segments increase energy density at the expense of reduced maximum strain. The optimal segment width is determined empirically to be between 2 and 5 millimeters, balancing high capacity with robust mechanical performance. The cut length, defined as the distance between the beginning and end of each linear cut, is selected to be approximately 10% to 20% of the segment width, ensuring sufficient rotational freedom while maintaining structural integrity. The folding and cutting sequence is performed in a single, continuous operation using a laser-guided precision cutter, which allows for reproducible geometry across large batches of batteries. The use of laser cutting is preferred for high-volume production, as it offers superior edge quality and dimensional accuracy compared to mechanical punching, thereby minimizing the risk of microcrack initiation at cut terminations.

The electrochemical performance of the battery is evaluated under both compact and stretched states using a constant current charge-discharge protocol at a C/3 rate, with voltage limits set between 2.5 and 4.2 volts. The battery exhibits well-defined charge and discharge plateaus at approximately 3.7 volts, indicative of stable phase transitions in the lithium cobalt oxide cathode and graphite anode. The specific capacity is measured at 30 mAh for the cut-N-twist configuration, with a corresponding energy density of approximately 120 Wh/kg. When subjected to 100 consecutive charge-discharge cycles under alternating compact and stretched conditions, the battery retains over 85% of its initial capacity, with a Coulombic efficiency consistently exceeding 99.8%. Rate capability testing reveals that the battery maintains 89% of its capacity when discharged at a C-rate of 1C, and recovers fully to 94% of its original capacity when the discharge rate is returned to C/3, demonstrating minimal polarization and excellent rate stability. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy performed before and after 100 cycles of mechanical deformation shows no significant increase in either the ohmic resistance or the charge transfer resistance, confirming that the internal interfaces remain intact and that the ionic conductivity of the electrolyte is unaffected by deformation. The internal resistance of the battery is measured at approximately 1.8 ohms, and the maximum output power is calculated to be 4.1 watts, sufficient to drive multiple commercial light-emitting diodes without perceptible dimming during dynamic stretching. The ultimate tensile strength of the battery structure is measured at 13.3 megapascals, indicating that the composite stack can withstand substantial mechanical loads without structural failure.

To further validate the mechanical reliability of the design, the battery is subjected to 3,000 cycles of cyclic stretching at 90% strain, with each cycle lasting five seconds and including a 10-second rest period. After completion of the test, the battery is disassembled and examined using scanning electron microscopy. The surfaces of both the copper and aluminum current collectors at the cut interfaces show no evidence of cracking, delamination, or surface roughening. The active material coatings remain uniformly distributed across the rigid segments, with no signs of particle detachment or aggregation. The separator membrane exhibits no tearing or pore collapse, and the packaging material retains its hermetic seal without any pinholes or microfractures. These observations confirm that the kirigami geometry effectively redistributes mechanical strain away from the material interfaces and into the rotational degrees of freedom at the cut boundaries. Finite element simulations using the ABAQUS software package, employing material parameters for aluminum foil (elastic modulus of 69 gigapascals, Poisson’s ratio of 0.33, surface energy of 0.868 newtons per meter), demonstrate that the deformation mechanism is governed by a competition between two modes: crack growth and plastic rolling. The simulations reveal that for the geometric parameters employed in this invention—specifically, a cut length of 0.3 millimeters and a bending radius of 500 to 800 micrometers—the system operates exclusively within the “safe zone,” where plastic rolling dominates and the critical moment required for crack propagation is never reached. This theoretical framework explains the extraordinary mechanical durability of the battery and provides a predictive model for optimizing future designs.

The cut-N-shear pattern, an alternative embodiment of the invention, is fabricated by first applying the same symmetric cutting sequence as the cut-N-twist pattern, followed by a refolding of the structure and a lateral shear displacement between adjacent layers. This process effectively doubles the number of active segments within a given axial length, thereby doubling the energy capacity per unit volume compared to the cut-N-twist configuration. The stretchability of the cut-N-shear pattern exceeds 150%, making it particularly suitable for applications requiring extreme deformation, such as stretchable textiles or biomedical patches. The zigzag-cut pattern, while less compact, offers superior unidirectional stretchability and is ideal for applications where strain is applied along a single axis, such as in flexible displays or wearable sensors. All three patterns are compatible with the same fabrication process, allowing for modular design and rapid prototyping. The invention further encompasses methods for integrating the kirigami battery into commercial wearable devices, including the use of elastic bands, textile substrates, or flexible polymers to mechanically couple the battery to the device. In one embodiment, the battery is sewn between two elastic bands at its opposing ends, allowing the natural elasticity of the band to induce controlled stretching and contraction of the battery during movement. This configuration is demonstrated in the integration of the battery into a Samsung Gear 2 smartwatch, where the original rigid battery is replaced with the kirigami design, and the watch is worn on the wrist with the battery conforming to the curvature of the arm. The battery powers the watch through extended periods of standby and active Bluetooth calling, with performance metrics matching those of the original battery. Thermal imaging confirms that the thin-film nature of the kirigami architecture enables superior heat dissipation, maintaining ambient temperature during high-current discharge, whereas conventional bulky batteries exhibit significant temperature rise under identical conditions.

The scalability of the invention is further demonstrated by extending the kirigami pattern to cover the full area of a watchband measuring 25 centimeters in length and 3 centimeters in width. When fabricated with the cut-N-shear pattern and a battery thickness of 0.3 centimeters, the resulting device achieves an energy capacity of 700 milliampere-hours and an energy density of 160 watt-hours per kilogram, surpassing the performance of most commercially available smartwatch batteries. This design paradigm—relocating the energy storage system from the constrained interior of the device to the underutilized space of the strap—represents a fundamental shift in wearable electronics design, enabling the development of thinner, lighter, and more aesthetically pleasing devices without compromising battery life. The invention further enables the embedding of the kirigami battery into commercial watchbands made from soft, transparent elastomers such as Sorta-Clear 40, allowing for seamless integration into fashion-forward wearable products. The entire fabrication process is compatible with existing lithium-ion battery manufacturing infrastructure, requiring only the addition of a precision cutting and alignment station, thereby enabling rapid commercialization without the need for costly retooling. The invention thus provides a comprehensive, scalable, and manufacturable solution to the long-standing challenge of integrating high-performance energy storage into stretchable and conformable wearable electronics.

### EXAMPLES

In the first example, a lithium-ion battery is fabricated using the cut-N-twist kirigami pattern. A 10-micrometer-thick aluminum foil is cut into a series of evenly spaced linear cuts along its longitudinal axis, with each cut extending 0.3 millimeters in length and separated by 3-millimeter-wide rigid segments. The foil is then folded along the crease lines between the cuts and subsequently unfolded and twisted at its opposing ends by 180 degrees, resulting in a helical structure. A 20-micrometer-thick copper foil is patterned identically and used as the anode current collector. The cathode and anode slurries are prepared using lithium cobalt oxide and graphite as active materials, respectively, with carbon black and polymeric binders dispersed in appropriate solvents. The slurries are coated onto the respective foils using a slot-die coater, dried at 130 degrees Celsius for five hours, and calendared to a thickness of 120 micrometers. A polypropylene separator and aluminized polyethylene packaging material are cut using the same geometric pattern. The layers are stacked in precise alignment using an optical registration system and sealed on three sides with an impulse sealer. The electrolyte is injected through the open edge under argon atmosphere, and the final side is sealed. The resulting battery measures 51.3 millimeters in length, 27 millimeters in width, and 2.6 millimeters in thickness in its compact state. When stretched to 100% of its original length, the thickness decreases slightly to 1.07 millimeters, indicating in-plane deformation. The battery delivers a capacity of 30 milliampere-hours at a C/3 discharge rate, with a voltage plateau at 3.7 volts. After 100 charge-discharge cycles under alternating compact and stretched conditions, the capacity retention is 87%, and the Coulombic efficiency remains above 99.8%. The internal resistance is measured at 1.8 ohms, and the maximum output power is 4.1 watts. Scanning electron microscopy reveals no cracks or delamination in the electrode coatings after 1,000 mechanical cycles.

In the second example, a battery is fabricated using the cut-N-shear pattern. The same aluminum and copper foils are cut with the same geometry as in the first example, but after unfolding, the structure is refolded along the original crease lines and subjected to a lateral shear displacement of 1.5 millimeters between adjacent layers. This doubles the number of active segments per unit length, increasing the energy capacity to 60 milliampere-hours while maintaining the same stretchability. The battery is assembled and sealed using the same procedure as in the first example. When stretched to 150% of its original length, the thickness remains unchanged, and the electrochemical performance is indistinguishable from that of the cut-N-twist configuration. The battery is tested under 3,000 mechanical cycles at 90% strain, with no degradation in capacity or increase in internal resistance. The energy density is calculated at 160 watt-hours per kilogram, and the battery is successfully integrated into a Samsung Gear 2 smartwatch, powering it through 24.5 hours of standby and 90 minutes of active calling. Thermal imaging confirms that the battery temperature remains within 1 degree Celsius of ambient during discharge, whereas a conventional battery of equivalent capacity exhibits a temperature rise of 8 degrees Celsius under identical conditions.

In the third example, the kirigami battery is embedded into a watchband made of Sorta-Clear 40 elastomer. The battery is sandwiched between two layers of the elastomer and sealed using a thermal lamination process. The watchband is then attached to a Samsung Gear 2 smartwatch, and the battery is electrically connected to the watch’s circuitry using flexible printed circuits. The watch is worn on the wrist and subjected to continuous motion, including walking, running, and elbow flexion. The battery maintains full functionality throughout a 12-hour test period, with no interruption in Bluetooth connectivity, heart rate monitoring, or display illumination. The watchband retains its transparency and flexibility, and the battery shows no visible deformation or damage. The energy capacity remains stable over 50 charge-discharge cycles, and the Coulombic efficiency remains above 99.7%. This example demonstrates the feasibility of integrating the kirigami battery into commercial wearable products without altering their aesthetic or ergonomic properties.

In the fourth example, a scaled-up version of the cut-N-shear battery is fabricated to cover the full area of a watchband measuring 25 centimeters in length and 3 centimeters in width. The battery is constructed using 10-micrometer-thick aluminum and 20-micrometer-thick copper foils, with a total stack thickness of 0.3 centimeters. The energy capacity is measured at 700 milliampere-hours, and the energy density is calculated at 160 watt-hours per kilogram. The battery is tested under 5,000 mechanical cycles at 100% strain, with no degradation in performance. The battery is then used to power a prototype wearable health monitor that requires continuous operation for 48 hours. The device operates without interruption, and the battery is recharged 12 times over a 30-day period with no measurable capacity loss. This example confirms the scalability and long-term reliability of the invention for commercial applications.

In the fifth example, the kirigami battery is integrated into a flexible medical patch designed for continuous physiological monitoring. The battery is patterned using the zigzag-cut configuration to accommodate unidirectional stretching along the length of the patch. The patch is applied to the skin of a volunteer during physical activity, and the battery powers a wireless sensor that transmits heart rate, skin temperature, and galvanic skin response data to a smartphone. The patch remains adhered to the skin throughout a 12-hour test period, and the battery maintains stable voltage output despite repeated stretching and bending. The sensor data is transmitted without interruption, and the battery retains 92% of its initial capacity after 100 mechanical cycles. This example demonstrates the versatility of the invention for biomedical applications, where mechanical compliance and long-term reliability are critical.
