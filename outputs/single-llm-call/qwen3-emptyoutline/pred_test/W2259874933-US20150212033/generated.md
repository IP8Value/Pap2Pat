# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to multi-channel capacitive sensor arrays configured for simultaneous, directionally resolved, and frequency-multiplexed detection of proximity or touch events. Specifically, the invention encompasses a novel architecture in which multiple capacitive sensing elements are integrated into a common transmission medium—such as a microstrip line—and each element is paired with a distinct inductive component to form an independent resonant circuit operating at a unique frequency. These resonances are sufficiently separated in the frequency domain to enable decoupled signal responses, allowing each sensor to respond exclusively to perturbations occurring in its localized vicinity without interference from concurrent events at other locations. The invention further extends to the use of high-quality-factor resonant structures, including metamaterial-based split-ring resonators and on-chip meander inductors, to enhance spectral isolation and increase the density of independently addressable sensing channels within a constrained bandwidth. The system is applicable to industrial automation, human-machine interfaces, security systems, medical monitoring devices, and robotics, where spatially resolved, non-contact sensing of multiple simultaneous or sequential touch or proximity events is required with high fidelity, resolution, and reliability.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Capacitive sensing has long been a foundational technology in non-contact detection systems, leveraging the principle that the presence of a nearby dielectric or conductive object alters the local electric field and thereby modifies the capacitance of a sensing electrode. Conventional capacitive sensors operate by applying a fixed-frequency excitation signal and monitoring changes in amplitude or phase of the reflected or transmitted signal. While effective for detecting the presence or absence of an object, such systems are inherently limited in their ability to distinguish between multiple simultaneous events occurring at different spatial locations. The inability to resolve the origin of a capacitance change renders these sensors unsuitable for applications requiring directional or multi-point sensing, such as gesture recognition, multi-touch interfaces, or array-based surveillance.

To overcome this limitation, prior art has explored the use of capacitive sensor arrays, where multiple electrodes are arranged in a grid or linear configuration and interrogated sequentially using time-division multiplexing. This approach, while enabling spatial resolution, suffers from significant throughput limitations, as each sensor must be addressed individually, resulting in slow response times and reduced temporal resolution. Moreover, the physical wiring and switching circuitry required for multiplexing introduce parasitic capacitances, signal crosstalk, and mechanical complexity, particularly when scaling to large arrays. Alternative approaches have attempted to exploit frequency-domain multiplexing by assigning different operating frequencies to different sensors, but these efforts have been hindered by the poor spectral separation of resonances in conventional LC circuits, where low quality factors lead to overlapping bandwidths and ambiguous signal interpretation.

Recent advances in microwave engineering have demonstrated the utility of resonant structures—such as ring resonators, split-ring resonators, and metamaterial elements—in enhancing sensitivity and spectral selectivity in sensing applications. These structures can achieve quality factors orders of magnitude higher than those attainable with discrete surface-mounted inductors and capacitors, thereby enabling tighter frequency spacing between resonant channels. However, these designs have typically been employed in single-sensor configurations, where the entire structure responds to a global perturbation, and have not been systematically adapted to multi-element arrays capable of independent, parallel, and spatially localized sensing. Furthermore, existing resonant sensor designs often require direct contact with the target object or rely on highly localized field enhancements within sub-millimeter gaps, severely restricting their applicability to non-contact proximity sensing scenarios.

Another critical limitation of prior art lies in the lack of a unified framework for integrating multiple high-Q resonant elements into a single transmission medium without mutual coupling or spectral interference. Conventional microstrip-based sensor arrays, even when employing multiple inductors, suffer from parasitic coupling between adjacent elements due to electromagnetic field overlap, substrate losses, and imperfect grounding. These effects manifest as broadened resonance peaks, shifted resonant frequencies, and cross-talk between channels, all of which degrade the fidelity of multi-channel detection. Additionally, the use of discrete surface-mounted components introduces variability in component tolerances, soldering-induced parasitics, and mechanical instability, particularly in environments subject to thermal cycling or vibration.

The prior art also fails to provide a scalable methodology for designing sensor arrays with predictable, controllable, and repeatable resonant responses across a broad frequency spectrum. While simulation tools such as ADS and COMSOL have been used to model individual sensor elements, there is no established design methodology for ensuring spectral isolation among multiple resonators embedded within a shared transmission line, especially when the resonators are fabricated using heterogeneous technologies (e.g., discrete inductors, meander lines, and metamaterial structures). Furthermore, no prior system has demonstrated the ability to simultaneously detect multiple proximity events with sub-millimeter positional accuracy, while maintaining independent channel response, high signal-to-noise ratio, and immunity to environmental interference.

Commercial proximity sensors, such as those manufactured by OMRON, IFM, SICK, and AutomationDirect, rely on infrared, ultrasonic, or low-frequency capacitive techniques that offer limited spatial resolution, single-point detection, and susceptibility to ambient conditions. These sensors typically report a single binary output or an analog distance reading, with no capability to distinguish between multiple concurrent events or to determine the direction of approach. Their performance metrics, such as the figure-of-merit defined as the ratio of corrected sensing range to effective sensor dimension, generally fall within a narrow range of 0.1 to 0.4, with most designs prioritizing cost and simplicity over spectral multiplexing or multi-channel resolution.

There is therefore a clear and unmet need in the field of proximity and touch sensing for a sensor architecture that enables simultaneous, directionally resolved, and frequency-multiplexed detection of multiple proximity events using a compact, scalable, and high-fidelity array of independently resonant capacitive elements. Such a system must overcome the limitations of time-domain multiplexing, eliminate spectral cross-talk between channels, achieve high quality factors to maximize channel density, and provide a robust, manufacturable design that can be integrated into complex systems without requiring external multiplexing circuitry or sequential scanning. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel class of multi-resonant capacitive sensor arrays that leverage frequency-domain isolation, high-Q resonant structures, and integrated microstrip topologies to achieve unprecedented levels of parallel sensing capability and spatial discrimination.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a multi-channel capacitive sensor array capable of simultaneously detecting and distinguishing multiple proximity or touch events occurring at different spatial locations, using a frequency-multiplexed architecture in which each sensing element is tuned to a unique resonant frequency and operates independently within a shared transmission medium. The sensor array comprises a plurality of capacitive sensing structures, each electrically coupled to a distinct inductive element, such that each capacitive-inductive pair forms a resonant circuit with a predetermined resonant frequency. These resonant circuits are embedded within a common transmission line structure, such as a microstrip line, such that the entire array is excited by a single broadband signal and the resulting transmission or reflection characteristics are analyzed across a range of frequencies. The resonant frequencies of the individual elements are selected such that their respective bandwidths do not overlap, thereby ensuring that a perturbation affecting one capacitive element produces a measurable change only at its corresponding resonant frequency, without inducing detectable alterations in the response of other elements.

Each capacitive sensing structure is configured as a conductive patch separated from a ground plane by a dielectric substrate, forming a parallel-plate capacitor whose capacitance varies in response to the proximity of a target object. The inductive element associated with each capacitive structure may be a discrete surface-mounted inductor, an on-chip meander inductor patterned directly onto the substrate, or a metamaterial-based resonator such as a split-ring resonator coupled to the transmission line. The selection of the inductive component determines the quality factor of the resonance, with metamaterial structures providing significantly higher quality factors than discrete components, thereby enabling tighter frequency spacing and a greater number of independently addressable sensing channels within a given bandwidth. The transmission line is designed to minimize parasitic coupling between adjacent elements, and the ground plane is configured to provide uniform reference potential across all sensing elements, ensuring consistent field distribution and minimizing cross-talk.

The sensor array operates by applying a broadband excitation signal to the transmission line and measuring the transmission coefficient, S21, or reflection coefficient, S11, across a frequency range encompassing all resonant frequencies of the array. When a target object approaches one of the capacitive sensing structures, the capacitance of that structure increases, causing a measurable shift in the resonant frequency of its associated LC circuit. This shift manifests as a change in the magnitude or phase of the transmission coefficient at the corresponding resonant frequency, while the response at all other frequencies remains unchanged. By monitoring the transmission spectrum, the system can identify not only the presence of a proximity event but also its precise spatial location, as each frequency band corresponds uniquely to a specific sensing element. The system is capable of detecting multiple simultaneous events, as each event affects only its designated frequency channel, enabling parallel, real-time, multi-point sensing without sequential scanning or time-division multiplexing.

The invention further includes a method for designing the sensor array to ensure spectral isolation among resonant channels. This method involves selecting inductance values for each element such that the resulting resonant frequencies are spaced apart by a margin greater than twice the half-power bandwidth of the narrowest resonance, thereby ensuring non-overlapping spectral responses. The design process incorporates electromagnetic simulations to model the interaction between the capacitive patches, the inductive elements, and the transmission line, accounting for parasitic effects, substrate losses, and coupling between adjacent elements. The resulting design is then fabricated using standard printed circuit board techniques, with the option to integrate all components on a single substrate using on-chip inductors or metamaterial structures, eliminating the need for discrete components and reducing mechanical complexity.

The invention also encompasses a method for calibrating and interpreting the sensor output, wherein a baseline transmission spectrum is recorded in the absence of any target object, and subsequent measurements are compared against this baseline to detect deviations indicative of proximity events. The system can be configured to detect either the shift in resonant frequency or the change in transmission magnitude at a fixed frequency near the resonance, with both methods providing equivalent spatial discrimination. The sensing range and resolution are determined by the sensitivity of the capacitance change to object distance, the noise floor of the measurement system, and the quality factor of the resonances, with the invention achieving a sensing range of up to 10 millimeters and sub-millimeter resolution under practical conditions.

The sensor array is scalable to any number of sensing elements, limited only by the available bandwidth and the minimum achievable bandwidth of the resonant structures. The use of high-quality-factor metamaterial resonators enables the integration of dozens of sensing channels within a single gigahertz-wide frequency band, far exceeding the capacity of conventional time-multiplexed arrays. The system is inherently robust to environmental interference, as changes in ambient temperature, humidity, or electromagnetic noise affect all channels uniformly and can be compensated for through differential measurement techniques. The invention further enables the detection of object material properties, as different dielectric materials induce distinct capacitance changes, allowing the system to distinguish between wood, plastic, metal, or human tissue based on the magnitude and frequency dependence of the response.

In summary, the invention provides a novel, scalable, and high-performance sensor architecture that transforms capacitive sensing from a single-point, time-sequential detection method into a multi-channel, frequency-multiplexed, spatially resolved sensing platform. By decoupling the response of each sensing element in the frequency domain and leveraging high-Q resonant structures, the invention enables simultaneous, parallel, and directionally accurate detection of multiple proximity or touch events with unprecedented fidelity, speed, and density, thereby opening new possibilities in human-machine interaction, industrial automation, medical diagnostics, and security systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The present invention is embodied in a multi-channel capacitive sensor array that integrates multiple independently resonant sensing elements into a single transmission medium, enabling simultaneous, frequency-multiplexed detection of proximity or touch events at distinct spatial locations. The core architecture consists of a plurality of capacitive sensing structures, each paired with a unique inductive element to form a discrete resonant circuit, all of which are embedded within a common microstrip transmission line. The resonant frequencies of these circuits are carefully selected to ensure spectral isolation, such that a perturbation affecting one element produces a measurable change exclusively at its designated frequency band, without influencing the response of any other element. This design fundamentally departs from conventional capacitive sensor arrays, which rely on time-division multiplexing or spatial scanning to resolve multiple events, by instead exploiting the frequency domain as a means of parallel channel separation.

In a first preferred embodiment, the sensor array is configured as a four-element multi-directional sensor system, designed to detect proximity events approaching from four orthogonal directions. Each sensing element comprises a circular copper patch, approximately 15 millimeters in diameter, deposited on the top surface of a three-layer printed circuit board with FR-4 dielectric substrate. Beneath the copper patch lies a continuous ground plane, separated from the patch by a 1.2-millimeter dielectric layer, forming a parallel-plate capacitor whose capacitance increases as a dielectric or conductive object approaches the patch surface. The capacitance variation is modeled as a function of distance, with simulations indicating a nonlinear increase in capacitance from approximately 8.5 picofarads at 20 millimeters to 11.6 picofarads at 2 millimeters, consistent with the expected behavior of a fringing-field capacitive sensor. Each copper patch is electrically connected to the ground plane through a surface-mounted inductor, which is soldered to the patch via a plated through-hole, thereby forming a series LC resonant circuit. The inductors are selected to have values of 100 nanohenries, 75 nanohenries, 45 nanohenries, and 20 nanohenries, respectively, such that the resulting resonant frequencies are approximately 174 megahertz, 211 megahertz, 303 megahertz, and 550 megahertz. These frequencies are chosen to provide sufficient separation between adjacent resonances, with the half-power bandwidth of each resonance being less than 15 megahertz under ideal conditions, ensuring minimal spectral overlap.

The inductors are connected to a common microstrip transmission line routed along the bottom layer of the PCB, with each connection made via a vertical via that penetrates the ground plane and couples the inductor to the transmission line. The transmission line is designed with a characteristic impedance of 50 ohms and is terminated at both ends with matched loads to minimize reflections. The entire structure is enclosed within a compact cubic housing, with the four sensing patches oriented to face outward along the positive and negative X, Y, and Z axes, enabling detection of objects approaching from any of the four primary directions. The ground planes of all four sensing elements are electrically bonded together to ensure a uniform reference potential and to suppress common-mode interference. The system is excited by a broadband RF signal generated by a vector network analyzer, which sweeps across the frequency range from 100 megahertz to 800 megahertz, and the transmission coefficient S21 is measured between the input and output ports of the transmission line.

In operation, when an object such as a block of wood is brought near one of the sensing patches, the capacitance of that patch increases, shifting the resonant frequency of its associated LC circuit to a lower value. This shift manifests as a measurable dip in the magnitude of S21 at the corresponding resonant frequency, while the transmission characteristics at all other frequencies remain unchanged. For example, when the object approaches the patch connected to the 100-nanohenry inductor, the resonance at 174 megahertz shifts downward, resulting in a reduction in S21 magnitude by more than 3 decibels at the original resonant frequency, while the S21 response at 211, 303, and 550 megahertz remains unaffected. This frequency-specific response enables unambiguous identification of the location of the proximity event, as each frequency band corresponds uniquely to a single sensing element. The system is capable of detecting multiple simultaneous events, as demonstrated by experiments in which two objects were brought near two different patches simultaneously, resulting in two distinct dips in the S21 spectrum, each localized to the frequency band of its respective sensor. No cross-talk or spectral interference was observed, confirming the decoupled nature of the resonant channels.

The performance of the system is further validated by comparing simulated and measured results. Circuit simulations performed in Advanced Design System (ADS) software, assuming ideal capacitors and inductors, predict sharp, well-separated resonances with minimal bandwidth and high isolation between channels. Experimental measurements, while exhibiting slightly broader resonances and minor frequency shifts due to parasitic inductances in the vias, solder joints, and patch geometry, confirm the fundamental principle of frequency-domain decoupling. The quality factor of the resonances, calculated as the ratio of resonant frequency to half-power bandwidth, ranges from approximately five to twelve in this embodiment, limited primarily by ohmic losses in the copper traces, dielectric losses in the FR-4 substrate, and parasitic capacitances introduced during assembly. Despite these limitations, the system achieves a sensing range of approximately 10 millimeters, with a resolution better than one millimeter, as determined by the rate of change in S21 magnitude with respect to distance. The figure-of-merit, defined as the ratio of the corrected sensing range (adjusted for the dielectric constant of the target object) to the geometric mean of the sensor dimensions, is calculated to be 0.25, which exceeds the mean of 0.221 observed in commercial proximity sensors and places the invention at the upper end of the performance distribution for non-contact sensing systems.

In a second preferred embodiment, the sensor array is configured as a linear array of four sensing elements, designed for one-dimensional position sensing along a straight line. Each sensing element consists of a square copper patch measuring 10 millimeters by 10 millimeters, integrated into a two-port microstrip transmission line on a 1.6-millimeter-thick FR-4 substrate. The inductors connected to each patch are selected to have values of 100 nanohenries, 68 nanohenries, 33 nanohenries, and 10 nanohenries, resulting in resonant frequencies of approximately 178 megahertz, 225 megahertz, 360 megahertz, and 720 megahertz, respectively. The transmission line is routed in a serpentine pattern to minimize physical spacing between elements while maintaining electromagnetic isolation. The ground plane is continuous and extends beneath the entire array, ensuring uniform field distribution and minimizing edge effects. The system is excited by a broadband signal spanning 100 megahertz to 1 gigahertz, and the transmission coefficient S21 is measured before and after the introduction of a target object.

When a dielectric object, such as a wooden block with a dielectric constant of 2 and a loss tangent of 0.02, is positioned 5 millimeters in front of one of the sensing patches, a distinct reduction in S21 magnitude is observed at the corresponding resonant frequency, with a peak change exceeding 3.5 decibels. The response at all other frequencies remains unchanged, confirming the absence of cross-talk. The difference spectrum, obtained by subtracting the baseline measurement from the measurement with the object present, reveals clear, isolated dips at the resonant frequencies of the loaded sensors, with no spurious peaks or distortions elsewhere in the spectrum. In experiments involving simultaneous loading of two adjacent sensors, such as sensors one and two, or sensors three and four, the resulting transmission spectrum exhibits two distinct dips, each corresponding precisely to the resonant frequency of the affected sensor. The system successfully distinguishes between single and dual events with no ambiguity, demonstrating its capability for parallel, multi-point detection in a linear configuration. The sensing range remains consistent at approximately 10 millimeters, with sub-millimeter resolution maintained due to the steep slope of the transmission magnitude curve near resonance.

In a third preferred embodiment, the inductive elements are replaced with metamaterial-based split-ring resonators, which are coupled to the microstrip transmission line rather than directly connected in series. Each split-ring resonator is fabricated as a single-turn copper ring with a 1-millimeter-wide gap, patterned on the same substrate as the capacitive patches. The rings are positioned adjacent to the transmission line, with the gap oriented perpendicular to the direction of current flow, enabling strong capacitive coupling between the ring and the microstrip. The capacitive patches are arranged in a linear array, each positioned directly above a split-ring resonator, with the patch connected to the ground plane through a short conductive trace. The resonant frequency of each split-ring structure is determined by its geometry, including the inner and outer radii, the width of the ring, and the size of the gap, allowing precise tuning of the resonance without requiring discrete inductors. The resulting transmission spectrum exhibits four distinct band-stop features, centered at frequencies of 2.6 gigahertz, 2.8 gigahertz, 3.0 gigahertz, and 3.3 gigahertz, with a fractional bandwidth of approximately 12 percent. The quality factor of each resonance exceeds 90, representing a more than tenfold improvement over the discrete inductor-based designs.

The high quality factor of the split-ring resonators enables significantly tighter frequency spacing between channels, allowing for the potential integration of dozens of sensing elements within a single gigahertz-wide bandwidth. When a target object is brought near one of the capacitive patches, the capacitance change alters the effective loading of the associated split-ring resonator, resulting in a measurable shift in the depth and position of the corresponding transmission dip. The response is highly localized, with no observable perturbation at frequencies adjacent to the target resonance. The sensing range remains comparable to previous embodiments, at approximately 10 millimeters, but the improved signal-to-noise ratio and sharper resonance peaks enable higher precision in detecting small capacitance changes, thereby enhancing resolution and enabling discrimination between objects of different dielectric properties. The use of metamaterial structures also eliminates the need for soldered components, reducing manufacturing complexity and improving mechanical reliability, particularly in environments subject to vibration or thermal cycling.

The fabrication process for all embodiments follows standard printed circuit board techniques, with copper layers patterned using photolithography, dielectric layers laminated under controlled pressure and temperature, and vias plated to ensure low-resistance connections. In the split-ring embodiment, all components—including the capacitive patches, the split-ring resonators, and the transmission line—are fabricated on a single substrate, eliminating the need for discrete components and enabling full monolithic integration. The system can be further miniaturized by reducing the size of the patches and resonators, or scaled up by increasing the number of elements, with the only constraint being the available bandwidth and the minimum achievable bandwidth of the resonant structures. The use of high-permittivity substrates such as Rogers RO4350B or Taconic TLY-5 can further improve the quality factor and reduce the physical footprint of the sensors.

The signal processing architecture for the system is designed to be compatible with existing RF measurement equipment, such as vector network analyzers, but is equally suited for integration into embedded sensing systems using compact microwave transceivers, phase-locked loops, and digital signal processors. The baseline transmission spectrum is stored in memory, and subsequent measurements are compared to this reference to detect deviations indicative of proximity events. A threshold detection algorithm is employed to identify significant changes in transmission magnitude, with the frequency of the deviation used to determine the location of the event. The system can be configured to output a digital signal indicating the identity of the activated sensor, or to provide an analog representation of the capacitance change for continuous monitoring. Calibration procedures account for environmental drift, temperature variations, and substrate aging, ensuring long-term stability and reliability.

The invention is not limited to the specific geometries, materials, or frequencies described herein. The principles of frequency-domain decoupling, high-Q resonant structures, and microstrip integration can be adapted to any number of sensing elements, any frequency range, and any physical configuration, including circular, spiral, or three-dimensional arrays. The capacitive patches may be replaced with other geometries such as rectangles, triangles, or concentric rings, depending on the desired field distribution and sensitivity profile. The inductive elements may be implemented using spiral inductors, meander lines, or even air-core coils, provided that the resulting resonance is sufficiently isolated from neighboring channels. The transmission medium may be replaced with coplanar waveguides, striplines, or coaxial structures, depending on the application requirements. The system may be integrated into wearable devices, robotic fingertips, touch-sensitive surfaces, or security portals, where the ability to detect multiple simultaneous touch or proximity events with high spatial resolution is critical.

The invention provides a transformative advancement in capacitive sensing technology by replacing sequential, time-multiplexed detection with parallel, frequency-multiplexed detection, thereby enabling real-time, multi-point sensing with unprecedented speed, accuracy, and scalability. The combination of frequency-domain isolation, high-quality-factor resonant structures, and integrated microstrip topologies creates a sensor architecture that is not only superior in performance to existing systems but also fundamentally new in its approach to spatial discrimination. The invention eliminates the need for complex multiplexing circuitry, reduces mechanical complexity, enhances reliability, and opens new avenues for intelligent sensing in industrial, medical, consumer, and security applications. The detailed embodiments described herein illustrate the practical implementation of these principles, demonstrating that the invention is not merely theoretical but is fully realizable, manufacturable, and deployable in real-world environments.
