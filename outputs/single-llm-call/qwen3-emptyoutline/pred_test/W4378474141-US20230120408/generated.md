# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### 1. Field of the Invention

The present invention relates to the field of superconducting thin film materials and devices, specifically to the fabrication of high-transition-temperature magnesium diboride (MgB₂) thin films with exceptional uniformity, smoothness, and structural integrity on commercially viable semiconductor substrates such as silicon. The invention further encompasses methods for the controlled deposition, annealing, and patterning of these films into functional superconducting microdevices, including microwave resonators, transmission lines, and kinetic inductance-based quantum circuits. The technology enables the scalable production of high-performance superconducting components operating at temperatures significantly above those of conventional low-temperature superconductors such as niobium, while maintaining compatibility with standard semiconductor fabrication processes. This advancement addresses long-standing challenges in the synthesis of MgB₂ films, particularly those arising from the high volatility of magnesium, its susceptibility to oxidation, and the difficulty in achieving stoichiometric control and grain growth without compromising surface morphology. The invention provides a robust, repeatable, and industrially scalable pathway to produce MgB₂ thin films with critical transition temperatures exceeding 37 K, surface roughness below 0.5 nm root-mean-square, and kinetic inductance values tunable over an unprecedented range, thereby unlocking new possibilities in high-frequency superconducting electronics, quantum information systems, and cryogenic sensing technologies.

### 2. Description of the Related Art

Prior efforts to fabricate magnesium diboride thin films have been hindered by fundamental material challenges inherent to the chemistry and physics of magnesium and boron. Magnesium, with its low melting point of approximately 650 °C and high vapor pressure at elevated temperatures, tends to evaporate during high-temperature processing, leading to non-stoichiometric films, void formation, and severe surface roughening. Boron, while possessing a high melting point of over 2000 °C, is notoriously difficult to sputter due to its low deposition rate, brittle nature, and tendency to form insulating surface oxides that impede uniform film growth. Conventional in-situ sputtering techniques, which attempt to deposit MgB₂ directly under elevated substrate temperatures, have consistently yielded films with transition temperatures below 22 K, attributed to magnesium loss, oxidation, and poor crystallinity. Post-deposition annealing has been explored as a remedy, but early approaches suffered from uncontrolled magnesium evaporation during thermal treatment, resulting in transition temperatures no higher than 6 K and surface roughness exceeding 10 nm rms, rendering such films unsuitable for device applications requiring nanoscale precision.

Attempts to mitigate magnesium loss through capping layers have been attempted using materials such as tantalum, which is chemically inert at moderate annealing temperatures. However, tantalum introduces its own complications: it cracks above 700 °C, introduces mechanical stress, and must be removed post-annealing via etching processes that risk damaging the underlying MgB₂ layer. Moreover, the removal of tantalum often leaves behind residues or induces surface contamination that degrades superconducting performance. Alternative capping strategies involving boron itself have been proposed, but the formation of surface boron oxide—while beneficial in its viscous, crack-free behavior at elevated temperatures—was not previously understood or harnessed in a controlled manner to serve as a self-healing, diffusion-blocking barrier. The potential for boron oxide to form a transient, liquid-like cap that encapsulates the Mg-B precursor during annealing, thereby suppressing magnesium evaporation while permitting interdiffusion and crystallization into MgB₂, has not been disclosed or demonstrated in prior art.

Substrate selection has also posed significant challenges. Sapphire substrates, despite their hexagonal crystal structure and minimal lattice mismatch with MgB₂, have been shown to facilitate oxygen diffusion from the substrate into the superconducting layer due to the lower oxidation enthalpy of magnesium relative to aluminum. This interdiffusion leads to stoichiometric deviations near the interface, particularly in thin films under 100 nm, resulting in degraded critical temperatures and inconsistent device performance. Silicon, while widely used in semiconductor manufacturing and economically advantageous, reacts with magnesium to form magnesium silicide (Mg₂Si), which is non-superconducting and disrupts the integrity of the MgB₂ layer. Buffer layers such as silicon nitride have been employed to prevent this reaction, but previous implementations lacked the precision in thickness control, stress management, and interface quality necessary to preserve the superconducting properties of the overlying MgB₂ film. Furthermore, the integration of silicon nitride as a buffer layer in conjunction with a boron capping strategy for high-temperature annealing has not been previously reported.

In terms of device fabrication, prior attempts to pattern MgB₂ into microwave resonators or quantum circuits have been limited by poor film uniformity, high surface roughness, and the absence of reliable passivation and contact schemes. Conventional photolithography and etching techniques, when applied to MgB₂, often result in oxidation, delamination, or chemical degradation due to exposure to ambient moisture and oxygen during processing. Existing contact metallization schemes, such as titanium or aluminum, have proven incompatible with the reactive nature of MgB₂, leading to interfacial reactions that increase contact resistance or suppress superconductivity. Additionally, the kinetic inductance of MgB₂ films—while theoretically advantageous due to its strong dependence on resistivity and the material’s two-gap superconducting nature—has never been systematically controlled or measured across a wide range of stoichiometries in a reproducible, wafer-scale context. Prior studies have relied on doping or non-stoichiometric compositions to tune resistivity, but these approaches are incompatible with reactive sputtering environments where gas partial pressures introduce uncontrollable hysteretic effects, particularly in materials like niobium nitride or titanium nitride.

The theoretical framework for understanding the two-gap superconductivity of MgB₂, as described by the Mattis-Bardeen formalism and extended by models incorporating inter-gap coupling, has remained largely untested in thin film geometries due to the lack of high-quality, uniform, and smooth films. Measurements of the temperature-dependent frequency shift in resonators, which are critical for quantifying the relative contributions of the large and small energy gaps, have been constrained by poor quality factors and high dielectric losses from substrate and interface effects. Consequently, the role of the smaller energy gap in determining kinetic inductance and microwave losses has remained speculative, with no experimental data from well-characterized, high-Tc MgB₂ films available to validate or refine theoretical predictions.

Moreover, the scalability of MgB₂ fabrication has been severely limited by the absence of a process that combines room-temperature deposition, controlled annealing, and compatible device processing into a single, repeatable workflow. While some groups have reported localized improvements in transition temperature or surface smoothness, none have demonstrated a method that simultaneously achieves: (1) sub-nanometer surface roughness on wafer-scale substrates; (2) transition temperatures exceeding 35 K; (3) precise stoichiometric tuning via sputtering power control; (4) integration with silicon-based semiconductor processes; (5) reliable passivation and contact formation; and (6) reproducible fabrication of microscale superconducting devices with quantifiable microwave performance metrics. The present invention overcomes these limitations by introducing a novel combination of deposition parameters, capping strategies, buffer layer engineering, and thermal processing protocols that collectively enable the first truly practical and industrially viable pathway to high-performance MgB₂ thin films and devices.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method for fabricating high-transition-temperature magnesium diboride thin films with exceptional uniformity, smoothness, and structural integrity on silicon substrates, enabling the scalable production of superconducting microdevices for microwave and quantum applications. The invention comprises a multi-step process that begins with the co-sputtering of magnesium and boron at room temperature to form a composite precursor film, wherein the surface roughness is minimized through the application of a low radiofrequency substrate bias, resulting in root-mean-square roughness values below 0.5 nm. This as-deposited film is then capped with a thin layer of elemental boron, which, during subsequent rapid thermal annealing, forms a transient, viscous, crack-free surface layer of boron oxide that effectively suppresses magnesium evaporation while permitting interdiffusion and crystallization into stoichiometric MgB₂. The annealing process is carefully controlled to occur below the melting point of magnesium, ensuring that no unreacted magnesium is lost to vaporization, thereby preserving stoichiometry and minimizing surface roughening. The use of a silicon nitride buffer layer, deposited via low-pressure chemical vapor deposition, prevents detrimental interdiffusion between the silicon substrate and the magnesium diboride layer, while maintaining mechanical compatibility and thermal stability throughout the process. The resulting films exhibit critical transition temperatures exceeding 37 K, with sheet resistivities that can be precisely tuned over a range of fivefold by adjusting the relative sputtering power applied to the magnesium target, thereby enabling direct control over kinetic inductance without the need for chemical doping or gas-phase reactive processes. These films are patterned into superconducting microdevices using a tri-purpose titanium-gold bilayer as a hardmask and passivation layer, which protects the MgB₂ during photolithography and etching, serves as an etch stop during ion milling, and remains as the final electrical contact after removal of the underlying MgB₂ in non-device regions. The fabricated devices demonstrate internal quality factors exceeding 10⁴ at 4.5 K, with negligible temperature dependence down to 1 K, indicating that losses are dominated by dielectric and interfacial effects rather than intrinsic superconducting quasiparticle dissipation. Kinetic inductance values are measured to range between 5 and 50 pH per square, with the potential to reach near-nH per square values through further film thinning, making these films uniquely suited for high-impedance superconducting circuits, parametric amplifiers, and kinetic inductance quantum bits. The invention further enables the fabrication of critical features as small as 0.5 micrometers with high yield, demonstrating full compatibility with standard semiconductor manufacturing infrastructure. The method is fully scalable to 200-mm wafer sizes, utilizes commercially available deposition and annealing equipment, and requires no specialized gases or hazardous chemicals, making it immediately implementable in existing foundry environments. The resulting films and devices exhibit superior performance metrics compared to any previously reported sputtered MgB₂ films, and establish a new benchmark for high-temperature superconducting thin films on silicon substrates.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Technical Description

The invention encompasses a comprehensive and integrated process for the fabrication of high-performance magnesium diboride thin films and their integration into functional superconducting microdevices, with particular emphasis on achieving exceptional control over film morphology, stoichiometry, and superconducting properties while maintaining compatibility with standard semiconductor manufacturing practices. The process begins with the preparation of a high-resistivity silicon substrate, which is first cleaned using standard semiconductor wet-chemical protocols to remove organic and particulate contaminants. A buffer layer of silicon nitride is then deposited on the substrate using low-pressure chemical vapor deposition, with process parameters tuned to produce a film with tensile stress between 50 and 150 megapascals and a thickness ranging from 20 to 150 nanometers. This buffer layer is critical to preventing the formation of magnesium silicide, a non-superconducting interfacial compound that would otherwise form due to the thermodynamic favorability of magnesium reacting with silicon at elevated temperatures. The silicon nitride layer must be sufficiently thick to eliminate pinholes or defects that could expose the underlying silicon, yet thin enough to minimize stress-induced cracking or delamination during subsequent thermal cycling. The deposition is performed using standard industrial equipment, and the resulting film exhibits excellent conformality, uniformity, and adhesion, with no measurable diffusion of oxygen or nitrogen into the MgB₂ layer during subsequent processing, as confirmed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy depth profiling.

Following buffer layer deposition, the substrate is transferred under vacuum to a magnetron sputtering system equipped with separate elemental magnesium and boron targets. The deposition is performed at a base pressure of approximately 1 to 2 millitorr, with the substrate maintained at room temperature throughout the entire co-deposition process. The boron target is sputtered at a fixed radiofrequency power of 580 watts, chosen to maximize the deposition rate while minimizing target poisoning and arcing, which are common issues due to boron’s low electrical conductivity and tendency to form insulating surface layers. The magnesium target is sputtered at variable radiofrequency powers ranging from 50 to 250 watts, allowing precise control over the magnesium-to-boron atomic ratio in the as-deposited film. The use of room temperature deposition is a critical innovation, as it prevents the premature evaporation of magnesium and ensures a uniform distribution of both elements across the substrate surface. The co-deposited film is inherently amorphous or nanocrystalline, with no discernible superconducting transition prior to annealing, as confirmed by four-point probe resistance measurements and transport characterization.

To further reduce surface roughness, a low radiofrequency substrate bias of 15 watts is applied during deposition. This bias provides sufficient ion energy to densify the growing film and smooth out surface asperities without inducing excessive heating or causing preferential sputtering of magnesium. The resulting as-deposited films exhibit root-mean-square surface roughness values between 0.34 and 0.45 nanometers, as measured by atomic force microscopy over a 10-micrometer scan area. This level of smoothness is unprecedented for Mg-B composite films and is essential for the subsequent formation of high-quality, continuous MgB₂ grains during annealing. Higher substrate bias levels, exceeding 20 watts, were found to cause excessive etching of the magnesium component, leading to non-uniform stoichiometry and the formation of localized regions of boron-rich or magnesium-deficient material, which degrade the final superconducting properties. The 15-watt bias represents an optimal balance between surface smoothing and compositional integrity.

After deposition, the Mg-B composite film is immediately capped with a thin layer of elemental boron, deposited at the same 580-watt RF power used for the precursor layer. The thickness of this capping layer is maintained between 10 and 50 nanometers, chosen to be sufficient to fully encapsulate the underlying film and prevent magnesium vapor escape during annealing, yet thin enough to allow for complete diffusion and reaction during the thermal treatment. The boron cap is not merely a passive barrier; upon heating, the surface of the boron layer oxidizes in trace amounts of residual oxygen present in the vacuum chamber, forming a thin, viscous layer of boron oxide with a melting point of approximately 450 degrees Celsius. This oxide layer becomes liquid during the annealing process, creating a self-sealing, crack-free, and conformal enclosure over the entire film surface. This liquid oxide layer acts as a dynamic diffusion barrier, suppressing magnesium evaporation while permitting the diffusion of magnesium atoms upward into the underlying boron-rich matrix, where they react to form crystalline MgB₂. The formation of this transient liquid cap is a key innovation that distinguishes the present invention from all prior art, as it eliminates the need for external encapsulation materials such as tantalum, which introduce mechanical stress, require removal steps, and are prone to cracking.

The annealing process is performed using a rapid thermal processor, which provides precise, uniform, and fast thermal ramping across the entire wafer surface. The annealing temperature is held below 650 degrees Celsius, the melting point of magnesium, to prevent any unreacted magnesium from evaporating. The ramp rate is controlled between 1 and 33 degrees Celsius per second, depending on the desired grain structure: slower ramp rates (1–5 °C/s) are used for co-sputtered films to promote homogeneous nucleation and fine-grained microstructure, while faster ramp rates (10–33 °C/s) are employed for layered Mg-B structures to induce massive magnesium migration and the formation of large, columnar MgB₂ grains. The annealing time is maintained between 1 and 5 minutes, with longer durations favoring increased grain size and improved crystallinity. The resulting films exhibit a transition temperature (Tc,0) of up to 37.2 Kelvin, the highest ever reported for sputtered MgB₂ films, with a sharp superconducting transition width of less than 1.5 Kelvin. This performance is achieved on silicon substrates with silicon nitride buffers, a combination previously considered incompatible with high-Tc MgB₂ due to the risk of interdiffusion and silicide formation.

The crystalline structure of the resulting MgB₂ films is confirmed through X-ray diffraction, which reveals a strong (002) preferred orientation, consistent with hexagonal crystal symmetry. Transmission electron microscopy, performed on focused ion beam-prepared lamellae, shows a dense, continuous MgB₂ layer with grain sizes ranging from 50 to 500 nanometers, depending on the annealing protocol. In films annealed with fast ramp rates and layered precursors, large grains exceeding 300 nanometers in lateral dimension are observed, accompanied by the formation of voids at grain boundaries. These voids, while detrimental to surface smoothness, are acceptable for applications requiring high kinetic inductance and large critical current densities, provided the active device area is sufficiently small. The films exhibit no evidence of secondary phases such as MgB₄ or MgB₇, as confirmed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, which show only the expected binding energies for magnesium and boron in the MgB₂ stoichiometry. The absence of these byproducts is attributed to their higher formation energy relative to MgB₂, ensuring thermodynamic favorability of the desired phase.

The electrical properties of the films are characterized using a four-point probe technique, revealing a direct linear correlation between the resistivity of the as-deposited Mg-B composite and the resistivity of the post-annealed MgB₂ film. This correlation arises because the resistivity of the precursor film is determined by the percolation pathways of magnesium, which, upon reaction with boron, become the percolating superconducting channels of MgB₂. By varying the magnesium sputtering power, the sheet resistance of the final film can be tuned from 10 to 100 ohms per square, corresponding to a kinetic inductance range of 5 to 50 picohenries per square for films of approximately 40 nanometers thickness. This level of tunability is unprecedented in superconducting thin films deposited via reactive sputtering, as it avoids the complex gas-phase chemistry required for materials such as niobium nitride or titanium nitride, where small variations in nitrogen partial pressure lead to large, hysteretic changes in composition and properties. The kinetic inductance values are measured using microwave resonator techniques, with the films patterned into quarter-wave coplanar waveguide resonators. The measured values are consistent with theoretical predictions based on the Mattis-Bardeen formalism, with the kinetic inductance scaling linearly with sheet resistance and inversely with film thickness.

The fabrication of superconducting microdevices begins with the removal of the boron cap using an argon ion mill, performed under ultra-high vacuum conditions to prevent oxidation of the exposed MgB₂ surface. Immediately following cap removal, a tri-purpose titanium-gold bilayer is deposited onto the entire wafer surface. The titanium layer, approximately 20 nanometers thick, serves as an adhesion promoter and etch stop, while the gold layer, approximately 100 nanometers thick, functions as a passivation layer against ambient moisture and oxygen. This bilayer is patterned using standard photolithography with AZ5209-E or OiR 620 photoresists, followed by chlorine-based inductively coupled plasma etching to define the contact pads and hardmask regions. Once the pattern is transferred, the photoresist is stripped with acetone, and the entire wafer is subjected to argon ion milling to remove all exposed MgB₂ material outside the defined device areas. The titanium-gold bilayer remains intact, protecting the underlying MgB₂ and serving as the final electrical contact. After ion milling, a dielectric passivation layer—either silicon dioxide, silicon nitride, or aluminum nitride—is deposited over the entire structure to further shield the superconducting film from environmental degradation. The resulting devices exhibit excellent yield, with over 95% of fabricated resonators showing superconducting behavior and no signs of delamination or oxidation.

Microwave characterization is performed in a liquid helium cryostat using a vector network analyzer, with the devices coupled via microstrip probes. The internal quality factor (Qi) of the resonators is extracted by fitting the S21 transmission response to a standard coupled-resonator model. The highest measured Qi value exceeds 10⁴ at 4.5 Kelvin, with no measurable temperature dependence between 1.5 and 1.0 Kelvin, strongly suggesting that losses are dominated by dielectric and interfacial effects rather than intrinsic superconducting quasiparticle dissipation. The consistency of Qi across multiple devices fabricated on different wafers confirms the reproducibility of the process. The quality factor is found to scale inversely with the width of the center conductor, with narrower traces exhibiting higher dielectric fill factors and correspondingly lower Qi values, as demonstrated by measurements on five resonators with widths ranging from 3 to 10 micrometers. This behavior confirms that the dominant loss mechanism is not the BCS surface resistance of the MgB₂, which would exhibit an f² dependence, but rather the dielectric losses associated with the substrate and passivation layers. Further optimization of the dielectric stack, including the use of low-loss materials such as sapphire or high-purity silicon dioxide, is expected to push Qi values beyond 10⁵.

The temperature-dependent frequency shift of the resonators is analyzed using a modified two-gap model derived from prior theoretical work, with constraints applied to the gap values to ensure physical consistency. The fit reveals that the smaller energy gap contributes approximately 12% to the overall frequency shift, a non-negligible contribution that had not been quantified in polycrystalline MgB₂ films before. This finding suggests that even in the presence of grain boundaries and structural disorder, the two-gap nature of MgB₂ persists and contributes measurably to the microwave response. The kinetic inductance, which is dominated by the smaller gap, remains high and stable across the measured temperature range, confirming the suitability of these films for high-impedance quantum circuits. The ability to tune the kinetic inductance over such a wide range, combined with the high transition temperature and low microwave losses, opens new avenues for the design of kinetic inductance-based qubits, parametric amplifiers, and frequency multipliers operating at temperatures above 4 Kelvin, where conventional niobium-based devices suffer from reduced performance due to higher thermal noise.

The films are also shown to be compatible with post-fabrication thinning techniques. Using either argon ion milling or bromine plasma etching, the MgB₂ layer can be reduced in thickness from 40 nanometers down to 15 nanometers without degradation of the superconducting properties, as confirmed by transport and microwave measurements. Thinner films exhibit higher kinetic inductance per square, with values approaching 100 picohenries per square, and the potential to reach near-nanohenry per square values is demonstrated through extrapolation. This tunability is critical for applications requiring high impedance, such as single-photon detectors and quantum-limited amplifiers, where traditional materials such as aluminum or niobium are limited by their low kinetic inductance and low transition temperatures.

The entire process is fully compatible with standard semiconductor manufacturing infrastructure, utilizing equipment commonly found in foundries for CMOS, MEMS, and RF device fabrication. The use of RF magnetron sputtering, rapid thermal processing, LPCVD, photolithography, and plasma etching ensures that the method can be scaled to 200-millimeter wafers without requiring specialized or proprietary tools. The absence of toxic gases, high-pressure environments, or cryogenic deposition steps makes the process safe, cost-effective, and suitable for high-volume production. The resulting MgB₂ films and devices represent a transformative advancement in superconducting electronics, offering a path to practical, high-performance, wafer-scale superconducting circuits that operate at temperatures previously accessible only with cryogenic cooling systems designed for niobium-based devices.

## CONCLUSION

The invention described herein establishes a fundamentally new paradigm for the fabrication of high-transition-temperature superconducting thin films on silicon substrates, overcoming decades of persistent challenges associated with magnesium volatility, oxidation sensitivity, and poor interfacial control. By integrating a room-temperature co-sputtering process with a boron capping strategy that leverages the formation of a transient, viscous boron oxide layer during annealing, the invention achieves unprecedented control over film stoichiometry, surface morphology, and crystalline structure. The resulting MgB₂ films exhibit critical transition temperatures exceeding 37 K, surface roughness below 0.5 nm rms, and kinetic inductance values that can be precisely tuned over a fivefold range through simple adjustment of sputtering power—capabilities that have never been simultaneously realized in any prior art. The use of a silicon nitride buffer layer ensures compatibility with silicon-based semiconductor platforms, while the tri-purpose titanium-gold bilayer enables robust, reproducible device fabrication without compromising superconducting properties. The demonstrated internal quality factors exceeding 10⁴, the absence of temperature-dependent losses down to 1 K, and the successful patterning of sub-micrometer features confirm the maturity of the process for real-world applications in microwave and quantum technologies. The ability to fabricate these films on commercially available, large-area silicon wafers using standard industrial equipment renders the invention immediately scalable and economically viable. This technology enables the next generation of high-temperature superconducting devices, including kinetic inductance quantum bits, parametric amplifiers, frequency multipliers, and ultra-sensitive photon detectors, all operating at temperatures significantly higher than those of conventional superconductors. The invention not only resolves longstanding material science challenges but also opens a new frontier in superconducting electronics, where tunability, scalability, and performance converge for the first time in a single, manufacturable platform.
