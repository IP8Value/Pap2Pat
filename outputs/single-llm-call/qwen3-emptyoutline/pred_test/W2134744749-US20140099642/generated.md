# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of noninvasive prenatal diagnostics, specifically to a method, system, and computer-readable medium for the detection of fetal genetic abnormalities using massively parallel sequencing of cell-free DNA from maternal plasma. More particularly, the invention provides a novel, statistically robust, GC-content-correlated analytical framework that enables highly accurate identification of autosomal and sex chromosomal aneuploidies, including trisomy 13, trisomy 18, trisomy 21, Turner syndrome (45,X), Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY), and XYY syndrome, without the need for invasive procedures such as amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling. The invention leverages the intrinsic relationship between the genomic GC content of individual chromosomes and the sequencing bias introduced during library preparation and high-throughput sequencing, applying a dual-hypothesis t-test model calibrated by chromosome-specific GC-correlation curves and fetal DNA fraction estimation to achieve unprecedented sensitivity and specificity. This method is applicable across all gestational stages from ten to thirty-four weeks and is compatible with standard Illumina sequencing platforms, making it suitable for integration into routine clinical prenatal screening workflows. The invention further encompasses a computational system and machine-readable medium capable of automating the entire analytical pipeline, from raw read alignment through to diagnostic decision-making, thereby enabling scalable, reproducible, and operator-independent diagnosis of fetal chromosomal disorders.

## BACKGROUND ART

Prenatal diagnosis of chromosomal abnormalities has long been a cornerstone of obstetric care, with the goal of identifying life-altering conditions such as Down syndrome, Edwards syndrome, and Patau syndrome prior to birth. Traditional diagnostic methods, including karyotyping, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), and quantitative fluorescent polymerase chain reaction (QF-PCR), rely on the direct analysis of fetal cells obtained through invasive procedures such as amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling. While these techniques offer high diagnostic accuracy, they carry a nontrivial risk of procedure-related miscarriage, estimated to range between 0.1% and 0.5% per intervention, which limits their utility as first-line screening tools. Noninvasive alternatives based on maternal serum biomarkers and ultrasound measurements, such as the combined first-trimester screen, have been widely adopted due to their safety profile; however, these approaches suffer from low sensitivity and specificity, with false positive rates exceeding 5% and detection rates for trisomy 18 and trisomy 13 often falling below 70%, rendering them inadequate for definitive clinical decision-making.

A paradigm shift occurred with the discovery that cell-free fetal DNA (cff-DNA) is present in maternal plasma as early as four weeks of gestation, originating from apoptotic trophoblastic cells and rapidly cleared from circulation postpartum. This biological phenomenon opened the door to noninvasive prenatal testing (NIPT) through the analysis of fragmented fetal DNA in maternal blood. However, the fraction of fetal DNA in maternal plasma typically ranges between 5% and 10%, with considerable inter-individual variability, making the detection of fetal-specific genetic alterations a formidable analytical challenge. Early attempts to exploit this biomarker relied on targeted molecular techniques such as allele-specific PCR or quantitative real-time PCR, but these methods were inherently limited to cases where the fetus carried a heterozygous paternal allele, thereby excluding a substantial proportion of pregnancies from testing.

The advent of massively parallel sequencing (MPS) technologies revolutionized the field by enabling the unbiased, genome-wide quantification of cell-free DNA fragments. Initial studies demonstrated that the relative abundance of sequencing reads mapping to each chromosome could be used to infer chromosomal copy number, with aneuploidies manifesting as statistically significant deviations in read counts. For example, trisomy 21 was shown to produce a measurable increase in chromosome 21-derived reads relative to the expected diploid baseline. However, these early approaches, which employed standard z-score normalization based on the mean and standard deviation of read counts across reference samples, encountered significant limitations in detecting trisomy 13 and trisomy 18. These chromosomes, which possess inherently lower GC content compared to chromosome 21, exhibited disproportionately reduced sequencing coverage due to biases introduced during library preparation, PCR amplification, and sequencing chemistry. This GC-dependent sequencing bias, which arises from the preferential amplification and sequencing of DNA fragments with intermediate GC content, introduced systematic noise that obscured true fetal aneuploidy signals, particularly for chromosomes with GC content below 41%.

Subsequent efforts to mitigate this bias included the development of GC-correction algorithms that attempted to normalize read counts by fitting them to a global GC content curve derived from euploid controls. While these methods improved detection rates for trisomy 18 and trisomy 13, they remained vulnerable to confounding factors such as fetal sex, gestational age, placental mosaicism, and maternal copy number variants. Moreover, the detection of sex chromosomal aneuploidies remained problematic, as methods relying on internal chromosome controls—such as using chromosome 13 as a reference to normalize chromosome X—were prone to misclassification due to the variable baseline coverage of autosomes and the complex dynamics of X-chromosome dosage compensation. Additionally, the estimation of fetal DNA fraction, a critical parameter for interpreting read count deviations, was often performed using locus-specific markers such as Y-chromosome sequences in male pregnancies, which rendered the method inapplicable to female fetuses and introduced bias in the calculation of fetal fraction across diverse populations.

Furthermore, prior methods typically relied on a single statistical hypothesis—either assuming euploidy and detecting deviations as positive signals, or assuming aneuploidy and testing for its rejection—without incorporating a dual-hypothesis framework that simultaneously evaluates the likelihood of both states. This limitation reduced diagnostic confidence, particularly in borderline cases where sequencing depth was suboptimal or fetal fraction was low. The absence of a comprehensive quality control system that integrated multiple direct and indirect metrics—such as sequencing depth, PCR duplication rate, base quality scores, and consistency of GC-correlation residuals—further compromised the reliability of results across different laboratories and sequencing platforms. As a result, despite the theoretical promise of MPS-based NIPT, its clinical implementation remained inconsistent, with variable performance across aneuploidy types, gestational windows, and fetal sexes, and with insufficient sensitivity for detecting mosaic cases or rare sex chromosome abnormalities.

There remains, therefore, a critical unmet need in the field for a noninvasive prenatal diagnostic method that achieves near-perfect sensitivity and specificity for all major autosomal and sex chromosomal aneuploidies, regardless of gestational age, fetal sex, or underlying GC content of the affected chromosome. Such a method must account for the complex interplay between sequencing bias, fetal DNA fraction, and chromosomal architecture, while providing a statistically rigorous, automated, and reproducible diagnostic output that can be reliably deployed in clinical settings. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel analytical framework that systematically corrects for GC bias through chromosome-specific correlation modeling, estimates fetal DNA concentration using a sex chromosome-based dual-reference model, and applies a dual-hypothesis t-test with logarithmic likelihood odds ratio to produce a diagnostic decision with quantifiable confidence, thereby enabling the first truly comprehensive and robust noninvasive detection of fetal aneuploidies.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method, system, and computer-readable medium for the noninvasive detection of fetal genetic abnormalities through the analysis of cell-free DNA in maternal plasma using massively parallel sequencing. The invention is based on the discovery that the relationship between sequencing coverage and the inherent GC content of individual chromosomes is not only consistent across euploid pregnancies but also exhibits distinct, chromosome-specific patterns that can be modeled with high fidelity. By establishing these GC-correlation curves for each chromosome using a large cohort of euploid reference samples, the invention enables the prediction of expected sequencing coverage under normal diploid conditions, thereby allowing the detection of deviations attributable to fetal aneuploidy with exceptional precision. The method further incorporates a novel estimation of fetal DNA concentration that leverages the differential inheritance patterns of the X and Y chromosomes in male and female fetuses, enabling accurate quantification of fetal fraction even in the absence of paternal genetic information. This fetal fraction estimate is then integrated into a dual-hypothesis statistical model that simultaneously evaluates the likelihood of euploidy and trisomy for each chromosome, producing a logarithmic likelihood odds ratio that serves as a quantitative measure of diagnostic confidence. The invention further includes a gender classification system based on both threshold-based criteria and logistic regression, which enables the targeted detection of sex chromosomal aneuploidies such as Turner syndrome, Klinefelter syndrome, and XYY syndrome with high specificity. The method is further enhanced by a comprehensive quality control protocol that evaluates sequencing data integrity through multiple direct and indirect metrics, ensuring that only high-quality samples proceed to diagnostic analysis. The invention is implemented as a fully automated computational pipeline that processes raw sequencing reads, aligns them to a reference genome, computes coverage and GC content, applies GC-correlation correction, estimates fetal fraction, performs statistical testing, classifies fetal sex, and generates a diagnostic report—all without manual intervention. The result is a diagnostic system capable of detecting autosomal trisomies with 100% sensitivity and greater than 99.7% specificity, and sex chromosomal aneuploidies with sensitivity exceeding 75% and specificity greater than 99.9%, across all gestational ages from ten to thirty-four weeks. The invention eliminates the need for invasive procedures, overcomes the limitations of prior GC-correction methods, and provides a scalable, reproducible, and clinically actionable diagnostic tool for the comprehensive screening of fetal chromosomal abnormalities.

### II. ESTABLISHING A RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN COVERAGE DEPTH AND GC CONTENT

The foundation of the present invention lies in the systematic characterization and modeling of the relationship between sequencing coverage depth and the inherent GC content of individual chromosomes. Prior methods in the field treated GC bias as a uniform, genome-wide artifact that could be corrected using a single global adjustment factor. However, the inventors discovered that this assumption is fundamentally flawed, as the effect of GC bias varies significantly across chromosomes due to their distinct genomic architectures and baseline GC compositions. Chromosomes such as 19 and 22, which are rich in gene-dense regions and exhibit high GC content, consistently demonstrate elevated sequencing coverage compared to chromosomes such as 4 and 13, which are GC-poor and contain more repetitive and heterochromatic regions. This variation is not random but is instead a predictable, reproducible phenomenon that can be quantified and modeled with high accuracy using a large cohort of euploid reference samples. To establish this relationship, a set of 300 maternal plasma samples from pregnancies with confirmed euploid fetuses was selected, and each sample was subjected to massively parallel sequencing using standardized library preparation protocols and Illumina sequencing platforms. After alignment of sequencing reads to the human reference genome (hg18), the number of uniquely mapped reads for each chromosome was counted, and the relative coverage for each chromosome was calculated by normalizing the absolute read count for that chromosome by the mean read count across all 22 autosomes in the same sample. Concurrently, the GC content of each chromosome was computed as the average percentage of guanine and cytosine bases across all possible 35-base-pair subsequences (35-mers) contained within that chromosome, thereby capturing the intrinsic compositional bias of each chromosome independent of sequencing artifacts. The resulting data revealed a non-linear, chromosome-specific correlation between relative coverage and GC content. For chromosomes with an average GC content greater than 41%, a significant positive correlation was observed: as GC content increased, so did the relative sequencing coverage. Conversely, for chromosomes with an average GC content less than 41%, a significant negative correlation emerged: higher GC content was associated with lower relative coverage. Chromosomes with GC content near 41% exhibited no significant correlation, indicating a transition point at which the sequencing bias was neutralized. This biphasic relationship was consistent across all 300 reference samples, demonstrating that the effect of GC bias is not stochastic but is instead a deterministic function of chromosomal sequence composition. To further elucidate this phenomenon, the inventors clustered all 36 possible 35-mer classes—ranging from 0 to 35 GC bases—into a matrix and performed hierarchical clustering on the chromosomes based on their 35-mer frequency profiles. The resulting dendrogram revealed that chromosomes with similar GC content profiles clustered together: chromosomes 19 and 22, both GC-rich, formed a tight cluster, while chromosomes 4 and 13, both GC-poor, grouped together. This clustering confirmed that the sequencing bias is not merely a function of overall GC content but is deeply rooted in the fine-scale nucleotide composition of the genome. The inventors then performed a logistic regression analysis to model the relationship between relative coverage and GC content for each chromosome individually, yielding a unique fitted curve for each chromosome. These curves were not linear but exhibited sigmoidal or quadratic shapes, reflecting the complex interplay between PCR amplification efficiency, sequencing yield, and fragment length distribution. The fitted curves were then validated by applying them to an independent set of 150 euploid samples, where the predicted coverage based on GC content closely matched the observed coverage, with residuals distributed normally around zero. The standard deviation of these residuals was calculated for each chromosome and found to be stable when the reference cohort exceeded 100 samples, indicating that the model reached statistical saturation and was robust to sample size. This chromosome-specific GC-correlation model forms the core of the diagnostic method, as it provides a precise, empirically derived expectation of coverage for each chromosome under euploid conditions, eliminating the need for arbitrary normalization or global scaling. By replacing the assumption of uniform coverage with a biologically grounded, chromosome-specific prediction, the invention dramatically reduces the noise inherent in prior methods, thereby enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio for the detection of true aneuploidies. This approach is fundamentally distinct from prior GC-correction techniques, which either applied a single correction factor to all chromosomes or used a linear fit across the entire genome, both of which failed to capture the nuanced, non-uniform nature of GC bias. The invention’s ability to model this relationship with such precision enables the detection of subtle deviations in coverage that would otherwise be masked by technical variability, particularly for chromosomes such as 13 and 18, which have historically been difficult to assess due to their low GC content and correspondingly low coverage. The establishment of this relationship is not merely a technical refinement but represents a paradigm shift in the interpretation of sequencing data, transforming GC bias from a confounding artifact into a predictive feature that enhances diagnostic accuracy.

### III. DETERMINING A FETAL GENETIC ABNORMALITY

The determination of fetal genetic abnormalities in the present invention is achieved through a novel dual-hypothesis statistical framework that simultaneously evaluates the likelihood of euploidy and aneuploidy for each chromosome, using the GC-correlation model as the foundation for expected coverage. For each test sample, the relative coverage of each chromosome is first calculated and compared to the fitted coverage value predicted by the chromosome-specific GC-correlation curve. This comparison yields a residual, representing the deviation between observed and expected coverage. The standard deviation of these residuals, derived from the reference cohort, is then used to normalize the deviation, producing a t-statistic. However, unlike prior methods that apply a single hypothesis test assuming euploidy and flagging deviations as positive, the present invention performs two complementary t-tests for each chromosome. The first test evaluates the null hypothesis that the fetus is euploid against the alternative hypothesis that the fetus is trisomic. The t-value for this test is calculated as the difference between the observed relative coverage and the fitted coverage, divided by the chromosome-specific standard deviation of residuals. The second test reverses the hypotheses: it evaluates the null hypothesis that the fetus is trisomic against the alternative hypothesis that the fetus is euploid. Crucially, this second test incorporates the estimated fetal DNA fraction into the model, adjusting the expected coverage under trisomy to account for the dilution effect of maternal DNA. Specifically, under a trisomic condition, the expected relative coverage is not simply the fitted value but is instead the fitted value multiplied by (1 + ε/2), where ε is the estimated fetal DNA fraction. This adjustment is based on the biological principle that in a trisomic fetus, the contribution of fetal DNA to the total cell-free DNA pool increases the relative abundance of the affected chromosome by half the fetal fraction, since the fetus contributes one extra copy of the chromosome. The t-value for the second test is then calculated as the difference between the observed coverage and this adjusted expected coverage, again divided by the standard deviation. The two t-values, derived from opposing hypotheses, are then used to compute a logarithmic likelihood odds ratio, which quantifies the relative probability of the data under the two competing models. This ratio is expressed as the natural logarithm of the probability of the first t-value under the null hypothesis (euploidy) divided by the probability of the second t-value under the null hypothesis (trisomy). A high logarithmic likelihood odds ratio indicates strong evidence in favor of euploidy, while a low or negative ratio indicates strong evidence in favor of trisomy. A diagnostic threshold is established such that if the logarithmic likelihood odds ratio exceeds a predefined value—such as 1—the sample is classified as euploid; if it falls below -1, the sample is classified as trisomic; and if it lies between -1 and 1, the result is deemed inconclusive. This dual-hypothesis approach is fundamentally superior to single-hypothesis methods because it does not rely on the assumption that aneuploidy is the only possible explanation for coverage deviation. Instead, it explicitly considers both possibilities and quantifies the evidence for each, thereby reducing false positives that arise from technical noise or biological variability. For example, in a sample with low fetal fraction, a minor deviation in coverage might be interpreted as aneuploidy by a single-hypothesis method, but the dual-hypothesis framework will recognize that the deviation is more likely due to sampling error than true trisomy, because the adjusted trisomy model does not fit the data well. Conversely, in a sample with high fetal fraction and a clear deviation, the adjusted model will fit the data significantly better than the euploid model, leading to a strong negative odds ratio and a confident diagnosis. This approach is particularly powerful for detecting trisomy 13 and trisomy 18, which are often missed by conventional methods due to their low coverage and high technical noise. The inclusion of fetal fraction in the trisomy model ensures that the diagnostic test is calibrated to the biological reality of each pregnancy, rather than applying a one-size-fits-all threshold. The method further enhances specificity by applying chromosome-specific thresholds, as the standard deviation of residuals varies across chromosomes due to differences in GC content, repeat content, and sequencing efficiency. Chromosomes with higher variability, such as those with extreme GC content, are assigned higher thresholds, while chromosomes with low variability, such as chromosome 21, are assigned lower thresholds, ensuring that diagnostic confidence is proportional to the reliability of the underlying data. The result is a diagnostic system that achieves 100% sensitivity and greater than 99.7% specificity for autosomal trisomies, with zero false negatives and only a single false positive among 891 euploid controls. This level of performance is unattainable by any prior method, which either overcorrected for GC bias and introduced false negatives or undercorrected and generated excessive false positives. The dual-hypothesis framework, combined with the GC-correlation model and fetal fraction adjustment, represents a quantum leap in diagnostic accuracy, transforming noninvasive prenatal testing from a probabilistic screening tool into a definitive diagnostic modality.

### IV. COMPUTER READABLE MEDIUM AND SYSTEM FOR DIAGNOSIS OF A FETAL GENETIC ABNORMALITY

The present invention further encompasses a computer-readable medium and a dedicated system for the automated diagnosis of fetal genetic abnormalities, enabling the seamless integration of the diagnostic method into clinical laboratories and diagnostic platforms. The computer-readable medium is a non-transitory storage device, such as a solid-state drive, hard disk, or optical disc, encoded with a set of executable instructions that, when loaded into a processor, cause the system to perform the steps of the diagnostic method. These instructions include modules for raw read alignment, GC content calculation, GC-correlation modeling, fetal fraction estimation, dual-hypothesis statistical testing, fetal sex classification, and diagnostic report generation. The system comprises a computing apparatus with one or more central processing units, random-access memory, persistent storage, and input/output interfaces, and is operatively connected to a massively parallel sequencing instrument or to a data feed from a sequencing facility. Upon receipt of raw sequencing data in FASTQ format, the system first performs alignment of sequencing reads to the human reference genome (hg18) using a high-efficiency alignment algorithm that filters out reads with mismatches or ambiguous mapping, retaining only uniquely mapped reads that correspond to the universal unique reads set. The system then calculates the coverage for each chromosome by counting the number of aligned reads per chromosome and normalizes this coverage by dividing by the mean coverage across all 22 autosomes to obtain the relative coverage. Concurrently, the system computes the GC content of each chromosome by analyzing the frequency of guanine and cytosine bases in all possible 35-mers within that chromosome. The system then retrieves the precomputed, chromosome-specific GC-correlation curves from a database stored in persistent memory, and uses these curves to predict the expected relative coverage for each chromosome based on its GC content. The difference between the observed and predicted coverage is calculated as a residual, and the system retrieves the chromosome-specific standard deviation of residuals from the reference cohort database. The system then estimates the fetal DNA fraction using a dual-reference model that compares the relative coverage of the X and Y chromosomes against fitted values derived from male and female reference cohorts. For male fetuses, the system calculates fetal fraction using the Y chromosome as the primary marker, and for female fetuses, it uses the X chromosome in conjunction with the absence of Y signals. The system then performs the dual-hypothesis t-test for each chromosome, computing the two t-values and the logarithmic likelihood odds ratio, and applies the diagnostic threshold to classify each chromosome as euploid, trisomic, or inconclusive. Simultaneously, the system performs fetal sex classification using a combination of threshold-based criteria and logistic regression, where the probability of male sex is computed based on the relative coverage of X and Y chromosomes using parameters derived from the reference cohort. Based on the sex classification, the system initiates targeted analysis for sex chromosomal aneuploidies: for female fetuses, it performs a t-test on the X chromosome to detect monosomy or trisomy, and for male fetuses, it performs a t-test on the X chromosome while incorporating the Y-based fetal fraction estimate to detect XXY or XYY. The system also evaluates multiple quality control metrics, including the Q20% value, the PCR duplication rate, the number of unique reads, and the consistency of the GC-correlation residuals, and flags samples that fail predefined thresholds for inclusion in the diagnostic output. Finally, the system compiles all results into a structured diagnostic report, which includes the diagnosis for each aneuploidy type, the fetal sex, the estimated fetal DNA fraction, the diagnostic confidence score for each chromosome, and a quality assurance summary. The report is generated in a standardized format compatible with electronic health record systems and may be transmitted directly to a clinician’s interface. The system is designed to operate autonomously, requiring no manual intervention beyond sample loading and result interpretation, and is capable of processing hundreds of samples per day with consistent accuracy. The computer-readable medium may be distributed as a software package for installation on laboratory servers, or as a cloud-based service accessible via secure web interface. The system is further configured to update its reference databases and GC-correlation curves as new data becomes available, ensuring continuous improvement in diagnostic performance. This automated system eliminates human error, ensures reproducibility across laboratories, and enables the widespread adoption of the diagnostic method in both high-volume clinical settings and resource-limited environments, thereby fulfilling the promise of noninvasive prenatal testing as a universal standard of care.

### V. EXAMPLES

The following examples illustrate the practical application and superior performance of the invention in a clinical setting. In a prospective cohort study involving 903 pregnant women, maternal plasma samples were collected between 10 and 34 weeks of gestation, and all pregnancies were confirmed by full karyotyping of amniotic fluid. Of these, 866 fetuses were euploid and 37 were aneuploid, including two cases of trisomy 13, twelve cases of trisomy 18, sixteen cases of trisomy 21, four cases of Turner syndrome (45,X), two cases of Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY), and one case of XYY syndrome. Each sample was sequenced using an Illumina platform, yielding an average of 1.7 million uniquely aligned reads per sample. The diagnostic method of the invention was applied to each sample using the automated system described above. For autosomal aneuploidies, the method achieved 100% sensitivity and 100% specificity for trisomy 13 and trisomy 21, correctly identifying all affected cases without a single false positive. For trisomy 18, the method detected all twelve aneuploid cases and correctly classified 890 out of 891 euploid controls, yielding a specificity of 99.7% and a false positive rate of 0.3%. The single false positive occurred in a sample from a 21-week gestation, where the fetal DNA fraction was unusually low and the sequencing depth was borderline, but the diagnostic confidence score was still below the threshold for a positive call, indicating that the system correctly flagged the result as inconclusive upon re-evaluation. For sex chromosomal aneuploidies, the method detected three out of four Turner syndrome cases, with the single false negative occurring in a mosaic case with only 46% of cells carrying the 45,X karyotype and a gestational age of 25 weeks. The sensitivity for Turner syndrome was therefore 75%, with a specificity of 99.9% and a false positive rate of 0.1%. For Klinefelter syndrome and XYY syndrome, the method achieved 100% sensitivity and specificity, correctly identifying all affected cases without any false positives. The fetal sex classification was accurate in 99.9% of cases, with only one inconclusive result among 896 non-aneuploid fetuses. The method was further validated by comparison with three previously published approaches: the standard z-score method without GC correction, the GC-corrected z-score method, and the internal chromosome control method. The coefficient of variation for the standard z-score method was significantly higher across chromosomes 13, 18, and 21, indicating greater technical noise and reduced sensitivity. The GC-corrected z-score method performed well for autosomal trisomies but failed to reliably detect sex chromosomal aneuploidies due to misclassification of fetal sex. The internal chromosome control method exhibited elevated variation for autosomal chromosomes and a high risk of false negatives for XXY and XYY cases. In contrast, the method of the invention demonstrated the lowest coefficient of variation across all chromosomes and the highest diagnostic accuracy for both autosomal and sex chromosomal aneuploidies. The method’s performance was further analyzed across gestational ages and sequencing depths. Detection power increased with both gestational week and the number of unique reads, with the highest sensitivity observed in samples with fetal DNA fractions above 10% and sequencing depths exceeding 2 million reads. The method also demonstrated consistent performance across fetal sexes, with no significant difference in diagnostic accuracy between male and female fetuses, a critical advantage over prior methods that relied on Y-chromosome detection. The quality control metrics confirmed the robustness of the method: samples with Q20% below 85% or PCR duplication rates above 15% were automatically excluded from diagnostic analysis, ensuring that only high-quality data contributed to the final result. The system generated a diagnostic report for each sample within eight hours of data acquisition, with no manual intervention required. These examples demonstrate that the invention provides a comprehensive, accurate, and reliable method for noninvasive prenatal diagnosis of fetal genetic abnormalities, surpassing all prior technologies in sensitivity, specificity, and clinical utility.
