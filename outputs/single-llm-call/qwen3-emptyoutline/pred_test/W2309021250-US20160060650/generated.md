# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The cultivation of potato (Solanum tuberosum) has long been constrained by the genetic limitations of widely adopted cultivars, particularly those that exhibit sterility or reduced fertility under natural reproductive conditions. Among these, the Bintje cultivar stands as one of the most economically significant potato varieties in Europe and beyond, prized for its high yield, adaptability to diverse agroclimatic conditions, and superior culinary qualities including texture, flavor, and cooking consistency. Despite these advantages, Bintje has been genetically static since its introduction in the early 20th century, with no meaningful genetic improvement achieved through conventional breeding due to its persistent female sterility. This sterility manifests as the complete abscission of flowers following blooming, preventing the development of seed berries and rendering the plant incapable of producing viable seeds even when exposed to compatible pollen. As a result, propagation of Bintje has been exclusively vegetative, perpetuating its genetic uniformity and rendering it vulnerable to emerging pathogens, particularly Phytophthora infestans, the causal agent of late blight—a disease that continues to threaten global potato production and food security.

The molecular basis of this sterility remains poorly understood, though it is widely assumed to involve disruptions in hormonal signaling pathways, particularly those governing auxin homeostasis and ovary development. In many Solanaceous species, including tomato and potato, successful fruit and seed berry formation is tightly coupled with fertilization, which triggers a cascade of hormonal and transcriptional events leading to ovary enlargement, tissue differentiation, and nutrient allocation. In the absence of fertilization, these pathways remain inactive, and floral organs undergo programmed senescence. While parthenocarpy—fruit development without fertilization—has been engineered in tomato through targeted overexpression of auxin biosynthesis genes in specific floral tissues, no such mechanism has been demonstrated in potato, nor has any exogenous protein expression been shown to rescue ovary development in a naturally sterile cultivar like Bintje.

Cellulose binding domains (CBDs) are evolutionarily conserved modules found predominantly in microbial cellulolytic enzymes, where they facilitate the attachment of catalytic domains to insoluble cellulose substrates. These domains belong to distinct families based on sequence homology and structural fold, with Family 1 CBDs being among the most widely studied. In saprophytic fungi and bacteria, CBDs enhance enzymatic efficiency by concentrating hydrolytic activity at the surface of crystalline cellulose. However, certain CBDs have also been observed in pathogenic organisms, notably in oomycetes such as Phytophthora infestans, where they are not associated with cellulolytic enzymes but instead are expressed as standalone proteins tightly integrated into the hyphal cell wall. In P. infestans, the CBD1 protein has been shown to bind directly to cellulose microfibrils, contributing to structural integrity and possibly shielding the pathogen from host-derived cell wall-degrading enzymes. The presence of such a domain in a plant pathogen, rather than a saprophyte, suggests a potential role in host-pathogen interface modulation, possibly through molecular mimicry or physical barrier formation.

Prior attempts to express microbial carbohydrate-binding modules in transgenic plants have yielded mixed results. Some studies have reported minor alterations in growth rates or cell wall architecture, particularly when cellulose-binding modules from bacterial sources were introduced into tobacco or potato. For instance, expression of a Family 3 CBD from Clostridium cellulovorans in Desiree potato resulted in accelerated early growth, but only at low copy numbers; higher expression levels led to growth inhibition, likely due to interference with normal cell wall remodeling processes. In contrast, expression of a Family 22 xylan-binding module in tobacco produced no discernible phenotypic changes, suggesting that the biological impact of such domains is highly context-dependent, influenced by the host species, the specific module, its expression level, subcellular localization, and the nature of the plant cell wall matrix. Importantly, none of these prior studies reported any effect on reproductive development, floral organ retention, or seed berry formation.

The unexpected observation that transgenic expression of the P. infestans CBD1 protein in Bintje potato not only prevented floral abscission but also induced the formation of fully developed seed berries—regardless of fertilization status—represents a fundamental departure from all previously documented effects of carbohydrate-binding modules in plants. This phenomenon is not attributable to general stress responses, hormonal imbalances from vector elements, or off-target transgene effects, as control transgenic lines expressing unrelated proteins or even tandem CBDs from the same pathogen (CBD4) failed to reproduce this outcome. Moreover, the CBD1 protein was found to accumulate specifically within the locular and ovary wall tissues, suggesting a direct interaction with plant cellulose structures during critical stages of ovary development. The fact that these seed berries, though initially seedless, could be fertilized by external pollen and subsequently produce viable progeny with novel trait combinations—including late blight resistance and altered tuber pigmentation—demonstrates that the CBD1-mediated rescue of ovary development is not merely a morphological aberration but a functionally robust platform for genetic improvement.

This discovery has profound implications for plant breeding, particularly for clonally propagated crops with limited sexual reproduction. The ability to restore female fertility in a cultivar that has been genetically isolated for over a century opens a previously inaccessible route for introgression of desirable traits—disease resistance, nutritional enhancement, stress tolerance—into elite germplasm without the need for complex interspecific hybridization or transgenic persistence in commercial varieties. Furthermore, the fact that the CBD1 transgene can be selectively bred out in subsequent generations, reverting plants to their original sterile phenotype while retaining the newly acquired traits, provides a powerful mechanism for generating non-transgenic, conventionally bred varieties with the genetic advantages of a previously inaccessible gene pool. This innovation thus bridges the gap between biotechnological intervention and traditional breeding, offering a sustainable, regulatory-compliant pathway for crop improvement in regions with strict limitations on genetically modified organisms.

The underlying mechanism by which a microbial cellulose-binding domain, originally evolved to interact with pathogen cell walls, can trigger developmental reprogramming in a plant ovary remains to be fully elucidated. Hypotheses range from direct physical stabilization of the extracellular matrix during cell expansion, to indirect modulation of cell wall signaling cascades that influence auxin transport or perception, to the sequestration of endogenous cell wall fragments that normally act as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) triggering senescence. Regardless of the precise mechanism, the functional outcome—restoration of ovary development in a sterile cultivar—is both novel and reproducible, and its utility in plant breeding is immediate and scalable. This invention, therefore, is not merely a method of genetic transformation, but a transformative tool for unlocking the latent genetic potential of clonally propagated crops, enabling the creation of new varieties with improved resilience, quality, and adaptability in a world increasingly challenged by climate instability and emerging pathogens.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method for restoring female fertility in sterile potato cultivars through the transgenic expression of a cellulose binding domain protein derived from Phytophthora infestans, specifically the CBD1 variant. This method enables the formation of seed berries in cultivars that otherwise undergo complete floral abscission, thereby permitting sexual reproduction and the introgression of desirable traits from other potato varieties into genetically isolated germplasm. The invention further encompasses transgenic potato plants expressing the CBD1 protein, wherein the expression of this protein results in the development of viable seed berries even in the absence of fertilization, and wherein these seed berries, upon subsequent pollination with fertile pollen, yield viable seeds that give rise to progeny plants exhibiting novel combinations of phenotypic traits, including disease resistance and tuber pigmentation, without the persistent presence of the transgene in subsequent generations.

The invention is based on the unexpected discovery that the heterologous expression of the Phytophthora infestans CBD1 protein in the female-sterile potato cultivar Bintje induces a developmental reprogramming of the ovary, resulting in the retention and expansion of floral tissues that would otherwise senesce and abscise. This effect is not observed in control plants, nor in transgenic lines expressing other cellulose binding domains from the same pathogen or from unrelated organisms. The CBD1 protein accumulates in the locular and ovary wall tissues, where it interacts with cellulose microfibrils in the plant cell wall, leading to structural and signaling changes that mimic or bypass the hormonal cues normally triggered by fertilization. As a result, transgenic plants produce seed berries that are morphologically indistinguishable from those of fertile cultivars, and which, when pollinated with compatible pollen, develop into mature fruits containing viable seeds.

The invention further provides a system for the genetic improvement of sterile potato cultivars without the need for continuous transgene expression in commercial varieties. Progeny derived from crosses between CBD1-expressing Bintje plants and fertile donor varieties inherit the desired traits—such as late blight resistance conferred by Sarpo Mira or anthocyanin-mediated purple tuber pigmentation conferred by Peter Wilcox—while segregating away from the CBD1 transgene. This allows for the development of non-transgenic, conventionally bred potato varieties that retain the improved characteristics introduced through the transgenic bridge, thereby circumventing regulatory and market barriers associated with genetically modified organisms. The CBD1-mediated fertility restoration is specific to female reproductive tissues; male sterility persists, ensuring that the transgenic plant cannot act as a pollen donor and thus minimizing the risk of transgene flow to wild relatives or non-transgenic crops.

The invention further includes the use of the CBD1 protein as a molecular tool for inducing parthenocarpic fruit development in other Solanaceous species, including tomato, eggplant, and pepper, where similar barriers to sexual reproduction or fruit set exist. The method is applicable to any plant cultivar exhibiting female sterility due to developmental arrest of the ovary, regardless of the underlying genetic cause, provided that the cellulose-rich cell wall architecture of the ovary is accessible to the CBD1 protein. The expression of the CBD1 protein is driven by a constitutive promoter, such as the cauliflower mosaic virus 35S promoter, ensuring consistent expression throughout plant development, particularly during floral organogenesis. The transgene is integrated into the plant genome at non-coding regions, avoiding disruption of endogenous genes, and the resulting transgenic lines are stable across multiple generations.

The invention further encompasses a method for producing new potato varieties with enhanced traits through a two-step breeding process: first, the introduction of the CBD1 transgene into a sterile cultivar to enable seed berry formation; second, the crossing of these transgenic plants with donor varieties carrying desirable traits, followed by selection of progeny that have lost the CBD1 transgene but retained the introduced traits. This approach enables the rapid development of improved potato varieties with traits that were previously inaccessible due to the sterility of the recipient germplasm. The invention further provides transgenic potato plants that express the CBD1 protein, wherein the plants exhibit increased height, delayed floral senescence, and the ability to form seed berries upon pollination, and wherein the seed berries contain viable seeds that give rise to progeny with novel combinations of tuber color, shape, and disease resistance.

The invention further provides a method for identifying integration sites of the CBD1 transgene in the potato genome, using genome walking techniques to confirm that integration occurs in non-coding regions, thereby minimizing the risk of insertional mutagenesis. The invention further provides a method for confirming the presence and localization of the CBD1 protein in developing seed berries using immunolocalization techniques with peptide-specific antibodies, demonstrating that the protein is distributed throughout the ovary wall and locule tissues, consistent with a role in modulating cell wall dynamics during fruit development. The invention further provides a method for screening progeny for late blight resistance using controlled inoculation with Phytophthora infestans sporangia, demonstrating that resistance traits introduced from donor varieties are stably inherited and expressed in progeny that no longer carry the CBD1 transgene.

The invention further provides a method for producing potato varieties with enhanced nutritional and market value through the introgression of anthocyanin biosynthesis pathways, resulting in tubers with purple, red, or blue pigmentation, which are associated with increased antioxidant content and consumer preference. ## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention relates to a method for restoring female fertility in sterile potato cultivars through the transgenic expression of a cellulose binding domain protein derived from Phytophthora infestans, specifically the CBD1 variant. This method enables the formation of seed berries in cultivars that otherwise undergo complete floral abscission, thereby permitting sexual reproduction and the introgression of desirable traits from other potato varieties into genetically isolated germplasm. The invention further encompasses transgenic potato plants expressing the CBD1 protein, wherein the expression of this protein results in the development of viable seed berries even in the absence of fertilization, and wherein these seed berries, upon subsequent pollination with fertile pollen, yield viable seeds that give rise to progeny plants exhibiting novel combinations of phenotypic traits, including disease resistance and tuber pigmentation, without the persistent presence of the transgene in subsequent generations.

The method begins with the selection of a sterile potato cultivar, such as Bintje, which is characterized by the complete abscission of flowers following blooming, resulting in the absence of seed berry formation and the inability to produce viable seeds under natural conditions. This sterility is not due to a lack of pollen production, as male sterility is also observed in Bintje, but rather to a developmental arrest of the ovary, which fails to expand or differentiate into a mature fruit structure. The cultivar is maintained in tissue culture under sterile conditions using Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium supplemented with appropriate plant growth regulators to ensure the propagation of genetically uniform plantlets. These plantlets are then used as explants for Agrobacterium-mediated transformation, employing a binary vector system containing the CBD1 gene under the control of a constitutive promoter, such as the cauliflower mosaic virus 35S promoter, to ensure continuous expression throughout plant development.

The CBD1 gene, derived from Phytophthora infestans, encodes a protein of approximately 120 amino acids that contains a conserved cellulose-binding motif characteristic of Family 1 carbohydrate-binding modules. The gene is cloned into a binary vector such as pBI121, which includes a neomycin phosphotransferase II (nptII) gene for selection on kanamycin-containing media and a T-DNA border sequence for efficient transfer into the plant genome. The Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain LBA4404, harboring the recombinant binary vector, is cultured in liquid medium supplemented with acetosyringone to induce virulence gene expression. Four-week-old potato plantlets are excised, and leaf segments are immersed in the bacterial suspension for fifteen minutes to facilitate infection. Following co-cultivation on solid medium containing acetosyringone and antibiotics to suppress bacterial growth, explants are transferred to regeneration medium containing kanamycin to select for transformed tissues. Regenerated shoots are rooted on hormone-free medium and acclimatized to greenhouse conditions.

Transgenic plants are identified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using primers specific to the 35S promoter and the CBD1 coding sequence, followed by sequencing of the amplified product to confirm the integrity of the inserted gene. Plants exhibiting uniform growth and the absence of visible phenotypic abnormalities are selected for further characterization. Immunolocalization studies are conducted on developing seed berries using peptide-specific antibodies raised against the CBD1 protein, coupled with alkaline phosphatase-conjugated secondary antibodies and chromogenic substrate detection. These studies reveal the presence of the CBD1 protein throughout the locular and ovary wall tissues, with no detectable signal in non-transgenic controls or in transgenic plants treated with pre-immune serum, confirming the specific and localized expression of the protein in reproductive tissues.

The key phenotypic outcome of this transformation is the retention and expansion of seed berries in transgenic plants, even in the absence of pollination. This phenomenon, which has not been observed in any other transgenic potato line expressing unrelated proteins or even other cellulose-binding domains from the same pathogen, indicates a direct and specific role for CBD1 in overcoming the developmental block to ovary formation. The seed berries formed in transgenic plants are morphologically similar to those of fertile cultivars, with a fleshy pericarp surrounding a central locule. In the absence of fertilization, these berries remain seedless, but they do not abscise, persisting on the plant until senescence. Upon pollination with viable pollen from a fertile donor variety, such as Sarpo Mira or Peter Wilcox, the seed berries increase in size and develop into mature fruits containing numerous viable seeds, demonstrating that the CBD1-mediated ovary development is fully compatible with normal fertilization and seed maturation processes.

The male sterility of the transgenic plants persists, as no viable pollen is produced, ensuring that the transgenic plants cannot act as pollen donors. This feature is critical for preventing the unintended spread of the transgene into wild populations or non-transgenic crops. The transgene is integrated into the potato genome at non-coding regions, as confirmed by genome walking techniques using a commercial genome walking kit. Sequencing of the flanking regions reveals that the integration events occur within intronic or intergenic sequences, with no disruption of annotated coding genes. For example, in one transgenic line, the integration site corresponds to a region within a 1089-base-pair intron of a putative ODORANT 1 gene, a transcription factor with no known role in reproductive development and no expressed transcripts spanning the integration site. This confirms that the fertility restoration is not due to insertional mutagenesis of an endogenous gene but rather to the functional activity of the expressed CBD1 protein.

The CBD1 protein is not expressed in vegetative tissues such as leaves, stems, or tubers, and its expression is confined to the developing ovary and locule tissues, suggesting a tissue-specific interaction with cellulose microfibrils during cell expansion. The protein does not interfere with photosynthetic efficiency, tuber yield, or storage quality, and transgenic plants exhibit normal growth patterns, with height increases observed in later stages of development, consistent with improved resource allocation to reproductive structures. The transgene is stably inherited in subsequent generations, and segregation analysis confirms Mendelian inheritance patterns, with some lines exhibiting single-copy integration and others showing multi-copy arrays, both of which are sufficient to induce fertility restoration.

The invention further provides a method for breeding new potato varieties through the crossing of CBD1-expressing Bintje plants with fertile donor varieties carrying desirable traits. Pollen from late blight-resistant cultivars such as Sarpo Mira or purple-skinned, yellow-fleshed cultivars such as Peter Wilcox is manually applied to the stigmas of transgenic Bintje flowers. The resulting seed berries are allowed to mature, and seeds are harvested, dried, and planted under controlled conditions. Progeny are screened for the presence of the CBD1 transgene using PCR and for the presence of introduced traits using visual phenotyping and controlled pathogen challenge assays. Late blight resistance is assessed by inoculating detached leaflets with sporangia of Phytophthora infestans race US 11 and scoring disease progression over six days. Progeny that exhibit complete resistance, partial resistance, or susceptibility are classified accordingly, and those with resistance are selected for further breeding.

Similarly, tuber pigmentation is assessed by harvesting and examining tubers for coloration, with purple, red, and blue hues indicating the expression of anthocyanin biosynthesis genes inherited from donor varieties. The segregation of these traits in the progeny demonstrates that the CBD1-mediated fertility restoration enables the recombination of quantitative and qualitative traits that were previously inaccessible in the Bintje gene pool. Importantly, a subset of progeny lacks the CBD1 transgene entirely, as confirmed by PCR and Southern blot analysis, yet retains the introduced traits, including late blight resistance and novel tuber coloration. These non-transgenic progeny are indistinguishable from conventionally bred varieties and can be propagated vegetatively or sexually, depending on breeding objectives.

The invention further provides a method for producing multiple generations of improved potato varieties, with each generation allowing for the selection of plants that have lost the transgene but retained the desired traits. This process can be repeated over successive cycles, enabling the development of elite varieties that combine the superior agronomic characteristics of Bintje with the disease resistance, nutritional quality, and market appeal of other cultivars. The method is scalable and applicable to other sterile potato cultivars, including Russet Burbank, which also exhibits female sterility, although initial attempts with this cultivar show reduced vigor in progeny, suggesting that the effectiveness of CBD1 may vary depending on the genetic background of the recipient.

The invention further provides a method for analyzing the molecular mechanism underlying CBD1-mediated fertility restoration. While the precise biochemical interaction remains to be fully characterized, it is hypothesized that the CBD1 protein binds to cellulose microfibrils in the ovary wall, stabilizing the extracellular matrix during cell expansion and thereby mimicking or bypassing the cell wall signaling events normally triggered by fertilization. This interaction may prevent the release of cell wall-derived damage-associated molecular patterns that would otherwise initiate senescence, or it may alter the mechanical properties of the cell wall to facilitate auxin transport or perception. The fact that other cellulose-binding domains, including tandem CBDs from the same pathogen, do not produce this effect suggests that the specific structural features of CBD1 are critical for this function.

The invention further provides a method for optimizing the expression of CBD1 through the use of tissue-specific promoters, such as those derived from ovary-specific genes, to restrict expression to reproductive tissues and minimize potential pleiotropic effects. The invention further provides a method for combining CBD1 expression with other biotechnological tools, such as CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene editing, to simultaneously introduce multiple traits into the Bintje genome. The invention further provides a method for producing transgenic plants that express CBD1 under inducible promoters, allowing for temporal control of expression during critical stages of floral development.

The invention further provides a method for producing transgenic potato plants that exhibit no adverse effects on tuber yield, dormancy, or cooking quality, ensuring that the fertility restoration does not compromise the agronomic value of the cultivar. ## EXAMPLES

### Plant Transformation and Selection

The method for transforming the sterile potato cultivar Bintje with the cellulose binding domain protein CBD1 from Phytophthora infestans involves a series of precisely controlled steps to ensure efficient gene delivery, stable integration, and phenotypic validation. The process begins with the maintenance of healthy, axenic tissue culture plantlets of Bintje potato, derived from nodal explants cultured on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium supplemented with 3% sucrose, 0.8% agar, and a balanced mixture of plant growth regulators, including 0.5 mg/L 6-benzylaminopurine and 0.1 mg/L naphthaleneacetic acid, to promote shoot proliferation. After four weeks of growth under controlled conditions of 22°C, 16-hour photoperiod, and 70% relative humidity, healthy, uniformly sized plantlets are selected for transformation. Leaf segments, approximately 1.5 cm in length, are excised from the distal portion of the leaves and cut laterally to expose fresh tissue surfaces, thereby enhancing the efficiency of Agrobacterium-mediated infection.

The binary vector pBI121, containing the CBD1 gene (EU179903) under the transcriptional control of the cauliflower mosaic virus 35S promoter and the neomycin phosphotransferase II (nptII) gene for kanamycin selection, is introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain LBA4404 via electroporation. The transformed bacterial culture is grown overnight in liquid Luria-Bertani medium supplemented with 50 mg/L kanamycin and 25 mg/L rifampicin to maintain plasmid stability. The culture is then harvested by centrifugation, washed twice with sterile distilled water, and resuspended in a transformation medium containing 100 μM acetosyringone to induce the expression of virulence genes on the Ti plasmid. The bacterial suspension is adjusted to an optical density at 600 nm of 0.6 to ensure a high concentration of viable, virulent cells.

The excised leaf segments are immersed in the bacterial suspension for a period of fifteen minutes with gentle agitation to facilitate bacterial attachment and T-DNA transfer. Following immersion, the explants are blotted dry on sterile filter paper and placed on co-cultivation medium consisting of MS agar supplemented with 3% sucrose and 100 μM acetosyringone. The explants are incubated in the dark at 22°C for 48 hours to allow for the completion of T-DNA transfer and integration into the plant genome. After co-cultivation, the explants are transferred to regeneration medium containing MS salts, 3% sucrose, 0.8% agar, 0.5 mg/L 6-benzylaminopurine, 0.1 mg/L naphthaleneacetic acid, and 50 mg/L kanamycin to select for transformed tissues while suppressing bacterial growth. The medium is supplemented with cefotaxime (300 mg/L) to eliminate residual Agrobacterium.

After three to four weeks, callus formation is observed at the cut edges of the explants, followed by the emergence of shoots. These shoots are carefully excised and transferred to rooting medium containing half-strength MS salts, 3% sucrose, 0.8% agar, and 50 mg/L kanamycin, with the addition of 0.5 mg/L indole-3-butyric acid to promote root development. Rooted plantlets are acclimatized to greenhouse conditions by gradually reducing humidity over a period of ten days, followed by transfer to soil in 10 cm pots. The plants are maintained in a controlled greenhouse environment with a 16-hour photoperiod, 22°C day temperature, 18°C night temperature, and 60–70% relative humidity.

To confirm the presence of the transgene, genomic DNA is extracted from young leaves of regenerated plantlets using a modified cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) protocol. PCR amplification is performed using primers specific to the 35S promoter (35S-F: GATAATCATCGCAAGACCGGC; 35S-R: GACGTAAGGGATGACGCACAATCCC) to amplify a 480-base-pair fragment. Amplification products are visualized on a 1.5% agarose gel, and positive bands are purified and sequenced to confirm the identity and integrity of the inserted CBD1 gene. Transgenic lines exhibiting a single, clean PCR product are selected for further characterization. Plants that fail to amplify the transgene are discarded as non-transformants.

To assess the stability of transgene integration and to rule out the possibility of somaclonal variation, a subset of transgenic lines is subjected to genome walking using the APAgene GOLD Genome Walking Kit. Genomic DNA is digested with restriction enzymes that do not cut within the T-DNA insert, and adaptor-ligated fragments are subjected to three rounds of nested PCR using vector-specific forward primers and reverse primers provided by the kit. The resulting amplicons are separated on a 1% agarose gel, purified, and cloned into a TOPO-TA vector for sequencing. Analysis of the flanking sequences reveals that the transgene is integrated at unique genomic loci in different transgenic lines, with no evidence of tandem repeats or rearrangements. In one line, designated B7, a single band of approximately 1.2 kb is obtained, suggesting a single-copy integration event. In another line, designated B48, multiple bands of varying sizes are observed, indicating a more complex integration pattern, possibly involving multiple insertion sites or partial T-DNA rearrangements.

Immunolocalization studies are conducted to confirm the expression and subcellular localization of the CBD1 protein in developing reproductive tissues. Developing seed berries are collected from transgenic and non-transgenic plants at various stages of development, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and embedded in paraffin. Thin sections (8 μm) are cut and mounted on glass slides. Sections are rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline, and incubated overnight at 4°C with a primary antibody raised against a synthetic peptide corresponding to the conserved cellulose-binding region of CBD1. After washing, sections are incubated with a goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G antibody conjugated to alkaline phosphatase at a dilution of 1:1000 for two hours at room temperature. Following additional washes in Tris buffer (pH 9.5), the enzyme substrate 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl phosphate and nitro blue tetrazolium (BCIP/NBT) is applied, and purple precipitate is allowed to develop over several hours. Control sections are treated with pre-immune rabbit serum instead of the CBD1-specific antibody. The results demonstrate a strong, specific signal localized throughout the fleshy pericarp and locule tissues of seed berries in all transgenic lines, with no signal detected in non-transgenic controls or in transgenic samples treated with pre-immune serum.

Height measurements are recorded weekly beginning at five weeks after transfer to soil, with plants measured from the base of the stem to the tip of the tallest shoot. Transgenic lines exhibit a range of growth patterns, with some lines showing no significant difference from non-transgenic controls, while others display a marked increase in height, particularly after ten weeks of growth. This variability is consistent with differences in transgene copy number and integration site, as confirmed by genome walking and Southern blot analysis. Lines B7 and B48, selected for crossing studies, exhibit robust growth and consistent seed berry formation, confirming their suitability for further breeding experiments.

All transgenic lines are maintained under greenhouse conditions for at least two generations to ensure phenotypic stability. Flowering is monitored daily, and the presence or absence of seed berries is recorded. Non-transgenic Bintje plants consistently exhibit floral abscission within three to five days after anthesis, with no formation of seed berries. In contrast, all transgenic lines expressing CBD1 retain their flowers and develop seed berries, regardless of pollination status. The seed berries are initially small and green, gradually increasing in size over a period of four to six weeks. In some lines, such as B48, the seed berries are significantly larger than those in other transgenic lines, with some berries reaching up to 2.5 cm in length and containing over 200 seeds following successful pollination. This variation in berry size and seed number is consistent with differences in transgene expression levels and integration efficiency, and provides a basis for selecting the most promising lines for breeding applications.

### Example 2

Following the successful transformation and phenotypic characterization of CBD1-expressing Bintje potato plants, a series of controlled crosses were conducted to evaluate the potential for introgression of desirable traits from fertile donor varieties into the otherwise genetically isolated Bintje germplasm. Due to the persistent male sterility observed in both transgenic and non-transgenic Bintje plants, pollen was not available from the Bintje genotype itself, necessitating the use of external pollen sources. Pollen was collected from two distinct donor varieties: Sarpo Mira, a late blight-resistant cultivar developed through interspecific hybridization with wild Solanum species, and Peter Wilcox, a purple-skinned, yellow-fleshed cultivar known for its high anthocyanin content and unique tuber morphology.

Pollen was harvested from mature anthers of donor plants grown under identical greenhouse conditions as the transgenic Bintje lines. The anthers were carefully excised and placed in sterile Petri dishes to allow for natural dehiscence. Pollen viability was assessed using a tetrazolium chloride staining assay, which confirmed that pollen from both donor varieties was viable and capable of germination. The pollen was then manually applied to the stigmas of open flowers on transgenic Bintje lines B7 and B48, as well as to non-transgenic Bintje control plants, using a fine-tipped paintbrush. Each flower received approximately 50–100 pollen grains, and multiple flowers per plant were pollinated to maximize the probability of successful fertilization. The pollinated plants were covered with fine mesh bags to prevent contamination by airborne pollen and were maintained under controlled conditions of 22°C, 16-hour photoperiod, and 70% relative humidity.

Within two weeks of pollination, the seed berries on transgenic Bintje plants began to swell visibly, a clear indication of ovary development and fruit set. In contrast, the seed berries on non-transgenic Bintje control plants, despite receiving the same pollen treatment, underwent complete abscission within seven days, with no observable enlargement or maturation. This stark contrast confirmed that the CBD1-mediated restoration of fertility was not simply a consequence of enhanced pollen viability or pollination efficiency, but rather a direct effect of the transgene on ovary development. The seed berries on transgenic plants continued to expand over the next four to six weeks, turning from green to yellow as they matured. In line B48, the largest seed berries reached dimensions of up to 3.0 cm in length and 2.0 cm in diameter, containing an estimated 150–200 seeds per berry, compared to fewer than ten seeds in the largest berries from other transgenic lines.

To assess seed viability, a subset of mature seed berries was harvested and dried at room temperature for two weeks. Seeds were extracted by gentle maceration and washing in distilled water, followed by air-drying on filter paper. A total of 1,200 seeds were collected from the B7 × Sarpo Mira cross, 950 seeds from the B48 × Sarpo Mira cross, 800 seeds from the B7 × Peter Wilcox cross, and 700 seeds from the B48 × Peter Wilcox cross. A control group of 500 seeds was collected from non-transgenic Bintje plants pollinated with Sarpo Mira pollen, but none of these seeds germinated, confirming the complete absence of viable seed production in the absence of the CBD1 transgene.

The collected seeds were surface-sterilized with 10% sodium hypochlorite for five minutes, rinsed three times in sterile distilled water, and sown on MS medium supplemented with 3% sucrose and 0.8% agar. Germination rates were recorded after two weeks, with over 85% of seeds from the transgenic crosses germinating, compared to 0% germination in the control group. The resulting seedlings were transferred to soil and grown under greenhouse conditions. At the seedling stage, all progeny exhibited normal morphology, with no signs of developmental abnormalities, suggesting that the CBD1 transgene did not interfere with embryogenesis or early seedling development.

To determine the inheritance of the CBD1 transgene, genomic DNA was extracted from leaf tissue of 120 randomly selected progeny plants from each cross. PCR amplification using 35S-specific primers revealed that approximately 70% of the progeny from the B7 × Sarpo Mira cross and 68% of the progeny from the B48 × Sarpo Mira cross were positive for the transgene, consistent with Mendelian segregation of a single dominant transgene. In the crosses with Peter Wilcox, similar segregation ratios were observed, with approximately 72% of progeny testing positive for the transgene. Importantly, a significant proportion of the progeny—approximately 30% in each cross—were negative for the CBD1 transgene, as confirmed by both PCR and Southern blot analysis.

These non-transgenic progeny were then evaluated for the presence of traits inherited from the donor parents. Late blight resistance was assessed by inoculating detached leaflets with sporangia of Phytophthora infestans race US 11, as previously described. The severity of disease symptoms was scored on a scale from 0 (no symptoms) to 5 (complete necrosis). Progeny that tested negative for the CBD1 transgene but exhibited a disease score of 0 or 1 were classified as resistant, while those with scores of 3 or higher were classified as susceptible. Of the 36 non-transgenic progeny from the B7 × Sarpo Mira cross, 28 exhibited complete resistance, 5 exhibited moderate susceptibility, and 3 were fully susceptible. Similarly, of the 32 non-transgenic progeny from the B48 × Sarpo Mira cross, 25 were resistant, 6 were moderately susceptible, and 1 was fully susceptible. These results demonstrate that late blight resistance, a complex trait controlled by multiple genes in Sarpo Mira, was successfully introgressed into the Bintje background and stably inherited in the absence of the CBD1 transgene.

Tuber pigmentation was assessed by harvesting tubers from plants that had reached full maturity. Progeny from the crosses with Peter Wilcox exhibited a wide range of tuber colors, including deep purple, red-purple, and yellow with purple blush, consistent with the inheritance of anthocyanin biosynthesis genes from the donor parent. Some tubers displayed uniform purple skin with yellow flesh, while others exhibited mottled patterns, indicating the segregation of multiple alleles controlling pigment deposition. The presence of these novel color phenotypes in non-transgenic progeny confirms that the CBD1-mediated fertility restoration enabled the recombination of quantitative traits that were previously inaccessible in the Bintje germplasm.

The ability to produce non-transgenic progeny with stable, heritable traits represents a critical innovation of this invention. It allows for the development of improved potato varieties that combine the superior agronomic qualities of Bintje with the disease resistance and nutritional value of other cultivars, without the regulatory, consumer, or market barriers associated with genetically modified organisms. This method effectively serves as a genetic bridge, enabling the introgression of traits into elite germplasm through a single, transient transgenic intervention, followed by conventional breeding to eliminate the transgene. The success of this approach in multiple crosses, with consistent segregation ratios and stable trait inheritance, demonstrates its robustness and reproducibility across different genetic backgrounds.

### Example 3

To evaluate the potential of the CBD1-mediated fertility restoration system for broader application in potato breeding, a series of controlled pathogen resistance assays and tuber quality analyses were conducted on progeny derived from crosses between CBD1-expressing Bintje plants and donor varieties. The primary objective was to determine whether the introduced traits—late blight resistance and tuber pigmentation—were stably expressed in the absence of the transgene, and whether the agronomic performance of the progeny matched or exceeded that of the original Bintje cultivar.

Late blight resistance was assessed using a standardized inoculation protocol. Detached leaflets from 10-week-old plants were placed on moistened paper towels in sealed incubation trays and inoculated with a suspension of Phytophthora infestans sporangia (race US 11) at a concentration of 10^4 sporangia per milliliter. Each leaflet received five 50-μL droplets applied to the abaxial surface, spaced evenly to avoid overlap. The trays were sealed with plastic wrap and incubated in a dark chamber at 18°C for 24 hours to allow for zoospore release and infection, followed by a 14-hour light/10-hour dark cycle for six days. Disease progression was scored daily using a standardized scale: 0 = no symptoms, 1 = small chlorotic spots, 2 = small necrotic lesions (<5 mm), 3 = medium necrotic lesions (5–15 mm), 4 = large necrotic lesions (>15 mm) with extensive sporulation, and 5 = complete leaf necrosis. Control plants included non-transgenic Bintje, Sarpo Mira (resistant), and Peter Wilcox (susceptible).

The results demonstrated that progeny lacking the CBD1 transgene but inheriting resistance alleles from Sarpo Mira exhibited disease scores of 0 or 1, comparable to the resistant donor parent. In contrast, non-transgenic Bintje controls and susceptible progeny exhibited scores of 4 or 5, with rapid lesion expansion and abundant sporulation. Of the 28 resistant non-transgenic progeny from the B7 × Sarpo Mira cross, 25 showed complete resistance with no visible lesions, and three exhibited minor chlorotic spots that did not progress. Similarly, 25 of the 32 non-transgenic progeny from the B48 × Sarpo Mira cross were fully resistant. These results confirm that the resistance trait, which is known to be polygenic and derived from wild Solanum species, was successfully transferred and stably expressed in the Bintje background.

To assess the stability of resistance across generations, selected resistant non-transgenic progeny were propagated vegetatively and subjected to a second round of pathogen challenge. All plants retained their resistance phenotype, with no evidence of breakdown or reduced efficacy, indicating that the resistance was not a transient or environmentally induced response but a genetically fixed trait. Furthermore, these plants were crossed among themselves to produce F2 progeny, and segregation analysis confirmed a 3:1 ratio of resistant to susceptible plants, consistent with inheritance of a single dominant resistance gene. This finding suggests that at least one major resistance locus from Sarpo Mira was successfully introgressed into the Bintje genome, providing a strong foundation for future breeding programs.

Tuber quality was evaluated in a separate cohort of progeny derived from the crosses with Peter Wilcox. Tubers were harvested at physiological maturity, washed, and dried. Skin color was assessed visually and categorized as yellow, purple, red-purple, or mottled. Flesh color was determined by cutting tubers transversely and comparing to a standardized color chart. Tuber shape was classified as round, oblong, or irregular, and surface texture was scored as smooth, rough, or lumpy. The results revealed a wide spectrum of phenotypes, with 42% of progeny exhibiting purple skin, 30% exhibiting red-purple skin, 18% exhibiting yellow skin with purple blush, and 10% exhibiting yellow skin with no pigmentation. Flesh color was predominantly yellow, with a subset of tubers showing a faint purple ring near the periderm, consistent with anthocyanin accumulation in the cortex.

Tuber yield per plant was measured by harvesting all tubers from a single plant and weighing them fresh. The average yield of non-transgenic progeny from the B7 × Peter Wilcox cross was 1.8 kg per plant, compared to 1.6 kg for non-transgenic Bintje controls, indicating no yield penalty from the introgression process. Tuber size distribution was also analyzed, with 65% of tubers from resistant progeny falling within the commercial size range of 50–150 g, compared to 62% in Bintje controls. Cooking quality was assessed by boiling and frying tubers and evaluating texture, color retention, and flavor. All progeny exhibited the characteristic floury texture and mild flavor of Bintje, with no detectable off-flavors or textural defects. The purple-skinned tubers retained their color during cooking, with no significant leaching of pigments, indicating stability of anthocyanins under thermal processing.

To determine whether the introduced traits were linked to the CBD1 transgene, a subset of progeny was genotyped for both the transgene and the trait markers. The results confirmed that resistance and pigmentation traits segregated independently of the CBD1 transgene, with multiple non-transgenic individuals exhibiting both traits simultaneously. This demonstrates that the CBD1-mediated fertility restoration did not cause linkage drag or unintended co-inheritance of deleterious alleles from the donor parent. Instead, it enabled the precise recombination of favorable traits, allowing breeders to select for combinations that were previously impossible due to the sterility of the Bintje cultivar.

The combination of late blight resistance and purple tuber pigmentation in a single non-transgenic progeny line represents a breakthrough in potato breeding. Such a variety would combine disease resistance, enhanced nutritional value due to anthocyanin content, and consumer appeal based on novel coloration—all within the agronomic framework of the globally recognized Bintje cultivar. The ability to produce such varieties without the persistence of the transgene ensures regulatory compliance and market acceptance in regions with strict GMO regulations. Furthermore, the fact that these traits are stably inherited across generations confirms that the CBD1 system serves not merely as a transient fertility enhancer but as a powerful genetic tool for unlocking the latent potential of clonally propagated crops. This invention thus provides a new paradigm for crop improvement, enabling the rapid development of elite varieties through a single, targeted intervention that bridges the gap between biotechnology and conventional breeding.
