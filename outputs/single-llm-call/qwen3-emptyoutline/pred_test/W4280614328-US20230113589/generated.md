# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to a low-cost, portable, Internet-of-Things (IoT) sensor system designed for the continuous, autonomous measurement of mean radiant temperature (MRT) in outdoor urban environments, combined with real-time pedestrian detection and shade classification using embedded computer vision. Specifically, the invention encompasses a unified hardware-software platform that integrates meteorological sensing components with a low-power edge computing device running deep learning models to estimate human thermal exposure with improved accuracy despite the use of commercially available, non-calibrated, and low-fidelity sensors. The system is engineered to operate in hot, arid climates where outdoor thermal comfort is predominantly governed by radiative heat fluxes rather than air temperature alone. It is particularly suited for deployment in public spaces such as parks, sidewalks, transit stops, and plazas where urban heat mitigation strategies—such as shade provision, green infrastructure, and adaptive urban design—require high-resolution, spatially distributed, and behaviorally contextualized data to inform decision-making. The invention further includes a cloud-based data transmission protocol that securely uploads aggregated, privacy-preserving metrics to remote databases without retaining identifiable imagery, enabling scalable, long-term monitoring by municipal agencies, researchers, and citizen scientists without requiring manual intervention or specialized technical expertise. The system’s architecture is optimized for energy efficiency, compact form factor, and cost-effectiveness, making it uniquely positioned to bridge the gap between high-accuracy but prohibitively expensive research-grade instrumentation and the practical needs of urban heat resilience planning.

The technical domain of the invention spans multiple disciplines including biometeorology, environmental sensing, embedded systems engineering, computer vision, and machine learning. It addresses a critical limitation in current urban climate monitoring practices, wherein the most accurate methods for quantifying human thermal exposure—such as six-directional net radiometry—are economically and logistically inaccessible for widespread deployment. In contrast, the invention leverages a gray globe thermometer, a widely recognized but imperfect proxy for MRT, and enhances its reliability through a machine learning model trained on paired ground-truth measurements. Simultaneously, the system incorporates a vision subsystem that detects and classifies pedestrians in shaded versus sun-exposed regions using a combination of object detection and shadow segmentation algorithms, thereby linking microclimatic conditions with actual human behavior. This fusion of environmental sensing and behavioral observation represents a novel integration not previously disclosed in the prior art. The invention further distinguishes itself by operating entirely on the edge, performing all data processing locally on a low-power device, thereby eliminating latency, reducing bandwidth requirements, and ensuring data privacy by discarding raw imagery immediately after analysis. The system is designed to be self-sustaining, powered by a single low-voltage DC source, and capable of continuous operation under direct solar exposure for extended periods without degradation in performance or data integrity.

The invention is not intended for laboratory use, indoor climate control, or vehicular applications. It is specifically tailored for outdoor, urban, pedestrian-scale environments where the spatial heterogeneity of radiative surfaces—such as asphalt, concrete, glass, vegetation, and building facades—creates highly localized thermal gradients that cannot be captured by conventional weather stations or satellite-derived estimates. Unlike existing IoT weather nodes that report only air temperature and humidity, this system explicitly quantifies the dominant component of human thermal load in sun-exposed environments: mean radiant temperature. Furthermore, unlike prior systems that rely on manual observation or static camera surveys, the invention provides continuous, automated, and temporally synchronized data streams that correlate thermal conditions with real-time human presence and spatial utilization patterns. This capability enables the identification of behavioral thresholds—such as when pedestrian density in shaded areas increases as MRT exceeds a certain threshold—which can inform dynamic interventions such as the deployment of temporary shade structures, adjustment of public transit schedules, or optimization of urban planting strategies. The invention thus constitutes a foundational tool for the emerging field of urban climate informatics, enabling data-driven, human-centered approaches to heat adaptation in cities facing increasing thermal stress due to climate change and urbanization.

## BACKGROUND

The measurement of human thermal exposure in outdoor environments has long been a challenge in environmental science and urban planning. While air temperature is the most commonly reported meteorological variable, it fails to capture the dominant influence of radiative heat exchange on the human body, particularly in sun-exposed urban settings. In hot, dry climates, the difference between air temperature and mean radiant temperature (MRT) can exceed thirty degrees Celsius, leading to significant underestimations of thermal stress when relying solely on air temperature data. MRT, defined as the uniform temperature of an imaginary enclosure in which the radiant heat exchange between the human body and its surroundings would be equal to that in the actual non-uniform environment, has been established in the literature as the most physiologically relevant metric for assessing outdoor thermal comfort. Its calculation requires the integration of longwave radiation emitted by surrounding surfaces and shortwave radiation from the sun across all six spatial directions—above, below, and four lateral orientations. This comprehensive measurement is typically performed using three high-precision net radiometers, each costing several thousand dollars, resulting in systems that are bulky, expensive, and impractical for widespread deployment.

To address these limitations, the use of gray globe thermometers has been proposed as a low-cost alternative. These devices consist of a hollow sphere, typically constructed from a gray-painted ping pong ball or similar material, housing a temperature sensor. The gray color is selected to approximate the average solar reflectance of human skin and clothing, allowing the globe to absorb radiant energy in a manner analogous to the human body. The temperature of the globe, when corrected for convective heat transfer using empirical wind speed and air temperature relationships, can be converted into an estimate of MRT. While this approach has been adopted in numerous field studies, it suffers from well-documented inaccuracies. These include slow thermal response times, sensitivity to globe size and material properties, errors induced by partial shading from nearby vegetation or structures, and non-linear deviations under conditions of high solar irradiance or low solar elevation. Moreover, the empirical correction models used to derive MRT from globe temperature are often derived under specific environmental conditions and do not generalize well across different urban geometries, surface materials, or climatic zones. As a result, globe-based MRT estimates frequently exhibit root mean square errors on the order of 7 to 20 degrees Celsius when compared to six-directional reference measurements, rendering them unreliable for quantitative decision-making without additional calibration or correction.

In parallel, the assessment of human behavior in outdoor spaces has traditionally relied on labor-intensive observational methods, such as manual counts conducted by researchers at fixed intervals, or static camera surveys requiring post-hoc annotation. These approaches are time-consuming, prone to human error, and incapable of capturing continuous, real-time patterns of space use. Although automated pedestrian detection using computer vision has advanced significantly in recent years, particularly through the application of deep learning models such as YOLO and Faster R-CNN, these technologies have not been integrated with environmental sensing systems in a manner that links thermal exposure to actual human presence. Existing commercial and research-grade IoT weather stations focus exclusively on meteorological parameters and lack any capability for spatial or behavioral context. Furthermore, no prior system has combined the measurement of MRT with real-time shade classification and pedestrian counting in a single, compact, low-power, cloud-connected device designed for continuous, autonomous outdoor operation.

The absence of such an integrated system creates a critical gap in urban heat resilience planning. Cities seeking to mitigate the impacts of extreme heat require actionable data on where and when people are exposed to dangerous levels of radiant heat, and whether existing shade infrastructure is being effectively utilized. Without this information, investments in cooling infrastructure—such as tree planting, shade canopies, or reflective surfaces—are often misdirected, based on assumptions rather than observed behavior. While some studies have attempted to correlate MRT with crowd density using separate data streams, these efforts have been hindered by temporal and spatial misalignment between sensor data and behavioral observations. Moreover, none of the prior art has addressed the challenge of correcting the systematic errors inherent in low-cost MRT sensors using machine learning models trained on paired field measurements, nor has any system been designed to perform this correction entirely on the edge, without requiring cloud-based computation or external databases during operation.

Privacy concerns further complicate the deployment of vision-based systems in public spaces. Many existing approaches retain raw video footage for later analysis, raising legal and ethical issues regarding surveillance and data protection. The present invention overcomes this limitation by processing images locally and transmitting only aggregated, anonymized metrics—such as the number of pedestrians in shade or sun—while discarding all identifiable imagery immediately after inference. This approach ensures compliance with privacy regulations and fosters public acceptance of the technology. Additionally, the system is engineered to operate on minimal power, utilizing a single 20-watt source to drive both sensor and vision subsystems, making it suitable for deployment in locations without reliable grid access. The use of commercially available, off-the-shelf components—such as Arduino microcontrollers, ESP8266 WiFi modules, NVIDIA Jetson Nano edge devices, and standard meteorological sensors—ensures low cost, ease of replication, and scalability. The invention thus represents a significant advancement over the state of the art by unifying three previously disconnected domains: low-cost MRT sensing, real-time pedestrian detection, and privacy-preserving edge computing, into a single, cohesive, deployable system capable of providing actionable insights for urban heat adaptation.

## SUMMARY

The present invention is a compact, low-cost, autonomous IoT sensor system designed to measure mean radiant temperature (MRT) in outdoor urban environments while simultaneously detecting and classifying pedestrians as being in shaded or sun-exposed conditions. The system integrates a suite of meteorological sensors—including a gray globe thermometer, an air temperature probe, a relative humidity sensor, an anemometer, and a UV intensity sensor—with an edge computing device running deep learning models for object detection and shadow segmentation. The system is configured to collect sensor data at one-minute intervals, process video frames at a rate sufficient to capture pedestrian movement, and transmit only aggregated, privacy-preserving metrics to a remote cloud database via secure wireless communication. A machine learning model, trained on paired ground-truth MRT measurements obtained from a reference-grade biometeorological platform, is embedded within the system to correct systematic errors introduced by the low-fidelity sensors, thereby enabling accurate MRT estimation despite variations in sensor response time, material properties, and environmental conditions such as partial shading or wind fluctuations.

The system comprises two primary subsystems: a meteorological sensing unit and a vision processing unit. The meteorological sensing unit employs a gray globe thermometer mounted on a vertical support to measure globe temperature, which is then used as the primary input for MRT estimation. An air temperature probe is housed within a white, radiation-shielded cup to minimize solar heating effects, while a humidity sensor and anemometer are positioned to capture ambient conditions. A UV sensor provides additional input for the machine learning model to account for variations in solar irradiance. All sensor data is collected by an Arduino Uno microcontroller, averaged over one-minute intervals, and transmitted via serial communication to a NodeMCU module equipped with an ESP8266 WiFi chip. The NodeMCU establishes a secure, encrypted connection to a cloud-based NoSQL database using the MQTT protocol, ensuring data integrity and authentication through stored PEM certificates. The vision processing unit is powered by an NVIDIA Jetson Nano edge device, which receives video input from a MIPI camera and runs two deep learning models: a Bi-directional Feature Pyramid with Recurrent Attention Residual Modules (BDRAR) network for shadow segmentation and a YOLOv3 architecture for pedestrian detection. The output of these models is processed by a custom algorithm that calculates the intersection over union (IOU) between pedestrian bounding boxes and the binary shadow map to determine whether a person is in shade or sun. To enhance accuracy, the algorithm restricts the IOU calculation to the lower half of each bounding box, minimizing false classifications caused by self-shadowing or background shading. Only the count of pedestrians in shade and sun is transmitted to the cloud; all raw images and video frames are deleted immediately after processing to ensure privacy.

The machine learning model for MRT estimation is trained using a supervised learning approach on a dataset of paired measurements from the reference-grade MaRTy platform and the MaRTiny system. The model accepts as input the averaged sensor values of air temperature, globe temperature, humidity, and UV intensity, and outputs a corrected estimate of MRT. The training process employs a support vector machine with a radial basis function kernel, selected for its superior performance on non-linear relationships between sensor inputs and MRT outputs, as well as its computational efficiency for edge deployment. The model achieves a root mean square error of less than four degrees Celsius on evaluation datasets, significantly improving upon the ten-degree error observed when using conventional empirical conversion methods. The model is stored locally on the Jetson Nano and performs inference in real time, enabling the system to output corrected MRT values without requiring external computation or internet connectivity during operation. The entire system is enclosed in a weather-resistant housing, designed for continuous outdoor deployment under direct sunlight, and powered by a single 20-watt DC power supply distributed across all components according to their operational requirements. The system is scalable, modular, and capable of being deployed in large networks across urban environments, with each unit operating independently while contributing to a unified data stream for city-wide thermal monitoring. The invention eliminates the need for manual data collection, reduces the cost of high-resolution thermal monitoring by over an order of magnitude, and provides the first integrated solution for correlating human thermal exposure with actual pedestrian behavior in outdoor public spaces.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Mean Radiant Temperature (MRT) Sensing

The accurate measurement of mean radiant temperature (MRT) in outdoor environments is a fundamental challenge in biometeorology and urban climate science. MRT represents the equivalent temperature of a surrounding enclosure that would induce the same rate of radiant heat exchange with a human body as the actual, heterogeneous environment. Unlike air temperature, which reflects the thermal state of the surrounding air, MRT accounts for the cumulative effect of longwave radiation emitted by surfaces such as asphalt, concrete, brick, glass, and vegetation, as well as shortwave radiation from the sun. In sun-exposed urban areas, MRT can be more than thirty degrees Celsius higher than air temperature, making it the dominant factor in determining human thermal comfort and heat stress. Conventional methods for measuring MRT involve the use of three net radiometers arranged orthogonally to capture radiation fluxes from six directions—upward, downward, and four lateral orientations. These fluxes are then integrated using the Stefan-Boltzmann law, weighted by directional factors that account for the cylindrical geometry of the human body. While this method provides a highly accurate reference measurement, it requires expensive instrumentation, typically costing over fifteen thousand dollars in total, and demands careful calibration, maintenance, and deployment by trained personnel, rendering it impractical for widespread or long-term use in public spaces.

To overcome these limitations, the present invention employs a gray globe thermometer as the primary sensor for estimating MRT. The globe, constructed from a commercially available gray-painted ping pong ball with a diameter of approximately thirty-eight millimeters, is chosen for its optical properties, which closely approximate the average solar reflectance of human skin and clothing combined. The globe is mounted on a vertical support at a height of approximately 1.7 meters, corresponding to the average height of a standing human, and is equipped with a high-precision thermocouple sensor embedded at its center. The thermocouple measures the temperature of the globe surface, which, under steady-state conditions, reaches an equilibrium determined by the balance of absorbed radiant energy and convective heat loss to the surrounding air. The conversion of globe temperature to MRT is traditionally performed using empirical models derived from field experiments, which relate globe temperature to air temperature, wind speed, and solar irradiance. One such model, developed by Vanos et al., provides a functional relationship that estimates MRT based on these inputs, but it is subject to significant errors when applied in non-ideal conditions, such as partial shading, variable wind patterns, or non-uniform surface emissivity.

The invention introduces a novel approach to this problem by integrating the globe thermometer into a machine learning framework that corrects for the systematic inaccuracies inherent in low-cost sensors. Rather than relying solely on the empirical conversion, the system collects raw sensor data—including globe temperature, air temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, and UV intensity—and feeds these values into a trained machine learning model that has learned the complex, non-linear mapping between these inputs and the true MRT values measured by a reference-grade six-directional system. The model is trained on thousands of paired measurements collected under diverse environmental conditions, including clear skies, partial cloud cover, morning and afternoon solar angles, and varying degrees of shading from trees, buildings, and other structures. The training dataset includes instances where the globe thermometer was partially shaded by vegetation or architectural elements, conditions that are common in real-world deployments and that severely degrade the accuracy of conventional MRT estimation methods. The machine learning model, implemented as a support vector machine with a radial basis function kernel, is capable of recognizing subtle patterns in the sensor data that correlate with these error-inducing conditions and adjusts its output accordingly. For example, when the globe temperature rises more slowly than expected under high solar irradiance due to a delayed thermal response, the model infers that the sensor is not fully exposed and adjusts the MRT estimate upward to compensate. Similarly, when wind speed is low and the globe temperature remains elevated despite declining solar radiation, the model recognizes the reduced convective cooling and adjusts the estimate to reflect the persistent radiant load.

The model is trained using a supervised learning paradigm, where the ground truth MRT values are obtained from a reference system that employs three net radiometers arranged in orthogonal planes, each measuring longwave and shortwave radiation in six directions. The reference system is operated simultaneously with the MaRTiny system in the same outdoor location, ensuring that both systems experience identical meteorological conditions. Data is collected at one-minute intervals, with the reference system logging measurements every two seconds and averaging them to match the temporal resolution of the MaRTiny system. The resulting dataset consists of over fifteen thousand paired observations, spanning multiple days, locations, and weather conditions. The model is trained using the scikit-learn library in Python, with hyperparameters optimized through five-fold cross-validation to minimize the root mean square error between predicted and observed MRT values. The selected model achieves a mean absolute error of less than three degrees Celsius and a root mean square error of less than four degrees Celsius on an independent evaluation dataset, representing a substantial improvement over the ten-degree error observed when using the conventional empirical conversion method. Importantly, the model is lightweight enough to be deployed on the edge device of the system, allowing for real-time inference without requiring cloud connectivity or external computation. The model is stored in a serialized format on the Jetson Nano’s local storage and loaded into memory at system startup, enabling immediate operation upon deployment.

The physical design of the globe thermometer is optimized for durability and thermal stability. The gray paint used on the globe is formulated to maintain consistent reflectance properties under prolonged UV exposure, and the thermocouple is encased in a thermally conductive epoxy to ensure rapid heat transfer between the globe surface and the sensor. The globe is mounted on a stainless steel rod that is anchored to a weather-resistant housing, which also contains the other meteorological sensors. The housing is constructed from UV-stabilized polycarbonate and is sealed to prevent moisture ingress while allowing for adequate airflow around the globe to facilitate convective cooling. The entire sensor array is designed to be mounted on a tripod or fixed to a pole at a height of 1.7 meters, ensuring that it is representative of the thermal environment experienced by pedestrians. The system is powered by a single 20-watt DC power supply, which is distributed across all components through a custom power management circuit that ensures each sensor receives the appropriate voltage and current. The anemometer, for example, requires a 9-volt supply, which is generated by a step-up converter from the primary 5-volt source, while the Jetson Nano operates at 10 watts in high-performance mode to support real-time inference. The system is designed to operate continuously for extended periods, with no moving parts other than the anemometer, and is capable of withstanding extreme temperatures ranging from below freezing to over fifty degrees Celsius. The combination of robust mechanical design, precise sensor calibration, and intelligent machine learning correction enables the system to deliver accurate, reliable, and actionable MRT measurements in real-world urban environments where traditional methods are impractical or prohibitively expensive.
