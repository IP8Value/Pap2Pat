# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to an automated radiosynthesizer system designed for the precise, reproducible, and scalable production of positron-emission tomography (PET) radiotracers under high-pressure, high-temperature, and chemically aggressive conditions. Specifically, the invention encompasses a modular, computer-controlled platform that integrates three independently operable reaction reactors, a programmable reagent and gas handling robot, and disposable fluidic cassettes configured to enable dynamic reconfiguration of fluid pathways without the use of permanently installed tubing or fixed valves. This system is particularly suited for the synthesis of complex nucleoside analogs and other radiolabeled compounds that require sealed reaction environments, precise thermal control, and the handling of volatile, corrosive, or air-sensitive reagents—conditions that exceed the operational limits of conventional automated radiosynthesizers. The invention further includes an integrated control architecture that coordinates mechanical motion, thermal regulation, pneumatic actuation, and fluidic routing through a unified software interface, allowing users to define and execute multi-step synthesis protocols as sequences of standardized unit operations without requiring physical reconfiguration of the instrument. The system is engineered to eliminate cross-contamination, reduce operator radiation exposure, and facilitate rapid transition from research-scale tracer development to routine clinical production, thereby addressing longstanding limitations in the field of automated radiochemistry for molecular imaging.

## BACKGROUND

The field of molecular imaging has been profoundly shaped by the advent of positron-emission tomography (PET), which enables non-invasive, quantitative visualization of biochemical processes in living organisms. Among the most widely used radiotracers is 2-[18F]fluoro-2-deoxy-d-glucose ([18F]FDG), whose metabolic trapping in glucose-avid tissues has made it indispensable for oncology, neurology, and cardiology applications. However, the growing demand for a broader array of PET tracers—particularly those targeting cell proliferation, gene expression, enzyme activity, and drug metabolism—has exposed critical limitations in existing automated synthesis platforms. Many of these novel tracers, such as nucleoside analogs like 2-deoxy-2-[18F]fluoro-β-d-arabinofuranosylcytosine (d-[18F]FAC) and 2-deoxy-2-[18F]fluoro-5-methyl-β-l-arabinofuranosyluracil (l-[18F]FMAU), require reaction conditions that involve elevated temperatures exceeding 150°C, prolonged exposure to volatile organic solvents such as acetonitrile or dimethylformamide, and the use of highly reactive or corrosive reagents including triflates, silylating agents, and strong acids. These conditions are incompatible with commercially available radiosynthesizers, which typically rely on fixed fluidic networks composed of permanently connected tubing, valves, and fittings that are incapable of withstanding the mechanical stresses, chemical degradation, or pressure differentials inherent in such syntheses.

Previous attempts to automate the synthesis of these advanced tracers have generally involved compromises to the underlying chemistry—such as substituting less reactive precursors, lowering reaction temperatures, or employing alternative solvents with lower vapor pressures—to remain within the operational envelope of existing instrumentation. These adaptations, while enabling partial automation, often result in diminished radiochemical yields, reduced specific activity, or the formation of undesirable byproducts, thereby undermining the scientific validity and clinical utility of the final product. Moreover, the reliance on fixed fluidic architectures necessitates extensive cleaning and sterilization procedures between syntheses, increasing the risk of cross-contamination and prolonging turnaround times. The presence of multiple permanent connections also introduces points of failure, including leaks, valve fouling, and seal degradation, which are exacerbated under repeated cycles of high-pressure operation and thermal cycling.

To address these challenges, earlier prototypes introduced movable reaction vessels that could be sealed against inert gaskets during high-pressure reactions, thereby eliminating the need for fixed tubing connections to the reaction chamber. While this innovation improved pressure tolerance, it did not resolve the broader issue of reagent delivery, solvent evaporation, purification, and product transfer—all of which remained constrained by static fluidic paths and manual intervention. Furthermore, the handling of sensitive reagents, such as those stored in sealed vials under inert atmosphere, was not fully automated, requiring manual transfer and increasing radiation exposure to operators. The absence of an integrated, programmable robotic system for reagent handling, coupled with the lack of a modular, disposable fluidic architecture, meant that transitioning between different synthesis protocols still required significant manual setup, instrument recalibration, and validation of fluidic integrity.

Existing systems also lacked the ability to perform simultaneous, independent syntheses in multiple reaction vessels, limiting throughput and efficiency. The integration of purification steps, such as cartridge-based trapping and elution, was either absent or required external, non-automated components, introducing variability and increasing the potential for human error. Additionally, the control systems of prior art were often fragmented, relying on disparate software platforms for thermal control, motion actuation, and radioactivity monitoring, with no unified interface to coordinate the timing and sequencing of complex multi-step protocols. The absence of real-time visual feedback, precise pressure and temperature regulation, and fail-safe mechanisms for detecting missing reagents or misaligned components further compromised the reliability and reproducibility of automated syntheses.

Consequently, there exists a critical unmet need in the field of radiochemistry for a fully integrated, modular, and programmable radiosynthesizer capable of performing high-pressure, high-temperature syntheses of complex radiotracers without compromising chemical integrity, operator safety, or process consistency. Such a system must eliminate permanent fluidic connections, automate the handling of volatile and sensitive reagents, enable dynamic reconfiguration of fluid pathways through mechanical motion, support simultaneous multi-reactor operations, and provide a user-friendly software interface that abstracts the complexity of the underlying hardware. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel architecture that combines three independently controlled reactors, a dual-function reagent and gas handling robot, and disposable cassettes with integrated fluidic networks, all coordinated through a centralized control system that ensures precise, repeatable, and scalable production of PET radiotracers under conditions previously deemed incompatible with automation.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses an automated radiosynthesizer system designed for the reliable, high-yield production of complex positron-emission tomography (PET) radiotracers under demanding chemical and physical conditions. The system comprises three core components that operate in concert: a set of three independently controllable reaction reactors, a programmable reagent and gas handling robot, and disposable fluidic cassettes configured to eliminate permanent fluidic connections and enable dynamic reconfiguration of synthesis pathways. Each reactor is equipped with a three-segment spring-loaded chuck that securely holds a glass reaction vessel and provides precise, localized thermal control via individual cartridge heaters and thermocouples, enabling uniform heating and rapid cooling through an integrated liquid coolant loop. The reactors are mounted on linear actuators that allow precise vertical and horizontal movement of the reaction vessel to multiple predefined positions beneath a disposable cassette, each position corresponding to a distinct unit operation such as sealed reaction, solvent evaporation, reagent addition, or product transfer. By sealing the reaction vessel directly against a rubber gasket affixed to the underside of the cassette, the system eliminates the need for fixed tubing or valves connected to the vessel, thereby enabling operation at pressures and temperatures far beyond the limits of conventional synthesizers.

The reagent and gas handling robot is a two-axis mobile platform equipped with a pneumatically actuated gripper that retrieves sealed reagent vials from storage positions on the cassette and precisely positions them above dedicated delivery needles. The robot also includes a movable gas supplier that lowers to engage vacuum and inert gas ports on the cassette, enabling controlled pressurization of reagent vials for fluid delivery and facilitating solvent evaporation by simultaneously applying vacuum and inert gas flow. The gas supplier is mounted on a spring-loaded z-axis actuator to ensure consistent sealing without mechanical interference, and all movements are monitored by Hall effect sensors to prevent misalignment and ensure operational safety. The disposable cassettes, fabricated from molded polyurethane and containing stainless steel needles, chemically inert stopcock valves, and PTFE-coated silicone gaskets, serve as the primary fluidic network for each synthesis. Each cassette includes eleven inverted reagent vial storage positions, three reagent delivery positions, two independent sealed reaction zones, and integrated pathways for cartridge-based purification, waste collection, and product transfer between reactors. The cassettes are designed to be preassembled, sterile, and single-use, eliminating the need for cleaning, sterilization, or cross-contamination mitigation between syntheses.

The system is controlled by a Linux-based server that communicates with a programmable logic controller (PLC) via Ethernet, which in turn coordinates all mechanical, thermal, pneumatic, and radiochemical functions. The PLC drives linear servomotors for reactor and robot motion, solenoid valves for gas and vacuum distribution, solid-state relays for heater control, and a liquid coolant pump for active thermal regulation. A video server captures real-time visual feedback from cameras mounted behind each reactor, enabling remote observation of liquid levels, reaction progress, and phase transitions. The system employs a drag-and-drop software interface that allows users to construct synthesis protocols as sequences of standardized unit operations—including radioisotope trapping, reagent addition, sealed reaction, solvent evaporation, cartridge purification, and product transfer—without requiring any physical modification of the hardware. Each unit operation is defined by parameters such as temperature, duration, pressure, stirring speed, and sequence, which are stored in a database and can be reused, modified, or shared across laboratories. The system further incorporates an electronically controlled HPLC injection valve for semi-preparative purification, with manual loading of the HPLC loop currently supported and automated loading designed for future implementation.

The invention enables the synthesis of a wide range of radiotracers—including d-[18F]FAC, l-[18F]FMAU, [18F]FDG, [18F]FLT, [18F]fallypride, [18F]FHBG, and [18F]SFB—by simply changing the cassette and loading the corresponding software protocol. No hardware reconfiguration, tubing replacement, or valve recalibration is required between syntheses. The system achieves radiochemical yields comparable to or exceeding those of manual methods, with radiochemical purities exceeding 99% and specific activities sufficient for preclinical and clinical use. The disposable cassette architecture ensures sterility, eliminates cross-contamination, and reduces operator radiation exposure by minimizing manual handling. The integration of real-time monitoring, fail-safe detection, and automated reagent handling allows for unattended operation, making the system suitable for both research and clinical manufacturing environments. The invention represents a fundamental advancement in automated radiochemistry by decoupling chemical synthesis from fixed fluidic architectures and replacing them with a dynamic, motion-based, modular system that is both versatile and robust.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF ILLUSTRATED EMBODIMENTS

The automated radiosynthesizer system of the present invention is a comprehensive, integrated platform engineered to perform complex, multi-step radiotracer syntheses under conditions that were previously inaccessible to automated systems. The system is composed of three primary functional modules: the reactor assembly, the reagent and gas handling robot, and the disposable fluidic cassette, each of which is meticulously designed to operate in concert under centralized software control. The reactor assembly serves as the core reaction chamber, providing precise thermal regulation and dynamic positional control of the reaction vessel. Each reactor is constructed to accommodate a standard 5-mL glass V-vial, which is held firmly within a three-segment spring-loaded chuck designed to maximize thermal contact between the vessel walls and the heating elements. Each segment of the chuck contains an independent 100-W cartridge heater and a K-type thermocouple, allowing for individual feedback control of temperature across the vessel’s surface. This configuration ensures uniform heating and minimizes thermal gradients that could lead to uneven reaction kinetics or localized decomposition of sensitive reagents. The maximum operating temperature of each reactor is 185°C, with the potential for future upgrades to higher temperature tolerances through the use of alternative sealing materials and thermal fittings. Active cooling is achieved by circulating a propylene/ethylene glycol and water mixture through cooling channels integrated into all three reactors in series, with the coolant pumped by a dedicated liquid pump and dissipated through a radiator equipped with three 140-mm fans. This active cooling system enables rapid temperature reduction following a reaction, which is critical for quenching exothermic processes and preventing degradation of the radiolabeled product.

The reactor is mounted on a linear actuator that allows precise vertical movement along a fixed axis, enabling the reaction vessel to be raised and lowered into multiple predefined positions beneath the disposable cassette. These positions are engineered to correspond to distinct unit operations: one for sealed reaction, another for solvent evaporation, a third for reagent addition, and a fourth for product transfer. When the reactor is raised to a sealed reaction position, the top of the reaction vessel is pressed firmly against a custom polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE)-coated silicone gasket affixed to the underside of the cassette. This seal is designed to withstand internal pressures exceeding 100 psig and temperatures up to 185°C without leakage or degradation. The integrity of the seal is continuously monitored through feedback from the linear actuator and confirmed by Hall effect sensors that detect the fully raised position of the reactor. This eliminates the need for permanent tubing or valves connected to the reaction vessel, which are the primary sources of pressure limitations and failure points in conventional synthesizers. The reaction vessel is further equipped with a removable magnetic stir bar, which is rotated by a DC motor mounted externally to the reactor. The stirring speed and duration are programmable and can be synchronized with the reaction temperature to ensure homogeneity during prolonged syntheses. A high-resolution camera is mounted behind each reactor, providing real-time visual feedback of the reaction contents. This visual monitoring capability allows operators to observe solvent evaporation, confirm reagent addition, detect precipitate formation, and verify the clarity of the final product, all without opening the system or interrupting the synthesis.

The reagent and gas handling robot is a three-axis mobile platform that performs the dual functions of reagent delivery and gas supply. The x- and y-axis motions are driven by a pair of linear servomotors with stroke lengths of 350 mm and 100 mm, respectively, enabling the robot to access any of the eleven reagent vial storage positions or the three reagent delivery locations on the cassette. The z-axis motion is accomplished by a pneumatically actuated cylinder that lowers the gripper to grasp and lift reagent vials from their storage positions and then raises them to align with the delivery needles on the cassette. The gripper is designed to securely hold 13-mm crimped septum-cap vials in an inverted configuration, ensuring that the septum is oriented downward for needle penetration. Each reagent vial is sealed under inert atmosphere and contains a pre-measured volume of reagent, eliminating the need for manual dispensing and reducing the risk of contamination or exposure. To deliver the reagent, the robot first moves the vial to a designated delivery position, where it is lowered onto a pair of stainless steel needles. Simultaneously, the gas supplier—a separate z-axis actuator mounted adjacent to the gripper—is lowered to engage the inert gas and vacuum ports on the top of the cassette. The inert gas line, regulated to pressures between 3 and 60 psig depending on the application, pressurizes the vial, forcing the liquid contents through the delivery needle and into the reaction vessel. The vacuum port, positioned slightly above the inert gas port and mounted on a spring-loaded mechanism, is activated during evaporation cycles to remove vapor from the sealed reaction vessel. The spring-loaded design ensures that the vacuum port maintains contact with the cassette even if the inert gas port is not fully engaged, preventing mechanical collision and ensuring reliable sealing. An in-line check valve is installed on the inert gas line near the delivery point to prevent backflow of vapor into the gas supply system, and a cold trap cooled by a dry ice and methanol mixture is positioned between the vacuum port and the integrated vacuum pump to capture volatile organic compounds and protect the pump from contamination. All movements of the robot and gas supplier are monitored by Hall effect sensors to ensure that the gripper and gas supplier are fully retracted before any x- or y-axis motion is initiated, thereby preventing damage to the cassette or the robot components.

The disposable cassettes are the central innovation that enables the system’s flexibility and reliability. Fabricated from molded polyurethane, each cassette contains an integrated network of fluidic pathways, including stainless steel needles, chemically inert three-way stopcock valves, and PTFE-coated silicone gaskets. The cassettes are designed to be preassembled, sterile, and single-use, eliminating the need for cleaning, sterilization, or validation between syntheses. Each cassette contains eleven storage positions for reagent vials, arranged in an inverted configuration to facilitate delivery through downward-pointing needles. Three of these positions are designated as reagent delivery locations: two for direct addition to the reaction vessel and one for eluent delivery during purification steps. The fluid delivery needles in the two primary reagent addition positions terminate directly beneath the sealed reaction zones, ensuring that reagents are delivered precisely into the reaction vessel without exposure to external environments. The third delivery position is connected via internal tubing to a stopcock valve that controls the flow of eluent during cartridge purification. Two of the stopcock valves are configured in series to manage the trapping and release of radioisotopes such as [18F]fluoride. The upstream valve selects between incoming fluid from an external source, such as a cyclotron or elution vial, and the eluent delivery position. The output of this valve is connected to a cartridge—typically a QMA or silica cartridge—through which the radioisotope is trapped. The output of the cartridge is then routed to a second stopcock valve, which directs the flow either to a waste collection vial or to the reagent addition position, enabling the elution of the radioisotope into the reaction vessel. The third stopcock valve is used to control the purification of the crude product after synthesis. It is positioned between a dip tube, which extends from the reaction vessel into the cartridge, and an output line that can be directed to a waste vial or to the input of the next reaction vessel. This configuration allows for sequential trapping, washing, and elution of the product without requiring manual intervention or external equipment. The cassettes are inserted into the system along precision-machined rails and locked in place by clamps that engage alignment features to ensure consistent positioning relative to the reactors and robot. The stopcock valves are actuated by rotary pneumatic actuators that rotate the internal valve stems with high precision, and their positions are confirmed by limit switches to ensure accurate fluid routing.

The control system of the invention is a distributed architecture centered on a Linux server that communicates with a programmable logic controller (PLC) over Ethernet. The PLC serves as the real-time interface between the software and the hardware, managing the operation of linear actuators, pneumatics, heating elements, cooling systems, and radioactivity detectors. The system employs a variety of expansion modules, including digital input/output modules for sensor feedback, analog output modules for pressure regulation, temperature input modules for thermocouple readings, and relay modules for switching high-power heaters. Three linear servomotor controllers are connected to a RoboNET network gateway, which in turn is controlled by the PLC. These controllers drive the y-axis motion of each reactor, ensuring synchronized and precise positioning. Two additional pulse motor controllers manage the x- and y-axis motion of the reagent and gas handling robot, enabling high-precision navigation across the cassette. A source of inert gas is dynamically regulated by two analog pressure regulators that reduce the input pressure from greater than 60 psig to two distinct output pressures: one for driving pneumatic actuators at approximately 60 psig, and another for fluid transfers and evaporation at 3 to 15 psig. These pressure lines are distributed through solenoid valve banks located in the synthesis module, allowing the software to selectively activate gas flow to specific components based on the current unit operation. The system also includes solid-state relays to switch the 100-W cartridge heaters on and off, a coolant pump and reservoir for thermal regulation, and a video server that encodes analog signals from the reactor cameras into digital video streams accessible via Ethernet. An electronically controlled HPLC injection valve is integrated into the system, allowing for semi-preparative purification of the synthesized product. Although the current implementation requires manual loading of the HPLC loop, the architecture is designed to support automated loading in future iterations through the integration of a robotic sample injector.

The software interface is a drag-and-drop platform that allows users to construct synthesis protocols by selecting and sequencing predefined unit operations. Each unit operation—such as “Radioisotope Trapping,” “Reagent Addition,” “Sealed Reaction,” “Solvent Evaporation,” “Cartridge Purification,” and “Product Transfer”—is defined by a set of parameters including temperature, duration, pressure, stirring speed, and sequence dependencies. These parameters are stored in a database and can be modified, saved, and reused across multiple protocols. The interface provides real-time feedback on the status of each reactor, the position of the robot, the pressure and temperature of the reaction vessel, and the availability of reagents. Visual indicators alert the user to missing vials, misaligned components, or failed seals, and the system will halt operation if any safety condition is violated. The software also logs all operational data, including timestamps, temperature profiles, pressure readings, and radioactivity counts, for audit and regulatory compliance. The system is designed to be operated with minimal user intervention, allowing for unattended synthesis runs that can be initiated remotely and monitored via networked terminals. The integration of the HPLC injection valve allows for seamless transition from synthesis to purification, with the collected fractions automatically recorded and correlated with the radioactivity profile. The entire system is housed within a compact, shielded enclosure that minimizes radiation exposure and provides electromagnetic shielding for sensitive electronics. The modular design of the cassettes and the standardized interface between the reactors, robot, and control system ensure that the platform can be scaled to additional reactors or expanded with new functionalities without requiring a complete redesign of the hardware. The system has been validated through the successful synthesis of multiple radiotracers, including d-[18F]FAC and l-[18F]FMAU, with radiochemical yields exceeding 60%, radiochemical purities greater than 99%, and specific activities sufficient for preclinical and clinical imaging applications. The elimination of permanent fluidic connections, the automation of reagent handling, and the integration of real-time monitoring and fail-safe mechanisms represent a paradigm shift in automated radiochemistry, enabling the routine production of complex radiotracers that were previously inaccessible to automated systems.
