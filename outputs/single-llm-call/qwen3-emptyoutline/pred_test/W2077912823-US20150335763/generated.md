# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to novel therapeutic compositions and methods for the treatment of inflammatory bowel disease and other inflammatory conditions characterized by elevated levels of high mobility group box 1 protein. Specifically, the invention encompasses immobilized DNA constructs covalently attached to solid support matrices that selectively bind and sequester extracellular high mobility group box 1 protein, thereby reducing its pro-inflammatory activity in the gastrointestinal lumen and systemic circulation. The compositions are formulated for oral administration and are particularly effective in mitigating the pathological consequences of chronic intestinal inflammation, including epithelial barrier disruption, mucosal ulceration, cytokine hyperproduction, and tissue remodeling. The invention further encompasses methods for diagnosing and monitoring inflammatory bowel disease through the detection and quantification of high mobility group box 1 protein captured from biological samples using these immobilized DNA constructs. The therapeutic compositions are designed to function as inert, non-immunogenic, non-toxic, and pH-stable sequestration agents that operate independently of immune modulation or systemic absorption, offering a mechanistically distinct approach to the management of inflammatory bowel disease that circumvents the limitations of conventional immunosuppressive and biologic therapies.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Inflammatory bowel disease represents a group of chronic, relapsing disorders of the gastrointestinal tract that include ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease, affecting millions of individuals worldwide and imposing a substantial burden on healthcare systems due to recurrent hospitalizations, surgical interventions, and long-term pharmacological management. Despite decades of research and the development of increasingly sophisticated biologic agents targeting specific cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukin-12/23, a significant proportion of patients fail to achieve sustained remission, experience loss of response over time, or develop serious adverse effects including infections and malignancies. The underlying pathophysiology of inflammatory bowel disease remains incompletely understood, but it is widely accepted that a dysregulated immune response to commensal gut microbiota, coupled with defects in epithelial barrier integrity and aberrant release of damage-associated molecular patterns, plays a central role in disease initiation and perpetuation. Among these damage-associated molecular patterns, high mobility group box 1 protein has emerged as a critical mediator of inflammation, acting not merely as a passive marker of cellular damage but as an active signaling molecule capable of amplifying innate immune responses through engagement of pattern recognition receptors such as toll-like receptor 4 and the receptor for advanced glycation end products.

High mobility group box 1 protein is a highly conserved nuclear protein that functions in chromatin architecture, transcriptional regulation, and DNA repair under physiological conditions. However, upon cellular stress, necrosis, or activation of immune cells, it is actively secreted or passively released into the extracellular space, where it assumes a potent pro-inflammatory role. In the context of inflammatory bowel disease, elevated levels of extracellular high mobility group box 1 protein have been consistently detected in the serum, feces, and inflamed mucosal tissues of both human patients and experimental animal models. Its presence correlates strongly with disease severity, and its neutralization through pharmacological inhibitors or neutralizing antibodies has been shown to attenuate colonic injury, reduce weight loss, and improve histological scores in preclinical models. Importantly, the biological activity of extracellular high mobility group box 1 protein is exquisitely regulated by its redox state, which is determined by the oxidation status of three conserved cysteine residues. The disulfide form, characterized by an intramolecular bond between cysteine 23 and cysteine 45 and a reduced cysteine 106, is the primary isoform responsible for cytokine-inducing activity, whereas the fully reduced form promotes chemotaxis, and the terminally oxidized sulfonyl form is biologically inert. Furthermore, post-translational modifications such as acetylation of lysine residues within nuclear localization sequences facilitate its export from the nucleus, thereby enabling its extracellular release. These multiple isoforms coexist in the inflamed intestinal lumen, suggesting that any effective therapeutic strategy must be capable of capturing a broad spectrum of high mobility group box 1 protein variants, not merely a single conformation.

Traditional approaches to neutralizing high mobility group box 1 protein have relied on monoclonal antibodies, small molecule inhibitors, or recombinant protein antagonists, all of which face significant challenges in the gastrointestinal environment. Antibodies are expensive to produce, susceptible to proteolytic degradation in the gut, and may elicit immunogenic responses with chronic use. Small molecules often lack sufficient specificity and may interfere with essential intracellular functions of high mobility group box 1 protein if absorbed systemically. Moreover, the dynamic and heterogeneous nature of high mobility group box 1 protein isoforms in the inflamed intestine necessitates a therapeutic agent capable of binding multiple structural variants with high affinity and without discrimination based on redox or acetylation state. Previous attempts to exploit the intrinsic DNA-binding property of high mobility group box 1 protein have been limited to soluble DNA oligonucleotides, which, while capable of neutralizing its activity in cell culture, are rapidly degraded by nucleases in biological fluids, have poor bioavailability when administered orally, and may themselves trigger unintended immune responses through activation of cytosolic DNA sensors. There exists, therefore, a critical unmet need for a stable, non-immunogenic, orally deliverable therapeutic agent that can effectively sequester high mobility group box 1 protein directly within the intestinal lumen, thereby preventing its interaction with immune and epithelial cells without systemic absorption or interference with endogenous physiological processes.

The present invention addresses this need by introducing a class of engineered biomaterials composed of high-affinity DNA oligonucleotides covalently immobilized onto inert, biocompatible, and mechanically stable sepharose beads. These beads are designed to function as molecular sponges that selectively and irreversibly capture high mobility group box 1 protein from the gastrointestinal lumen, regardless of its redox or acetylation state, while exhibiting no binding affinity for other structurally similar proteins such as histones or cytokines. The DNA sequences employed are derived from previously characterized high-affinity binding motifs that have been optimized for maximal interaction with high mobility group box 1 protein, and their covalent attachment to the bead matrix ensures resistance to enzymatic degradation, including exposure to pancreatic nucleases and gastric acid. The sepharose bead platform provides a physically robust scaffold that maintains structural integrity under the extreme pH conditions of the stomach and the proteolytic milieu of the intestine, while its size distribution allows for uniform suspension in oral formulations and efficient transit through the gastrointestinal tract without causing obstruction or mechanical irritation. The resulting composition is non-toxic, non-absorbed, and does not elicit innate immune activation, making it uniquely suited for chronic oral administration in patients with inflammatory bowel disease. This invention represents a paradigm shift in the therapeutic approach to inflammatory bowel disease by shifting the focus from systemic immunomodulation to localized, target-specific sequestration of a key driver of mucosal inflammation.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel therapeutic composition for the treatment of inflammatory bowel disease and related inflammatory conditions, comprising a solid support matrix composed of sepharose beads with a diameter ranging from 45 to 165 micrometers, wherein the surface of the beads is covalently conjugated with one or more DNA oligonucleotides that bind with high affinity to high mobility group box 1 protein. The DNA oligonucleotides are selected from a group consisting of linear sequences with phosphorothioate backbone modifications and a non-immunogenic sequence profile, as well as structured DNA motifs such as four-way junctions and kinked duplexes, each of which exhibits a dissociation constant for high mobility group box 1 protein in the nanomolar range. The covalent attachment of the DNA to the sepharose matrix is achieved through a carbodiimide-mediated coupling reaction that ensures stable linkage between a terminal amino group on the DNA and activated cyanogen bromide groups on the bead surface, resulting in a binding capacity for high mobility group box 1 protein of at least one microgram per fifty microliters of bead slurry. The composition is formulated as an oral suspension in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, and is administered to a subject suffering from inflammatory bowel disease in a dosage sufficient to reduce the concentration of extracellular high mobility group box 1 protein in the gastrointestinal lumen and serum, thereby ameliorating clinical symptoms including weight loss, diarrhea, rectal bleeding, and abdominal pain, as well as histological markers of mucosal injury such as crypt distortion, epithelial erosion, and transmural inflammation.

In a preferred embodiment, the DNA oligonucleotides are selected from a group consisting of S1, S2, and S3 sequences, each of which demonstrates superior binding affinity and stability under acidic conditions compared to other DNA constructs, with S2 being the most effective in capturing high mobility group box 1 protein from complex biological matrices such as fecal extracts and inflamed colonic tissue. The S1 and S2 sequences are linear, phosphorothioated oligonucleotides with a length of approximately 20 nucleotides, while the S3 sequence is a four-way junction structure that exhibits a higher binding stoichiometry due to its multivalent architecture. The sepharose beads are manufactured to have a uniform particle size distribution with a mean diameter of approximately 90 micrometers, ensuring optimal flow characteristics and resistance to aggregation during oral administration. The composition is stable under acidic conditions (pH 1–2) for at least one hour, retains its binding capacity after exposure to physiological concentrations of DNase I, and does not release detectable amounts of free DNA into biological fluids during transit through the gastrointestinal tract. The composition is non-toxic to intestinal epithelial cells, does not induce the production of type I interferons or other pro-inflammatory cytokines, and exhibits no binding affinity for histone H3, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, or other common inflammatory mediators.

The invention further encompasses a method of treating inflammatory bowel disease in a subject in need thereof, comprising orally administering to the subject an effective amount of the composition described above for a period sufficient to achieve a clinically meaningful reduction in disease activity. The method is applicable to both acute and chronic forms of inflammatory bowel disease, including dextran sulfate sodium-induced colitis and spontaneous colitis in interleukin-10 knockout mice, and is equally effective in human patients with ulcerative colitis or Crohn’s disease. The administration is performed daily or every other day over a course of several weeks, and the therapeutic benefit is evidenced by improvements in body weight, reduction in serum and fecal levels of high mobility group box 1 protein, decreased expression of colonic interleukin-6 and interleukin-1 beta mRNA, and improvement in histological scoring of colonic tissue injury. The method further includes the step of monitoring disease progression by measuring high mobility group box 1 protein levels in fecal samples collected prior to and during treatment, using the same composition as a capture agent in an ex vivo assay. The invention also provides a diagnostic kit for the detection of high mobility group box 1 protein in biological samples, comprising the immobilized DNA-bead composition, a buffer solution for washing and elution, and reagents for quantification of captured protein by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay or western blot analysis.

The invention is distinguished from prior art by its unique combination of features: the use of immobilized DNA rather than soluble oligonucleotides, the selection of DNA sequences that bind multiple redox and acetylation isoforms of high mobility group box 1 protein, the covalent attachment to a stable bead matrix that confers resistance to degradation and enables oral delivery, and the absence of immunogenicity or cytotoxicity despite prolonged exposure to the gastrointestinal environment. Unlike prior approaches that rely on systemic administration of biologics or small molecules, this invention operates locally within the intestinal lumen, minimizing off-target effects and eliminating the need for parenteral delivery or immunosuppression. The composition is self-limiting in its action, as it captures only the excess extracellular high mobility group box 1 protein without interfering with its intracellular functions, thereby preserving essential cellular processes. This invention represents a fundamentally new therapeutic modality for inflammatory bowel disease, grounded in the specific molecular recognition of a key pathogenic mediator and engineered for practical, safe, and durable clinical application.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention is directed to a therapeutic composition and method for the treatment of inflammatory bowel disease through the targeted sequestration of high mobility group box 1 protein from the gastrointestinal lumen and systemic circulation using immobilized DNA constructs. The composition comprises a solid support matrix composed of sepharose beads, which are chemically modified to covalently bind one or more specific DNA oligonucleotides with high affinity for high mobility group box 1 protein. The sepharose beads are manufactured from cross-linked agarose and are available in a range of particle sizes, with the optimal diameter for oral administration falling between 45 and 165 micrometers, with a mean diameter of approximately 90 micrometers. This size range ensures that the beads remain suspended in liquid formulations, pass unimpeded through the gastrointestinal tract without causing mechanical obstruction, and provide a sufficient surface area for high-density DNA conjugation. The beads are activated using cyanogen bromide to generate reactive iminoester groups on the hydroxyl moieties of the agarose matrix, which then react with primary amino groups introduced at the 5′ terminus of the DNA oligonucleotides. The coupling reaction is carried out under controlled pH and temperature conditions to maximize conjugation efficiency while minimizing non-specific binding or bead aggregation. The efficiency of DNA immobilization is quantified by measuring the absorbance at 260 nanometers before and after conjugation, with coupling efficiencies consistently exceeding 85% for all DNA constructs tested.

The DNA oligonucleotides employed in the invention are selected based on their previously established high-affinity binding to high mobility group box 1 protein, as demonstrated through surface plasmon resonance analysis and competitive binding assays. Four distinct sequences were initially evaluated: S1 and S2, which are linear, double-stranded DNA motifs with phosphorothioate backbone modifications; S3, a four-way junction structure formed by the hybridization of four complementary strands; and S4, a kinked duplex DNA containing a central bulge. The phosphorothioate modification replaces a non-bridging oxygen atom in the phosphate backbone with sulfur, rendering the oligonucleotides resistant to degradation by nucleases commonly present in serum, intestinal secretions, and fecal matter. The S1 and S2 sequences are each approximately 20 nucleotides in length and share a common structural motif that has been shown to interact with the B-box domain of high mobility group box 1 protein. The S3 sequence forms a stable four-way junction that presents multiple binding interfaces, allowing for a higher stoichiometric binding capacity per bead. The S4 sequence, while capable of binding high mobility group box 1 protein, exhibits significantly lower affinity and was excluded from further development due to its inferior performance in binding assays. The sequences are synthesized using solid-phase phosphoramidite chemistry with a 5′ terminal hexylamine linker, which provides a primary amine group for covalent coupling to the activated sepharose beads. The synthesis is performed under strict quality control to ensure purity, sequence fidelity, and absence of contaminants such as truncated sequences or residual protecting groups.

The binding capacity of the resulting DNA-bead constructs is determined through saturation binding assays in which increasing concentrations of recombinant high mobility group box 1 protein are incubated with a fixed volume of beads. The amount of protein bound to the beads is quantified by western blot analysis of the eluted fraction following washing to remove unbound protein. The maximum binding capacity is calculated as the point at which further increases in protein concentration do not result in increased binding, indicating saturation of available binding sites. For the S1 and S2 constructs, the binding capacity is approximately one microgram of high mobility group box 1 protein per fifty microliters of bead slurry, corresponding to a molar binding ratio of approximately six to one. The S3 construct, due to its multivalent architecture, exhibits a higher binding capacity of approximately twelve to one, although its performance in biological matrices is compromised under acidic conditions. The binding kinetics are rapid, with maximal binding achieved within thirty minutes for S1 and S2 constructs and within fifteen minutes for S3, indicating that the interaction is not rate-limited by diffusion or conformational rearrangement. The dissociation constant for the interaction between the immobilized DNA and high mobility group box 1 protein is estimated to be in the low nanomolar range, comparable to or exceeding the affinity of monoclonal antibodies against high mobility group box 1 protein. Importantly, the binding is not affected by the redox state of high mobility group box 1 protein, as the DNA-bead constructs effectively capture the fully reduced, disulfide, and sulfonyl isoforms with equal efficiency. This is a critical advantage over other therapeutic modalities that target only a single conformation of the protein.

The stability of the DNA-bead composition under physiologically relevant conditions is a cornerstone of its clinical utility. When exposed to simulated gastric fluid at pH 1 or 2 for one hour, the S1 and S2 constructs retain full binding capacity, whereas the S3 construct demonstrates a significant reduction in efficiency, likely due to structural destabilization of the four-way junction under acidic conditions. This finding establishes S1 and S2 as the preferred constructs for oral administration, as they are capable of surviving transit through the stomach and remaining functional in the duodenum and beyond. The resistance of the immobilized DNA to enzymatic degradation is confirmed by pre-treating the beads with physiological concentrations of DNase I (40 nanograms per milliliter) prior to exposure to high mobility group box 1 protein. Under these conditions, the binding capacity remains unchanged, demonstrating that the phosphorothioate backbone and the physical protection afforded by the bead matrix effectively shield the DNA from nuclease activity. The absence of free DNA release into the surrounding medium is verified using fluorescently labeled S2 beads incubated in fecal extracts; no significant increase in fluorescence is detected in the supernatant, indicating that the DNA remains tightly bound to the beads throughout the duration of exposure. This is essential for safety, as the release of free DNA could potentially trigger unintended immune responses through activation of cytosolic DNA sensors such as cGAS-STING.

The specificity of the DNA-bead composition for high mobility group box 1 protein is rigorously established through binding assays with a panel of structurally related proteins. Histone H3, a core nucleosomal protein that also binds DNA with high affinity, shows no detectable binding to the DNA-bead constructs, even at concentrations tenfold higher than those of high mobility group box 1 protein. Similarly, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, interleukin-6, and other cytokines commonly elevated in inflammatory bowel disease do not bind to the beads, confirming that the interaction is not mediated by non-specific electrostatic or hydrophobic forces. The lack of binding to histone H3 is particularly significant, as histones are abundant in necrotic tissue and are themselves potent damage-associated molecular patterns. The inability of the beads to capture histone H3 suggests that the interaction with high mobility group box 1 protein is highly sequence- and structure-specific, likely involving precise molecular contacts between the DNA backbone and the DNA-binding domains of high mobility group box 1 protein. This specificity ensures that the therapeutic effect is attributable solely to the removal of high mobility group box 1 protein and not to the non-selective sequestration of other inflammatory mediators.

The therapeutic efficacy of the composition is demonstrated in two well-established murine models of inflammatory bowel disease: the dextran sulfate sodium-induced acute colitis model and the interleukin-10 knockout spontaneous colitis model. In the dextran sulfate sodium model, mice are administered 4% dextran sulfate sodium in drinking water for five days, followed by normal water for three days, resulting in severe mucosal inflammation, epithelial erosion, crypt loss, and weight loss. Oral administration of the S2 DNA-bead composition at a dose of 300 microliters of a 50% slurry on days 0, 1, 2, 4, and 6 results in a significant attenuation of weight loss compared to control mice receiving beads without DNA or beads with the non-binding S4 construct. The improvement in body weight is accompanied by a marked reduction in serum and fecal levels of high mobility group box 1 protein, decreased expression of colonic interleukin-6 and interleukin-1 beta mRNA, and significant improvement in histological scores for inflammation severity, extent, crypt damage, and percentage of involvement. In the interleukin-10 knockout model, which develops spontaneous colitis beginning at approximately 12 weeks of age, daily administration of the S2 DNA-bead composition for six weeks results in a sustained increase in body weight, reduction in serum high mobility group box 1 protein, and significant improvement in colonic histology compared to untreated or control bead-treated mice. The therapeutic effect is dose-dependent and requires continuous administration, suggesting that the composition acts by continuously removing newly released high mobility group box 1 protein rather than by inducing long-term immune tolerance.

The mechanism of action of the composition is entirely extracellular and localized to the gastrointestinal lumen. No systemic absorption of the beads or the conjugated DNA is detected, and there is no evidence of immune activation, as measured by the absence of type I interferon mRNA induction in macrophage cell lines exposed to the beads. The beads do not alter the viability of intestinal epithelial cells, as demonstrated by lactate dehydrogenase release assays in Caco-2 and Hela cell lines. The composition does not interfere with the normal function of the gut microbiota, nor does it alter the composition of fecal flora, as confirmed by 16S rRNA sequencing. The therapeutic benefit is therefore attributable solely to the sequestration of high mobility group box 1 protein, which prevents its interaction with toll-like receptor 4 and the receptor for advanced glycation end products on immune and epithelial cells. This interaction, when unimpeded, leads to the activation of nuclear factor kappa B and mitogen-activated protein kinase pathways, resulting in the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, increased epithelial permeability, and recruitment of neutrophils and macrophages to the mucosal lamina propria. By removing high mobility group box 1 protein from the lumen, the composition interrupts this pathogenic cascade at its source, allowing for mucosal healing and restoration of barrier function without the need for immunosuppression.

The composition can be formulated as a stable oral suspension in a variety of pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, including aqueous solutions containing buffering agents such as sodium bicarbonate or citrate, stabilizers such as hydroxypropyl methylcellulose, and flavoring agents to improve palatability. The suspension is administered using a syringe or oral dosing device, and the beads remain suspended in the liquid medium for the duration of administration. The formulation is designed to be taken with food or immediately after meals to enhance mixing with intestinal contents and maximize contact with the inflamed mucosa. The dosage regimen is tailored to the severity of disease, with initial treatment involving daily administration for the first two weeks, followed by maintenance dosing every other day or twice weekly, depending on clinical response. The composition is suitable for long-term use, as it lacks systemic toxicity, does not induce antibody formation, and does not lead to the development of resistance. The invention further encompasses the use of the composition as a diagnostic tool for monitoring disease activity, wherein fecal samples are incubated with the beads, the captured high mobility group box 1 protein is eluted and quantified, and the concentration is correlated with clinical disease activity indices. This application enables non-invasive, real-time assessment of mucosal inflammation and guides therapeutic decision-making.

## EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS

The experimental methodology employed to develop and validate the invention involved a comprehensive series of in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo assays designed to characterize the binding properties, stability, specificity, and therapeutic efficacy of the DNA-bead composition. Recombinant high mobility group box 1 protein was expressed in Escherichia coli and purified to homogeneity using standard chromatographic techniques, followed by endotoxin removal via Triton X-114 phase separation to ensure that all observed biological effects were attributable to high mobility group box 1 protein and not to contaminating lipopolysaccharide. The purity and concentration of the protein were confirmed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and Bradford assay, and the endotoxin content was verified to be less than ten picograms per milligram using the chromogenic limulus amebocyte lysate assay. Redox-modified isoforms of high mobility group box 1 protein were generated by treating the recombinant protein with either dithiothreitol to produce the fully reduced form or hydrogen peroxide to generate the disulfide and sulfonyl isoforms, as previously described in the literature. The redox state of each isoform was confirmed by non-reducing western blot analysis and mass spectrometry.

The DNA oligonucleotides S1, S2, S3, and S4 were synthesized by Genemed Synthesis, Inc., with phosphorothioate backbone modifications for S1 and S2 and phosphodiester linkages for S3 and S4. Each sequence was modified at the 5′ terminus with a hexylamine linker to enable covalent coupling to the sepharose beads. The coupling reaction was performed by incubating 1 milliliter of CNBr-activated sepharose beads with 8 nanomoles of S1 or S2 DNA or 4 nanomoles of S3 or S4 DNA in 0.1 M sodium bicarbonate buffer at pH 8.5 for 24 hours at room temperature with gentle rotation. Unreacted sites were blocked with ethanolamine, and the beads were washed extensively with phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 to remove unbound DNA. The concentration of immobilized DNA was determined by measuring the absorbance at 260 nanometers before and after coupling, and the coupling efficiency was calculated as the percentage of DNA retained on the beads. The resulting bead preparations were designated B1, B2, B3, and B4, corresponding to the DNA sequences S1, S2, S3, and S4, respectively. Control beads were prepared using the same procedure but without the addition of DNA.

Binding assays were conducted by incubating 5 microliters of drained DNA beads with varying concentrations of recombinant high mobility group box 1 protein ranging from 0 to 3 micrograms in a total volume of 50 microliters of phosphate-buffered saline at room temperature for two hours with rotation. After incubation, the beads were collected by centrifugation, washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline to remove unbound protein, and resuspended in sodium dodecyl sulfate loading buffer. The bound protein was eluted by boiling and analyzed by western blot using a monoclonal antibody specific for high mobility group box 1 protein. The intensity of the bands was quantified by densitometry, and the binding capacity was calculated as the maximum amount of protein bound per unit volume of beads. To determine binding kinetics, 20 microliters of B2 beads were incubated with 500 nanograms of high mobility group box 1 protein for time intervals ranging from 0 to 960 minutes, and the amount of bound protein was measured at each time point. The data were fitted to a one-phase association model to determine the rate constant and time to reach equilibrium.

To assess the binding of the DNA-bead composition to different isoforms of high mobility group box 1 protein, recombinant protein was first modified to generate the fully reduced, disulfide, and sulfonyl forms, then incubated with B1 and B2 beads. The bound protein was eluted and analyzed by western blot, demonstrating that all three isoforms were captured with comparable efficiency. To determine the acetylation status of high mobility group box 1 protein captured from biological samples, fecal extracts from dextran sulfate sodium-treated mice were incubated with B2 beads, and the captured protein was eluted using 8 molar urea. The eluate was subjected to tryptic digestion and analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry using a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer equipped with a C18 column. The resulting spectra were searched against a human and mouse protein database using Mascot software, and the presence of acetylated lysine residues in the nuclear localization sequences was confirmed by the detection of signature fragment ions corresponding to acetylated peptides. The results demonstrated that the captured high mobility group box 1 protein contained hyper-acetylated forms consistent with active secretion from immune cells, in contrast to the hypo-acetylated form found in cultured epithelial cells, which reflects passive release from necrotic cells.

The stability of the DNA-bead composition under acidic conditions was assessed by incubating 20 microliters of B1, B2, or B3 beads with 500 nanograms of high mobility group box 1 protein in either phosphate-buffered saline at pH 7.0 or hydrochloric acid at pH 1 or 2 for one hour at room temperature. After washing, the bound protein was quantified by western blot, revealing that B1 and B2 beads retained full binding capacity at pH 1 and 2, whereas B3 beads showed a marked reduction in binding. To evaluate resistance to DNase I degradation, B2 beads were pre-incubated with 40 nanograms per milliliter of DNase I for 30 minutes at 37°C, followed by washing and incubation with high mobility group box 1 protein. The binding capacity was unchanged compared to untreated beads, confirming that the phosphorothioate backbone and bead matrix conferred protection against enzymatic cleavage.

The immunogenicity of the DNA oligonucleotides and beads was evaluated by stimulating primary human macrophages with HMGB1 or lipopolysaccharide in the presence of S1, S2, or S3 DNA at a concentration of 1 micromolar. TNF-alpha release was measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, and no increase in cytokine production was observed in the presence of any of the DNA constructs. In murine RAW 264.7 cells, the induction of interferon-beta mRNA was assessed by quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction using primers specific for interferon-beta and hypoxanthine phosphoribosyltransferase as a housekeeping gene. The results showed that neither the free oligonucleotides nor the DNA-bead constructs induced interferon-beta expression, whereas lipopolysaccharide and polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid served as positive controls.

Cytotoxicity was evaluated using the lactate dehydrogenase release assay in human cervical cancer Hela cells and human colorectal adenocarcinoma Caco-2 cells. Cells were incubated with increasing concentrations of B2 beads or control beads for up to 72 hours, and the supernatant was analyzed for lactate dehydrogenase activity using a commercial kit. No significant difference in lactate dehydrogenase release was observed between cells treated with B2 beads and untreated controls, confirming the absence of cytotoxic effects.

The ability of the DNA-bead composition to capture high mobility group box 1 protein from complex biological matrices was demonstrated in several ex vivo models. Activated RAW 264.7 cell supernatants were concentrated tenfold using centrifugal filters and incubated with B1, B2, or control beads. High mobility group box 1 protein was efficiently captured by B1 and B2 beads but not by control beads. Fecal extracts from dextran sulfate sodium-treated mice were prepared by homogenizing colon contents in phosphate-buffered saline containing protease inhibitors, centrifuging to remove debris, and incubating the supernatant with B1 or B2 beads. Western blot analysis confirmed the capture of high mobility group box 1 protein from these samples. Similarly, serum from mice subjected to cecal ligation and puncture, a model of sepsis with elevated circulating high mobility group box 1 protein, was incubated with B2 beads, and a significant reduction in free high mobility group box 1 protein was observed in the supernatant, accompanied by its detection on the beads.

In vivo efficacy was evaluated in two murine models of inflammatory bowel disease. For the dextran sulfate sodium model, female BALB/c mice were administered 4% dextran sulfate sodium in drinking water for five days, followed by normal water for three days. On days 0, 1, 2, 4, and 6, mice received 300 microliters of a 50% slurry of B2 beads, B4 beads, or control beads by oral gavage. Body weight was monitored daily, and on day 8, mice were euthanized, and blood, feces, and colon tissues were collected. Serum and fecal high mobility group box 1 protein levels were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, and colonic mRNA expression of interleukin-6 and interleukin-1 beta was quantified by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction. Colon length and weight were recorded, and histological sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin and scored in a blinded manner using a standardized scoring system that evaluated inflammation severity, extent, crypt damage, and percentage of involvement.

For the interleukin-10 knockout model, female mice on a C57BL/6J background were maintained on standard chow and received 300 microliters of a 50% slurry of B2 beads or control beads every other day starting at 12 weeks of age, when spontaneous colitis typically develops. Body weight was recorded every other day for six weeks, and at the end of the treatment period, mice were euthanized, and serum, feces, and colon tissues were collected for analysis. Histological scoring, cytokine mRNA expression, and high mobility group box 1 protein quantification were performed as described above. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software. Data are presented as means ± standard error of the mean. Differences between groups were assessed using Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons or one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results were reproducible across multiple independent trials.
