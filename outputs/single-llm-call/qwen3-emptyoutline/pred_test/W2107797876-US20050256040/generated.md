# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a novel synthetic peptide-based therapeutic agent designed for the targeted disruption of cell membranes in tumor tissues, and more specifically to a de novo engineered polypeptide capable of inducing rapid and potent cytotoxic effects in malignant cells upon direct intratumoral administration, while remaining non-toxic to surrounding healthy tissues due to the protective barrier provided by extracellular matrix components. The invention further encompasses methods of using this peptide for the treatment of solid tumors, including but not limited to carcinomas, sarcomas, and melanomas, in both human and veterinary clinical settings. The peptide exhibits a unique mechanism of action that is distinct from conventional chemotherapeutic agents, antibiotics, or pore-forming toxins, as it physically destabilizes lipid bilayers through a structure-dependent, non-enzymatic interaction that is selectively inhibited by components of the tumor microenvironment such as matrigel and fibronectin polymers. This selectivity enables the peptide to achieve potent anti-tumor efficacy in vivo without systemic toxicity, making it a groundbreaking therapeutic modality for localized tumor eradication. The invention also includes variants of the peptide with modified amino acid sequences that retain or enhance membrane-disrupting activity, as well as pharmaceutical formulations suitable for direct injection into tumor masses. The field of application spans oncology, particularly in the treatment of unresectable, metastatic, or anatomically inaccessible tumors where conventional therapies demonstrate limited efficacy or unacceptable side effects.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The development of effective anticancer therapies has long been hindered by the fundamental challenge of achieving tumor-specific cytotoxicity without damaging healthy tissues. Conventional chemotherapeutic agents, radiation, and even many targeted biologics often cause systemic toxicity due to their lack of selectivity, leading to dose-limiting side effects that compromise patient quality of life and therapeutic outcomes. While significant advances have been made in immunotherapy and molecularly targeted drugs, a substantial proportion of solid tumors remain refractory to these modalities, particularly those with dense stromal components, low mutational burden, or poor vascularization. Alternative approaches involving naturally occurring antimicrobial peptides have been explored over the past several decades, with agents such as magainin, cecropin, granulysin, and NK-lysin demonstrating cytotoxic potential against tumor cells in vitro. However, these peptides have consistently failed to translate into effective in vivo therapies due to rapid degradation, poor bioavailability, off-target toxicity, or inability to penetrate the complex extracellular matrix that surrounds and protects solid tumors. Similarly, bacterial pore-forming toxins such as verotoxin and diphtheria toxin have been engineered for tumor targeting, but their immunogenicity, systemic toxicity, and reliance on specific cell surface receptors have severely limited their clinical utility. Moreover, many of these agents require internalization or receptor-mediated endocytosis to exert their effects, rendering them ineffective against cells with downregulated receptors or those residing in hypoxic, poorly vascularized regions of the tumor. The need for a novel class of anticancer agents that can directly and rapidly disrupt the integrity of malignant cell membranes without dependence on cellular uptake mechanisms, while remaining inert in the presence of normal tissue architecture, has remained unmet. Existing membrane-disrupting agents such as detergents or surfactants are too non-specific and cause widespread tissue damage, precluding their therapeutic use. Furthermore, no prior art describes a synthetic, de novo designed protein that achieves potent and selective tumor cell lysis in vivo through a mechanism that is physically inhibited by extracellular matrix components, thereby conferring a built-in spatial restriction of activity. The discovery that a synthetic peptide, when injected directly into tumor masses, can induce complete tumor regression without causing damage to adjacent normal tissues—despite its potent membrane-disrupting activity in cell culture—represents a paradigm shift in cancer therapeutics. This phenomenon, previously unexplained and unanticipated, forms the basis of the present invention, which leverages the differential physical environment between tumor tissue and healthy tissue to achieve unprecedented selectivity. The absence of skin or organ toxicity following direct injection of the peptide into normal dermal or subcutaneous tissue further underscores the uniqueness of this mechanism. No prior patent or publication discloses a synthetic polypeptide with the specific structural features, membrane-disrupting kinetics, and microenvironment-dependent activity profile described herein. The invention thus fills a critical gap in oncology by providing a therapeutic agent that exploits the physical properties of the tumor microenvironment rather than its biochemical markers, offering a broadly applicable, mechanism-based solution to the longstanding problem of tumor selectivity.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a synthetic polypeptide, designated herein as SGP, composed of a defined sequence of amino acids arranged to form a stable helical structure capable of physically disrupting lipid bilayer membranes upon direct contact, thereby inducing rapid and irreversible cell death in tumor cells. The polypeptide is not derived from any naturally occurring protein and is engineered de novo to possess a specific amphipathic conformation that enables insertion into and destabilization of plasma membranes without requiring receptor binding, endocytosis, or enzymatic activity. The polypeptide exhibits potent cytotoxic activity against a broad spectrum of human tumor cell lines in vitro, including carcinomas, sarcomas, and melanomas, with cell death occurring within seconds to minutes depending on concentration. Importantly, this cytotoxic activity is completely abrogated in the presence of extracellular matrix components such as matrigel or polymeric fibronectin, which act as physical barriers that prevent the polypeptide from accessing the cell membrane. This property, which is entirely unexpected and unprecedented in the field of anticancer therapeutics, enables the polypeptide to be administered directly into solid tumors in vivo, where it effectively eliminates tumor cells while sparing surrounding healthy tissues that are protected by their native extracellular matrix. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising the polypeptide in a sterile, isotonic, buffered solution suitable for intratumoral injection, as well as variants of the polypeptide with substitutions at specific residues that modulate its helical stability, hydrophobicity, or charge distribution, thereby fine-tuning its membrane-disrupting potency and selectivity. The polypeptide demonstrates no detectable toxicity when injected into normal skin, muscle, or subcutaneous tissue, and histological examination of major organs in treated animals reveals no pathological changes, confirming its localized action. In vivo studies using multiple human tumor xenograft models—including breast carcinoma, Kaposi’s sarcoma, prostate carcinoma, and lung carcinoma—demonstrate complete tumor regression following repeated intratumoral injections, with no recurrence observed for up to four months post-treatment. The mechanism of action is distinct from apoptosis, necrosis, or autophagy, as it involves direct physical disruption of the plasma membrane, resulting in immediate loss of membrane integrity, rapid ion flux, and osmotic lysis. The polypeptide’s activity is dependent on its three-dimensional helical conformation, as structural analogs with altered amino acid sequences that disrupt helical continuity or reduce amphipathicity exhibit dramatically reduced or absent cytotoxic activity. The invention thus provides a novel therapeutic modality that exploits the physical architecture of the tumor microenvironment to achieve spatial selectivity, enabling the use of a potent membrane-disrupting agent in a clinical setting without systemic toxicity. This represents a fundamental departure from conventional strategies that rely on biochemical targeting, and opens new avenues for the treatment of solid tumors that are resistant to existing therapies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to a synthetic polypeptide, designated SGP, that possesses a unique combination of structural and functional properties enabling it to act as a potent and selective tumor cell-lysing agent. The polypeptide is not found in nature and has been designed de novo through rational protein engineering to adopt a stable amphipathic helical conformation that facilitates direct interaction with lipid bilayers. The sequence of the polypeptide is composed of a specific arrangement of hydrophobic and cationic amino acid residues that, when folded into a helical structure, expose a continuous hydrophobic face capable of inserting into the lipid core of cell membranes, while the hydrophilic face interacts with the aqueous environment and stabilizes the orientation of the helix. This structural arrangement is critical to its function, as modifications that disrupt the helical integrity or alter the balance of hydrophobic and charged residues completely abolish its membrane-disrupting activity. The polypeptide does not require any cellular receptor, transporter, or internalization mechanism to exert its cytotoxic effect; instead, it acts through a purely physical mechanism involving the insertion of its hydrophobic domain into the lipid bilayer, leading to transient pore formation, loss of membrane potential, uncontrolled ion flux, and ultimately osmotic lysis. This mechanism is distinct from the action of detergents, which solubilize membranes by micelle formation, or of pore-forming toxins, which oligomerize into defined channels. The polypeptide induces a more chaotic and irreversible disruption, resulting in immediate cell death without the characteristic morphological features of apoptosis or necrosis at high concentrations. At lower concentrations, however, the same polypeptide can induce delayed cell death through apoptotic pathways, suggesting that the mode of action is concentration-dependent, with membrane disruption being the primary and most rapid mechanism. The polypeptide is effective against a wide range of human tumor cell lines, including those derived from breast carcinoma, Kaposi’s sarcoma, prostate carcinoma, lung carcinoma, melanoma, and leukemia, indicating that its mechanism of action is not dependent on specific tumor markers or genetic mutations. Importantly, the polypeptide exhibits no preferential toxicity toward malignant versus non-malignant cells in vitro, as it induces similar rates of lysis in normal fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and glial cells. This lack of inherent selectivity would normally preclude its therapeutic use, as systemic administration would result in catastrophic tissue damage. However, the invention reveals a previously unknown and entirely unexpected phenomenon: when administered directly into solid tumors in vivo, the polypeptide causes complete tumor regression without damaging surrounding healthy tissues. This remarkable selectivity is not due to differential expression of membrane components, receptor binding, or immune-mediated clearance, but rather to the physical barrier presented by the extracellular matrix. In normal tissues, the extracellular matrix is dense, highly organized, and rich in structural proteins such as collagen, fibronectin, and laminin, forming a tightly packed network that physically impedes the diffusion and access of the polypeptide to cell membranes. In contrast, the extracellular matrix within tumors is often disorganized, fragmented, and less dense, allowing the polypeptide to penetrate and reach tumor cells. This differential accessibility creates a spatially restricted therapeutic window, wherein the polypeptide is active only within the tumor mass and inert in healthy tissue. This mechanism is corroborated by in vitro experiments demonstrating that pre-coating tumor cells with matrigel or polymeric fibronectin completely protects them from polypeptide-induced lysis, whereas the same matrix does not protect cells from ethanol or doxorubicin, which readily diffuse through the matrix. This distinction confirms that the polypeptide’s activity is not merely a function of concentration or diffusion rate, but is fundamentally dependent on direct physical contact with the plasma membrane, which is prevented by the matrix. The polypeptide’s stability, solubility, and resistance to proteolytic degradation in physiological conditions further enhance its suitability for clinical use. It remains stable in buffered saline at 37°C for extended periods, retains its helical structure under physiological pH and ionic strength, and is not significantly degraded by serum proteases, allowing it to maintain its activity during intratumoral delivery. The polypeptide can be synthesized using standard solid-phase peptide synthesis techniques, purified by high-performance liquid chromatography, and formulated into sterile, pyrogen-free solutions suitable for injection. Its molecular weight, determined by fast atom bombardment mass spectrometry, is approximately 7555 Da, consistent with its calculated sequence. The polypeptide is administered directly into the center of the tumor mass via fine-gauge needle, using small volume increments to ensure even distribution throughout the tumor. A single injection of 40 microliters of a 100 μM or 1 mM solution is sufficient to induce complete tumor regression in multiple xenograft models, with no recurrence observed over extended observation periods. Histological analysis of treated tumors reveals widespread loss of cellular architecture, condensed nuclei, and absence of viable tumor cells, while the extracellular matrix remains intact, indicating that the polypeptide selectively targets cells without degrading the surrounding stromal framework. This is a critical advantage over conventional therapies that often destroy both tumor and stromal components, leading to fibrosis and impaired tissue function. Importantly, no signs of inflammation, necrosis, or immune cell infiltration are observed in the surrounding normal tissue, confirming the absence of off-target effects. Systemic administration of the polypeptide has not been evaluated, as its mechanism of action is designed for localized delivery. However, the lack of toxicity following intradermal or subcutaneous injection into normal skin further supports the safety profile of localized administration. The polypeptide’s activity is exquisitely sensitive to its three-dimensional structure, as demonstrated by the complete loss of cytotoxicity in two engineered analogs: SGP-L, in which the central helical domain composed of alanine residues is replaced with leucine, and SGP-E, in which positively charged lysine residues are substituted with negatively charged glutamic acids. Both analogs retain the same molecular weight and amino acid composition as the original polypeptide but fail to adopt the necessary amphipathic conformation, resulting in a loss of membrane-inserting capability. This confirms that the specific sequence and resulting helical structure are essential to the function of the invention. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising the polypeptide in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, such as phosphate-buffered saline, HEPES-buffered saline, or isotonic glucose solution, optionally containing stabilizing agents such as albumin or polysorbate 80 to prevent aggregation. The polypeptide may be lyophilized for long-term storage and reconstituted immediately prior to administration. The invention also includes methods of manufacturing the polypeptide using automated peptide synthesizers, purification by reverse-phase HPLC, and characterization by amino acid analysis, mass spectrometry, and circular dichroism spectroscopy to confirm helical content. The invention further encompasses the use of the polypeptide in combination with other therapeutic modalities, such as radiation therapy, chemotherapy, or immunotherapy, to enhance overall tumor control, particularly in cases where complete eradication is not achieved with monotherapy. The polypeptide may also be delivered via catheter-based systems for tumors located in anatomically complex regions such as the brain, pancreas, or liver, where direct injection is technically feasible. The invention represents a paradigm shift in cancer therapy by introducing a mechanism of action that is not based on biochemical targeting but on physical interaction with the tumor microenvironment. It provides a solution to the longstanding problem of tumor selectivity by exploiting the structural differences between normal and malignant tissues, rather than their molecular signatures. This approach is broadly applicable across tumor types and is not limited by genetic heterogeneity, drug resistance, or immune evasion mechanisms. The polypeptide’s ability to induce complete and durable tumor regression without systemic toxicity makes it a transformative therapeutic agent with significant potential for clinical translation.

### EXAMPLE 1

The synthetic polypeptide designated SGP was produced using standard solid-phase peptide synthesis techniques employing the Fmoc (9-fluorenylmethyloxycarbonyl) chemistry protocol on a Miligen automatic peptide synthesizer (Model 9050). The synthesis began with Fmoc-Leu-PEG resin, which served as the solid support and provided a polyethylene glycol linker to facilitate cleavage and solubility. The amino acid sequence of the polypeptide was assembled stepwise, with each residue added in the C-to-N direction. After coupling of each amino acid, the Fmoc protecting group was removed using a 20% piperidine solution in dimethylformamide, and the deprotection was monitored in real time by measuring the absorbance of the released Fmoc group at 210 nm, ensuring complete removal before proceeding to the next coupling step. Coupling efficiency was verified by the Kaiser test, and any incomplete couplings were addressed by double coupling with additional amino acid and activator. Once the full-length sequence was assembled, the polypeptide was cleaved from the resin using a mixture of trifluoroacetic acid, water, and triisopropylsilane in a ratio of 95:2.5:2.5, incubated for three hours at room temperature to ensure complete deprotection and cleavage. The crude peptide solution was then precipitated in cold diethyl ether, centrifuged, and the pellet washed multiple times to remove residual acid and organic solvents. The resulting crude peptide was dissolved in a minimal volume of water and subjected to preparative reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using an ODS column (20 × 250 mm) with a gradient elution system consisting of water and acetonitrile, both containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid as an ion-pairing agent. The gradient was programmed to increase acetonitrile concentration from 10% to 60% over 60 minutes at a flow rate of 5 mL/min, and fractions were collected every minute. The purity and identity of the target fraction were confirmed by analytical HPLC and mass spectrometry. The collected peak corresponding to the desired molecular weight was pooled, lyophilized, and stored at −80°C until use. The final product was analyzed for amino acid composition following acid hydrolysis in 5.7 M hydrochloric acid at 110°C for 24 hours under sealed conditions, yielding the following composition: glycine, 6.2 mol%; alanine, 9.5 mol%; leucine, 26.5 mol%; aspartic acid, 3.0 mol%; proline, 2.9 mol%; tyrosine, 3.1 mol%; and lysine, 18.9 mol%. These values closely matched the theoretical composition based on the designed sequence, confirming the fidelity of the synthesis. The molecular weight of the polypeptide was determined by fast atom bombardment mass spectrometry using a JEOL JMX-HX100 instrument, which yielded a base peak at 7555.1 Da, in excellent agreement with the calculated mass of C367H639O77N91·H⁺ (7554.8 Da). The polypeptide was quantified spectrophotometrically using its absorbance at 280 nm, with an extinction coefficient of 8000 M⁻¹cm⁻¹, derived from the presence of three tyrosine residues and one tryptophan residue in the sequence. The polypeptide was dissolved in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (10 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) to prepare stock solutions of 10 mM, which were then serially diluted to obtain working concentrations for biological assays. The solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to ensure sterility and stored at 4°C for short-term use or aliquoted and frozen at −80°C for long-term storage. Circular dichroism spectroscopy confirmed that the polypeptide adopted a predominantly α-helical conformation in aqueous buffer, with a helical content of approximately 75% as determined by the mean residue ellipticity at 222 nm. The polypeptide exhibited no aggregation or precipitation under physiological conditions and remained stable for at least six months when stored frozen. These results demonstrate that the polypeptide can be reliably synthesized in high purity, with structural and physicochemical properties consistent with its designed function.

### EXAMPLE 2

To evaluate the cytotoxic activity of the polypeptide against human tumor cell lines, a series of in vitro cell viability assays were conducted using multiple established cell lines derived from different tumor types. The Kaposi’s sarcoma-derived cell line KS1767, the breast carcinoma-derived cell line MDA-MB-435, the prostate carcinoma-derived cell line PC-3, the lung carcinoma-derived cell line H358, and the melanoma-derived cell line A375 were obtained from commercial sources and maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and antibiotics. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. The polypeptide was added at concentrations ranging from 0.1 μM to 1 mM, and cells were incubated for varying durations from 1 minute to 24 hours. Cell viability was assessed using a dual fluorescent staining method employing acridine orange and ethidium bromide. After incubation, the culture medium was aspirated, and cells were gently washed once with phosphate-buffered saline at 37°C to remove non-adherent cells and residual polypeptide. A 20-fold dilution of a staining solution containing 100 μg/mL acridine orange and 100 μg/mL ethidium bromide in phosphate-buffered saline was then added to each well, and cells were immediately visualized under an inverted fluorescence microscope equipped with appropriate filters. Viable cells exhibited uniform green nuclear fluorescence indicative of intact membranes and normal chromatin structure. Cells undergoing early or mid-stage apoptosis displayed condensed and fragmented nuclei with bright green fluorescence, while cells with compromised plasma membranes exhibited red nuclear fluorescence due to ethidium bromide intercalation into DNA. A minimum of 500 cells per condition were scored manually by two independent observers blinded to the treatment groups. At concentrations above 10 μM, the polypeptide induced complete loss of viability within 60 seconds in KS1767 cells, characterized by immediate loss of membrane integrity and diffuse red staining, consistent with rapid osmotic lysis. At concentrations between 5 and 10 μM, cell death occurred over a 60-minute period, with cells displaying swelling, membrane blebbing, and eventual rupture, consistent with necrotic morphology. At concentrations below 5 μM, cell death was delayed and occurred over 24 hours, with cells exhibiting nuclear condensation, chromatin margination, and formation of apoptotic bodies, which were confirmed by caspase-3 activation assays. Similar kinetics and morphological patterns were observed across all tumor cell lines tested, with no significant variation in sensitivity between different tumor types. Control peptides, including scrambled sequences and unrelated synthetic peptides of similar length and charge, showed no cytotoxic activity at concentrations up to 100 μM. The polypeptide also induced rapid lysis in non-tumor cell lines, including dermal microvessel endothelial cells, fibroblasts isolated from human skin and lung, and primary glial cells, with identical kinetics and morphology, indicating that its activity is not selective for malignant cells at the molecular level. These results demonstrate that the polypeptide is a potent, non-specific membrane-disrupting agent whose cytotoxic effect is concentration-dependent and independent of cell origin or phenotype.

### EXAMPLE 3

To investigate the mechanism underlying the differential activity of the polypeptide in vitro versus in vivo, a series of experiments were conducted to determine whether extracellular matrix components could modulate its access to cell membranes. Human KS1767 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. The wells were then coated with either matrigel or polymeric fibronectin (sFN) to mimic the extracellular environment. For matrigel coating, 100 μL of a 1:10 dilution of matrigel in serum-free medium was gently pipetted onto each well and incubated at 37°C for 10 minutes to allow polymerization. For fibronectin coating, a solution of purified human fibronectin was applied at a concentration of 10 μg/mL and incubated overnight at 4°C, followed by washing with phosphate-buffered saline to remove unbound protein. Control wells received no coating. After matrix coating, cells were treated with 1 mM polypeptide for 10 minutes, and viability was assessed using the acridine orange/ethidium bromide staining method. In the absence of matrix, 1 mM polypeptide induced complete cell lysis within 10 minutes, as evidenced by near-total red fluorescence and loss of cellular structure. In contrast, cells pre-coated with matrigel exhibited no significant loss of viability, maintaining green nuclear fluorescence and intact morphology, indistinguishable from untreated controls. Similar protection was observed in cells coated with polymeric fibronectin, confirming that the protective effect is not unique to matrigel but is a general property of extracellular matrix polymers. To determine whether this protection was due to physical barrier formation rather than biochemical signaling, parallel experiments were conducted using ethanol and doxorubicin as control cytotoxic agents. Ethanol at 1% concentration and doxorubicin at 20 μg/mL were applied to cells in the presence and absence of matrigel. Both agents induced complete cell death regardless of matrix presence, demonstrating that the matrix does not impede diffusion of small molecules or membrane-permeant agents. This contrast confirms that the polypeptide’s activity is not merely hindered by reduced diffusion but is physically blocked from reaching the plasma membrane by the matrix structure. Additional experiments using fluorescently labeled polypeptide showed that the labeled peptide accumulated on the surface of the matrix but failed to penetrate to the cell layer, whereas fluorescently labeled doxorubicin diffused freely through the matrix and reached the cells. These findings establish that the polypeptide’s mechanism of action requires direct, unimpeded contact with the plasma membrane and that the extracellular matrix acts as a physical filter that selectively prevents this interaction in normal tissues but permits it in tumors, where the matrix is disorganized and porous. This provides a mechanistic explanation for the observed in vivo selectivity and forms the foundation of the invention’s therapeutic strategy.

### EXAMPLE 4

To evaluate the in vivo antitumor efficacy of the polypeptide, human tumor xenograft models were established in immunodeficient nude mice. Female or male Balb/c nude mice, aged two months, were anesthetized with Avertin and injected subcutaneously with 1 × 10⁶ tumor cells suspended in 200 μL of serum-free Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium. Tumor cells included MDA-MB-435 (breast carcinoma), KS1767 (Kaposi’s sarcoma), PC-3 (prostate carcinoma), and H358 (lung carcinoma). Tumors were allowed to grow until they reached a volume of approximately 100 to 600 mm³, at which point treatment was initiated. Mice were divided into three groups: control group receiving intratumoral injections of phosphate-buffered saline, low-dose group receiving 100 μM polypeptide, and high-dose group receiving 1 mM polypeptide. Each injection consisted of 40 μL delivered in five 5-μL increments directly into the center of the tumor mass to ensure even distribution. Injections were administered once weekly for four consecutive weeks. Tumor volume was measured twice weekly using digital calipers, and volume was calculated using the formula V = (L × W²)/2, where L is the longest diameter and W is the perpendicular diameter. After four weeks of treatment, mice were monitored for an additional four weeks without further intervention. At the end of the observation period, surviving mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised for histological analysis. In all tumor models, mice treated with either concentration of polypeptide exhibited dramatic tumor regression compared to controls. The average tumor volume in the polypeptide-treated groups was reduced by more than fivefold compared to the PBS-treated group. In many cases, tumors were completely eradicated, with no palpable mass remaining. Mice treated with polypeptide remained tumor-free for up to four months, whereas all control mice developed large, necrotic tumors requiring euthanasia by week 8. Histological examination of excised tumors revealed extensive cell death in polypeptide-treated samples, with widespread loss of cellular architecture, condensed and fragmented nuclei, and absence of viable tumor cells. The extracellular matrix remained intact, with no degradation of collagen or fibronectin fibers, indicating that the polypeptide selectively targets cells without damaging the stromal framework. No signs of inflammation, fibrosis, or immune cell infiltration were observed in the surrounding normal tissue. Importantly, mice treated with polypeptide showed no signs of systemic toxicity, including weight loss, lethargy, or organ dysfunction. These results demonstrate that the polypeptide is highly effective in eradicating established human tumor xenografts across multiple tumor types, with durable remission and no recurrence, and that its therapeutic effect is localized to the site of injection.

### EXAMPLE 5

To assess the safety profile of the polypeptide in normal tissues, intradermal and subcutaneous injection experiments were conducted in nude mice. Two-month-old female nude mice were anesthetized with Avertin, and 10 μL of either 100 μM polypeptide in phosphate-buffered saline or an equal volume of phosphate-buffered saline alone was injected into the dorsal skin. The injection sites were marked and monitored daily for two weeks for signs of erythema, edema, necrosis, ulceration, or inflammation. At the end of the observation period, the injected skin was excised, fixed in Bouin’s solution, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin for histological evaluation. Microscopic examination of the skin from polypeptide-injected mice revealed no pathological changes compared to saline-injected controls. The epidermis, dermis, and subcutaneous fat layers were intact, with no evidence of cell death, inflammatory infiltration, vascular damage, or fibrosis. The density and organization of collagen fibers, elastin networks, and hair follicles were indistinguishable from untreated skin. No signs of necrosis, blistering, or scarring were observed. In contrast, positive control injections of ethanol or sodium dodecyl sulfate into the same sites resulted in immediate tissue necrosis, ulceration, and inflammatory response within 24 hours. These findings confirm that the polypeptide, despite its potent membrane-disrupting activity in vitro, is completely non-toxic to normal skin and subcutaneous tissues in vivo. This safety profile is attributed to the protective barrier provided by the dense, organized extracellular matrix of normal skin, which prevents the polypeptide from reaching the plasma membranes of dermal and adipose cells. The absence of toxicity in normal tissues, combined with its potent antitumor efficacy, establishes the polypeptide as a uniquely safe and selective therapeutic agent for localized tumor treatment.

### EXAMPLE 6

To determine the structural specificity of the polypeptide’s activity, two analogs were synthesized and tested for cytotoxic function. The first analog, designated SGP-L, was created by replacing the central helical domain composed of alanine residues with an all-leucine sequence, while maintaining the overall length and charge distribution of the original polypeptide. The second analog, SGP-E, was generated by substituting all lysine residues with glutamic acid residues, thereby reversing the charge profile from cationic to anionic while preserving the hydrophobic content. Both analogs were synthesized using identical Fmoc-based solid-phase methods and purified to homogeneity by HPLC. Their molecular weights were confirmed by mass spectrometry to be identical to the original polypeptide. Circular dichroism spectroscopy revealed that SGP-L retained a helical conformation but with altered hydrophobic packing, while SGP-E exhibited a significant reduction in helical content and adopted a more disordered structure. In vitro cytotoxicity assays using KS1767 cells showed that SGP-L and SGP-E had no detectable cytotoxic activity at concentrations up to 1 mM, whereas the original polypeptide induced complete lysis at 10 μM. The LC₅₀ for SGP-L and SGP-E was increased by more than 10-fold compared to the original polypeptide, confirming that the specific arrangement of hydrophobic and charged residues is essential for membrane disruption. Furthermore, when tested in vivo using the MDA-MB-435 xenograft model, neither analog induced any tumor regression, even after repeated high-dose injections. Histological analysis of tumors from mice treated with SGP-L or SGP-E showed no evidence of cell death, with tumor architecture remaining intact and indistinguishable from PBS-treated controls. These results demonstrate that the polypeptide’s activity is not merely a function of its amino acid composition or molecular weight, but is critically dependent on its precise three-dimensional structure. The invention thus encompasses not only the original sequence but also any variant that maintains the amphipathic helical conformation and the specific spatial arrangement of hydrophobic and cationic residues required for membrane insertion. Any substitution that disrupts this conformation, whether by altering hydrophobicity, charge, or helical stability, abolishes activity, underscoring the structural precision required for therapeutic function.

### EXAMPLE 7

To further characterize the mechanism of membrane disruption, biophysical assays were performed using synthetic lipid vesicles and planar lipid bilayers. Large unilamellar vesicles composed of phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylethanolamine, and cholesterol in a ratio mimicking the composition of mammalian plasma membranes were prepared by extrusion through polycarbonate membranes. The polypeptide was added to these vesicles at concentrations ranging from 1 to 100 μM, and membrane integrity was monitored by the release of encapsulated carboxyfluorescein, a fluorescent dye that is quenched at high concentrations and becomes fluorescent upon dilution. Upon addition of the polypeptide, a rapid and concentration-dependent increase in fluorescence was observed, indicating immediate and complete leakage of vesicle contents within seconds. This effect was not observed with the inactive analogs SGP-L and SGP-E. In parallel experiments using planar lipid bilayers formed across a small aperture in a Teflon partition, the polypeptide induced transient, non-selective ion conductance events when applied to the cis chamber, as measured by patch-clamp electrophysiology. These events were characterized by irregular, non-repeating current spikes of variable amplitude, consistent with the formation of transient, non-pore-like disruptions rather than stable ion channels. The frequency and amplitude of these events increased with polypeptide concentration, and no discrete conductance states were observed, indicating that the polypeptide does not assemble into defined oligomeric pores. Instead, it induces a chaotic disruption of the lipid bilayer, leading to uncontrolled ion flux and osmotic imbalance. These biophysical findings confirm that the polypeptide acts through a non-pore-forming, physical mechanism of membrane destabilization, distinct from the action of classical pore-forming toxins such as melittin or α-hemolysin. The absence of reproducible conductance states further supports the conclusion that the polypeptide does not form stable transmembrane structures but instead causes transient, localized defects in membrane integrity. This mechanism explains the rapidity of cell death observed in vitro and the lack of resistance development, as the target is the lipid bilayer itself, which cannot be mutated or downregulated by the cell. The findings also explain why the polypeptide is ineffective against cells protected by extracellular matrix: the matrix physically prevents the polypeptide from reaching the membrane, thereby blocking the initiation of this physical disruption process.

### EXAMPLE 8

To explore the potential for clinical translation, pharmaceutical formulations of the polypeptide were developed and tested for stability, solubility, and compatibility with delivery systems. The polypeptide was formulated in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (10 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4), with or without the addition of 0.1% human serum albumin to prevent surface adsorption and aggregation. The solution was filtered through 0.22 μm membranes, sterilized by autoclaving, and stored at 4°C or −80°C. Stability studies over six months showed no degradation, aggregation, or loss of helical content as assessed by circular dichroism and HPLC. The polypeptide remained soluble at concentrations up to 50 mg/mL, allowing for high-dose delivery in small volumes. To evaluate compatibility with clinical delivery devices, the polypeptide was loaded into 1 mL syringes fitted with 27-gauge needles and injected into agarose gel phantoms simulating tumor tissue. The solution flowed smoothly without clogging, and distribution was uniform upon slow injection. The polypeptide was also tested for compatibility with cryopreservation and lyophilization. Lyophilized powder was reconstituted in sterile water, and no loss of activity was observed after five freeze-thaw cycles. The polypeptide was found to be non-pyrogenic, non-hemolytic, and non-irritating in rabbit skin and eye irritation tests. These results demonstrate that the polypeptide can be manufactured, formulated, and delivered using standard pharmaceutical practices, making it suitable for clinical development. The invention thus encompasses not only the polypeptide itself but also its use in pharmaceutical compositions for intratumoral administration, including lyophilized formulations, pre-filled syringes, and controlled-release depots for sustained delivery. The ability to deliver the polypeptide in small volumes with high local concentration, combined with its safety profile and potent efficacy, positions it as a viable therapeutic candidate for the treatment of solid tumors in clinical oncology.
