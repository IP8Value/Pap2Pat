## DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention disclosed herein relates to a novel and industrially scalable method for the fractionation of lignocellulosic biomass using low-cost protic ionic liquids, specifically triethylammonium hydrogen sulfate, to simultaneously isolate high-purity cellulose-rich pulp, recoverable lignin, and a recyclable ionic liquid solvent with minimal degradation of process components. The method operates under mild thermal conditions, typically at temperatures between 100 °C and 130 °C, with biomass loadings ranging from 5% to 20% by weight relative to the ionic liquid solvent, and in the presence of water concentrations between 15% and 30% by weight of the total solvent mixture. The process achieves efficient deconstruction of the lignocellulosic matrix through selective dissolution of hemicellulose and lignin while preserving the structural integrity of cellulose in a solid, filterable residue. This selective fractionation enables the production of cellulose pulp with glucan recovery exceeding 85% of the original biomass content, with enzymatic saccharification yields reaching up to 77% of theoretical glucose potential, without requiring high-temperature, high-pressure, or corrosive chemical environments typically associated with conventional pretreatment technologies. The ionic liquid solvent, triethylammonium hydrogen sulfate, is synthesized from inexpensive, commercially available precursors—triethylamine and sulfuric acid—resulting in a solvent with a bulk production cost below $1.24 per kilogram, which is comparable to conventional organic solvents such as acetone or toluene, and represents a reduction of more than an order of magnitude compared to traditional imidazolium-based ionic liquids. The method further enables the recovery and reuse of the ionic liquid solvent across multiple processing cycles with recovery rates exceeding 98%, and in multiple instances achieving 99% or higher, thereby minimizing solvent make-up requirements and substantially reducing the operating cost of the process. The recycled solvent maintains its fractionation efficacy over at least four consecutive cycles, with no measurable accumulation of degradation products, inorganic contaminants, or lignin-derived inhibitors that would impair cellulose saccharification or lignin recovery. The lignin fraction recovered from the process is precipitated by dilution with water, yielding a solid product with consistent chemical composition, molecular weight distribution, and functional group profile across multiple recycles, suitable for downstream valorization as a source of aromatic chemicals, carbon materials, or fuel. The hemicellulose-derived sugars are partially recovered as monomeric xylose and arabinose during the initial stages of pretreatment but are predominantly converted into furfural under acidic conditions, which remains dissolved in the ionic liquid phase and can be efficiently removed by distillation during solvent recovery, enabling its isolation as a high-value co-product. The process is distinguished by its ability to achieve high mass balance closure for all major biomass components—cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin—with minimal unaccounted material, and by the absence of significant formation of pseudo-lignin or insoluble humins under optimized conditions. The method further exhibits a self-cleaning property in the ionic liquid phase, wherein lignin fragments and carbohydrate degradation products that accumulate during successive cycles undergo controlled condensation reactions that promote their precipitation as part of the lignin fraction, thereby preventing their accumulation in solution and maintaining the purity and reactivity of the solvent for reuse. The entire process operates at atmospheric pressure, eliminating the need for high-pressure reactors, reducing capital expenditure, and enabling continuous or semi-continuous operation with minimal mechanical wear on equipment. The combination of low solvent cost, high solvent recyclability, mild operating conditions, and high product purity renders this method uniquely suited for industrial-scale biorefining applications, offering a technically and economically superior alternative to dilute acid, steam explosion, organosolv, and other conventional pretreatment technologies.

## EXAMPLE 1. DECONSTRUCTION OF BIOMASS AND EXTRACTION OF METALS IN VARIOUS IONIC LIQUIDS

The invention provides a method for the deconstruction of lignocellulosic biomass using triethylammonium hydrogen sulfate as the primary solvent, wherein the biomass is contacted with a solution of the ionic liquid containing between 15% and 30% water by weight at a temperature between 100 °C and 130 °C for a duration sufficient to achieve selective dissolution of hemicellulose and lignin while retaining cellulose as a solid residue. The biomass feedstock may include any non-food lignocellulosic material, including but not limited to Miscanthus x giganteus, wheat straw, corn stover, sugarcane bagasse, switchgrass, poplar, eucalyptus, or other herbaceous or woody energy crops. The biomass is typically dried to a moisture content of less than 15% prior to processing to minimize dilution of the ionic liquid solvent, although the process is tolerant of moderate moisture levels due to the hygroscopic nature of the hydrogen sulfate anion. The biomass is mixed with the ionic liquid solution at a solids loading of between 5% and 20% by weight, with a preferred range of 8% to 12%, to ensure efficient heat and mass transfer while maintaining manageable viscosity and pumpability. The reaction is conducted in a sealed vessel, such as a pressure-resistant autoclave or round-bottom flask equipped with a reflux condenser, under atmospheric pressure or slightly elevated pressure to prevent solvent evaporation, with agitation provided by magnetic stirring or mechanical impellers to ensure homogeneity. The reaction duration is optimized based on the desired balance between hemicellulose removal, lignin extraction, and cellulose preservation, with a preferred time range of 4 to 12 hours, and a most optimal duration of approximately 8 hours when operating at 120 °C with a 1:1 acid-to-base ratio in the ionic liquid. During this time, the hydrogen sulfate anion catalyzes the hydrolysis of glycosidic bonds in hemicellulose and ether linkages in lignin, leading to depolymerization and solubilization of these components into the liquid phase. The cellulose, due to its highly ordered crystalline structure and limited solubility in acidic protic ionic liquids under these conditions, remains largely intact and is recovered as a fibrous, filterable solid. The solid residue is separated from the liquid phase by filtration or centrifugation, washed with water or a water-ethanol mixture to remove residual ionic liquid and soluble sugars, and then dried under vacuum at temperatures below 60 °C to preserve its structural integrity. The washed pulp exhibits a glucan content of between 85% and 96% of the original biomass glucan content, with hemicellulose content reduced to less than 5% and lignin content reduced to between 10% and 20%, depending on pretreatment time. The enzymatic saccharification of this pulp, conducted using commercial cellulase and β-glucosidase enzyme cocktails under standard NREL protocols, yields glucose concentrations corresponding to 70% to 77% of the theoretical maximum based on glucan content, which is comparable to or exceeds the yields achieved by other pretreatment methods such as dilute acid or organosolv processes. The liquid phase, containing dissolved hemicellulose sugars, lignin fragments, acetic acid, furfural, and residual ionic liquid, is subjected to lignin precipitation by the addition of water in a volume ratio of 1:2 to 1:5 relative to the volume of the ionic liquid solution. The precipitated lignin is collected by filtration or centrifugation, washed with water and ethanol to remove residual ionic liquid and soluble sugars, and dried under vacuum. The lignin yield, expressed as a percentage of the original lignin content in the biomass, reaches up to 82% under optimal conditions, with a number-average molecular weight between 1000 and 1500 Da and a weight-average molecular weight between 4000 and 9000 Da, as determined by gel permeation chromatography. The lignin structure, as characterized by 1H-13C HSQC NMR spectroscopy, shows extensive cleavage of β-O-4 ether linkages, reduction in p-coumarate ester content, and an increase in condensed guaiacyl and syringyl units, consistent with acid-catalyzed depolymerization followed by recondensation. The ionic liquid solution, after removal of the precipitated lignin, is concentrated by evaporation under reduced pressure at temperatures below 70 °C to remove water and volatile organic compounds such as acetic acid and furfural. The concentrated solution is then reconstituted with distilled water to restore the original water content of 20% by weight, and optionally adjusted for acidity by the addition of small quantities of sulfuric acid to maintain catalytic activity across multiple cycles. The recovered ionic liquid is then reused in subsequent pretreatment cycles without any significant loss in fractionation efficiency. The process demonstrates a remarkable ability to maintain solvent performance over multiple recycles, with no detectable accumulation of ionic liquid degradation products such as diethylammonium or monoethylammonium ions, as confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy. The inorganic ash content of the recovered pulp remains low, with elemental analysis indicating minimal incorporation of alkali or alkaline earth metals from the biomass, and any trace inorganic residues are predominantly in the form of silica, which does not interfere with downstream enzymatic hydrolysis. The method further enables the recovery of metals such as calcium, potassium, and sodium that may be present in the biomass, as these are largely retained in the aqueous wash streams and do not accumulate in the ionic liquid phase, thereby preventing catalyst poisoning or equipment fouling. The overall mass balance for the process, accounting for glucan, hemicellulose, and lignin fractions, achieves closure of 85% to 95% across multiple cycles, with the remaining unaccounted material attributed to the formation of soluble oligomers, furanic compounds, or low-molecular-weight degradation products that are not quantified by standard analytical methods. The process is scalable to industrial throughput, with the ability to process 10 kilograms of biomass per kilogram of ionic liquid solvent, and the solvent cost per kilogram of biomass processed is estimated at less than $0.10, which is significantly lower than the cost of conventional pretreatment solvents. The method eliminates the need for expensive, moisture-sensitive, and thermally unstable ionic liquids such as 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate, and instead utilizes a solvent that is synthesized from commodity chemicals at low cost, with minimal purification requirements, and with high thermal and chemical stability under process conditions. The resulting cellulose pulp is suitable for direct use in enzymatic saccharification, microbial fermentation, or chemical conversion to glucose, ethanol, or other platform chemicals, while the recovered lignin is amenable to depolymerization, functionalization, or pyrolysis for the production of aromatic monomers, carbon fibers, or bio-oils. The method thus provides a complete, integrated, and economically viable pathway for the valorization of lignocellulosic biomass into multiple high-value streams, with minimal waste generation and maximum resource efficiency.

## EXAMPLE 2 COPPER SOLUBILITY AND ELECTRODEPOSITION OF COPPER FROM VARIOUS IONIC LIQUIDS COPPER SOLUBILITY

The invention further encompasses the use of triethylammonium hydrogen sulfate as a solvent medium for the dissolution and electrochemical recovery of copper and other transition metals from biomass-derived ash or metal-contaminated lignocellulosic residues, thereby enabling the simultaneous extraction of valuable metals and the purification of biomass for downstream biorefining applications. In this embodiment, the ionic liquid solvent is employed not only for the fractionation of lignocellulose but also as a medium for leaching and recovering metals such as copper, zinc, manganese, iron, and aluminum that may be naturally present in the biomass feedstock or introduced during agricultural processing. The method involves the pretreatment of biomass with triethylammonium hydrogen sulfate under the conditions described in Example 1, followed by the separation of the solid pulp and lignin fractions. The residual ash content of the cellulose-rich pulp, which may contain trace amounts of metal oxides, silicates, or sulfates, is then subjected to leaching in a fresh or recycled batch of triethylammonium hydrogen sulfate at elevated temperatures between 80 °C and 120 °C for a duration of 1 to 6 hours. The acidic nature of the hydrogen sulfate anion facilitates the dissolution of metal species through protonation of metal oxides and the formation of soluble metal hydrogen sulfate complexes. Copper, in particular, exhibits high solubility in this medium, with concentrations reaching up to 500 mg/L in solution after leaching, as determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy. The dissolved copper remains stable in the ionic liquid phase without precipitation or hydrolysis, even in the presence of residual lignin fragments or carbohydrate degradation products. The metal-laden ionic liquid solution is then separated from any remaining solid residues by filtration and subjected to electrochemical deposition using a conventional three-electrode cell configuration. A platinum or graphite cathode is immersed in the solution, and a constant current or controlled potential is applied between -0.8 V and -1.5 V versus a saturated calomel electrode, resulting in the quantitative reduction of copper(II) ions to metallic copper, which deposits as a dense, adherent layer on the cathode surface. The deposition efficiency exceeds 95%, with current efficiencies approaching 90%, and the deposited copper exhibits high purity, as confirmed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy and X-ray diffraction, with no detectable contamination from organic species or other metal ions. The electrolyte solution, now depleted of copper, can be recycled back into the biomass pretreatment process or regenerated by the addition of fresh sulfuric acid to restore acidity. The method is particularly advantageous in applications where biomass is grown on marginal or contaminated soils, as it enables the recovery of valuable metals such as copper from biomass ash without the need for additional chemical reagents, high-temperature smelting, or acid leaching with mineral acids such as sulfuric or hydrochloric acid. The ionic liquid medium provides a non-volatile, non-corrosive, and thermally stable alternative to aqueous acid leaching, eliminating the risk of acid mist formation, equipment corrosion, and wastewater generation. The process can be integrated into a biorefinery workflow such that the metal recovery step occurs either prior to or after lignocellulose fractionation, depending on the metal content and desired product stream. In cases where the biomass contains high concentrations of metals, such as in certain agricultural residues or energy crops grown in metal-rich soils, the ionic liquid can be used in a sequential extraction process, first for metal leaching, followed by lignocellulose fractionation, thereby maximizing resource recovery. The electrochemical deposition of copper from triethylammonium hydrogen sulfate is more efficient than from aqueous sulfate solutions due to the higher ionic conductivity and lower water activity of the ionic liquid, which suppresses hydrogen evolution and enhances metal deposition kinetics. The method further enables the recovery of other metals such as zinc and manganese, which exhibit moderate solubility in the ionic liquid and can be selectively deposited by adjusting the applied potential. The integration of metal recovery into the ionoSolv pretreatment process not only adds economic value through the recovery of critical metals but also enhances the environmental sustainability of the overall biorefinery by preventing the release of heavy metals into waste streams. The ionic liquid solvent, once depleted of metals, retains its full capacity for biomass fractionation, demonstrating that the presence of dissolved metal ions does not interfere with the hydrolysis of glycosidic or ether linkages in lignocellulose. This dual functionality—biomass deconstruction and metal extraction—represents a novel and industrially significant advancement in the field of biorefining, as it transforms a single solvent system into a multi-functional platform capable of recovering both organic and inorganic components of biomass with high efficiency and minimal environmental impact. The method is scalable to industrial throughput, with the potential for continuous metal leaching and electrodeposition in flow-through reactors, and the recovered copper can be directly used in electrical, electronic, or catalytic applications without further refining. The process eliminates the need for separate metal recovery units, reduces the overall footprint of the biorefinery, and provides a closed-loop system for the recovery of both carbon-based and mineral-based resources from lignocellulosic feedstocks.
