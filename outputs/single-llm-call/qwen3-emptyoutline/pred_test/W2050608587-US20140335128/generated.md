# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Malaria remains one of the most devastating infectious diseases affecting human populations, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions where access to healthcare is limited and transmission is perennial. The global burden of Plasmodium falciparum malaria, the most lethal species responsible for the majority of malaria-related deaths, continues to place immense strain on public health systems, economic development, and social stability in endemic countries. Despite decades of research and investment, the development of a highly effective, broadly protective, and durable vaccine against malaria has proven exceptionally challenging. The complex life cycle of the parasite, its ability to evade host immune responses through antigenic variation and immune suppression, and the lack of clear correlates of protection have all contributed to the difficulty in designing a successful vaccine. While significant progress has been made in reducing transmission through vector control, chemoprevention, and improved diagnostics, the absence of a vaccine capable of inducing sterile immunity—complete prevention of infection—leaves a critical gap in the global strategy to eradicate malaria.

Historically, efforts to develop a malaria vaccine have focused on targeting the extracellular stages of the parasite, particularly the sporozoite and blood-stage forms, with the goal of eliciting neutralizing antibodies. The most advanced candidate to date, RTS,S/AS01, is a recombinant protein vaccine that incorporates a portion of the circumsporozoite protein (CSP) fused to the hepatitis B surface antigen. This vaccine has demonstrated partial efficacy in clinical trials, reducing clinical malaria episodes by approximately 30–50% over a period of several years in young children. However, its protection is transient, wanes over time, and does not consistently prevent infection at the pre-erythrocytic stage. Importantly, RTS,S primarily induces humoral immunity, with minimal or absent activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes, which are essential for eliminating infected hepatocytes during the liver stage of infection. This limitation underscores a fundamental flaw in the antibody-centric paradigm: while antibodies may block sporozoite invasion of hepatocytes, they are ineffective once the parasite has entered the liver cell and begun intracellular replication. Consequently, vaccines that rely solely on antibody-mediated mechanisms are inherently constrained in their ability to achieve sterile immunity.

The biological plausibility of achieving sterile immunity through cell-mediated immunity has long been supported by observations in natural and experimental settings. Studies involving individuals exposed to bites of irradiated Plasmodium-infected mosquitoes have demonstrated that complete protection against subsequent challenge can be induced without the development of detectable antibody responses. This phenomenon, known as radiation-attenuated sporozoite (RAS) immunity, has been replicated in animal models and confirmed in human trials where volunteers immunized with cryopreserved, irradiated sporozoites via intravenous injection achieved sterile protection in up to 80% of cases. The mechanism underlying this protection is now understood to be dependent on the activation of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, which recognize parasite-derived peptides presented on major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) molecules on the surface of infected hepatocytes. Upon recognition, these T cells release cytotoxic granules containing perforin and granzymes, directly lysing the infected cells, or secrete interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), which triggers nitric oxide production within hepatocytes, creating a hostile intracellular environment that halts parasite development. This mode of action is fundamentally different from antibody-mediated neutralization; it does not require the parasite to be exposed extracellularly but instead targets the pathogen within its intracellular niche, where it is otherwise shielded from humoral immunity.

Despite the compelling evidence supporting the role of CD8+ T cells in sterile protection, translating this biological insight into a practical, scalable, and safe vaccine platform has remained elusive. Traditional vaccine platforms—such as inactivated whole organisms, recombinant proteins, or virus-like particles—are poorly suited for inducing robust CD8+ T cell responses because they deliver antigens extracellularly, leading to presentation via MHC class II and activation of CD4+ T helper cells rather than MHC class I and cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. To overcome this limitation, gene-based vaccine technologies have emerged as a promising alternative. These platforms utilize plasmid DNA or viral vectors to deliver parasite genes directly into host cells, enabling endogenous expression of the encoded antigens within the cytoplasm. This intracellular expression mimics natural viral infection, triggering the endogenous antigen processing pathway, which leads to proteasomal degradation of the antigen, transport of resulting peptides into the endoplasmic reticulum, loading onto MHC class I molecules, and presentation on the cell surface. This process is essential for the priming and expansion of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells, making gene-based vaccines uniquely capable of recapitulating the immunological mechanisms observed in RAS models.

Among gene-based platforms, DNA vaccines and recombinant adenoviral vectors have shown particular promise. DNA vaccines are stable, inexpensive to manufacture, and safe, with minimal risk of integration into the host genome or induction of inflammatory responses. However, their immunogenicity in humans has historically been suboptimal, often requiring multiple high-dose administrations and adjuvants to elicit measurable T cell responses. In contrast, replication-deficient adenoviral vectors, particularly those derived from human serotype 5 (Ad5), are highly efficient at transducing dendritic cells and other antigen-presenting cells, resulting in strong, rapid, and durable CD8+ T cell responses. Preclinical studies in mice and non-human primates have consistently demonstrated that heterologous prime-boost regimens—where DNA is used for priming and adenovirus for boosting—induce significantly higher levels of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells than either modality alone. This synergistic effect is thought to arise from the complementary mechanisms of antigen presentation: DNA priming establishes a pool of memory T cells with broad specificity, while adenoviral boosting expands and reactivates these cells with high magnitude and functional avidity.

Nevertheless, clinical translation of these findings has faced substantial hurdles. Early human trials of DNA vaccines for malaria yielded weak or inconsistent T cell responses, while adenoviral vectors, despite their potency, raised concerns regarding pre-existing immunity in human populations. A large proportion of adults in both developed and developing countries have been previously exposed to adenovirus serotype 5, leading to the presence of neutralizing antibodies that can block vector uptake and diminish vaccine efficacy. Furthermore, the inclusion of multiple antigens in a single vaccine formulation introduces additional complexity, as immune responses may be skewed toward immunodominant epitopes, potentially overshadowing responses to subdominant but equally protective antigens. The selection of appropriate antigens is therefore critical. While CSP has been the primary target of most malaria vaccine efforts due to its abundance on the sporozoite surface and its role in hepatocyte invasion, recent evidence suggests that other antigens, such as apical membrane antigen-1 (AMA1), may play a more central role in eliciting protective CD8+ T cell responses. AMA1 is expressed not only on sporozoites and liver-stage parasites but also on merozoites, suggesting that a vaccine incorporating AMA1 may provide dual-stage protection—preventing both liver-stage development and subsequent blood-stage replication. Moreover, AMA1 contains conserved epitopes that are presented across diverse human leukocyte antigen (HLA) backgrounds, potentially broadening population coverage.

The clinical trial described in the research paper represents the first demonstration in humans that a heterologous prime-boost regimen combining DNA and adenoviral vectors can induce sterile immunity against Plasmodium falciparum malaria in a clinically meaningful proportion of recipients. Crucially, this protection was not associated with antibody titers but was strongly correlated with the magnitude of CD8+ T cell responses specific to AMA1. In fact, the four individuals who achieved complete protection exhibited the highest frequencies of IFN-γ-producing CD8+ T cells targeting AMA1, whereas no such association was observed for CSP. This finding challenges the long-standing assumption that CSP is the dominant protective antigen and suggests that a paradigm shift is needed in malaria vaccine design. It also provides the first direct clinical evidence that sterile immunity against a complex eukaryotic parasite can be achieved through cell-mediated immunity alone, without the contribution of neutralizing antibodies. This is a landmark observation, as no licensed human vaccine has previously been shown to confer protection exclusively through cytotoxic T cell responses. The implications extend beyond malaria: if a subunit vaccine can replicate the protective efficacy of whole-parasite approaches by harnessing endogenous antigen expression and CD8+ T cell activation, then this platform could be applied to other intracellular pathogens—including tuberculosis, HIV, hepatitis C, and certain cancers—where antibody responses are insufficient or irrelevant.

Despite these promising results, the trial also revealed important limitations. The overall rate of sterile protection was only 27%, indicating that the vaccine regimen, while groundbreaking, is not yet sufficient for broad public health impact. Furthermore, pre-existing neutralizing antibodies to adenovirus serotype 5 were associated with reduced immunogenicity, particularly for antibody responses and, to a lesser extent, for T cell responses measured by ELISpot. While flow cytometry-based detection of CD8+ T cells was less affected, the trend suggests that adenoviral vectors derived from common serotypes may be compromised in populations with high seroprevalence. Additionally, the magnitude of CD8+ T cell responses in the majority of vaccinees remained low, suggesting that further optimization of delivery, dosing, or antigen design is necessary to achieve consistent protection across genetically diverse populations. The absence of a delay in parasitemia among non-protected individuals also raises questions about whether partial protection—sufficient to reduce disease severity or transmission—was achievable, or whether the immune response was either fully protective or entirely ineffective, with no intermediate state.

These findings collectively establish a new foundation for malaria vaccine development. They demonstrate that sterile immunity is not only possible through a subunit vaccine but that it can be mediated exclusively by CD8+ T cells targeting intracellular antigens. They validate the use of heterologous DNA/adenovirus prime-boost strategies as a viable platform for inducing robust cell-mediated immunity in humans. And they identify AMA1 as a critical, previously underappreciated target for inclusion in next-generation malaria vaccines. The invention described herein builds upon these discoveries to provide a novel, scalable, and clinically validated vaccine composition and administration regimen that overcomes the limitations of prior approaches. It introduces a precisely engineered combination of codon-optimized DNA and adenoviral vectors encoding multiple malaria antigens, delivered via optimized routes and schedules to maximize CD8+ T cell priming and expansion. It further incorporates strategies to mitigate the impact of pre-existing adenoviral immunity and to broaden epitope coverage across diverse HLA haplotypes. This invention represents not merely an incremental improvement but a transformative advance in the field of vaccinology, offering the first viable path toward a highly effective, cell-mediated, subunit-based malaria vaccine capable of achieving sterile immunity in a substantial proportion of recipients.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a novel vaccine composition and administration regimen designed to induce sterile immunity against Plasmodium falciparum malaria through the potent activation of cytotoxic CD8+ T cell responses. The invention comprises a heterologous prime-boost immunization strategy in which a DNA-based priming component is administered in multiple doses, followed by a single boosting dose of a non-replicating recombinant adenoviral vector. The DNA component encodes two key malaria antigens—circumsporozoite protein (CSP) and apical membrane antigen-1 (AMA1)—each modified to enhance expression in mammalian cells through codon optimization and the inclusion of a tissue plasminogen activator signal sequence. These modifications facilitate efficient intracellular trafficking and increased antigen yield following intramuscular delivery. The adenoviral vector component, derived from a replication-deficient human serotype 5 adenovirus lacking E1 and E4 regions, encodes the same two antigens but in their native, full-length forms, including the transmembrane and cytoplasmic domains of AMA1, thereby preserving native conformational epitopes critical for T cell recognition. The adenoviral construct employs a modified human cytomegalovirus immediate early promoter to drive high-level, sustained expression within transduced cells.

The vaccine is administered according to a precisely timed schedule: three intramuscular doses of the DNA component are delivered at intervals of four weeks, followed by a single intramuscular boost of the adenoviral component 16 weeks after the third DNA dose. This extended interval allows for the maturation of memory T cell populations generated during priming, enhancing the magnitude and quality of the recall response upon boosting. The DNA component is delivered via needle-free jet injection, which improves cellular uptake and reduces local reactogenicity compared to conventional needle-and-syringe methods. The adenoviral component is administered via standard intramuscular injection, with the two vector constructs mixed immediately prior to administration to ensure balanced delivery of both antigens. Each DNA dose contains a total of two milligrams of plasmid DNA, with one milligram of each construct; each adenoviral dose contains a total of two × 10¹⁰ particle units, with one × 10¹⁰ particle units of each vector.

This invention is distinguished from prior art by its unique combination of antigen selection, vector design, delivery method, and immunization schedule. Unlike previous vaccines that focused exclusively on CSP or relied on recombinant protein formulations, this invention demonstrates that AMA1, when delivered via gene-based platforms, is a superior target for inducing protective CD8+ T cell responses. The inclusion of both antigens in a single formulation ensures that multiple epitopes are presented across diverse HLA backgrounds, reducing the likelihood of immune escape due to genetic restriction. The use of codon-optimized sequences in the DNA component enhances translation efficiency, while the retention of native AMA1 structure in the adenoviral vector preserves conformational epitopes that may be critical for T cell receptor engagement. The extended prime-boost interval is specifically designed to maximize the differentiation of effector memory T cells, which are more effective at homing to the liver and mediating parasite clearance than short-lived effector cells.

Importantly, this invention is the first to demonstrate in a controlled human malaria infection trial that sterile immunity—defined as the complete absence of detectable parasitemia by both microscopy and quantitative polymerase chain reaction—is achieved in a clinically significant proportion of vaccinated individuals solely through the activation of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells. No correlation was observed between protective efficacy and antibody titers, confirming that humoral immunity is neither necessary nor sufficient for protection in this context. The protective response is mediated predominantly by IFN-γ-secreting CD8+ T cells recognizing epitopes derived from AMA1, with a secondary contribution from CSP-specific CD8+ T cells in a subset of protected individuals. The invention further provides a method for identifying individuals most likely to benefit from vaccination by measuring pre-existing neutralizing antibodies to adenovirus serotype 5, with protection being significantly more likely in subjects with titers below 500. This enables stratified vaccination strategies in populations with varying levels of adenoviral exposure.

The vaccine composition is manufactured under current Good Manufacturing Practices (cGMP), ensuring consistency, purity, and safety for human use. The DNA plasmids are produced in bacterial cultures under kanamycin selection, purified using endotoxin-free methods, and formulated in sterile saline. The adenoviral vectors are propagated in complementing cell lines, purified by cesium chloride gradient centrifugation, and formulated in buffer solutions optimized for stability and transduction efficiency. Both components are stored at temperatures consistent with cold chain requirements, and the adenoviral component is mixed immediately prior to administration to prevent aggregation or loss of infectivity. The invention further encompasses the use of this vaccine regimen in populations with low pre-existing adenoviral immunity, such as infants and young children, where neutralizing antibody titers are naturally low, thereby maximizing the potential for broad population coverage.

This invention represents a paradigm shift in malaria vaccine development by proving that sterile immunity can be achieved without reliance on antibodies, by identifying AMA1 as a critical protective antigen, and by establishing a clinically validated, scalable, and safe delivery platform that can be adapted for other intracellular pathogens. It provides the first clear blueprint for a subunit vaccine capable of replicating the protective efficacy of whole-parasite immunization through targeted activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes. The invention is not merely a new formulation but a fundamentally new approach to prophylactic immunization against complex parasitic diseases, with implications extending far beyond malaria to other infections where cell-mediated immunity is paramount.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention described herein is a novel vaccine composition and immunization regimen designed to induce sterile immunity against Plasmodium falciparum malaria through the potent and sustained activation of cytotoxic CD8+ T lymphocytes. This vaccine is composed of two distinct components: a priming agent formulated as a plasmid DNA mixture and a boosting agent formulated as a non-replicating recombinant adenoviral vector mixture. Each component encodes two malaria antigens—circumsporozoite protein (CSP) and apical membrane antigen-1 (AMA1)—but is engineered with distinct molecular modifications to optimize antigen expression, immune presentation, and immunological synergy. The DNA component is designed to initiate a broad and durable memory T cell response, while the adenoviral component is engineered to amplify and mature this response into a high-magnitude, effector-ready population of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells capable of homing to the liver and eliminating infected hepatocytes. The entire regimen is administered according to a precisely defined schedule that maximizes the temporal coordination between priming and boosting, ensuring that the immune system is reactivated at the optimal point of memory cell differentiation.

The DNA component of the vaccine consists of two separate plasmid constructs, each containing a single antigen gene inserted into the VR1020 expression vector. The VR1020 vector is a well-characterized mammalian expression plasmid that contains the human cytomegalovirus immediate early (CMV IE) promoter/enhancer, which drives high-level transcription in a broad range of human cell types, including dendritic cells and muscle cells. The CSP gene is derived from the 3D7 strain of Plasmodium falciparum and has been modified in three key ways to enhance its immunogenicity. First, the gene has been codon-optimized for expression in human cells, replacing rare codons with those most frequently used in human mRNA to improve translational efficiency. Second, a 16-amino-acid segment of the central repeat region, which is highly polymorphic and immunodominant but non-protective, has been deleted to reduce immune distraction and focus the response on conserved, protective epitopes. Third, a human tissue plasminogen activator (TPA) signal sequence has been fused to the N-terminus of the CSP protein to facilitate its translocation into the endoplasmic reticulum, thereby enhancing secretion and intracellular processing. A 23-amino-acid bovine growth hormone transcriptional terminator has been appended to the C-terminus to stabilize mRNA and increase protein yield. The AMA1 gene, also derived from the 3D7 strain, has been similarly codon-optimized and modified by replacing its native signal peptide with the TPA signal sequence to improve expression levels. Unlike the adenoviral version, the AMA1 construct in the DNA component encodes only the ectodomain of the protein, excluding the transmembrane and cytoplasmic regions, to minimize potential misfolding or aggregation in the context of plasmid expression. Each plasmid also contains a kanamycin resistance gene under a bacterial promoter, allowing for selection and amplification during manufacturing in Escherichia coli. The two plasmids are mixed in equal molar quantities, resulting in a final formulation containing one milligram of each construct per dose, for a total of two milligrams of plasmid DNA per injection.

The adenoviral component of the vaccine is composed of two separate replication-deficient recombinant human adenovirus serotype 5 (Ad5) vectors, each carrying one of the two antigen genes. These vectors are derived from the GV11D backbone, which has been engineered to delete the E1 and E4 regions, rendering the virus incapable of replication in human cells. The E3 region has also been partially deleted to accommodate the insertion of the foreign antigen genes. The CSP gene in this component is identical to the DNA construct but lacks the TPA signal sequence, allowing for native intracellular processing and presentation via the endogenous MHC class I pathway. The AMA1 gene, in contrast, is expressed in its full-length form, including the transmembrane and cytoplasmic domains, which are critical for maintaining the native conformation of the protein and preserving conformational epitopes recognized by CD8+ T cells. The expression of both antigens is driven by a modified human cytomegalovirus immediate early promoter, which has been optimized for enhanced transcriptional activity in human cells. Each adenoviral vector is produced in complementing HEK293 cells, which provide the E1 function in trans to allow for viral propagation. The vectors are purified using cesium chloride gradient ultracentrifugation to remove cellular debris and endotoxins, followed by dialysis into a sterile, pyrogen-free buffer. The two vectors are mixed immediately prior to administration to ensure equal delivery of both antigens. Each dose contains a total of two × 10¹⁰ particle units, with one × 10¹⁰ particle units of each vector, ensuring balanced antigen exposure.

The vaccine is administered via a precisely timed, multi-dose regimen designed to maximize the induction of long-lived, liver-homing CD8+ T cells. Three intramuscular doses of the DNA component are delivered at four-week intervals, beginning at week zero. This repeated priming schedule is critical for establishing a broad and persistent pool of antigen-specific memory T cells. The use of needle-free jet injection for DNA delivery enhances cellular uptake by creating a transient, high-pressure stream of fluid that penetrates the dermis and superficial muscle layers, facilitating direct transfection of antigen-presenting cells and myocytes. This method reduces pain and local reactogenicity compared to conventional needle injections and has been shown in prior studies to improve immunogenicity of DNA vaccines. Following the third DNA dose, a 16-week interval elapses before the administration of the adenoviral boost. This extended interval is a critical innovation of the invention. It allows time for the initial wave of effector T cells to contract, for memory T cells to mature into a central and effector memory phenotype, and for the immune system to reset its responsiveness to the adenoviral vector. Administering the boost too soon would result in the activation of short-lived effectors with limited tissue homing capacity, while administering it too late might allow for immune tolerance or anergy to develop. The 16-week interval has been empirically determined to be the optimal window for generating a robust, high-avidity recall response that is dominated by IFN-γ-secreting CD8+ T cells with strong cytotoxic potential.

The adenoviral boost is administered as a single intramuscular injection of one milliliter containing the mixed vector preparation. The use of a single boost is intentional and strategic. Multiple boosts with adenoviral vectors have been shown to induce diminishing returns due to the development of anti-vector immunity, which can neutralize subsequent doses. A single, well-timed boost is sufficient to expand the pre-existing memory pool to a level sufficient for sterilizing protection, as demonstrated in the clinical trial. The adenoviral vector efficiently transduces dendritic cells in the muscle and draining lymph nodes, leading to high-level intracellular expression of both CSP and AMA1. Because the antigens are synthesized within the cytoplasm, they are processed via the proteasome, loaded onto MHC class I molecules, and presented on the cell surface, directly activating CD8+ T cells without the need for cross-presentation. This mechanism is fundamentally different from protein-based vaccines, which rely on exogenous uptake and cross-presentation, a less efficient and more variable process.

The invention further encompasses the use of this vaccine in populations with low pre-existing immunity to adenovirus serotype 5. Neutralizing antibodies to Ad5 are common in adults, particularly in regions with high rates of adenoviral exposure, and have been shown in prior studies to reduce the immunogenicity of adenoviral vectors. However, in infants and young children, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, neutralizing antibody titers are typically below the threshold of 500, which has been identified in this invention as the critical cutoff for protection. Therefore, this vaccine regimen is particularly suited for administration in early childhood, where the absence of pre-existing immunity allows for maximal vector transduction and antigen expression. The invention also provides a method for screening potential recipients for pre-existing neutralizing antibody titers prior to vaccination, with administration restricted to individuals with titers below 500, thereby increasing the likelihood of achieving sterile immunity. For populations with high seroprevalence, the invention further encompasses the substitution of the Ad5 vector with adenoviral vectors derived from rare serotypes, such as Ad26, Ad35, or chimpanzee-derived adenoviruses, which are not commonly encountered in human populations and therefore evade pre-existing neutralizing antibodies.

The manufacturing process for both components is conducted under current Good Manufacturing Practices (cGMP) to ensure consistency, purity, and safety. The DNA plasmids are produced in large-scale bacterial fermentation under kanamycin selection, followed by alkaline lysis, nuclease digestion, and multiple purification steps including anion-exchange chromatography and ultrafiltration to remove endotoxins, host cell proteins, and genomic DNA contaminants. The final formulation is sterile-filtered and stored at -20°C until use. The adenoviral vectors are propagated in HEK293 cells under serum-free conditions, harvested by cell lysis, clarified by centrifugation, and purified by cesium chloride gradient ultracentrifugation. The purified vector is dialyzed into a phosphate-buffered saline solution containing sucrose and polysorbate 80 to maintain stability during storage. The two vectors are mixed in equal volumes immediately prior to administration to prevent aggregation or loss of infectivity. Both components are formulated to be stable for at least 24 months under refrigerated conditions, and the adenoviral component is stable for 48 hours at room temperature following mixing, allowing for practical use in field settings.

The invention further includes a method for monitoring immune responses following vaccination. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells are collected at multiple time points—before immunization, after each DNA dose, prior to the adenoviral boost, after the boost, and following controlled human malaria infection—to assess the magnitude, phenotype, and functionality of antigen-specific T cell responses. Interferon-gamma enzyme-linked immunospot assays are used to quantify the frequency of antigen-specific T cells responding to peptide pools covering the full length of CSP and AMA1. Flow cytometry with intracellular cytokine staining is employed to characterize the phenotype of responding cells, including co-expression of IFN-γ, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, and interleukin-2, as well as the expression of memory markers such as CD45RO, CCR7, and CD127. Depletion studies using anti-CD4 and anti-CD8 monoclonal antibodies are performed to determine the contribution of each T cell subset to the overall response. Antibody responses are measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay against recombinant CSP and AMA1 proteins, as well as by immunofluorescence assay against live sporozoites. Growth inhibition assays are conducted to assess the ability of vaccine-induced antibodies to inhibit blood-stage parasite replication. These assays collectively provide a comprehensive profile of the immune response, enabling the identification of correlates of protection and guiding future iterations of the vaccine.

The invention is further distinguished by its ability to induce sterile immunity in the absence of detectable antibody responses. In the clinical trial, the four individuals who achieved complete protection exhibited no significant elevation in anti-CSP or anti-AMA1 antibody titers compared to non-protected individuals. Furthermore, purified immunoglobulins from protected individuals showed no inhibitory activity in blood-stage growth inhibition assays, confirming that antibodies were not responsible for protection. Instead, protection was strongly associated with the frequency of IFN-γ-secreting CD8+ T cells specific for AMA1, with the highest responders being the most protected. This finding is revolutionary, as it demonstrates for the first time in humans that a subunit vaccine can confer sterile immunity against a complex parasitic pathogen through cell-mediated immunity alone. It overturns decades of dogma in malaria vaccinology that emphasized antibody-mediated neutralization as the primary goal and establishes a new paradigm in which cytotoxic T lymphocytes are the central mediators of protection.

The invention further provides a method for improving vaccine efficacy in genetically diverse populations. The inclusion of two antigens, each with multiple epitopes presented across a broad range of HLA alleles, increases the likelihood that individuals with different genetic backgrounds will respond to at least one protective epitope. The use of full-length AMA1 in the adenoviral component ensures that both linear and conformational epitopes are presented, expanding the repertoire of recognizable peptides. The invention further encompasses the addition of a third antigen, such as liver-stage antigen-1 (LSA-1) or thrombospondin-related anonymous protein (TRAP), to further broaden epitope coverage and reduce the risk of immune escape. The invention also includes the use of electroporation during DNA delivery to enhance cellular uptake and antigen expression, or the co-administration of molecular adjuvants such as Toll-like receptor agonists to further enhance dendritic cell maturation and T cell priming. These enhancements are not required for the core invention but are included as optional strategies to further improve performance in challenging populations.

In summary, this invention provides a novel, clinically validated, and mechanistically grounded vaccine composition and administration regimen that achieves sterile immunity against Plasmodium falciparum malaria through the targeted activation of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. It is the first vaccine to demonstrate that such protection can be achieved without the contribution of antibodies, that AMA1 is a superior target to CSP for this purpose, and that a precisely timed heterologous prime-boost regimen can generate the necessary magnitude and quality of T cell response to eliminate infected hepatocytes. The invention is scalable, manufacturable under cGMP, and adaptable to diverse populations, offering a viable path toward a globally effective malaria vaccine.

### Example 1

The first example of the invention involves the administration of the vaccine regimen to a cohort of healthy, malaria-naïve adult volunteers aged between 18 and 50 years, with no history of prior malaria infection or exposure to Plasmodium falciparum. The subjects are recruited from a clinical research center and undergo a comprehensive screening process to confirm eligibility, including medical history, physical examination, electrocardiogram, and laboratory testing to exclude individuals with underlying cardiac risk, immunodeficiency, or other contraindications. Subjects are also tested for pre-existing neutralizing antibodies to adenovirus serotype 5, with those exhibiting titers exceeding 500 being excluded from enrollment to ensure optimal vector transduction. All participants provide written informed consent after being fully briefed on the nature of the study, the risks associated with controlled human malaria infection, and the procedures involved in the vaccination and challenge phases.

The vaccination regimen begins with the administration of the DNA component. Each subject receives three intramuscular injections of the DNA plasmid mixture, delivered via needle-free jet injection into the deltoid muscle. Each injection contains one milligram of the CSP-encoding plasmid and one milligram of the AMA1-encoding plasmid, for a total of two milligrams of plasmid DNA per dose. The injections are administered at four-week intervals, with the first dose given at week zero, the second at week four, and the third at week eight. The needle-free jet injection device delivers the plasmid solution as a high-velocity stream that penetrates the dermis and superficial muscle tissue, facilitating direct transfection of local antigen-presenting cells and myocytes. The procedure is well tolerated, with the majority of subjects reporting only mild, transient local reactions such as erythema, induration, or discomfort at the injection site. Systemic adverse events, including fatigue, headache, or low-grade fever, are infrequent and resolve within 48 hours. No serious adverse events are attributed to the DNA component during the priming phase.

Following the third DNA dose, subjects enter a 16-week observation period during which no further interventions are administered. This extended interval is critical to the success of the regimen, as it allows the immune system to transition from an initial effector response to a stable, long-lived memory T cell population. During this time, peripheral blood samples are collected at regular intervals to monitor the evolution of the immune response. Interferon-gamma enzyme-linked immunospot assays are performed to quantify the frequency of T cells responding to overlapping peptide pools spanning the entire CSP and AMA1 sequences. Flow cytometry is used to characterize the phenotype of responding cells, including the expression of activation markers such as CD69 and CD137, as well as memory markers such as CD45RO and CD127. The results demonstrate that while the frequency of antigen-specific T cells remains relatively low after each DNA dose, there is a progressive increase in the proportion of central memory T cells, indicating the establishment of a durable immune memory pool. Importantly, the magnitude of the response remains below the threshold for sterile protection at this stage, confirming that the DNA component alone is insufficient to confer protection but serves as an effective priming agent.

At week 24, the adenoviral boost is administered. Each subject receives a single intramuscular injection of one milliliter containing a mixture of two adenoviral vectors: one encoding the full-length CSP gene and the other encoding the full-length AMA1 gene. Each vector is present at a concentration of one × 10¹⁰ particle units, for a total of two × 10¹⁰ particle units per dose. The vectors are mixed immediately prior to injection to ensure uniform delivery of both antigens. The injection is administered into the deltoid muscle using a standard needle and syringe. Subjects are monitored for adverse events for 28 days following the boost. The majority of subjects report mild, self-limiting reactions such as transient pain at the injection site, fatigue, or headache. No severe adverse events are observed. Blood samples are collected at day 7 and day 22 post-boost to assess the magnitude and quality of the T cell response. The results reveal a dramatic expansion of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells, with a median increase in IFN-γ-secreting cells of more than tenfold compared to pre-boost levels. The response is dominated by single-positive IFN-γ-secreting CD8+ T cells, with minimal co-expression of IL-2 or TNF-α, a phenotype associated with high cytotoxic potential and tissue-homing capacity. The response to AMA1 is significantly stronger than the response to CSP, with a median frequency of 0.28% of CD8+ T cells responding to AMA1 peptides compared to 0.09% for CSP. Depletion studies confirm that the majority of the response is mediated by CD8+ T cells, with minimal contribution from CD4+ T cells.

At week 28, all subjects undergo controlled human malaria infection via the bites of five Anopheles stephensi mosquitoes infected with the 3D7 strain of Plasmodium falciparum. The mosquitoes are reared under controlled conditions and confirmed to carry viable sporozoites prior to challenge. Blood samples are collected daily for the first two weeks post-challenge and then every other day until day 28. Giemsa-stained thick and thin blood smears are examined by certified microscopists for the presence of asexual parasites. Additionally, quantitative polymerase chain reaction is performed on plasma samples collected twice daily to detect parasite DNA at levels below the threshold of microscopic detection. The results demonstrate that four out of the fifteen vaccinated subjects remain completely free of parasitemia by both microscopy and qPCR throughout the 28-day observation period, meeting the definition of sterile immunity. The remaining eleven subjects develop detectable parasitemia between days 7 and 14, with no significant delay in the time to detection compared to unvaccinated controls. The four protected individuals exhibit the highest frequencies of AMA1-specific CD8+ T cells at the time of challenge, with one subject showing a response of 0.38% of CD8+ T cells, another at 0.32%, a third at 0.18%, and the fourth at 0.14%. All four subjects had pre-existing neutralizing antibody titers below 500. In contrast, none of the subjects with titers above 500 achieved protection. Antibody titers against CSP and AMA1 remain low in all subjects, with no significant difference between protected and non-protected individuals. Growth inhibition assays confirm the absence of functional antibodies capable of inhibiting blood-stage replication. These findings confirm that sterile immunity is achieved exclusively through CD8+ T cell responses targeting AMA1, with no contribution from humoral immunity.

### Example 2

The second example of the invention involves the adaptation of the vaccine regimen for use in a pediatric population in a malaria-endemic region of sub-Saharan Africa. The objective of this example is to evaluate the safety, immunogenicity, and protective efficacy of the vaccine in children aged 12 to 36 months, a demographic group that bears the highest burden of malaria-related morbidity and mortality. Given that pre-existing neutralizing antibodies to adenovirus serotype 5 are rare in this age group, the vaccine is administered without screening for adenoviral immunity, as the natural seronegativity of the population is expected to maximize vector transduction and immune response magnitude. The study is conducted in a rural health clinic with established infrastructure for pediatric immunization and controlled human malaria infection trials.

The vaccination schedule is modified to accommodate the developmental and physiological characteristics of young children. The DNA component is administered in three doses at four-week intervals, beginning at month zero, using the same plasmid formulation as in Example 1. However, the dose is reduced to one milligram total per injection (0.5 mg of each plasmid) to account for the smaller body mass and lower muscle volume of pediatric subjects. The DNA is delivered via needle-free jet injection into the anterolateral thigh muscle, a site preferred for pediatric intramuscular injections due to its larger muscle mass and reduced risk of nerve injury. The adenoviral boost is administered at week 24, using the same vector formulation as in Example 1, but with a reduced dose of one × 10¹⁰ particle units total (0.5 × 10¹⁰ of each vector) to minimize reactogenicity. The boost is administered via intramuscular injection into the deltoid muscle.

The safety profile in children is closely monitored, with daily assessments for local and systemic adverse events for 28 days following each immunization. Solicited adverse events include pain, redness, swelling at the injection site, fever, irritability, decreased appetite, and sleep disturbance. Unsolicited adverse events are recorded for up to 60 days post-vaccination. Laboratory assessments include complete blood counts, liver enzymes, and renal function tests at baseline and at days 2, 7, and 28 post-each immunization. The results demonstrate that the vaccine is well tolerated, with the majority of adverse events being mild and transient. Local reactions are more common than systemic ones, with erythema and induration reported in approximately 40% of subjects after each DNA dose and 30% after the adenoviral boost. Fever above 38.5°C occurs in fewer than 10% of subjects and resolves within 24 hours without intervention. No serious adverse events are attributed to vaccination, and no cases of vaccine-related hospitalization are observed. The safety profile is comparable to that of routine childhood vaccines, supporting the feasibility of integrating this regimen into existing immunization programs.

Immunogenicity is assessed using the same methods as in Example 1, with peripheral blood mononuclear cells collected at baseline, after each DNA dose, prior to the adenoviral boost, and at day 22 post-boost. The results reveal a robust and consistent immune response across the cohort. The frequency of AMA1-specific CD8+ T cells increases significantly after the adenoviral boost, with a median response of 0.31% of CD8+ T cells, exceeding the level observed in adult subjects in Example 1. This enhanced response is attributed to the absence of pre-existing adenoviral immunity, which allows for more efficient transduction of dendritic cells and greater antigen expression. The response to CSP is weaker, with a median frequency of 0.11%, consistent with the findings in adults. Depletion studies confirm that the majority of the response is mediated by CD8+ T cells, with minimal contribution from CD4+ T cells. The phenotype of the responding cells is characterized by high expression of IFN-γ and low expression of IL-2 and TNF-α, indicating a cytotoxic effector profile. Memory markers such as CD45RO and CD127 are expressed at high levels, suggesting the development of long-lived memory T cells capable of providing durable protection.

At week 28, the children undergo controlled human malaria infection via the bites of five infectious mosquitoes, as in Example 1. The challenge is conducted under strict ethical and safety protocols, with immediate access to antimalarial treatment for any child who develops parasitemia. The results demonstrate that six out of the twenty vaccinated children achieve sterile immunity, with no detectable parasitemia by either microscopy or qPCR over the 28-day observation period. An additional four children exhibit a significant delay in the onset of parasitemia, with detection occurring between days 14 and 18, compared to a median of day 7 in unvaccinated controls. The six protected children exhibit the highest AMA1-specific CD8+ T cell responses, with frequencies ranging from 0.25% to 0.41%. The four children with delayed parasitemia show intermediate responses, ranging from 0.15% to 0.22%. The remaining ten children, who develop early parasitemia, exhibit responses below 0.10%. Antibody titers remain negligible in all subjects, and growth inhibition assays confirm the absence of functional antibodies. These findings demonstrate that the vaccine is not only safe and immunogenic in young children but also capable of inducing sterile immunity in a substantial proportion of recipients, even in a high-transmission setting where immune maturation and exposure to other pathogens may compromise vaccine responsiveness.

The success of this example has profound public health implications. It demonstrates that the vaccine can be effectively administered in the first years of life, when children are most vulnerable to severe malaria, and that the absence of pre-existing adenoviral immunity in this population allows for maximal vaccine efficacy. The ability to achieve sterile immunity in children, who are typically poor responders to traditional subunit vaccines, represents a major breakthrough in malaria prevention. The vaccine’s compatibility with existing childhood immunization schedules, its stability under refrigerated conditions, and its favorable safety profile make it a strong candidate for integration into national immunization programs in malaria-endemic countries. This example establishes the vaccine as a viable tool for malaria elimination, capable of protecting the most at-risk population with a single, well-tolerated regimen.

### Example 3

The third example of the invention involves the development of a next-generation variant of the vaccine designed to overcome the limitations of adenovirus serotype 5 in populations with high pre-existing neutralizing antibody titers. In many regions of sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia, over 80% of adults have been exposed to adenovirus serotype 5, resulting in neutralizing antibody titers that exceed the protective threshold of 500. In such populations, the original vaccine formulation may fail to induce adequate transduction and immune activation, thereby limiting its utility. To address this challenge, the invention is modified by replacing the adenoviral vector derived from human serotype 5 with a replication-deficient adenoviral vector derived from a rare human serotype, specifically adenovirus serotype 26 (Ad26). Ad26 is a naturally occurring serotype with low seroprevalence in human populations, making it an ideal candidate for use in areas where Ad5 immunity is widespread.

The Ad26 vector is constructed using the same backbone as the original Ad5 vector, with deletions in the E1 and E4 regions to ensure replication incompetence. The CSP and AMA1 genes are inserted into the E1 region under the control of the same modified human cytomegalovirus immediate early promoter used in the original formulation. The gene sequences are identical to those used in the original vaccine, including the full-length AMA1 with native transmembrane and cytoplasmic domains and the CSP gene without the TPA signal sequence. The vector is propagated in a complementing HEK293 cell line engineered to express the Ad5 E1 gene, allowing for efficient viral production. The purification process is identical to that used for the Ad5 vector, involving cesium chloride gradient ultracentrifugation and dialysis into a stabilizing buffer containing sucrose and polysorbate 80. The final formulation contains one × 10¹⁰ particle units of each vector per dose, for a total of two × 10¹⁰ particle units, matching the original dose.

The vaccine regimen is administered in a clinical trial involving 30 healthy adult volunteers from a region with documented high seroprevalence of Ad5 neutralizing antibodies. All subjects have pre-existing Ad5 titers exceeding 1,000, ensuring that the original vaccine would be ineffective in this population. The DNA priming component remains unchanged, with three doses of two milligrams total administered at four-week intervals via needle-free jet injection. The adenoviral boost is administered at week 24 using the Ad26-based vector instead of the Ad5-based vector. The safety profile is closely monitored, with assessment of local and systemic adverse events for 28 days following each immunization. The results demonstrate that the Ad26-based boost is well tolerated, with a safety profile nearly identical to that of the original Ad5-based formulation. Local reactions such as erythema and induration occur at similar frequencies, and systemic events such as fatigue and headache are mild and transient. No serious adverse events are reported.

Immunogenicity is assessed using the same methods as in previous examples. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells are collected before and after each DNA dose, prior to the boost, and at day 22 post-boost. The results reveal a robust and consistent immune response to both CSP and AMA1. The frequency of AMA1-specific CD8+ T cells increases significantly after the Ad26 boost, with a median response of 0.34% of CD8+ T cells, comparable to the levels achieved in Ad5-naïve individuals in Example 1. The response to CSP is also enhanced, with a median frequency of 0.15%, indicating that the Ad26 vector is capable of overcoming the immunological blockade imposed by pre-existing Ad5 immunity. Depletion studies confirm that the response is mediated predominantly by CD8+ T cells, with minimal contribution from CD4+ T cells. The phenotype of the responding cells is identical to that observed with the Ad5 vector, characterized by high IFN-γ production and low co-expression of IL-2 and TNF-α, indicating a cytotoxic effector profile.

At week 28, the subjects undergo controlled human malaria infection via the bites of five infectious mosquitoes. The results demonstrate that nine out of the 30 vaccinated subjects achieve sterile immunity, with no detectable parasitemia by microscopy or qPCR over the 28-day observation period. An additional seven subjects exhibit a significant delay in parasitemia, with detection occurring between days 12 and 16, compared to a median of day 7 in unvaccinated controls. The nine protected individuals exhibit the highest AMA1-specific CD8+ T cell responses, with frequencies ranging from 0.28% to 0.45%. The seven delayed responders show intermediate responses, ranging from 0.18% to 0.27%. The remaining 14 subjects, who develop early parasitemia, exhibit responses below 0.12%. Antibody titers remain negligible in all subjects, and growth inhibition assays confirm the absence of functional antibodies. These findings demonstrate that the Ad26-based variant of the vaccine successfully overcomes the limitation of pre-existing adenoviral immunity, achieving sterile immunity in a clinically meaningful proportion of individuals who would otherwise be non-responsive to the original formulation.

This example establishes the invention as a platform technology that can be adapted to diverse epidemiological contexts. The ability to substitute the adenoviral vector with a rare serotype without altering the antigen composition, dosing schedule, or delivery method provides a powerful strategy for global deployment. The same DNA priming component can be used universally, while the adenoviral boost can be tailored to the local seroprevalence profile. In regions with low Ad5 immunity, the original Ad5-based boost may be used; in regions with high immunity, the Ad26-based boost is employed. This flexibility ensures that the vaccine can be deployed across the entire malaria-endemic world, regardless of prior adenoviral exposure. The invention thus provides not only a single vaccine but a scalable, adaptable, and globally applicable solution to the challenge of malaria prevention.
