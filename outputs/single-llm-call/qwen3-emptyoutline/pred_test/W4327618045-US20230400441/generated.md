# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to a method for the authentication and strain differentiation of donkey meat based on the volatile organic compound (VOC) profile of raw meat samples, utilizing gas chromatography–ion mobility spectroscopy (GC–IMS) in combination with multivariate statistical analysis. More specifically, the invention provides a non-destructive, rapid, and highly accurate analytical protocol that identifies strain-specific VOC fingerprints in uncooked donkey muscle tissue, enabling reliable discrimination between different donkey breeds or strains without the need for thermal processing, chemical extraction, or prolonged sample preparation. The method is particularly applicable in food authenticity control, supply chain traceability, quality assurance in premium meat markets, and regulatory compliance for geographical indication labeling. The invention further encompasses a database of characteristic VOC markers associated with specific donkey strains, which may be integrated into automated detection systems for real-time, on-site verification of meat origin. This technology addresses a critical gap in the livestock and food industry by providing a scientifically validated, instrument-based solution to the longstanding challenge of distinguishing between visually indistinguishable meat products derived from genetically distinct donkey populations, thereby preventing economic fraud, ensuring consumer transparency, and supporting sustainable breeding practices.

The technical field of the invention spans analytical chemistry, food science, agricultural biotechnology, and forensic food authentication. Traditional methods for meat authentication, such as DNA analysis, isotopic fingerprinting, and protein electrophoresis, are either costly, time-consuming, or incapable of detecting subtle metabolic differences arising from breed-specific feeding, physiology, or lipid metabolism. Spectroscopic methods such as near-infrared (NIR) or Raman spectroscopy offer speed but lack the molecular specificity required to distinguish between closely related strains. Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS), while highly sensitive, requires extensive sample preparation including heating, solvent extraction, and derivatization, which alters the native volatile profile and introduces artifacts. In contrast, the present invention leverages the unique capabilities of GC–IMS, a technique that combines the separation power of gas chromatography with the high-resolution ion mobility detection of charged volatile molecules, enabling direct, headspace-based analysis of raw meat without any pre-treatment. The integration of chemometric models—specifically principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA)—allows for the extraction of subtle, multivariate patterns in VOC abundance that are imperceptible through univariate statistical comparison. This combination of instrumental precision and statistical robustness constitutes a novel technological advancement in the field of animal product authentication, offering a level of discriminatory power and operational simplicity previously unattainable for raw donkey meat.

The invention is particularly suited for implementation in slaughterhouses, meat processing facilities, customs inspection points, and high-value retail environments where rapid, non-invasive verification of meat origin is required. It eliminates the need for destructive sampling or laboratory-based analysis with multi-day turnaround times, enabling real-time quality control during processing and distribution. Furthermore, the method is adaptable to other equine species and potentially to other livestock such as beef, pork, and lamb, where breed-specific flavor profiles are economically significant. The system’s portability, minimal reagent consumption, and compatibility with automated data interpretation make it ideal for deployment in field settings, mobile laboratories, and integrated food safety monitoring networks. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in meat authentication, transitioning from post-hoc, laboratory-dependent verification to proactive, in-line, and automated strain identification based on intrinsic metabolic signatures.

## BACKGROUND

The global demand for high-quality, ethically sourced, and authentically labeled meat products has intensified in recent decades, driven by increasing consumer awareness, regulatory scrutiny, and the commercial value associated with premium meat varieties. Donkey meat, long consumed in parts of Asia, Africa, and Southern Europe, has emerged as a high-value specialty product due to its favorable nutritional profile—characterized by low fat content, high protein, and elevated levels of polyunsaturated fatty acids—which aligns with modern dietary preferences for lean, health-conscious meats. In China, donkey husbandry has become a cornerstone of rural economic development, with state-backed initiatives promoting the breeding and commercialization of donkey meat as part of broader rural revitalization programs. The economic significance of donkey meat is underscored by its contribution of over eighty percent of the total revenue generated from donkey farming, making the integrity of its supply chain a matter of both economic and social importance.

Despite its growing market, donkey meat faces significant challenges related to authenticity and traceability. Multiple donkey breeds and strains exist within China alone, including the SanFen (SF) and WuTou (WT) strains, which are often raised under similar conditions, fed identical diets, and processed using identical methods. Visually, their muscle tissue is indistinguishable, and traditional morphological or genetic markers are either too slow, too expensive, or insufficiently discriminatory for routine use in commercial settings. As a result, fraudulent labeling—where lower-value or non-authentic meat is passed off as premium donkey meat—is a persistent and economically damaging problem. Consumers, regulators, and producers alike lack a reliable, rapid, and cost-effective means of verifying the strain origin of raw donkey meat, leading to market instability, loss of consumer trust, and erosion of brand value for legitimate producers.

Prior attempts to authenticate meat have relied on a variety of analytical techniques, each with significant limitations. DNA-based methods, while highly specific, require tissue destruction, are sensitive to degradation during processing, and cannot distinguish between animals raised under identical genetic lineages but differing in metabolic output. Isotopic ratio mass spectrometry can detect geographical origin based on feed and water sources but is ineffective in distinguishing between genetically distinct strains raised in the same region. Protein profiling via electrophoresis or immunoassays lacks the resolution to detect subtle biochemical differences arising from breed-specific lipid metabolism. Spectroscopic techniques such as NIR and mid-infrared spectroscopy offer speed and non-destructiveness but suffer from poor molecular specificity, often yielding overlapping spectral signatures that cannot reliably differentiate between closely related meat types.

Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) has been the gold standard for volatile compound analysis in meat, and prior studies have identified hundreds of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in cooked donkey and horse meat, including aldehydes, ketones, alcohols, and esters, many of which are formed during thermal processing via Maillard reactions and lipid oxidation. However, these methods are fundamentally unsuited for the authentication of raw meat, as the VOC profile of cooked meat is dominated by reaction products generated during heating, which obscure the innate, breed-specific metabolic signatures present in uncooked tissue. Moreover, GC–MS requires labor-intensive sample preparation—including homogenization, headspace equilibration, solid-phase microextraction (SPME), and thermal desorption—which introduces variability, prolongs analysis time, and risks the loss or alteration of labile compounds. These limitations render GC–MS impractical for real-time, on-site authentication in commercial settings.

More recently, gas chromatography–ion mobility spectroscopy (GC–IMS) has emerged as a promising alternative for volatile compound analysis in food matrices. GC–IMS offers several advantages over GC–MS: it requires no sample pre-treatment beyond minimal handling, operates at ambient or near-ambient temperatures, provides rapid analysis (typically under 20 minutes per sample), and generates highly reproducible, high-resolution ion mobility spectra that are sensitive to subtle differences in molecular structure and concentration. Previous applications of GC–IMS have demonstrated its efficacy in distinguishing between meat types such as beef, lamb, and chicken, as well as in detecting differences in bacon from different pig breeds and in aging ham based on salt content. However, no prior study has applied GC–IMS to the authentication of donkey meat, nor has any method been developed to identify strain-specific VOC fingerprints in raw donkey muscle tissue using multivariate statistical modeling.

Furthermore, while chemometric techniques such as principal component analysis and partial least squares discriminant analysis have been employed in food science to classify complex spectral datasets, their integration with GC–IMS for the purpose of donkey meat strain differentiation has never been achieved. The absence of a validated, standardized protocol for VOC-based authentication of raw donkey meat represents a critical technological void in the food industry. Without such a method, producers cannot guarantee the authenticity of their product, regulators cannot enforce labeling laws, and consumers remain vulnerable to deception. The present invention fills this void by establishing, for the first time, a robust, reproducible, and commercially viable method for identifying and differentiating donkey meat strains based on their intrinsic, unaltered volatile organic compound profiles, using GC–IMS coupled with advanced multivariate analysis. This innovation not only resolves a longstanding industry problem but also sets a new benchmark for the authentication of other animal products where genetic strain influences metabolic output and sensory quality.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method for the authentication and strain differentiation of raw donkey meat through the analysis of its volatile organic compound (VOC) profile using gas chromatography–ion mobility spectroscopy (GC–IMS) in combination with multivariate statistical analysis. The method enables the non-destructive, rapid, and highly accurate identification of donkey meat derived from distinct genetic strains, such as SanFen and WuTou, without the need for thermal processing, chemical extraction, or lengthy sample preparation. By analyzing headspace VOCs directly from frozen, uncooked muscle tissue, the invention captures the innate metabolic signature of each strain, revealing a unique fingerprint of volatile compounds that are consistently and significantly different between strains. The method involves the acquisition of GC–IMS spectra from raw donkey meat samples, followed by the application of principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) to extract discriminative patterns and identify a set of characteristic VOC biomarkers that reliably distinguish between strains. The invention further includes a reference database of these biomarkers, their retention indices, drift times, and relative abundances, which serves as a standardized template for automated classification and quality control.

The method is implemented by placing a small, representative sample of frozen donkey longissimus dorsi muscle tissue into a sealed headspace vial, incubating it at a controlled temperature of 60°C for 15 minutes under gentle agitation, and then injecting a precise volume of the headspace gas into a GC–IMS instrument equipped with a non-polar capillary column and an ion mobility drift tube operating under defined temperature, voltage, and gas flow conditions. The resulting two-dimensional chromatogram, comprising retention time and ion mobility drift time, is processed using specialized software to generate a VOC fingerprint that is unique to the strain of origin. Multivariate statistical models are then applied to a training set of samples with known strain identities to establish classification boundaries and identify the most discriminative VOCs. These VOCs, which include alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, and heterocyclic compounds such as tetrahydrofurane, are quantified and ranked by their variable importance in projection (VIP) scores and statistical significance (p < 0.05). A set of seventeen VOCs has been identified as definitive markers for distinguishing between SanFen and WuTou donkey meat strains, with ten being significantly elevated in SanFen meat and seven being significantly elevated in WuTou meat. The invention further provides a classification algorithm that, when applied to an unknown sample, assigns it to the correct strain with a classification accuracy exceeding 98%, as validated through permutation testing and cross-validation procedures that confirm the model’s robustness and absence of overfitting.

The invention is not limited to the specific strains or analytical parameters described herein; rather, it encompasses a generalizable framework for strain authentication in any animal species where genetic variation leads to distinct metabolic profiles in raw tissue. The method is scalable, requiring no specialized reagents beyond high-purity nitrogen gas and commercially available calibration standards, and can be adapted to different sample matrices, environmental conditions, or instrument configurations through standardized calibration protocols. The resulting VOC fingerprint database can be integrated into automated detection systems, mobile analytical platforms, or cloud-based authentication services, enabling real-time verification at any point in the supply chain—from slaughterhouse to retail shelf. The invention further provides a method for quality assurance in premium meat markets, where the provenance of the meat directly influences its market value, and for regulatory enforcement, where compliance with geographical indication or breed-specific labeling laws must be verifiable through objective, scientific means. By eliminating subjectivity, reducing analysis time from days to minutes, and providing a quantifiable, repeatable metric for authenticity, the invention represents a transformative advancement in food authentication technology, offering a scientifically rigorous, commercially practical, and legally defensible solution to the problem of meat mislabeling.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE EMBODIMENTS

The present invention is embodied in a comprehensive, reproducible, and industrially scalable method for the authentication of donkey meat based on the volatile organic compound (VOC) profile of raw, uncooked muscle tissue. The method is designed to overcome the limitations of prior techniques by leveraging the unique analytical capabilities of gas chromatography–ion mobility spectroscopy (GC–IMS) in conjunction with multivariate statistical modeling to identify and quantify strain-specific VOC signatures that are intrinsic to the biological metabolism of distinct donkey breeds. The invention is not merely a diagnostic tool but a complete analytical framework encompassing sample handling, instrumental parameters, data acquisition, statistical validation, and classification protocol, all of which are interdependent and must be implemented in precise sequence to achieve the high degree of accuracy and reliability demonstrated herein. The method is applicable to any raw donkey meat sample derived from muscle tissue, with particular preference for the longissimus dorsi muscle due to its homogeneity, consistent composition, and established relevance in meat quality studies. The sample must be obtained from animals slaughtered under standardized conditions, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until analysis to preserve the native VOC profile and prevent enzymatic or microbial degradation that could alter the volatile signature.

The first step in the method involves the preparation of the meat sample for headspace analysis. A precisely weighed quantity of frozen donkey muscle tissue, approximately 1.5 grams, is transferred under sterile conditions into a 20-milliliter glass headspace vial equipped with a septum-sealed cap. The vial is immediately sealed to prevent contamination or loss of volatiles and placed into an automated headspace sampler configured for thermal equilibration. The sample is incubated at a temperature of 60 degrees Celsius for a duration of fifteen minutes while being subjected to continuous agitation at a rotational speed of 500 revolutions per minute. This combination of temperature and agitation ensures uniform vaporization of volatile compounds from the meat matrix without inducing thermal degradation or chemical reactions that would alter the native profile. The incubation temperature is carefully selected to be sufficiently high to promote the release of low-volatility compounds such as aldehydes and ketones, yet below the threshold at which lipid peroxidation or Maillard-type reactions occur, thereby preserving the metabolic signature of the raw tissue. The agitation ensures that the headspace gas is in dynamic equilibrium with the meat surface, maximizing the reproducibility of VOC extraction across samples. After incubation, exactly 500 microliters of the headspace gas is automatically withdrawn by the sampler and injected into the GC–IMS instrument without any further manipulation, dilution, or chemical treatment, thereby maintaining the integrity of the native volatile composition.

The GC–IMS instrument used in the method is a FlavourSpec® system, commercially available from Gesellschaft für Analytische Sensorysteme GmbH, Dortmund, Germany, and is equipped with a non-polar capillary column of 15 meters in length, 0.53 millimeters in internal diameter, and a 1.0 micrometer film thickness (MXT-5), along with a CTC-PAL automated headspace sampler. The column temperature is maintained at a constant 40 degrees Celsius throughout the analysis to ensure optimal separation of volatile compounds with varying polarities. The carrier gas is ultra-high-purity nitrogen (≥99.999%) delivered at a programmed flow rate that increases linearly over time: from 2 milliliters per minute during the initial two minutes, to 20 milliliters per minute at ten minutes, and finally to 100 milliliters per minute at twenty minutes. This gradient elution profile enables the sequential elution of low-, medium-, and high-volatility compounds, ensuring that even the least volatile VOCs are efficiently transported through the column and detected. The ion mobility spectrometer component of the system features a 9.8-centimeter-long drift tube maintained at a temperature of 60 degrees Celsius, with the drift gas—also ultra-high-purity nitrogen—flowing at a constant rate of 150 milliliters per minute. The drift voltage is set at 5 kilovolts, and ionization is performed in positive ion mode using a tritium (³H) source, which generates stable, reproducible ionization of organic molecules without inducing fragmentation. The entire system is calibrated prior to each analytical batch using a mixture of C4 to C9 n-ketones obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Beijing Co., Ltd., to determine retention indices (RIs) and drift times (DTs) for known standards, which are then matched against reference spectra in the NIST 2014 library and the proprietary GC–IMS database maintained by G.A.S. Dortmund. This dual-reference system ensures unambiguous identification of VOCs based on both chromatographic retention and ion mobility behavior, which together provide a two-dimensional identifier that is far more specific than either parameter alone.

The raw data generated by the GC–IMS instrument consists of a two-dimensional matrix of signal intensity as a function of retention time and drift time, which is processed using the Reporter and Gallery Plot plug-ins provided by the manufacturer. These software tools generate a visual fingerprint of the VOC profile, where each peak corresponds to a specific volatile compound and its relative abundance is represented by peak height or area. The fingerprint is unique to the strain of donkey from which the sample was derived, and even minor variations in VOC concentration produce detectable differences in the spatial distribution of peaks. For example, in samples derived from WuTou donkeys, distinct peaks corresponding to hexanal-d, pentanal-d, oct-1-en-3-ol, and 3-octenal are consistently more intense and broader than those observed in SanFen samples, whereas SanFen samples exhibit elevated signals for ethanol, isopropyl alcohol, acetone, and 2-pentanone-m. These differences are not random but are statistically significant and reproducible across multiple biological replicates, indicating a genetic or metabolic basis for the variation. The fingerprint is then subjected to multivariate statistical analysis using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 online software, which performs principal component analysis (PCA) to reduce dimensionality and visualize the overall clustering of samples based on their VOC profiles. PCA reveals clear separation between SanFen and WuTou samples along the first principal component, indicating that the majority of variance in the dataset is attributable to strain origin rather than individual variation, sex, or other confounding factors. This separation is further confirmed by partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), which constructs a supervised model to maximize the separation between predefined classes, and orthogonal PLS-DA (OPLS-DA), which removes variation unrelated to class separation, thereby enhancing the interpretability of the model. The OPLS-DA model demonstrates exceptional performance, with a Q² value of 0.71 and an intercept of −0.26, indicating that the model is robust, predictive, and free from overfitting, as confirmed by permutation testing in which all permuted Q² values fall below the original model’s Q², thereby validating its statistical integrity.

To identify the specific VOCs responsible for the observed discrimination, variable importance in projection (VIP) scores are calculated for each compound in the OPLS-DA model. Compounds with a VIP score greater than 1.0 and a p-value less than 0.05, as determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test, are classified as differential markers. A total of seventeen such VOCs are identified, comprising seven alcohols, seven aldehydes, one heterocycle, and two ketones. Of these, ten compounds are significantly upregulated in SanFen donkey meat relative to WuTou, including 2-butanol, 2-methyl-1-propanol, benzaldehyde, ethanol, isopropyl alcohol, acetone, 2-pentanone-m, and three unidentified compounds. The remaining seven compounds are significantly upregulated in WuTou meat, including tetrahydrofurane, hexanal-m, pentan-1-ol-d, pentan-1-ol-m, 3-octenal, oct-1-en-3-ol, and pentanal-d. These compounds are not merely correlated with strain origin—they are causally linked to the underlying metabolic pathways of lipid oxidation and enzymatic degradation that differ between the two strains. For instance, the elevated levels of aldehydes such as hexanal and pentanal in WuTou meat suggest a higher rate of omega-6 fatty acid oxidation, while the increased abundance of alcohols and ketones in SanFen meat indicates greater activity of alcohol dehydrogenase and ketone-forming enzymes, possibly due to differences in muscle fiber composition, mitochondrial density, or endogenous antioxidant capacity. The presence of tetrahydrofurane, a cyclic ether formed from the cyclization of hydroperoxides, further supports the hypothesis that lipid peroxidation pathways differ between strains, with WuTou meat exhibiting a greater propensity for specific oxidative routes. The identification of these seventeen VOCs as diagnostic markers establishes a biochemical fingerprint that is both unique and stable, capable of distinguishing between strains with a classification accuracy exceeding 98% in blinded validation trials.

The invention further encompasses the creation of a reference database containing the retention indices, drift times, relative abundances, and VIP scores of these seventeen VOCs, along with their chemical structures, molecular weights, and CAS registry numbers. This database serves as a standardized reference for automated classification systems and may be integrated into cloud-based authentication platforms accessible to slaughterhouses, regulatory agencies, and quality control laboratories. When an unknown sample is analyzed using the method described herein, its GC–IMS fingerprint is compared against the reference database using a pattern-matching algorithm that calculates a similarity score based on the presence, absence, and relative intensity of the seventeen marker compounds. If the similarity score exceeds a predetermined threshold, the sample is classified as belonging to the corresponding strain. The algorithm can be trained on additional strains as new data becomes available, allowing the system to expand its discriminatory capacity without requiring re-engineering of the analytical protocol. The method is also adaptable to different sample sizes, incubation times, and instrumental configurations, provided that the relative relationships between the VOC markers are preserved. For example, if a different capillary column is used, the retention indices may shift, but the relative ranking of the VOCs by VIP score and the pattern of up- or down-regulation between strains remains consistent, allowing for recalibration without loss of discriminatory power.

The invention further includes a protocol for quality assurance and validation, wherein each analytical batch includes a set of positive controls (samples of known strain origin), negative controls (samples from unrelated species or processed meat), and blanks (headspace vials containing no tissue). The system is validated for precision, accuracy, specificity, and reproducibility according to international guidelines for analytical method validation. The intra-day and inter-day coefficients of variation for the seventeen marker VOCs are all below 5%, indicating exceptional repeatability. The limit of detection for the most abundant VOCs is below 10 parts per billion, and the limit of quantification is below 50 parts per billion, ensuring that even trace-level differences are reliably detected. The method is also shown to be unaffected by variations in animal sex, age (within the 2-year range studied), or minor differences in diet, as long as the animals are raised under comparable conditions, thereby confirming that the VOC profile is primarily determined by genetic strain rather than environmental factors. The invention thus provides not merely a method of analysis but a complete, legally defensible, and commercially deployable system for the authentication of donkey meat, capable of preventing fraud, ensuring transparency, and supporting the economic value of authentic, strain-specific products in global markets. The method is scalable, cost-effective, and requires minimal training for operators, making it suitable for implementation in both centralized laboratories and decentralized field settings, thereby revolutionizing the way meat authenticity is verified in the 21st century.
