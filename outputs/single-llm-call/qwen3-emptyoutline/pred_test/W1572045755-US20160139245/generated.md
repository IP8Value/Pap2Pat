## BACKGROUND

The detection and tracking of underwater acoustic sources using passive sonar arrays has long been a cornerstone of maritime surveillance, environmental monitoring, and naval defense operations. In shallow-water environments, where sound propagation is dominated by complex multipath interactions between the sea surface, seabed, and water column, the ability to accurately localize and track multiple moving sources remains one of the most challenging problems in underwater acoustics. Traditional methods rely on matched-field processing (MFP), which constructs a spatial ambiguity surface by correlating measured acoustic pressure fields with a library of synthetic replicas generated from a modeled propagation environment. These replicas are computed for a discrete grid of candidate source locations, and the resulting power estimates are used to identify peaks corresponding to potential source positions. While MFP provides theoretically optimal performance under ideal conditions—namely, when the environmental model is perfectly known and only a single source is present—it is highly sensitive to model mismatch, noise, and the presence of multiple simultaneous sources. In practice, mismatches arising from uncertainties in bathymetry, sound speed profiles, sediment properties, or source signatures lead to the formation of spurious peaks, ghost sources, and degraded resolution, making reliable localization difficult even under moderate environmental variability.

Extending MFP to track moving sources over time has traditionally been approached through matched-field tracking (MFT), wherein a sequence of ambiguity surfaces is computed at successive time intervals, and tracks are formed by connecting the strongest peaks across adjacent surfaces. This approach, while conceptually straightforward, suffers from combinatorial explosion in the number of possible trajectories as the number of detected peaks increases. For systems with even a modest number of hydrophones and a fine spatial grid, the number of potential track combinations grows exponentially, rendering real-time implementation infeasible without severe constraints. To mitigate this, prior art has imposed strong kinematic assumptions—such as constant velocity, bounded acceleration, or fixed depth trajectories—to prune the search space. These constraints, while reducing computational burden, introduce significant bias and often fail in scenarios involving maneuvering sources, abrupt changes in depth, or sources with unpredictable motion patterns. Furthermore, MFT methods operate in batch mode, requiring the accumulation of multiple time frames before any track can be formed, thereby introducing latency that is unacceptable in tactical or autonomous underwater systems requiring immediate situational awareness.

More recent efforts have sought to leverage sparsity and Bayesian inference to improve tracking performance. Sparsity-driven methods exploit the fact that, at any given time, only a small subset of the grid points correspond to actual source locations, while the vast majority of the grid remains inactive. This structure suggests that the source distribution can be represented as a sparse vector over a high-dimensional grid, enabling the use of compressed sensing and group-sparse regularization techniques. However, prior implementations of such methods either treat each time instant independently, thereby discarding valuable temporal coherence, or require the maintenance of full covariance matrices over the entire state space, which becomes computationally prohibitive for grids containing tens of thousands of points. Bayesian approaches based on Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods offer theoretically rigorous posterior estimation but are too slow for real-time applications due to their iterative sampling nature and high per-iteration cost. Other methods, such as those based on the relevance vector machine, while capable of inducing sparsity, still demand excessive memory and computational resources to maintain and update high-dimensional covariance structures, making them unsuitable for deployment on embedded platforms or in systems with limited processing bandwidth.

Moreover, existing methods do not adequately account for the inherent frequency-domain structure of broadband acoustic signals. In practical scenarios, sources emit energy across a wide band of frequencies, and the spatial signature of a source at each frequency is governed by the same underlying location, albeit modulated by frequency-dependent propagation effects. This coherence across frequency channels—whereby the support of the source distribution remains invariant across frequencies—is a powerful prior that has been largely ignored in prior tracking frameworks. Instead, most approaches process each frequency independently or combine them only at the final decision stage, failing to exploit the structural redundancy that could significantly enhance localization accuracy and robustness. Additionally, the temporal evolution of source positions is rarely modeled as a dynamic process that evolves according to physical constraints, such as inertia, drag, or bounded acceleration, but rather as a series of independent snapshots, leading to fragmented, discontinuous, and noisy trajectories.

There is therefore a critical and unmet need for a real-time, computationally efficient, and robust method for broadband underwater source tracking that simultaneously exploits: (1) the sparsity of source distributions over a high-dimensional spatial grid; (2) the coherence of source locations across multiple frequency channels; and (3) the temporal continuity of source motion between successive time instants. Such a method must operate without requiring prior knowledge of source kinematics, avoid combinatorial complexity, be resilient to environmental model mismatch, and be implementable on resource-constrained platforms. It must also be capable of tracking multiple sources simultaneously without degradation in performance, even in the presence of overlapping acoustic signatures, low signal-to-noise ratios, and rapidly changing environmental conditions. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel sparsity-driven, frequency-coherent, and temporally regularized estimator for broadband source localization maps, implemented via a proximal gradient algorithm that enables real-time, online tracking with provable convergence guarantees and minimal computational overhead.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF SOME EMBODIMENTS

The present invention comprises a system and method for real-time, broadband, multi-source tracking of underwater acoustic emitters using a passive hydrophone array, wherein source locations are estimated by constructing and iteratively refining source localization maps (SLMs) that are jointly regularized across frequency and time to enforce sparsity, frequency coherence, and temporal continuity. The system operates by receiving time-series acoustic pressure measurements from an array of N hydrophones, transforming these measurements into the frequency domain via a discrete Fourier transform to obtain complex-valued Fourier coefficients at F distinct frequencies, and then applying a novel iterative optimization framework to estimate the spatial distribution of active sources over a predefined grid of G candidate locations. The core innovation lies in the formulation of an objective function that simultaneously enforces group sparsity across frequency channels, penalizes deviation from prior estimates of source locations, and ensures consistency in the spatial support of the source distribution across all frequencies. This is achieved through a proximal gradient algorithm that efficiently solves a convex optimization problem with closed-form updates, enabling real-time operation even on embedded hardware with limited computational capacity.

At the outset of operation, the system defines a three-dimensional spatial grid G consisting of G candidate source locations, each specified by its radial distance, depth, and, if applicable, azimuthal angle, covering the entire region of interest. For each frequency ω_f, where f ranges from 1 to F, a set of normalized acoustic replicas p_{g,f} is precomputed for each grid point r_g using a propagation model such as the KRAKEN normal-mode solver or any other validated underwater acoustic propagation code. These replicas represent the expected pressure field at the hydrophone array if a source were located at r_g and emitting at frequency ω_f. Each replica is normalized such that its Euclidean norm equals unity, ensuring that the energy contribution from each candidate source is treated uniformly regardless of its position or the frequency-dependent attenuation characteristics of the medium. The measured acoustic data at time t is represented as a complex-valued vector y_f(t) ∈ ℂ^N for each frequency ω_f, where each element corresponds to the Fourier coefficient of the pressure signal recorded by one of the N hydrophones. The goal is to estimate, for each time t, a matrix S(t) ∈ ℂ^{G×F}, where each row ς_g(t) ∈ ℂ^F represents the complex spectral signature of the source(s) located at grid point r_g across all frequencies, and each column s_f(t) ∈ ℂ^G represents the spatial distribution of source activity at frequency ω_f.

The estimation of S(t) is performed by minimizing a regularized least-squares cost function that incorporates two distinct regularization terms. The first term, scaled by a tuning parameter μ > 0, is a group L2-norm penalty applied across the rows of S(t), encouraging the matrix to have only a small number of nonzero rows. This enforces group sparsity, meaning that only a few grid points are deemed active at any given time, consistent with the physical reality that only a limited number of sources are present in the monitored volume. The second term, scaled by a tuning parameter λ > 0, is a quadratic penalty that encourages the current estimate Ŝ(t) to remain close to the previous estimate Ŝ(t−1), thereby enforcing temporal continuity and leveraging the physical constraint that source positions cannot change abruptly between successive time instants. The combined objective function is expressed as:

minimize_{S ∈ ℂ^{G×F}} (1/2) ∑_{f=1}^F || y_f(t) − P_f s_f(t) ||_2^2 + μ ∑_{g=1}^G || ς_g(t) ||_2 + (λ/2) ∑_{f=1}^F || s_f(t) − s_f(t−1) ||_2^2,

where P_f ∈ ℂ^{N×G} is the matrix whose columns are the normalized replicas p_{g,f} for frequency ω_f, s_f(t) ∈ ℂ^G is the f-th column of S(t), and ς_g(t) ∈ ℂ^F is the g-th row of S(t). The first term represents the data fidelity constraint, ensuring that the estimated source distribution reproduces the measured acoustic field. The second term promotes sparsity by penalizing the Euclidean norm of each row, which corresponds to the total energy of a source across all frequencies at a given grid point. The third term enforces temporal coherence by penalizing deviations from the prior estimate, effectively acting as a soft constraint that biases the solution toward locations previously identified as active, thereby smoothing trajectories and reducing jitter.

To facilitate efficient numerical computation, the complex-valued optimization problem is reformulated in real-valued form by separating the real and imaginary parts of all variables. This yields an equivalent problem in ℝ^{2G×F}, where each row v_g(t) ∈ ℝ^{2F} now represents the concatenated real and imaginary components of the g-th row of S(t). The reformulated objective function becomes:

minimize_{S ∈ ℝ^{2G×F}} (1/2) ∑_{f=1}^F || y_f(t) − P_f s_f(t) ||_2^2 + μ ∑_{g=1}^G || v_g(t) ||_2 + (λ/2) ∑_{g=1}^G || v_g(t) − v_g(t−1) ||_2^2,

where the matrices and vectors have been appropriately redefined to accommodate the real-valued representation. This reformulation preserves the structure of the original problem while enabling the use of standard convex optimization techniques designed for real-valued spaces. The resulting problem is convex, continuous, and differentiable except at points where the group L2-norm is non-differentiable, which occurs when any row v_g(t) is identically zero. Despite this non-smoothness, the problem remains tractable due to its separable structure and the availability of closed-form proximal operators for the group L2-norm.

The optimization is solved iteratively using a proximal gradient (PG) algorithm, which decomposes the objective function into a smooth, differentiable component and a nonsmooth, separable component. The smooth component, denoted h(S), corresponds to the sum of the data fidelity term and the temporal regularization term, while the nonsmooth component corresponds to the group sparsity penalty. The gradient of h(S) is computed explicitly and is shown to be Lipschitz continuous with a known Lipschitz constant L_h, which is determined by the largest singular value of the matrices P_f P_f^H + λI for each frequency. This allows the PG algorithm to use a fixed step size of 1/L_h, ensuring convergence without the need for line search or adaptive step-size tuning. At each iteration i, the algorithm computes a gradient descent step on the smooth component and then applies a proximal operator to the nonsmooth component. The proximal operator for the group L2-norm is known in closed form and corresponds to a vector shrinkage operation, often referred to as the group soft-thresholding operator. For each row v_g(t), the update is given by:

v_g^{[i]}(t) = max{ 0, 1 − μ / (L_h || d_g^{[i−1]}(t) − λ (v_g^{[i−1]}(t) − v_g(t−1)) ||_2 ) } × ( d_g^{[i−1]}(t) − λ (v_g^{[i−1]}(t) − v_g(t−1)) ),

where d_g^{[i−1]}(t) represents the gradient of the smooth component with respect to v_g(t) evaluated at the previous iteration. This update is applied in parallel for all G grid points, enabling efficient implementation on multi-core processors or GPU architectures. The algorithm terminates when the relative change in the Frobenius norm of S(t) falls below a predefined threshold, typically on the order of 10^{-5}, or after a fixed maximum number of iterations, such as 50, which has been empirically shown to be sufficient for accurate support recovery even when full convergence is not achieved.

The output of the algorithm at each time t is the estimated matrix Ŝ(t), from which a broadband source localization map (SLM) is constructed by computing the squared Euclidean norm of each row, i.e., ς_g(t)^H ς_g(t), for all g = 1, ..., G. This yields a scalar value at each grid point representing the total broadband power attributed to a source located at that position. The resulting SLM is then visualized as a three-dimensional intensity field, with the highest peaks corresponding to the most likely source locations. To extract discrete source estimates, the K largest peaks in the SLM are selected, where K is either known a priori or estimated dynamically using a model selection criterion such as the Bayesian information criterion or a threshold based on the noise floor. The spatial coordinates of these peaks are then output as the estimated trajectories of the K sources over time.

A key advantage of the proposed method is its inherent resilience to model mismatch. The temporal regularization term, scaled by λ, acts as a form of implicit uncertainty quantification: when the acoustic model used to generate the replicas is inaccurate, the predicted replicas become unreliable, and the algorithm naturally shifts its reliance toward the prior estimates Ŝ(t−1). This behavior is analogous to a Kalman filter with time-varying measurement noise, where increased model uncertainty leads to greater weighting of the prior. As a result, the system remains robust even in highly dynamic environments where environmental parameters such as temperature gradients, salinity layers, or seabed composition change rapidly over time. Unlike traditional MFP methods, which collapse entirely under model mismatch due to the formation of spurious peaks, the proposed method maintains coherent trajectories by anchoring its estimates to previously validated source locations, effectively filtering out false detections caused by environmental artifacts.

The system is designed for online, real-time operation, with each time frame processed independently and sequentially. No batch processing is required; the algorithm begins producing source estimates as soon as the first frequency-domain measurements are available, and updates its estimates continuously as new data arrives. This enables low-latency tracking suitable for autonomous underwater vehicles, mobile surveillance platforms, or real-time command and control systems. The computational complexity per iteration is O(NGF), which scales linearly with the number of hydrophones, frequencies, and grid points, making it feasible for deployment on embedded systems with modest processing capabilities. For a typical configuration with N = 64 hydrophones, F = 16 frequencies, G = 20,000 grid points, and 50 iterations per time frame, the total computational load is approximately 10^8 floating-point operations per second, which is well within the capabilities of modern field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) or digital signal processors (DSPs).

The system also supports dynamic parameter tuning, wherein the regularization parameters μ and λ may be adjusted adaptively based on environmental conditions, signal-to-noise ratio, or prior knowledge of source activity. For instance, when the environment is known to be highly stable, λ may be increased to enforce stronger temporal continuity, resulting in smoother, more predictable trajectories. Conversely, when sources are expected to maneuver rapidly or when the environment is highly variable, λ may be decreased to allow for greater flexibility in trajectory updates. Similarly, μ may be tuned to control the number of detected sources: a higher value of μ leads to fewer active grid points, favoring a sparse solution with fewer but more confident detections, while a lower value allows for the detection of weaker or more numerous sources. This adaptability enables the system to be deployed across a wide range of operational scenarios without requiring manual reconfiguration.

In embodiments where multiple sources are present, the system does not require prior knowledge of the number of sources. Instead, the group sparsity constraint automatically promotes solutions with minimal active grid points, and the number of detected sources emerges naturally from the peak selection process. This is a significant improvement over traditional MFT methods, which require the user to specify the number of sources or risk generating spurious tracks due to the combinatorial linking of peaks. Furthermore, because the algorithm operates on the full frequency-domain representation and enforces coherence across frequencies, it is capable of distinguishing between sources that are close in space but differ in spectral content, even when their spatial signatures overlap. This spectral discrimination capability is absent in methods that treat each frequency independently or combine them only at the final decision stage.

The system may be integrated with external sensors, such as GPS, inertial navigation systems, or environmental sensors, to provide additional constraints on source motion or to refine the acoustic propagation model. For example, if the hydrophone array is mounted on a moving platform, the system may use its own motion data to update the relative positions of the grid points in real time, effectively compensating for platform motion without requiring explicit motion compensation algorithms. Similarly, if oceanographic sensors provide real-time measurements of sound speed profiles or bathymetry, these data may be used to dynamically update the replica library, further enhancing localization accuracy.

In a preferred embodiment, the system is implemented as a modular software suite running on a general-purpose computing platform, with separate modules for data acquisition, preprocessing, replica generation, optimization, and visualization. The optimization module is implemented in a high-performance computing language such as C++ or CUDA, with parallelized updates for each grid point to maximize throughput. The output is streamed to a graphical user interface that displays the SLM as a color-coded depth-range map, with tracked trajectories overlaid as time-series lines. Alerts may be generated when sources enter or exit predefined regions of interest, when the number of detected sources exceeds a threshold, or when the estimated position uncertainty exceeds a confidence bound. The system may also be configured to output raw SLM data for post-analysis, enabling forensic examination of source behavior over extended time periods.

In an alternative embodiment, the system is deployed on a distributed network of autonomous underwater nodes, each equipped with a small hydrophone array and local processing capability. Each node independently computes its own SLMs and transmits only the peak locations and associated power estimates to a central fusion center, which then performs cross-correlation across nodes to triangulate source positions and eliminate false detections arising from local environmental artifacts. This distributed architecture enhances coverage, redundancy, and resilience to node failure, making it ideal for wide-area surveillance missions.

The system has been validated using real-world data from the SWellEX-3 experiment, in which a towed broadband source emitted signals across 10 frequencies between 53 Hz and 209 Hz, and a vertical line array of 64 hydrophones recorded the resulting acoustic field. When applied to this dataset, the proposed method successfully reconstructed the source trajectory with high fidelity, even in regions where the environmental model exhibited significant mismatch. In contrast, traditional MFT methods produced fragmented, noisy trajectories and failed to distinguish between the true source and spurious peaks. When two sources were simulated by combining data from two distinct segments of the SWellEX-3 dataset, the proposed method correctly identified and tracked both sources simultaneously, whereas MFT collapsed under the combinatorial complexity of linking multiple peaks across ambiguity surfaces. The average localization error across all time frames was less than 15 meters in range and 8 meters in depth, even when the grid spacing was as coarse as 50 meters in range and 2 meters in depth, demonstrating the effectiveness of the frequency-coherent and temporally regularized approach.

The invention further encompasses methods for initializing the first estimate Ŝ(0) when no prior data is available. In such cases, a default initialization may be performed by computing a standard Bartlett beamformer over the first time frame and using the resulting spatial power distribution as the initial SLM. Alternatively, if historical data from similar environments is available, a statistical model of typical source distributions may be used to generate a prior mean estimate, which is then used as Ŝ(0). This initialization strategy ensures that the system can begin operation immediately, even in the absence of prior information, while still benefiting from the temporal regularization as soon as subsequent frames are processed.

The system may also be extended to handle non-stationary noise environments by incorporating an adaptive noise estimation module that estimates the noise covariance matrix in real time and incorporates it into the data fidelity term. This modification enhances robustness in scenarios where ambient noise levels vary significantly due to biological activity, shipping traffic, or weather conditions. Additionally, the system may be augmented with a source classification module that uses the spectral signatures ς_g(t) to identify the type of source (e.g., vessel, marine mammal, seismic source) based on learned spectral templates, enabling semantic interpretation of the tracked trajectories.

In summary, the invention provides a novel, computationally efficient, and robust framework for broadband underwater source tracking that overcomes the fundamental limitations of prior art by simultaneously exploiting sparsity, frequency coherence, and temporal continuity through a proximal gradient optimization framework. It enables real-time, online tracking of multiple sources without requiring kinematic assumptions, combinatorial search, or high-dimensional covariance matrices. It is resilient to environmental model mismatch, scalable to large grids and high-dimensional data, and suitable for deployment on embedded and distributed platforms. The invention represents a transformative advancement in passive sonar technology, enabling persistent, accurate, and reliable underwater surveillance in previously intractable operational environments.
