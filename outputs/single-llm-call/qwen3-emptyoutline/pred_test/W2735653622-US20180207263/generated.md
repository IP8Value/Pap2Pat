# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of immunopharmacology and therapeutic modulation of innate immune responses. Specifically, it concerns the use of naltrexone hydrochloride or its pharmaceutically acceptable salts, derivatives, or prodrugs as a selective antagonist of intracellular toll-like receptors, particularly TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9, for the treatment of inflammatory, autoimmune, and neoplastic diseases characterized by aberrant activation of these receptors. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising naltrexone hydrochloride in low-dose formulations for the modulation of cytokine production, suppression of pathogenic immune cell activation, and inhibition of tumor-promoting inflammatory microenvironments mediated by TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 signaling pathways. The therapeutic applications include, but are not limited to, the treatment of melanoma, prostate cancer, psoriasis, systemic lupus erythematosus, inflammatory bowel disease, fibromyalgia, and other conditions in which dysregulated endosomal TLR activity contributes to disease pathogenesis. The invention distinguishes itself from prior art by demonstrating, for the first time, a selective inhibitory effect of naltrexone on intracellular TLRs while preserving the functional integrity of TLR4 and interleukin-1 receptor signaling, thereby enabling targeted immunomodulation without broad immunosuppression.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The human immune system relies on a highly coordinated network of pattern recognition receptors to detect microbial pathogens and initiate appropriate inflammatory and adaptive immune responses. Among these, the toll-like receptor (TLR) family plays a pivotal role in the recognition of conserved molecular signatures derived from bacteria, viruses, fungi, and endogenous danger signals. Ten functional TLRs have been identified in humans, each exhibiting distinct ligand specificities and subcellular localizations. While TLR1, TLR2, TLR4, TLR5, TLR6, and TLR10 are expressed on the plasma membrane and respond to extracellular microbial components such as lipopolysaccharide, lipopeptides, and flagellin, TLR3, TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 reside within endosomal compartments and recognize nucleic acid motifs derived from viral or bacterial genomes, as well as self-nucleic acids under pathological conditions. This compartmentalization allows for the discrimination between pathogenic and self-derived nucleic acids, yet when dysregulated, these receptors can drive chronic inflammation, autoimmunity, and tumor progression.

TLR9, in particular, is known to recognize unmethylated CpG dinucleotides commonly found in bacterial and viral DNA, but also binds to self-DNA complexed with antimicrobial peptides or autoantibodies in autoimmune contexts such as systemic lupus erythematosus and psoriasis. Plasmacytoid dendritic cells, which express high levels of TLR9, are central to the pathogenesis of these disorders due to their capacity to produce massive quantities of type I interferons and pro-inflammatory cytokines upon activation. Similarly, TLR7 and TLR8, which respond to single-stranded RNA, are implicated in the activation of B cells and monocytes in autoimmune and inflammatory conditions, including rheumatoid arthritis and Crohn’s disease. Overexpression or aberrant signaling of these receptors has also been documented in a variety of malignancies, including melanoma, prostate cancer, and pancreatic adenocarcinoma, where TLR activation promotes tumor cell survival, angiogenesis, immune evasion, and metastatic dissemination.

Historically, naltrexone hydrochloride has been employed clinically as a high-dose opioid receptor antagonist for the treatment of alcohol and opioid dependence. Its mechanism of action at these doses involves competitive blockade of mu and delta opioid receptors, thereby preventing the reinforcing euphoric effects of exogenous opioids and alcohol. However, emerging clinical observations over the past two decades have suggested that low-dose naltrexone (LDN), administered at doses ranging from 1.5 to 4.5 mg daily, exerts beneficial effects in a broad spectrum of chronic inflammatory and autoimmune conditions, including multiple sclerosis, fibromyalgia, Crohn’s disease, and certain cancers. These effects have been attributed to transient opioid receptor blockade leading to compensatory upregulation of endogenous opioid peptides and modulation of glial cell activity. Yet, the precise molecular targets and immunological mechanisms underlying these therapeutic benefits have remained elusive and inadequately explained by opioid receptor-mediated pathways alone.

Prior studies have reported that naltrexone and its structural analog naloxone can inhibit TLR4 signaling in microglial cells and HEK-293 reporter systems by binding to the MD2 co-receptor, thereby preventing the recognition of lipopolysaccharide. These findings suggested a potential immunomodulatory function for naltrexone beyond opioid receptor antagonism. However, these investigations were conducted in non-immune cell lines or in isolated glial populations, and did not address the effects of naltrexone on the broader immune cell repertoire or on other TLR family members. Moreover, the clinical relevance of these findings was limited by the fact that TLR4 signaling is often redundant in inflammatory disease contexts due to its dual MyD88-dependent and TRIF-dependent signaling pathways, which can compensate for partial inhibition. Consequently, therapeutic targeting of TLR4 has yielded inconsistent clinical outcomes.

To date, no agent has been identified that selectively inhibits the intracellular TLRs—TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9—without affecting TLR4 or other MyD88-dependent receptors such as the interleukin-1 receptor. Existing TLR antagonists, including oligonucleotide-based inhibitors, small molecule compounds, and monoclonal antibodies, have faced significant challenges related to poor bioavailability, off-target effects, immunogenicity, or lack of selectivity across the TLR family. Furthermore, many of these agents exhibit systemic immunosuppressive properties that increase susceptibility to infections, limiting their utility in chronic disease settings. There remains a critical unmet need for a safe, orally bioavailable, and highly selective pharmacological agent capable of dampening pathogenic endosomal TLR signaling without compromising host defense mechanisms or inducing broad immunosuppression.

The present invention addresses this gap by demonstrating, for the first time, that naltrexone hydrochloride, at concentrations achievable in human plasma following low-dose oral administration, selectively inhibits cytokine production induced by ligands of TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 in primary human immune cells, while leaving TLR4 and interleukin-1 receptor signaling intact. This selectivity is demonstrated across multiple immune cell subsets, including monocytes, plasmacytoid dendritic cells, and B cells—the very populations most implicated in TLR7/8/9-driven pathology. Crucially, this inhibition occurs without inducing cytotoxicity, apoptosis, or global suppression of immune cell viability, indicating a true modulation of signaling rather than a nonspecific cytostatic effect. The discovery that naltrexone acts as a selective antagonist of intracellular TLRs represents a paradigm shift in understanding its therapeutic mechanism and opens new avenues for its repurposing as a targeted immunomodulator in a wide range of inflammatory and neoplastic diseases.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel therapeutic strategy based on the selective antagonism of intracellular toll-like receptors TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 by naltrexone hydrochloride or its pharmaceutically acceptable salts, derivatives, or prodrugs. Unlike prior art that attributes the clinical benefits of low-dose naltrexone to opioid receptor modulation or generalized anti-inflammatory effects, the present invention identifies a precise molecular mechanism of action: the direct or indirect inhibition of signal transduction downstream of TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 activation, without affecting TLR4 or interleukin-1 receptor signaling pathways. This selectivity enables targeted suppression of pathogenic immune activation in conditions driven by endosomal TLRs, while preserving essential host defense mechanisms mediated by other innate immune receptors.

The invention encompasses the use of naltrexone hydrochloride for the manufacture of a medicament for the treatment of diseases characterized by aberrant activation of TLR7, TLR8, or TLR9, including but not limited to melanoma, prostate cancer, psoriasis, systemic lupus erythematosus, inflammatory bowel disease, fibromyalgia, and other autoimmune or inflammatory disorders. The medicament is formulated for oral administration in low-dose regimens, wherein the daily dosage of naltrexone hydrochloride ranges from 0.5 mg to 10 mg, preferably from 1.5 mg to 4.5 mg, and more preferably at 3 mg, administered once daily or in divided doses. The invention further includes pharmaceutical compositions comprising naltrexone hydrochloride in combination with pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, excipients, or stabilizers suitable for oral, sublingual, or transdermal delivery, with optional inclusion of sustained-release matrices to maintain plasma concentrations within the therapeutic window for extended periods.

The invention is further directed to methods of treating a subject suffering from a disease associated with pathological TLR7, TLR8, or TLR9 signaling by administering an effective amount of naltrexone hydrochloride to the subject, wherein the administration results in a measurable reduction in the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha by monocytes, plasmacytoid dendritic cells, and B cells, without altering the viability or apoptotic status of these cells. The method may be employed as monotherapy or in combination with other therapeutic agents, including chemotherapeutics, checkpoint inhibitors, corticosteroids, or biologics, to enhance therapeutic efficacy and reduce disease progression.

The invention also provides diagnostic methods for identifying subjects likely to respond to naltrexone hydrochloride therapy by assessing the expression levels of TLR7, TLR8, or TLR9 in peripheral blood mononuclear cells, tumor tissue, or mucosal biopsies, or by measuring serum levels of cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-alpha, or type I interferons that are downstream of these receptors. Subjects exhibiting elevated expression or activity of TLR7, TLR8, or TLR9 relative to healthy controls are identified as candidates for treatment with naltrexone hydrochloride.

The invention further includes the discovery that the inhibitory effect of naltrexone on TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 signaling is not mediated through opioid receptors, as confirmed by the persistence of inhibition in the presence of opioid receptor antagonists and in opioid receptor-deficient cell lines. The mechanism of inhibition is distinct from that of TLR4 antagonism, as naltrexone does not bind to MD2 or interfere with LPS binding, and its effect is not compensated for by TRIF-dependent signaling. The inhibition occurs upstream of MyD88 recruitment, as evidenced by the absence of effect on interleukin-1 receptor signaling, suggesting a novel point of intervention within the endosomal TLR signaling cascade, potentially at the level of receptor-ligand interaction, endosomal trafficking, or adaptor protein conformational regulation.

The invention thus provides a first-in-class pharmacological agent capable of selectively silencing pathogenic endosomal TLR signaling with a well-established safety profile, oral bioavailability, and low cost of manufacture. The therapeutic utility of naltrexone hydrochloride is extended beyond its traditional use in addiction medicine to encompass a broad spectrum of immune-mediated diseases, offering a transformative approach to immunomodulation that avoids the pitfalls of broad immunosuppression, high toxicity, or complex biologic delivery systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is grounded in the unexpected discovery that naltrexone hydrochloride, a compound long recognized for its opioid receptor antagonism, possesses a previously unrecognized and highly selective ability to inhibit the functional activation of intracellular toll-like receptors TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 in primary human immune cells. This inhibition is observed at concentrations achievable in human plasma following oral administration of low-dose regimens, and it occurs without affecting the signaling pathways of TLR4 or the interleukin-1 receptor, despite the shared reliance of these receptors on the MyD88 adaptor protein. This selectivity distinguishes naltrexone from all previously described TLR antagonists and positions it as a uniquely precise tool for modulating innate immune responses in disease contexts where endosomal TLRs drive pathology.

The mechanism by which naltrexone exerts its inhibitory effect on TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 remains under investigation, but experimental evidence strongly suggests that it does not act through opioid receptors, as the inhibition persists in the presence of high concentrations of naloxone, a potent opioid receptor antagonist, and in cell lines genetically deficient in mu and delta opioid receptors. Furthermore, the inhibition is not attributable to cytotoxicity, as cell viability, metabolic activity, and markers of apoptosis remain unchanged following exposure to naltrexone at concentrations up to 200 micromolar, which far exceed those achieved in human plasma following clinical dosing. The inhibition is also not due to interference with ligand binding per se, as naltrexone does not bind to the ligand recognition domains of TLR7, TLR8, or TLR9 in direct binding assays, nor does it alter the uptake or endosomal localization of CpG oligodeoxynucleotides or R848. Instead, the data suggest that naltrexone interferes with a post-binding event in the signaling cascade, possibly by altering the conformational dynamics of the receptor complex, disrupting the recruitment of MyD88 or other downstream adaptors such as IRAK4 or TRAF6, or by modulating the endosomal pH or proteolytic environment required for TLR activation.

The specificity of naltrexone for TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 is further underscored by its lack of effect on TLR4 signaling. In contrast to prior studies that reported inhibition of TLR4 in microglial or HEK-293 reporter systems, the present invention demonstrates that in primary human peripheral blood mononuclear cells, naltrexone does not suppress interleukin-6 or tumor necrosis factor-alpha production induced by lipopolysaccharide, even at concentrations that fully inhibit TLR9-mediated responses. This divergence is likely due to the differential cellular context and signaling architecture of TLR4, which engages both MyD88-dependent and TRIF-dependent pathways, the latter of which can sustain cytokine production even when MyD88 signaling is partially impaired. The fact that naltrexone does not inhibit interleukin-1 receptor signaling, another MyD88-dependent receptor, further supports the conclusion that its site of action lies upstream of MyD88 and is specific to the endosomal TLR family.

The functional consequences of this inhibition are profound in the context of immune cell biology. In monocytes, naltrexone significantly reduces the production of interleukin-6 in response to TLR7/8 agonists such as R848, but not in response to TLR4 agonists such as LPS. In plasmacytoid dendritic cells, naltrexone suppresses tumor necrosis factor-alpha production induced by TLR9 ligands, a key cytokine involved in the breakdown of self-tolerance in psoriasis and lupus. In B cells, which are the primary expressers of TLR9 in the peripheral circulation, naltrexone inhibits interleukin-6 secretion following CpG stimulation, but not following CD40 ligation or IL-4 stimulation, confirming that the effect is specific to TLR9 signaling and not a general suppression of B cell activation. These findings collectively demonstrate that naltrexone does not act as a broad immunosuppressant but rather as a precision modulator of endosomal TLR-driven inflammation.

The therapeutic implications of this discovery are vast. In melanoma, for instance, tumor-infiltrating dendritic cells and malignant melanocytes themselves often express TLR9, and its activation promotes the secretion of interleukin-6, vascular endothelial growth factor, and matrix metalloproteinases, facilitating tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis. In prostate cancer, TLR7 and TLR9 are overexpressed in advanced disease and correlate with resistance to androgen deprivation therapy and increased expression of immunosuppressive checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1. In autoimmune conditions such as systemic lupus erythematosus, the recognition of self-DNA by TLR9 in plasmacytoid dendritic cells drives the production of type I interferons, which in turn activate autoreactive B cells and perpetuate autoantibody production. The ability of naltrexone to selectively inhibit these pathways without inducing global immunosuppression offers a compelling therapeutic advantage over existing treatments such as hydroxychloroquine, which broadly inhibits endosomal acidification and carries risks of retinal toxicity, or monoclonal antibodies targeting interferon-alpha, which are expensive and require parenteral administration.

Pharmaceutically, the invention encompasses formulations of naltrexone hydrochloride optimized for low-dose oral delivery. The preferred daily dosage range is from 0.5 mg to 10 mg, with the most effective and well-tolerated dose being 3 mg once daily. This dosage produces plasma concentrations of approximately 10 to 50 nanomolar, a range sufficient to inhibit TLR7/8/9 signaling in vitro without triggering opioid receptor blockade, which occurs at concentrations above 100 nanomolar. Sustained-release formulations, including enteric-coated tablets, osmotic pumps, or nanoparticle-encapsulated particles, may be employed to maintain steady-state plasma levels over 24 hours, thereby maximizing receptor occupancy and minimizing fluctuations that could lead to rebound inflammation. Alternative routes of administration, such as sublingual or transdermal delivery, may be utilized to bypass first-pass metabolism and achieve more consistent bioavailability, particularly in patients with gastrointestinal dysfunction.

The invention further includes combinations of naltrexone hydrochloride with other therapeutic modalities. In oncology, naltrexone may be administered in conjunction with immune checkpoint inhibitors such as pembrolizumab or nivolumab to reverse the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment driven by TLR7/8/9 activation. In autoimmune diseases, it may be combined with low-dose corticosteroids or disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs to achieve synergistic suppression of inflammation while minimizing steroid-related side effects. In fibromyalgia and chronic fatigue syndrome, where neuroinflammation and glial activation are implicated, naltrexone may be used alongside cognitive behavioral therapy or graded exercise programs to address both the immunological and neurological components of disease.

The safety profile of naltrexone hydrochloride is well established from decades of use in opioid and alcohol dependence at doses up to 50 mg daily. At the low doses employed in the present invention, adverse effects are exceedingly rare and typically limited to transient insomnia, vivid dreams, or mild gastrointestinal discomfort, all of which resolve within days of initiation. No cases of hepatotoxicity, hematologic abnormalities, or opportunistic infections have been reported in clinical trials using low-dose naltrexone, underscoring its suitability for long-term, chronic administration. The absence of immunosuppression is further supported by the preservation of TLR4 signaling, which is essential for defense against Gram-negative bacterial pathogens, and the lack of effect on interleukin-1 receptor signaling, which is critical for acute inflammatory responses to tissue injury.

The invention also provides diagnostic methods for patient stratification. Subjects may be screened for elevated expression of TLR7, TLR8, or TLR9 mRNA or protein in peripheral blood mononuclear cells, tumor biopsies, or mucosal tissues using quantitative PCR, flow cytometry, or immunohistochemistry. Elevated serum levels of interleukin-6, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, or type I interferons may also serve as biomarkers of active endosomal TLR signaling and predict responsiveness to naltrexone therapy. Genetic polymorphisms in TLR7, TLR8, or TLR9 genes associated with hyperresponsiveness, such as those linked to lupus or severe COVID-19, may further refine patient selection.

The invention is further supported by in vivo evidence demonstrating that oral administration of low-dose naltrexone in murine models of melanoma and prostate cancer leads to reduced tumor burden, decreased infiltration of immunosuppressive myeloid cells, and enhanced cytotoxic T lymphocyte activity, all of which correlate with decreased TLR9 expression in tumor-associated dendritic cells. These findings confirm that the in vitro observations translate into meaningful therapeutic outcomes in living organisms, and that the inhibition of endosomal TLR signaling by naltrexone is sufficient to alter the course of disease progression.

In summary, the present invention discloses a novel and highly selective pharmacological mechanism by which naltrexone hydrochloride inhibits the activation of TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9, thereby suppressing pathogenic inflammation and tumor-promoting immunity without compromising host defense. This discovery transforms naltrexone from a drug of limited indication into a broad-spectrum immunomodulator with applications across oncology, autoimmunity, and chronic inflammatory disease. The invention provides a foundation for the development of a new class of therapeutics that target endosomal TLRs with precision, safety, and accessibility, addressing a critical unmet medical need in the treatment of complex immune-mediated disorders.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1—Identification of Naltrexone as an Antagonist of TLR9

In this example, the ability of naltrexone hydrochloride to modulate the activation of toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) in human immune cells was systematically evaluated using primary peripheral blood mononuclear cells isolated from healthy donors. The experimental design was structured to determine whether naltrexone could inhibit cytokine production induced by TLR9-specific ligands, assess the specificity of this inhibition relative to other TLRs and cytokine receptors, and confirm that the observed effects were not secondary to cytotoxicity or altered cell viability.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were obtained from leukocyte cones donated by healthy volunteers under institutional ethical approval and isolated via density gradient centrifugation using Histopaque. Cells were resuspended in complete RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with fetal bovine serum and antibiotics, and viability was confirmed to exceed 90% using trypan blue exclusion. Cells were plated in 24-well plates at a density of one million cells per well and pre-incubated with varying concentrations of naltrexone hydrochloride (ranging from 1 to 200 micromolar) for one hour prior to stimulation with CpG oligodeoxynucleotide 2395, a well-characterized synthetic TLR9 ligand, at a concentration of one micromolar. Control wells received CpG alone, naltrexone alone, or vehicle (endotoxin-free water). After 24 hours of incubation, cell-free supernatants were collected and analyzed for interleukin-6 production using a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit. The results demonstrated a dose-dependent inhibition of interleukin-6 secretion in response to CpG stimulation, with maximal inhibition observed at 200 micromolar naltrexone, where cytokine levels were reduced by approximately 65 percent compared to vehicle-treated controls. This inhibition was statistically significant (p < 0.01), as determined by one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test.

To evaluate the specificity of this effect, parallel experiments were conducted using ligands for other TLRs. Lipopolysaccharide, a TLR4 agonist, was administered at one nanogram per milliliter in the presence of 200 micromolar naltrexone. Under these conditions, interleukin-6 production was unaffected, with no statistically significant difference observed between vehicle and naltrexone-treated groups (p > 0.05). Similarly, stimulation with R848, a dual TLR7/8 agonist, resulted in significant inhibition of interleukin-6 production at 200 micromolar naltrexone, confirming that the effect extended beyond TLR9 to include other endosomal TLRs. In contrast, stimulation of the interleukin-1 receptor with recombinant human interleukin-1 beta at 100 nanograms per milliliter produced robust interleukin-6 secretion that was completely resistant to the presence of naltrexone at all tested concentrations, indicating that the inhibitory effect was not a general consequence of MyD88 pathway suppression.

To further delineate the cellular source of this inhibition, monocytes and B cells were isolated from the peripheral blood mononuclear cell population using magnetic bead-based positive selection. CD14-positive monocytes were stimulated with CpG in the presence or absence of naltrexone, and interleukin-6 production was measured. No significant inhibition was observed in monocytes, consistent with the known low expression of TLR9 in this subset. However, when CD19-positive B cells were isolated and stimulated with CpG, a marked reduction in interleukin-6 secretion was observed in the presence of naltrexone, confirming that the inhibitory effect was localized to TLR9-expressing B cells. This finding was corroborated by flow cytometric analysis of intracellular cytokine staining, which revealed a significant decrease in the proportion of B cells producing interleukin-6 following CpG stimulation in the presence of naltrexone, without altering the overall frequency of B cells in culture.

To rule out the possibility that the reduction in cytokine production was due to decreased cell viability or induction of apoptosis, annexin V and 7-aminoactinomycin D staining was performed after 24 hours of treatment. The percentage of annexin V-positive and 7AAD-positive cells remained unchanged across all conditions, including those treated with the highest concentration of naltrexone and TLR9 ligand. Cell counts, as determined by automated cell counting, showed no significant difference between control and treated groups. These data collectively demonstrate that naltrexone does not exert a cytotoxic effect on immune cells, and that the inhibition of TLR9-mediated interleukin-6 production is a genuine pharmacological effect on signaling rather than a consequence of cell death.

Additional experiments were conducted to assess the kinetics of inhibition. Cells were stimulated with CpG and naltrexone for varying durations ranging from two to 24 hours, and supernatants were collected at each time point. Cytokine production was found to be suppressed at all time points, with the greatest inhibition occurring at the 24-hour mark, suggesting that naltrexone does not merely delay cytokine release but actively suppresses the magnitude of the response. Furthermore, pre-incubation with naltrexone for as little as 30 minutes prior to CpG stimulation was sufficient to achieve maximal inhibition, indicating that the compound acts rapidly and does not require prolonged exposure to exert its effect.

To confirm that the inhibition was not mediated through opioid receptors, experiments were repeated in the presence of naloxone, a potent opioid receptor antagonist. The addition of naloxone at concentrations sufficient to fully occupy mu and delta opioid receptors did not reverse the inhibitory effect of naltrexone on TLR9 signaling, confirming that the mechanism is opioid receptor-independent. Similarly, in human monocyte-derived dendritic cells genetically engineered to lack mu opioid receptor expression, naltrexone retained its ability to suppress TLR9-induced interleukin-6 production, further supporting the conclusion that the target of naltrexone lies outside the opioid receptor family.

These findings establish naltrexone hydrochloride as a potent and selective antagonist of TLR9 signaling in human B cells, with no effect on TLR4 or interleukin-1 receptor pathways. The inhibition is rapid, dose-dependent, reversible, and occurs without cytotoxicity or alteration of cell viability. This constitutes the first demonstration that naltrexone can directly modulate endosomal TLR function in a physiologically relevant immune context, and provides a mechanistic basis for its therapeutic efficacy in diseases driven by aberrant TLR9 activation.

### Example 2—LDN in the Treatment of Melanoma

This example demonstrates the therapeutic efficacy of low-dose naltrexone in the treatment of melanoma, a highly aggressive form of skin cancer characterized by dysregulated innate immune signaling and a tumor microenvironment enriched in TLR9-expressing myeloid and malignant cells. The study was conducted using a murine model of B16-F10 melanoma, a well-established syngeneic tumor line that recapitulates key features of human melanoma, including immune evasion, metastasis, and resistance to checkpoint blockade.

C57BL/6 mice were subcutaneously injected with 5 × 10⁵ B16-F10 melanoma cells on day zero. On day three, mice were randomized into three treatment groups: a control group receiving vehicle (distilled water), a group receiving low-dose naltrexone hydrochloride at 3 mg/kg administered orally once daily, and a group receiving the standard chemotherapeutic agent dacarbazine at 25 mg/kg intraperitoneally every three days. Treatment continued for 21 days, during which tumor volume was measured twice weekly using calipers, and mice were monitored for signs of distress or weight loss. On day 21, mice were euthanized, tumors were excised and weighed, and splenic and tumor-infiltrating immune cells were isolated for flow cytometric and molecular analysis.

Mice treated with low-dose naltrexone exhibited a statistically significant reduction in tumor volume compared to vehicle-treated controls, with a mean tumor weight of 0.42 grams versus 0.89 grams in the control group (p < 0.001). The reduction in tumor burden was comparable to that achieved with dacarbazine, although naltrexone-treated mice showed no signs of systemic toxicity, including no weight loss, no hematologic suppression, and no evidence of gastrointestinal distress. In contrast, dacarbazine-treated mice exhibited significant weight loss (mean 12 percent of baseline body weight) and reduced splenic cellularity.

Flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating leukocytes revealed a marked decrease in the proportion of myeloid-derived suppressor cells and tumor-associated macrophages in naltrexone-treated mice, accompanied by a significant increase in the ratio of CD8+ T cells to regulatory T cells. The expression of PD-L1 on tumor cells and myeloid cells was significantly downregulated in the naltrexone group, suggesting a reversal of the immunosuppressive phenotype. Analysis of cytokine levels in tumor homogenates demonstrated a substantial reduction in interleukin-6, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, and vascular endothelial growth factor, all of which are known to be induced by TLR9 signaling in melanoma.

To determine whether these effects were mediated through TLR9 inhibition, tumor tissues were analyzed for TLR9 mRNA expression using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction. TLR9 expression was significantly elevated in untreated tumors compared to normal skin, and this elevation was attenuated in naltrexone-treated animals. Furthermore, in vitro stimulation of tumor-infiltrating dendritic cells isolated from naltrexone-treated mice with CpG oligodeoxynucleotides resulted in markedly reduced interleukin-6 production compared to cells from vehicle-treated mice, confirming functional suppression of TLR9 signaling in vivo.

To assess the role of the adaptive immune system in mediating the antitumor effect, a subset of mice was depleted of CD8+ T cells using monoclonal antibody administration prior to tumor challenge. In these animals, the antitumor effect of naltrexone was completely abrogated, with tumor growth rates indistinguishable from those of vehicle-treated controls. This finding indicates that the therapeutic benefit of naltrexone is dependent on the restoration of cytotoxic T lymphocyte activity, which is enabled by the suppression of TLR9-driven immunosuppression.

In parallel experiments, human melanoma cell lines (A375 and SK-MEL-28) were cultured in the presence of naltrexone at concentrations ranging from 10 to 100 nanomolar. No direct cytotoxic effect on tumor cell proliferation or viability was observed, confirming that the antitumor effect is not due to direct tumor cell killing but rather to modulation of the tumor microenvironment. When these tumor cells were co-cultured with human peripheral blood mononuclear cells and stimulated with CpG, the addition of naltrexone significantly reduced interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha production, and enhanced the cytolytic activity of autologous CD8+ T cells against tumor targets, as measured by chromium release assays.

These findings demonstrate that low-dose naltrexone, administered orally at a dose equivalent to 3 mg in humans, effectively suppresses TLR9-mediated inflammation in the melanoma microenvironment, leading to reduced immunosuppression, enhanced T cell infiltration, and significant inhibition of tumor growth. The absence of toxicity, the oral bioavailability, and the ability to synergize with existing immunotherapies position naltrexone as a highly promising adjunctive agent in the treatment of melanoma, particularly in patients with high TLR9 expression or those who have failed checkpoint inhibitor therapy.

### Example 3—LDN in the Treatment of Prostate Cancer

This example presents the therapeutic application of low-dose naltrexone in the treatment of prostate cancer, a disease that frequently progresses to castration-resistant states characterized by chronic inflammation, TLR9 overexpression, and immune suppression. The study utilized the transgenic adenocarcinoma of the mouse prostate (TRAMP) model, which spontaneously develops prostate tumors that progress from prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia to invasive adenocarcinoma and metastatic disease, closely mirroring the clinical course of human prostate cancer.

Male TRAMP mice were enrolled at eight weeks of age and randomized into two cohorts: one receiving low-dose naltrexone hydrochloride at 3 mg/kg orally once daily, and the other receiving vehicle (distilled water). Treatment was initiated prior to the onset of detectable tumors and continued for 16 weeks. At the conclusion of the study, mice were euthanized, prostates and lymph nodes were harvested, and tissues were processed for histopathological analysis, cytokine quantification, and immune cell profiling. Serum levels of prostate-specific antigen were measured as a surrogate marker of disease burden.

Mice treated with low-dose naltrexone exhibited a significant delay in tumor progression, with a 60 percent reduction in the incidence of invasive adenocarcinoma compared to vehicle-treated controls (p < 0.01). The mean prostate weight was reduced by 45 percent in the naltrexone group, and serum prostate-specific antigen levels were significantly lower (p < 0.001). Histological examination revealed a marked reduction in the number of high-grade prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia lesions and a decrease in stromal fibrosis and inflammatory infiltrate in the naltrexone-treated animals.

Flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating immune cells demonstrated a significant reduction in the proportion of myeloid-derived suppressor cells and M2-polarized tumor-associated macrophages in the prostates of naltrexone-treated mice. Conversely, there was a marked increase in the frequency of activated CD8+ T cells and natural killer cells, along with enhanced expression of interferon-gamma and granzyme B in these populations. The expression of PD-L1 on tumor cells and myeloid cells was significantly downregulated, consistent with the suppression of TLR9-driven immunosuppressive signaling.

To determine whether TLR9 was functionally active in the TRAMP prostate tumor microenvironment, quantitative PCR was performed on laser-captured tumor epithelial cells and tumor-associated dendritic cells. TLR9 mRNA expression was elevated in both cell types compared to normal prostate tissue, and this elevation was significantly attenuated in naltrexone-treated mice. In vitro stimulation of isolated dendritic cells from naltrexone-treated mice with CpG oligodeoxynucleotides resulted in a 70 percent reduction in interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha production compared to cells from vehicle-treated mice, confirming that TLR9 signaling was pharmacologically suppressed.

To assess the role of TLR9 in disease progression, a subset of TRAMP mice was treated with a TLR9-specific inhibitory oligonucleotide, which also resulted in delayed tumor progression and reduced inflammation. However, the combination of low-dose naltrexone with the TLR9 inhibitor produced an additive effect, suggesting that naltrexone may act through additional mechanisms beyond direct TLR9 antagonism, such as modulation of endosomal trafficking or adaptor protein recruitment.

In parallel experiments, human prostate cancer cell lines (LNCaP, PC-3, and DU145) were cultured in the presence of naltrexone at concentrations of 10 to 100 nanomolar. No direct effect on cell proliferation or viability was observed, confirming that the antitumor effect is not mediated by cytotoxicity. When these cells were co-cultured with human peripheral blood mononuclear cells and stimulated with TLR9 ligands, the addition of naltrexone significantly reduced interleukin-6 and vascular endothelial growth factor secretion, and enhanced the cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T cells against tumor targets. The conditioned media from naltrexone-treated co-cultures inhibited the migration of endothelial cells in a tube formation assay, indicating suppression of angiogenesis.

To evaluate the clinical relevance of these findings, archival tissue samples from 50 patients with localized and metastatic prostate cancer were analyzed for TLR9 expression by immunohistochemistry. High TLR9 expression was strongly correlated with higher Gleason scores, increased stromal inflammation, and shorter time to biochemical recurrence. Patients with high TLR9 expression who were subsequently treated with low-dose naltrexone as part of a compassionate use program showed a statistically significant reduction in prostate-specific antigen velocity and improved progression-free survival compared to matched controls.

These results establish that low-dose naltrexone exerts a potent antitumor effect in prostate cancer by selectively inhibiting TLR9-mediated inflammation within the tumor microenvironment, thereby reversing immune suppression, enhancing cytotoxic T cell activity, and suppressing angiogenesis. The oral administration, safety profile, and lack of systemic toxicity make naltrexone an ideal candidate for long-term use in patients with advanced prostate cancer, particularly those with evidence of TLR9 overexpression or rising prostate-specific antigen levels despite androgen deprivation therapy. The invention provides a novel, non-toxic, and cost-effective strategy to reprogram the immunosuppressive landscape of prostate cancer and improve clinical outcomes.
