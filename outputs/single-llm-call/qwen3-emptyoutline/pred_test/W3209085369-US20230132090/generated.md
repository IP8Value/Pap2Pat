# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of computational linguistics and natural language processing, specifically to systems and methods for word sense disambiguation through the automated alignment of lexical definitions across multiple dictionaries and the subsequent training of a universal semantic equivalence recognizer. The invention enables the identification of the correct meaning of a polysemous word within a given contextual sentence by leveraging structured lexical knowledge derived from diverse, expert-compiled word sense inventories, without reliance on task-specific annotated datasets. It further provides a transfer learning framework that allows a general-purpose model, pretrained on cross-dictionary semantic alignments, to be adapted to specific downstream word sense disambiguation tasks with minimal additional supervision, thereby achieving superior performance on both common and rare word senses, including those with zero or few training examples. This technology is particularly applicable to machine translation, information extraction, question answering, and other natural language understanding systems where precise semantic interpretation of ambiguous lexical items is critical.

## BACKGROUND

The accurate interpretation of word meaning in context remains one of the most persistent challenges in natural language processing. Human language is inherently ambiguous: a single lexical item may carry multiple distinct meanings, and the correct sense is often determined not by the word itself, but by its surrounding linguistic context. This phenomenon, known as word sense ambiguity, poses significant difficulties for machines attempting to understand human language with fidelity. Early approaches to resolving this ambiguity relied on handcrafted rules, lexical databases, and knowledge-based heuristics such as the Lesk algorithm, which compared dictionary definitions with contextual words. While these methods provided foundational insights, they were limited by the scalability and coverage of manually curated resources and struggled to generalize across diverse linguistic domains.

With the advent of deep learning and large-scale pretrained language models, supervised approaches to word sense disambiguation have achieved remarkable success. These models typically fine-tune transformer-based architectures—such as BERT or RoBERTa—on annotated corpora where each occurrence of a target word is labeled with its corresponding sense from a predefined inventory, most commonly WordNet. By learning to map contextual embeddings to discrete sense labels, such systems have reached state-of-the-art performance on benchmark datasets like those compiled by Raganato et al. (2017) and Blevins et al. (2021). However, these methods are fundamentally constrained by the quality, quantity, and scope of their training data. When a word sense appears infrequently or not at all in the annotated corpus, the model lacks sufficient evidence to learn its distinguishing features, resulting in poor generalization to rare or unseen senses. This limitation is particularly acute in low-resource settings, where many word senses—especially those pertaining to technical, archaic, or domain-specific vocabulary—are absent from training data entirely.

Moreover, existing supervised systems are tightly bound to a single lexical inventory, typically WordNet, which imposes a rigid and often incomplete taxonomy of word meanings. This dependency restricts the model’s ability to incorporate richer, more nuanced, or alternative semantic representations that may be present in other authoritative dictionaries, such as those compiled for advanced language learners. These dictionaries, including the Oxford Advanced Learner’s Dictionary, Merriam-Webster’s Advanced Learner’s Dictionary, Collins COBUILD, Cambridge Advanced Learner’s Dictionary, and Longman Dictionary of Contemporary English, contain not only definitions (glosses) but also carefully curated example sentences that illustrate the contextual usage of each sense. Crucially, these glosses often express the same underlying meaning using different phrasings, syntactic structures, and lexical choices, reflecting the diversity of human linguistic expression. Yet, no prior system has systematically exploited this redundancy across inventories to construct a more robust, generalizable, and inventory-agnostic representation of word meaning.

Previous attempts to integrate external knowledge into word sense disambiguation have focused on enriching representations with information from Wikipedia, BabelNet, or WordNet’s synset graph structures. While these efforts have yielded incremental improvements, they remain confined to a single knowledge source and do not address the core issue of semantic equivalence across heterogeneous lexical resources. Furthermore, methods that rely on paraphrase detection or gloss-context matching, such as GlossBERT or the Bi-Encoder Model, still require fine-tuning on task-specific annotated data and do not decouple the learning of semantic equivalence from the constraints of a single inventory. As a result, these models fail to generalize beyond the distribution of senses present in their training corpora and exhibit brittle behavior when confronted with novel or underrepresented meanings.

There is therefore a critical need for a novel methodology that transcends the limitations of inventory-specific training, leverages the collective semantic wisdom of multiple authoritative dictionaries, and constructs a universal semantic equivalence recognizer capable of identifying correct word senses regardless of their frequency in annotated datasets. Such a system must be able to automatically align definitions across dictionaries, extract high-quality supervision signals from their structural parallels, and pretrain a model on this cross-inventory knowledge before adapting it to downstream tasks. This invention fulfills that need by introducing a novel gloss alignment algorithm grounded in maximum weighted bipartite matching and a two-stage transfer learning architecture that enables unprecedented performance on both high-resource and zero-shot word sense disambiguation tasks.

## SUMMARY

The invention comprises a novel system and method for word sense disambiguation that leverages semantic equivalence knowledge derived from the automated alignment of lexical definitions across multiple independent word sense inventories. The system operates in two distinct phases: a pretraining phase and an adaptation phase. In the pretraining phase, the system collects definition sentences (glosses) and associated example sentences from six authoritative English-language dictionaries and WordNet. For each word that appears across multiple inventories, the system constructs a bipartite graph where nodes represent glosses from different inventories, and edges are weighted by the semantic similarity between pairs of glosses, as measured by a pretrained sentence embedding model. The system then solves a maximum weighted bipartite matching problem to identify the optimal alignment of glosses that share the same underlying meaning, regardless of their surface phrasing. This process generates a large-scale corpus of semantically equivalent gloss-gloss pairs, which are used to construct positive and negative training instances by pairing each gloss with its corresponding example sentence (positive) or with example sentences from mismatched glosses (negative). These instances form the basis for training a universal semantic equivalence recognizer—a neural classifier based on a transformer encoder—that learns to determine whether a given context sentence and a gloss sentence express the same semantic meaning.

In the adaptation phase, the trained semantic equivalence recognizer, now referred to as the general model, can be deployed directly for word sense disambiguation without any task-specific fine-tuning, or it can be further fine-tuned on a smaller, task-specific annotated dataset to produce an expert model. When deployed as a general model, it achieves superior performance on rare and zero-shot word senses, outperforming supervised baselines by more than thirteen percentage points on unseen senses. When fine-tuned, the expert model consistently surpasses all prior state-of-the-art systems on standard word sense disambiguation benchmarks, including the all-words WSD tasks from Senseval and SemEval competitions and the low-shot FEWS dataset, achieving new record performance across all part-of-speech categories. The system further demonstrates its generalizability by improving performance on the Word-in-Context task, where it enhances the accuracy of a RoBERTa Large model by six percentage points without access to WordNet, even outperforming a language model with nine times its parameter count. The invention thus provides a paradigm shift in word sense disambiguation by decoupling semantic knowledge acquisition from task-specific annotation, enabling models to learn from the collective lexical wisdom of human-authored dictionaries rather than from limited, noisy, or inventory-bound training corpora.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention is a comprehensive computational system and method for word sense disambiguation that fundamentally rethinks the way semantic knowledge is acquired, represented, and applied to the task of identifying the correct meaning of a word within a given context. Unlike prior approaches that rely on supervised learning over manually annotated datasets tied to a single lexical inventory, this invention constructs a universal semantic equivalence recognizer through the automated alignment of lexical definitions from multiple independent dictionaries, thereby enabling the model to learn semantic equivalence from the intrinsic redundancy and consistency of expert-authored definitions. The system operates in two sequential and interdependent phases: a pretraining phase that generates a large-scale corpus of semantically aligned gloss-context pairs from diverse lexical resources, and an adaptation phase that either deploys the pretrained model as a general-purpose sense identifier or fine-tunes it for optimal performance on a specific downstream task. The entire process is grounded in a novel algorithm for gloss alignment, implemented as a maximum weighted bipartite matching problem, and executed using a transformer-based neural architecture that learns to discriminate between semantically equivalent and non-equivalent pairs of context and gloss sentences.

The pretraining phase begins with the systematic collection of lexical data from six distinct word sense inventories: WordNet 3.0 and five advanced learner’s dictionaries—the Oxford Advanced Learner’s Dictionary, Merriam-Webster’s Advanced Learner’s Dictionary, Collins COBUILD Advanced Dictionary, Cambridge Advanced Learner’s Dictionary, and Longman Dictionary of Contemporary English. Each of these inventories provides, for a given word, a set of definition sentences (glosses) that describe its possible meanings, along with one or more example sentences that illustrate the contextual usage of each sense. The collection process involves querying each inventory’s electronic corpus for every word that appears in the union of all inventories, ensuring that only words with multiple senses across multiple sources are retained for alignment. For each word, glosses and example sentences are grouped by their part of speech—noun, verb, adjective, or adverb—to ensure that comparisons are made only between semantically coherent categories. The resulting dataset comprises over five hundred thousand glosses and nearly five hundred thousand example sentences, forming a rich and diverse corpus of human-curated semantic knowledge. This corpus is not derived from web-scraped text or machine-generated paraphrases but from authoritative, editorially reviewed sources designed for precise linguistic instruction, ensuring high-quality, contextually grounded definitions that reflect nuanced distinctions in meaning.

Once the lexical data is collected, the system applies a novel gloss alignment algorithm to identify which glosses from different inventories correspond to the same underlying sense. This alignment is formulated as a maximum weighted bipartite matching problem, wherein each gloss from one inventory is treated as a node in one partition of a bipartite graph, and each gloss from another inventory is treated as a node in the complementary partition. An edge is drawn between every pair of glosses from different inventories for the same word and part of speech, and the weight of each edge is determined by the semantic similarity between the two glosses, computed using a pretrained sentence embedding model such as Sentence-BERT. This model encodes each gloss into a dense vector representation that captures its semantic content, and the cosine similarity between these vectors serves as the weight for the corresponding edge. The goal of the algorithm is to find a one-to-one matching between glosses from the two inventories that maximizes the total sum of edge weights, thereby identifying the most semantically aligned pairs of definitions. This optimization problem is solved efficiently using linear programming techniques, with integer constraints ensuring that each gloss is matched to at most one other gloss and that no gloss remains unpaired if a suitable match exists. The system performs this alignment across all possible pairs of the six inventories, resulting in over seven hundred thousand high-confidence gloss alignment links. Each link represents a verified semantic equivalence between two definitions that, despite differing in wording, structure, or lexical choice, express the same meaning.

From these aligned gloss pairs, the system generates a massive corpus of training instances for the semantic equivalence recognizer. For each aligned pair of glosses—one from inventory A and one from inventory B—the system pairs the gloss from inventory A with every example sentence associated with the gloss from inventory B, and vice versa, labeling these as positive instances because they represent the same underlying meaning. Conversely, the system pairs each gloss with example sentences from non-aligned glosses within the same inventory or across inventories, labeling these as negative instances because they represent distinct meanings. This process generates over four hundred thousand positive and over five hundred thousand negative cross-inventory gloss-context pairs. To further enrich the training data, the system also generates intra-inventory pairs by pairing each gloss with its own example sentences (positive) and with example sentences from other glosses of the same word (negative), yielding an additional one point three million positive and four hundred eighteen thousand negative pairs. Additionally, context-context pairs are constructed by comparing example sentences from different glosses of the same word, simulating the Word-in-Context task setting. The entire training corpus thus comprises over two million high-quality, semantically labeled instances derived not from human annotation of specific tasks, but from the intrinsic consistency of lexical knowledge across multiple authoritative dictionaries. This corpus is used to train a unified neural model for recognizing semantic equivalence, which serves as the core of the invention.

The neural model is a transformer-based binary classifier designed to determine whether a given context sentence and a gloss sentence express the same semantic meaning. The model architecture is inspired by the bi-encoder paradigm, wherein two distinct but shared transformer encoders—one for context sentences and one for gloss sentences—produce vector representations that are then compared to make a classification decision. The model is initialized with a pretrained language model, either BERT Base or RoBERTa Large, and employs the same encoder for both context and gloss inputs, ensuring that representations are learned in a shared semantic space. For a context sentence, the model extracts the contextualized embedding of the target word—the word whose sense is to be disambiguated—by locating its position in the input sequence and retrieving its corresponding output embedding from the final transformer layer. For a gloss sentence, the model uses the embedding of the special [CLS] token, which has been shown to capture the overall semantic representation of the sentence. These two embeddings, representing the target word in context and the candidate gloss, are then combined with their element-wise difference and element-wise product to form a four-dimensional feature vector. This combined representation is passed through a feedforward layer with a softmax activation function to produce a binary probability distribution over the classes “semantically equivalent” and “not semantically equivalent.” The model is trained using binary cross-entropy loss and the AdamW optimizer, with hyperparameters tuned for optimal convergence and generalization. Training is conducted over ten epochs with a batch size of sixty-four, dropout of twenty percent, and a learning rate ranging from one to five times ten to the negative five, depending on the model size and dataset. The result is a general semantic equivalence recognizer that has learned to identify semantic equivalence across a wide variety of lexical expressions, without ever being exposed to task-specific annotated data.

Once trained, the general model can be deployed directly for word sense disambiguation. In this mode, for a given context sentence containing a target word, the system retrieves all possible glosses for that word from a target inventory—typically WordNet 3.0—and computes the semantic equivalence score between the context and each gloss using the trained model. The gloss with the highest score is selected as the predicted sense. This approach requires no fine-tuning and operates independently of any annotated training corpus, making it uniquely suited for low-resource or zero-shot scenarios where traditional supervised models fail. In experimental evaluations, this general model achieves a thirteen-point-one percent improvement over supervised baselines on zero-shot word senses, demonstrating that the semantic equivalence knowledge derived from aligned dictionaries is sufficient to enable accurate sense identification even in the complete absence of task-specific training examples. This performance is not an artifact of data leakage or overlap with test sets, as the training data is drawn exclusively from inventories not used in the evaluation benchmarks, and the glosses used for prediction are sourced from a single inventory (WordNet) that was not used to generate any training instances.

To further enhance performance on standard benchmarks, the general model can be fine-tuned on the annotated training data of a specific word sense disambiguation task, such as the SemCor corpus used in the Raganato et al. (2017) all-words WSD benchmark or the FEWS dataset for low-shot evaluation. This fine-tuning step adapts the model to the specific distribution of senses and contexts present in the target task while preserving the general semantic understanding acquired during pretraining. The resulting expert model combines the broad, robust knowledge of the general model with the fine-grained, task-specific adaptation of supervised learning. On the all-words WSD benchmark, the expert model achieves a state-of-the-art F1 score of eighty point seven percent, surpassing all prior systems—including those that incorporate WordNet graph structures or use significantly larger language models—by more than one point two percent. On the FEWS low-shot dataset, the expert model achieves seventy-two point two percent accuracy on zero-shot senses and eighty-two point three percent on few-shot senses, outperforming the previous best system by over four point three percent. These gains are consistent across all parts of speech, with particularly pronounced improvements for verbs and adjectives, which are often more semantically complex and context-dependent than nouns. Importantly, the model’s performance on rare senses improves dramatically even when fine-tuned on a small dataset, indicating that the pretraining phase has equipped the model with a strong prior for distinguishing subtle semantic distinctions that are rarely observed in annotated corpora.

The invention further demonstrates its generalizability through application to the Word-in-Context task, a benchmark that evaluates a model’s ability to determine whether the same lemma word in two different sentences has the same meaning. In this evaluation, WordNet is entirely excluded from the training data to prevent any possibility of data leakage. Instead, the system uses only the context-context pairs generated from the five advanced learner’s dictionaries to train the semantic equivalence recognizer. Despite the absence of any direct supervision from WordNet, the model improves the accuracy of a RoBERTa Large baseline by six percentage points, achieving a score of seventy-five point nine percent. This result surpasses the performance of T5-3B, a language model with nine times the number of parameters, demonstrating that the quality and semantic richness of the aligned lexical knowledge far outweigh the benefits of sheer model scale. The system’s success on this task confirms that the learned representations are not merely memorizing surface patterns but have internalized a deep understanding of semantic equivalence that generalizes across diverse linguistic contexts.

The system is implemented as a modular software architecture that can be integrated into any natural language processing pipeline requiring precise word sense disambiguation. The gloss alignment component is implemented as a standalone preprocessing module that can be applied to any set of lexical inventories, making the system extensible to additional languages and dictionaries. The neural model is implemented using standard deep learning frameworks such as PyTorch or TensorFlow and can be trained on commodity GPU hardware within a reasonable time frame. The inference pipeline is optimized for low latency, enabling real-time deployment in applications such as machine translation, where sense disambiguation must occur during decoding, or in question answering systems, where precise interpretation of query terms is essential for retrieving accurate answers. The system can also be adapted to domain-specific applications, such as medical or legal text analysis, by incorporating domain-specific dictionaries into the pretraining phase, thereby enabling the model to acquire specialized semantic knowledge without requiring annotated domain data.

The invention provides several key advantages over prior art. First, it eliminates the dependence on task-specific annotated datasets, which are expensive to create, limited in coverage, and often biased toward frequent senses. Second, it leverages the collective semantic wisdom of multiple authoritative dictionaries, which contain richer, more nuanced, and more contextually grounded definitions than any single inventory. Third, it decouples the acquisition of semantic knowledge from the task of disambiguation, allowing the model to learn general principles of meaning that can be applied to any word sense inventory or downstream task. Fourth, it achieves state-of-the-art performance on both high-resource and low-resource settings, including zero-shot scenarios where no training examples are available, thereby addressing a fundamental limitation of all prior supervised approaches. Fifth, it demonstrates superior generalization to novel or rare senses, indicating that the model has learned to reason about meaning rather than simply memorize training examples. Sixth, it achieves these results with a model size comparable to standard transformer architectures, avoiding the need for prohibitively large parameter counts or computationally expensive training procedures.

The invention further enables future research directions in semantic representation learning. By demonstrating that semantic equivalence can be reliably learned from aligned lexical definitions without human annotation, it opens the door to the creation of universal semantic knowledge bases that are automatically constructed from multilingual dictionaries, encyclopedias, and other authoritative lexical resources. It also suggests that future language models may benefit from being pretrained not only on raw text but on structured, human-curated semantic alignments, thereby embedding linguistic knowledge directly into their representations. The two-stage transfer learning framework introduced here—pretraining on cross-inventory equivalence, then fine-tuning on task-specific data—provides a blueprint for other NLP tasks that suffer from data scarcity, such as named entity disambiguation, semantic role labeling, and coreference resolution. The system’s ability to retain strong performance on low-resource senses even after fine-tuning on high-resource data suggests that the general model acts as a robust semantic prior that protects against catastrophic forgetting, a long-standing challenge in transfer learning.

In summary, the invention constitutes a transformative advancement in word sense disambiguation by introducing a novel methodology for acquiring semantic knowledge from aligned lexical definitions and applying it through a universal, transferable neural model. It overcomes the fundamental limitations of supervised learning by shifting the source of semantic knowledge from annotated corpora to expert-authored dictionaries, thereby enabling models to understand word meaning in a way that mirrors human linguistic competence. The resulting system achieves unprecedented performance across all evaluation benchmarks, demonstrates exceptional generalization to unseen senses, and provides a scalable, extensible framework for future research in semantic representation and knowledge integration. This invention is not merely an incremental improvement over existing methods but a paradigm shift in how machines learn to understand the meaning of words in context.
