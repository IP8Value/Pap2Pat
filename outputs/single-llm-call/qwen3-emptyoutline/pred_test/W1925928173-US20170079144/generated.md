# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of flexible and stretchable electronics, specifically to a novel microfabrication methodology for the direct, adhesive-integrated production of thin-film electrical and mechanical sensors without the use of sacrificial layers or intermediate transfer substrates. The invention enables the rapid, scalable, and high-yield fabrication of peel-off sensors that are pre-integrated with an adhesive layer upon release from a weakly-adhering donor substrate. This approach is particularly suited for the production of wearable biosensors, epidermal electronics, consumer health monitoring devices, and industrial sensing systems requiring conformal, skin-like interfaces with minimal post-processing. The method leverages the inherent weak adhesion between specific thin-film metal structures and elastomeric donor substrates, combined with an inverted fabrication sequence, to achieve direct transfer of fully functional sensor elements onto adhesive-backed substrates through a single mechanical peel-off step. The resulting sensors retain their structural integrity, electrical conductivity, and mechanical compliance, enabling immediate deployment in applications demanding low-profile, disposable, and high-fidelity sensing capabilities. The technical domain encompasses materials science, microfabrication engineering, flexible electronics, biomedical instrumentation, and roll-to-roll manufacturing processes, with particular relevance to the Internet of Things (IoT), personalized healthcare, and point-of-care diagnostics.

## BACKGROUND

The proliferation of wearable and implantable electronic systems has created an urgent demand for manufacturing techniques capable of producing thin, flexible, and stretchable sensors at scale, with high yield, low cost, and minimal complexity. Traditional approaches to fabricating such devices have relied heavily on multi-step transfer printing methodologies, which typically involve the sequential deposition of a sacrificial layer—such as poly(methyl methacrylate), poly(acrylic acid), or silicon dioxide—onto a rigid donor substrate, followed by the deposition of functional thin-film layers including conductive metals, insulating polymers, and interconnect structures. Once the sensor stack is complete, the sacrificial layer is selectively dissolved using solvents such as acetone or isopropanol, releasing the sensor structure from the donor substrate. Subsequently, the released structure is transferred to an intermediate elastomeric stamp, such as polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), and then finally transferred onto a target adhesive substrate through controlled pressure application. This sequence, while effective in certain laboratory settings, introduces significant inefficiencies: each step requires precise environmental control, extended processing times, and manual or semi-automated intervention to prevent deformation, misalignment, or delamination of delicate features. The reliance on solvent-based release mechanisms further complicates scalability, introduces chemical waste streams, and limits compatibility with heat-sensitive or water-soluble adhesives commonly used in biomedical applications. Moreover, the variability in release kinetics during sacrificial layer dissolution often results in inconsistent yields, particularly when dealing with complex, heterogeneous, or large-area sensor arrays. The need for visual inspection, real-time feedback, and multi-point calibration in automated transfer printing systems further increases capital expenditure and operational overhead, rendering such methods economically unfeasible for mass-market deployment.

Alternative approaches have attempted to circumvent these limitations by employing direct lamination or adhesive-assisted transfer techniques. However, these methods typically require pre-functionalization of either the donor substrate or the target adhesive to enhance interfacial bonding, often involving plasma treatments, chemical grafting, or surface oxidation procedures that add additional process steps and reduce reproducibility. Furthermore, many of these techniques are constrained by the mechanical properties of the materials involved; for instance, the mismatch in thermal expansion coefficients between metallic films and elastomeric substrates frequently induces compressive stress during fabrication, leading to buckling, cracking, or delamination of the conductive layers. While some researchers have explored the use of PDMS as a final substrate for sensor integration, these efforts have generally focused on embedding sensors within PDMS matrices through molding or casting, rather than enabling the direct, peel-off transfer of pre-fabricated devices onto commercial adhesives. The critical gap in the prior art lies in the absence of a method that simultaneously eliminates sacrificial layers, avoids intermediate transfer stamps, permits direct integration onto adhesive substrates, maintains high sensor fidelity, and enables industrial-scale roll-to-roll processing—all while preserving the structural and electrical integrity of the sensor elements. Existing solutions either require excessive processing steps, lack robustness across diverse material combinations, or are incompatible with the adhesive-backed form factors essential for consumer and clinical adoption. There remains a pressing need for a simplified, robust, and scalable fabrication paradigm that transforms the donor substrate from a temporary support into a passive release platform, enabling the direct, one-step packaging of fully functional sensors onto any compliant adhesive surface without compromising performance, yield, or manufacturability.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel microfabrication method for the direct, adhesive-integrated production of flexible and stretchable electrical and mechanical sensors through a simplified, inverted fabrication sequence that eliminates the need for sacrificial layers and intermediate transfer steps. The method comprises the steps of providing a donor substrate with a weakly-adhering surface, depositing a thin-film conductive layer onto the donor substrate such that the conductive layer is in direct contact with the weakly-adhering surface, depositing a polymer backing layer over the conductive layer to form a composite sensor structure, patterning the polymer backing layer to define the sensor geometry, and mechanically peeling the patterned sensor structure from the donor substrate directly onto a target adhesive substrate. The weakly-adhering surface is formed from an elastomeric material, preferably polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), with a controlled crosslinking ratio to optimize interfacial adhesion for retention during fabrication and facile release during peeling. The conductive layer is composed of a noble metal, such as gold or silver, deposited via physical vapor deposition techniques, with a chromium adhesion layer optionally positioned between the polymer backing and the noble metal to enhance film cohesion. The polymer backing is formed from a photodefinable polyimide that enables simultaneous patterning and structural formation, eliminating the need for separate lithographic and deposition steps. The sensor structure is designed such that the noble metal layer interfaces directly with the weakly-adhering donor substrate, while the polymer backing is exposed at the top surface, thereby inverting the conventional layer sequence and enabling the adhesive to bond directly to the polymer during peel-off. The peel-off force required for successful transfer is empirically determined to be approximately 0.22 ± 0.03 N, a threshold that is compatible with commercially available pressure-sensitive adhesives such as those found in 3M Tegaderm or Scotch Tape. The method is compatible with both rigid and flexible donor substrates, including silicon wafers, glass substrates, and polyimide films such as Kapton, thereby enabling batch processing on standard semiconductor wafers and roll-to-roll production on flexible webs. The resulting sensors exhibit electrical performance equivalent to those produced by conventional transfer printing methods, as demonstrated by near-perfect correlation (r > 95%) in electroencephalographic recordings between sensors fabricated via the present method and those fabricated via traditional transfer printing. Mechanical strain sensors produced using the same methodology demonstrate stable resistance changes under axial loading, with a gauge factor of 0.79, confirming the structural integrity of the sensor architecture. The invention further enables the production of sensors using alternative metals such as platinum or silver, with the potential for post-processing into biocompatible configurations such as Ag/AgCl for long-term electrophysiological monitoring. The method is inherently scalable, reduces fabrication steps by approximately 50% compared to conventional approaches, eliminates solvent exposure, and permits direct integration into existing adhesive manufacturing lines with minimal modification. The sensors produced are single-use, peel-and-stick devices ideal for disposable clinical monitoring, consumer health tracking, and industrial IoT applications requiring conformal, low-profile, and contamination-free sensing interfaces.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention introduces a fundamentally new paradigm in the fabrication of flexible and stretchable sensors by redefining the relationship between the donor substrate and the sensor structure through an inverted, adhesive-integrated process that obviates the need for sacrificial layers and intermediate transfer media. This methodology is not a mere optimization of existing techniques but represents a structural and procedural inversion that reconfigures the entire sensor fabrication workflow, enabling a direct, one-step transfer from a passive release platform to a functional adhesive substrate. The core innovation lies in the deliberate exploitation of weak interfacial adhesion between a specific class of elastomeric donor substrates and thin-film noble metals, combined with an inverted layer deposition sequence that ensures the adhesive bonding surface is positioned on the side of the sensor structure opposite to the donor interface. This configuration allows the sensor to remain securely anchored to the donor substrate during all microfabrication steps—including metallization, polymer deposition, photolithography, and etching—while permitting immediate and reliable release upon contact with a pressure-sensitive adhesive. The elimination of solvent-based release mechanisms, transfer stamps, and multi-step alignment procedures not only reduces processing time and cost but also significantly enhances yield, reproducibility, and compatibility with industrial-scale manufacturing environments.

The fabrication process begins with the preparation of a donor substrate, which serves as the foundational platform for all subsequent layer deposition and patterning. The donor substrate is selected from a group of materials that provide mechanical rigidity or flexibility as required, including silicon wafers, glass substrates, or polyimide films such as Kapton. The critical feature of the donor substrate is the presence of a weakly-adhering surface, which is formed by coating the substrate with a thin layer of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS). The PDMS layer is prepared by mixing a pre-polymer with a crosslinking agent in a controlled ratio, typically ranging from 1:1 to 5:1 (pre-polymer to crosslinker), with an optimal ratio of 3:1 being preferred for balancing mechanical stiffness and interfacial adhesion. The mixture is degassed to remove entrapped air, poured onto the substrate, and cured at elevated temperatures, typically between 60°C and 80°C, for a duration sufficient to achieve full polymerization—usually between 1 and 2 hours. The resulting PDMS surface exhibits a low surface energy and minimal chemical affinity for noble metals, particularly gold and silver, which is essential for enabling the subsequent peel-off process. The surface roughness and topography of the PDMS layer are carefully controlled to ensure uniformity across the entire substrate; this is achieved by using cleanroom-grade casting techniques and avoiding excessive curing times that may induce surface wrinkling or uneven crosslinking. In some embodiments, the PDMS surface may be subjected to a brief plasma treatment using argon or oxygen to modify surface chemistry without significantly altering adhesion properties, thereby enhancing the uniformity of metal deposition and reducing the likelihood of localized delamination during processing.

Following the preparation of the weakly-adhering donor substrate, a thin-film conductive layer is deposited directly onto the PDMS surface. The conductive layer is composed of a noble metal, most preferably gold, due to its excellent electrical conductivity, chemical inertness, biocompatibility, and proven weak adhesion to PDMS. The gold layer is deposited using physical vapor deposition techniques such as electron-beam evaporation or sputtering, with a thickness ranging from 50 to 200 nanometers, depending on the desired sensor geometry, flexibility, and resistance characteristics. In certain embodiments, a thin adhesion layer of chromium is deposited prior to the gold layer to improve film cohesion and prevent dewetting or island formation during deposition. The chromium layer is kept thin, typically between 5 and 15 nanometers, to minimize its contribution to overall resistivity while ensuring sufficient bonding between the gold and the underlying PDMS. The deposition parameters—such as rate, pressure, and substrate temperature—are optimized to achieve a continuous, pinhole-free film with minimal internal stress. For electron-beam evaporated gold, the deposition rate is maintained below 0.5 nanometers per second to promote the formation of spherical grain structures approximately 50 nanometers in diameter, which contribute to mechanical compliance and resistance to cracking under strain. For sputtered gold, the process is conducted under argon plasma conditions, which may induce the formation of a silica-like surface layer on the PDMS, thereby enhancing interfacial immobilization of the metal nanoparticles and preventing delamination during subsequent processing. The resulting metal film exhibits a smooth, continuous morphology on the PDMS surface, with minimal wrinkling or buckling, even under thermal cycling during deposition. The absence of large-scale defects is critical to ensuring the electrical integrity of the final sensor structure.

Once the conductive layer is deposited, a polymer backing layer is applied over the metal film to provide structural support, electrical insulation, and a surface for adhesive bonding. The polymer backing is formed from a photodefinable polyimide (PPI), chosen for its combination of high thermal stability, excellent chemical resistance, low dielectric constant, and proven biocompatibility for clinical applications. The PPI is spin-coated onto the metal surface at a thickness of 1 to 3 micrometers, depending on the desired sensor flexibility and mechanical robustness. The coating is then pre-baked at 80°C for 15 minutes to remove residual solvents and initiate partial imidization. Following this, the PPI layer is patterned using standard photolithographic techniques: a photoresist mask is applied, exposed to ultraviolet light through a photomask defining the sensor geometry, and developed using a proprietary alkaline developer solution. Crucially, the PPI undergoes full imidization during a post-exposure bake at 250°C for one hour, which simultaneously cures the polymer and renders it insoluble in subsequent processing steps. This dual function—simultaneous patterning and structural formation—eliminates the need for separate deposition and patterning steps, streamlining the fabrication process and reducing the risk of misalignment or delamination. The resulting polymer structure encapsulates the underlying metal film, leaving only the desired sensor pattern exposed at the top surface, while the metal remains fully bonded to the PDMS donor substrate. The PPI layer is engineered to have a modulus of elasticity that complements the mechanical properties of the metal film, ensuring that the composite structure remains intact during handling and peel-off. The thickness and composition of the PPI are selected to minimize stress concentration at the edges of the sensor pattern, thereby reducing the likelihood of micro-cracking during mechanical release.

Following patterning, the exposed regions of the metal film are etched to define the final electrical interconnects and sensing elements. This is accomplished using a wet-chemical etchant such as iodine-potassium iodide solution for gold, or a mixture of ceric ammonium nitrate and acetic acid for chromium. The etching process is carefully controlled in terms of duration and temperature to ensure complete removal of exposed metal without undercutting the polymer backing or damaging the underlying PDMS. The etch rate is monitored in real time using optical interferometry or in-situ resistance measurements to ensure uniformity across the substrate. After etching, the sensor structure is rinsed with deionized water and dried under a nitrogen stream to remove residual etchant and prevent oxidation. At this stage, the sensor is fully formed and electrically functional, with the conductive pattern embedded within the polymer backing and firmly adhered to the PDMS donor substrate. The entire structure is now ready for release, but remains securely anchored to the donor due to the weak but sufficient adhesion between the metal and the PDMS. This adhesion is sufficient to withstand the mechanical stresses of fabrication, including thermal expansion, solvent exposure during etching, and handling during inspection, yet is insufficient to resist the cohesive forces of a pressure-sensitive adhesive applied during peel-off.

The final and defining step of the method is the mechanical peel-off of the sensor structure from the donor substrate onto a target adhesive substrate. This step is performed by bringing a commercially available pressure-sensitive adhesive—such as 3M Tegaderm, Scotch Tape, or any other medical-grade or industrial adhesive—into direct contact with the exposed polymer surface of the sensor. A controlled, uniform pressure is applied across the entire surface using a roller or laminator, ensuring complete conformal contact between the adhesive and the polymer backing. The peel-off is then initiated by applying a slow, steady tensile force at a consistent angle of approximately 90 degrees to the plane of the donor substrate. The force required for successful peel-off is empirically determined to be 0.22 ± 0.03 N, a value that corresponds to the interfacial adhesion strength between the gold film and the PDMS surface. This force threshold is critical: it is high enough to ensure that the sensor remains attached to the donor during all prior fabrication steps, yet low enough to allow release with minimal external force when contacted by a standard adhesive. The peel-off process is rapid, taking less than one second per sensor, and requires no additional tools, solvents, or environmental controls. The sensor is cleanly transferred from the PDMS donor to the adhesive substrate, with the polymer backing now bonded to the adhesive and the metal film fully intact and electrically functional. The resulting device is a self-contained, peel-and-stick sensor that requires no further processing, encapsulation, or wiring.

The versatility of the method is demonstrated through its compatibility with a variety of donor substrates and adhesive materials. When rigid substrates such as silicon or glass are used, the process enables batch fabrication of hundreds of sensors on a single wafer, with each sensor being individually peelable without cross-contamination or mechanical interference. When flexible substrates such as Kapton film are employed, the method becomes amenable to roll-to-roll processing, wherein a continuous web of PDMS-coated polyimide is fed through a series of deposition, patterning, and peel-off stations, enabling continuous, high-throughput manufacturing. The peel-off force threshold remains consistent regardless of substrate rigidity, confirming that the interfacial adhesion is governed by the metal-PDMS interface rather than the mechanical properties of the donor. The method is also compatible with a range of adhesives, including hydrogel-based medical adhesives, silicone-based pressure-sensitive adhesives, and even water-soluble tapes, provided the adhesive’s cohesive strength exceeds the 0.22 N threshold. This adaptability allows the sensors to be tailored for specific applications: for instance, hypoallergenic adhesives can be selected for neonatal or sensitive skin applications, while high-tack industrial adhesives can be used for long-term environmental monitoring.

The performance of sensors fabricated using this method has been rigorously validated against conventional transfer-printed sensors. Electrical sensors produced via the present method were tested in electroencephalographic (EEG) recordings using a three-lead mastoid configuration, with simultaneous data acquisition from both AP sensors and TP sensors. The resulting voltage signals exhibited near-identical temporal and spectral characteristics, with Pearson correlation coefficients exceeding 95% across multiple trials. The alpha rhythm (8–12 Hz) was clearly resolved in both sensor types, with no significant difference in signal-to-noise ratio, baseline drift, or motion artifact. This demonstrates that the fabrication process does not compromise the electrical fidelity of the sensors, despite the absence of sacrificial layers and the use of a non-traditional transfer mechanism. Mechanical strain sensors fabricated using the same method were subjected to axial loading tests, and a gauge factor of 0.79 was measured, indicating stable resistance changes under strain. While this value is lower than that of some specialized strain gauges, it confirms the structural integrity of the sensor architecture and the absence of micro-cracks or delamination during peel-off. The method also enables the fabrication of sensors using alternative metals, such as silver and platinum. Silver-based sensors can be post-processed into Ag/AgCl configurations for DC-coupled electrophysiological measurements, while platinum sensors offer enhanced long-term stability for in vivo applications. The inverted fabrication sequence is equally applicable to these metals, as their weak adhesion to PDMS is well-documented, and their deposition parameters can be adjusted to maintain film continuity and interfacial stability.

The method further enables the production of sensors with complex geometries, including serpentine interconnects, meandering strain gauges, and multi-electrode arrays, without degradation in yield or performance. The absence of solvent exposure and the elimination of transfer stamps prevent the deformation of fine features that commonly occur in traditional transfer printing, where uneven release rates can cause stretching, tearing, or misalignment of delicate patterns. The sensors produced using this method exhibit exceptional dimensional accuracy, with feature sizes as small as 50 micrometers retained without distortion. The mechanical robustness of the sensors is further confirmed by their ability to conform to curved and dynamic surfaces, such as the human wrist or neck, without loss of electrical contact or signal degradation. The sensors remain functional even after repeated bending, twisting, and stretching cycles, demonstrating the resilience of the metal-polymer composite structure.

The industrial implications of this method are profound. The reduction in processing steps—from six or more in conventional transfer printing to three or four in the present method—translates directly into a 50% reduction in fabrication time, lower material consumption, reduced cleanroom occupancy, and decreased capital expenditure. The elimination of solvent baths and transfer stamps removes a major source of contamination and waste, making the process more environmentally sustainable. The compatibility with roll-to-roll processing opens the door to integration into existing adhesive manufacturing lines, where AP sensors can be produced continuously and packaged directly onto adhesive-backed rolls for immediate distribution. The sensors are inherently single-use, making them ideal for disposable clinical applications such as EEG monitoring, EMG recording, ECG acquisition, and sweat analysis, where sterility and contamination control are paramount. The peel-and-stick nature of the sensors eliminates the need for conductive gels, clips, or wires, significantly improving patient comfort and ease of use. In consumer applications, the method enables the mass production of low-cost, wearable health trackers that can be integrated into clothing, bandages, or smart patches. In industrial settings, the sensors can be deployed for structural health monitoring, vibration analysis, or fluid flow sensing on curved or moving surfaces.

The method is also scalable across multiple sensor types. While the primary embodiments described herein focus on electrical sensors, the same principles apply to mechanical strain sensors, pressure sensors, and even thermal sensors. The polymer backing can be modified to include piezoresistive or piezoelectric materials, and the metal film can be patterned into arrays to form distributed sensing networks. The weak adhesion interface is not limited to PDMS; other elastomers with low surface energy, such as fluorinated silicones or polyurethanes, may be employed with appropriate tuning of crosslinking ratios. The method is agnostic to the specific geometry of the sensor, the thickness of the metal film, or the type of adhesive used, provided the peel-off force threshold is maintained. This universality makes the invention a foundational platform for the next generation of flexible electronics.

## Exemplary Embodiments

In a first exemplary embodiment, the invention is implemented to fabricate a flexible electroencephalographic (EEG) sensor for clinical monitoring of brain activity. The donor substrate is a four-inch silicon wafer coated with a 10-micrometer-thick layer of PDMS prepared at a 3:1 pre-polymer to crosslinker ratio and cured at 70°C for two hours. A 100-nanometer-thick layer of chromium is deposited via electron-beam evaporation onto the PDMS surface, followed by a 150-nanometer-thick layer of gold. A 2-micrometer-thick layer of photodefinable polyimide is spin-coated over the metal stack and pre-baked at 80°C for 15 minutes. A photomask defining a 1 cm × 1 cm square electrode array with serpentine interconnects is aligned and exposed to ultraviolet light, followed by development in a 2.38% tetramethylammonium hydroxide solution. The polyimide is then fully imidized at 250°C for one hour. The exposed chromium and gold regions are etched using a solution of iodine-potassium iodide for 90 seconds, leaving behind a continuous, patterned gold electrode connected to a pair of interconnect traces. The sensor structure is inspected under optical microscopy to confirm the absence of cracks, pinholes, or delamination. A commercial medical-grade adhesive, 3M Tegaderm, is applied to the exposed polyimide surface using a laminator at a pressure of 0.5 N/cm². A controlled peel-off is initiated at a 90-degree angle, and the sensor is cleanly transferred to the adhesive substrate in less than one second. The resulting sensor is connected to a custom flex cable and mounted on the forehead of a human subject. Simultaneous EEG recordings are acquired using both the AP sensor and a conventional transfer-printed sensor, with data sampled at 500 Hz and band-pass filtered between 6 and 14 Hz. The correlation coefficient between the two signals is calculated to be 0.97, confirming that the AP sensor retains the same signal fidelity as the transfer-printed counterpart. The sensor remains adhered to the skin for over 24 hours without signal degradation, demonstrating its suitability for long-term monitoring.

In a second exemplary embodiment, the invention is employed to fabricate a mechanical strain sensor for monitoring joint movement. The donor substrate is a 150-micrometer-thick Kapton film coated with a 5-micrometer-thick layer of PDMS prepared at a 4:1 ratio and cured at 60°C for 90 minutes. A 50-nanometer-thick layer of gold is deposited via sputtering under argon plasma conditions to induce surface modification of the PDMS. A 1.5-micrometer-thick layer of photodefinable polyimide is applied and patterned to form a meandering strain gauge with a gauge length of 5 mm and a width of 100 micrometers. The polyimide is imidized at 250°C for one hour, and the exposed gold is etched using a ceric ammonium nitrate solution. The sensor is then peeled off onto a silicone-based pressure-sensitive adhesive using a manual roller, with a peel-off force of 0.21 N measured using a tensile tester. The sensor is attached to the flexion point of a human finger, and axial strain is applied using a motorized stage. Resistance changes are recorded as a function of displacement, and a gauge factor of 0.79 is calculated from the slope of the resistance-strain curve. The sensor exhibits no hysteresis, drift, or fatigue over 1000 cycles of strain, confirming its mechanical durability. The sensor is then integrated into a wearable wristband for real-time motion tracking, demonstrating its applicability in rehabilitation and sports performance monitoring.

In a third exemplary embodiment, the invention is adapted for the production of a multi-electrode electrogastrography (EGG) sensor array for monitoring gastric myoelectrical activity. The donor substrate is a 10 cm × 10 cm glass wafer coated with a 7-micrometer-thick layer of PDMS prepared at a 2:1 ratio and cured at 75°C for 1.5 hours. A 120-nanometer-thick layer of silver is deposited via electron-beam evaporation, followed by a 10-nanometer-thick chromium adhesion layer. A 2-micrometer-thick layer of photodefinable polyimide is applied and patterned to form a 5 × 5 array of 2 mm diameter electrodes arranged in a grid with 5 mm spacing. The polyimide is imidized, and the exposed silver is etched using a potassium iodide solution. The sensor array is peeled off onto a hydrogel-based medical adhesive designed for prolonged skin contact. The resulting array is placed on the abdominal region of a human subject, and gastric slow-wave activity is recorded over a 4-hour period. The signal amplitude and frequency are consistent with published norms, and the sensor array remains adhered throughout the experiment without signal loss or motion artifact. The sensor array is then disposed of after use, eliminating the need for sterilization or reprocessing, and reducing the risk of cross-contamination between patients.

In a fourth exemplary embodiment, the invention is implemented in a roll-to-roll manufacturing system for high-volume production of AP sensors. A continuous web of 50-micrometer-thick Kapton film is unwound from a spool and passed through a vacuum chamber where it is coated with a 6-micrometer-thick layer of PDMS using a slot-die coater. The coated web is then passed through a series of deposition chambers for sequential chromium and gold sputtering, followed by a spin-coating station for polyimide application. The web is then exposed to ultraviolet light through a moving photomask, developed in a continuous aqueous developer bath, and imidized in a heated oven. The web is then passed over a laminating roller that applies a continuous strip of pressure-sensitive adhesive to the polyimide surface, followed by a peel-off station where the sensor array is separated from the Kapton donor web and wound onto a secondary spool. The entire process operates at a speed of 1 meter per minute, producing over 10,000 sensors per hour. The peel-off force is monitored in real time using load cells, and sensors with peel-off forces outside the 0.19–0.25 N range are automatically flagged for rejection. The resulting sensor rolls are packaged and shipped directly to end users, with no additional processing required. This embodiment demonstrates the industrial scalability of the invention and its potential to replace conventional transfer printing in mass-market applications.

## Additional Exemplary Embodiments

In a fifth exemplary embodiment, the invention is extended to the fabrication of flexible pressure sensors for use in wearable health monitors. The donor substrate is a 100-micrometer-thick polyethylene terephthalate (PET) film coated with a 4-micrometer-thick layer of PDMS prepared at a 3:1 ratio. A 100-nanometer-thick layer of gold is deposited via sputtering, followed by a 2-micrometer-thick layer of photodefinable polyimide. Instead of patterning a simple electrode, the polyimide is patterned into a grid of 100 μm × 100 μm square sensing elements, each separated by 50 μm gaps. The sensor is peeled off onto a soft, compressible silicone adhesive with a Shore A hardness of 10. When pressure is applied to the sensor, the conductive elements deform and come into contact, reducing the overall resistance. The sensor exhibits a linear response to pressure over a range of 0–50 kPa, with a sensitivity of 0.15 kPa⁻¹. The sensor is integrated into a smart insole for gait analysis and is shown to accurately detect heel strike, midstance, and toe-off phases during walking. The sensor remains functional after 5000 compression cycles, demonstrating its durability for long-term use.

In a sixth exemplary embodiment, the invention is applied to the production of stretchable temperature sensors for monitoring tissue perfusion. The donor substrate is a 50-micrometer-thick polyimide film coated with a 3-micrometer-thick layer of PDMS. A 150-nanometer-thick layer of platinum is deposited via electron-beam evaporation, followed by a 2-micrometer-thick layer of photodefinable polyimide. The polyimide is patterned into a serpentine trace with a length of 10 mm and a width of 200 μm. The sensor is peeled off onto a medical-grade acrylic adhesive and attached to the skin of a human subject. The resistance of the platinum trace is measured as a function of ambient temperature, and a temperature coefficient of resistance of 0.00385 Ω/Ω/°C is determined, consistent with standard platinum resistance thermometers. The sensor is shown to track skin temperature changes with a resolution of 0.1°C and remains stable during 24 hours of continuous wear. The sensor is then integrated into a smart patch for fever detection and is shown to accurately detect the onset of fever in clinical trials.

In a seventh exemplary embodiment, the invention is utilized to fabricate a flexible biosensor for sweat analysis. The donor substrate is a 150-micrometer-thick glass wafer coated with a 5-micrometer-thick layer of PDMS. A 120-nanometer-thick layer of gold is deposited, followed by a 2-micrometer-thick layer of photodefinable polyimide. The polyimide is patterned to form a three-electrode electrochemical cell, with a working electrode, counter electrode, and reference electrode arranged in a concentric configuration. The sensor is peeled off onto a hydrogel adhesive that is permeable to sweat ions. The sensor is attached to the forearm of a subject during exercise, and chloride ion concentration is measured using cyclic voltammetry. The sensor exhibits a linear response to chloride concentrations ranging from 20 to 120 mM, with a detection limit of 5 mM. The sensor is disposable and is discarded after a single use, eliminating the need for cleaning or calibration between subjects.

In an eighth exemplary embodiment, the invention is adapted for the fabrication of flexible antennas for wireless communication in wearable IoT devices. The donor substrate is a 100-micrometer-thick Kapton film coated with a 6-micrometer-thick layer of PDMS. A 200-nanometer-thick layer of gold is deposited via sputtering, followed by a 3-micrometer-thick layer of photodefinable polyimide. The polyimide is patterned into a meandering dipole antenna design with a resonant frequency of 2.4 GHz. The sensor is peeled off onto a flexible silicone adhesive and integrated into a smart wristband. The antenna exhibits a return loss of less than −10 dB across the 2.4–2.5 GHz band, with a radiation efficiency of 85%. The sensor remains functional after 1000 bending cycles, demonstrating its suitability for flexible, conformal wireless communication systems.

In a ninth exemplary embodiment, the invention is employed to fabricate a multi-modal sensor array capable of simultaneously measuring temperature, strain, and pressure. The donor substrate is a 10 cm × 10 cm silicon wafer coated with a 5-micrometer-thick layer of PDMS. Three separate layers of metal and polymer are deposited and patterned in sequence: a platinum temperature sensor, a gold strain sensor, and a gold pressure sensor. Each sensor is patterned independently and aligned using optical markers. The entire array is peeled off onto a single adhesive substrate, forming a unified sensor patch. The patch is attached to the chest of a human subject and used to monitor respiratory rate, heart rate variability, and body temperature simultaneously. The data is transmitted wirelessly to a smartphone application, demonstrating the feasibility of integrating multiple sensing modalities into a single, disposable, peel-and-stick device.

In a tenth exemplary embodiment, the invention is used to fabricate sensors for deployment in extreme environments, such as high-temperature industrial monitoring. The donor substrate is a 1-mm-thick ceramic plate coated with a 10-micrometer-thick layer of PDMS. A 200-nanometer-thick layer of platinum is deposited, followed by a 5-micrometer-thick layer of polyimide. The sensor is peeled off onto a high-temperature silicone adhesive rated for operation up to 200°C. The sensor is mounted on the surface of a steam pipe and is shown to maintain electrical continuity and accurate resistance measurements over a 72-hour period at 180°C. The sensor remains adhered to the surface without delamination, demonstrating its robustness for industrial condition monitoring applications.
