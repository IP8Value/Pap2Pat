# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to a system and method for the non-invasive, real-time monitoring of nuclear reactor fuel cycle activities through the detection of neutrons escaping from the reactor core at stand-off distances beyond the primary shielding. Specifically, the invention provides a means to verify the isotopic composition of fissile material within a nuclear reactor core by correlating the rate of neutron emission detected externally with the reactor’s thermal power output, thereby enabling the detection of unauthorized changes in fissile inventory—such as diversion, undeclared fuel replacement, or illicit transmutation—without requiring physical access to the reactor core or its fuel assemblies. The system is particularly suited for safeguarding non-traditional reactor types, including small modular reactors (SMRs), sealed-core reactors, research reactors, and pressurized heavy water reactors, where conventional safeguards methods such as physical inventory accounting, tamper-indicating seals, or in-core sampling are either impractical, insufficiently timely, or incompatible with operational constraints. The invention leverages the fundamental physical relationship between the neutron flux escaping a reactor core and the weighted sum of fissile isotope masses present within the core, as dictated by the fission cross-sections and energy yields of uranium and plutonium isotopes. By deploying an array of large-area, low-background neutron detectors at multiple strategic locations around the reactor facility, the system achieves robust, statistically significant monitoring of reactor behavior over time, while simultaneously distinguishing between normal operational variations and anomalous events indicative of nuclear material misuse. The method is applicable to both civilian and international safeguards contexts, providing a technically verifiable, economically scalable, and operationally resilient tool for compliance with international nuclear non-proliferation obligations.

## BACKGROUND

Nuclear safeguards systems have long relied on a combination of physical inspections, material accounting, tamper-indicating seals, and video surveillance to verify that nuclear materials are not diverted from peaceful uses. These methods, while effective in many contexts, face significant limitations when applied to modern reactor designs, particularly small modular reactors, sealed-core systems, and research reactors, which often feature compact geometries, continuous fueling cycles, high levels of shielding, and restricted access to core components. The International Atomic Energy Agency has identified the monitoring of process emanations—such as neutrons and gamma rays—as a critical priority for enhancing the timeliness and reliability of safeguards verification, especially in facilities where direct sampling of fuel is not feasible. Neutrons, as direct byproducts of fission, carry intrinsic information about the isotopic composition and total mass of fissile material within the core, and their leakage through shielding provides a measurable signature of the reactor’s operational state. However, prior efforts to exploit neutron emission for safeguards purposes have been largely confined to in-core or near-core detector placements, which are vulnerable to tampering, require costly integration into reactor design, and are incompatible with existing infrastructure. External neutron detection at stand-off distances has been explored in the context of reactor power estimation and criticality alarm systems, but these applications have not been extended to the quantitative determination of isotopic inventory changes over time. Moreover, existing neutron detection systems have not been configured to operate in coordinated networks capable of distinguishing between environmental interference, operational transients, and malicious attempts to mask fuel diversion. The absence of a method capable of correlating external neutron count rates with reactor power in a manner that isolates isotopic composition as the primary variable has left a critical gap in the safeguards toolkit. This gap is particularly acute in facilities where fuel is continuously or semi-continuously replenished, such as in molten salt reactors or heavy water reactors, where the total fissile mass may remain nominally constant while the isotopic balance shifts dramatically due to burn-up and transmutation. Without a means to detect these subtle but significant isotopic changes, the potential for undetected diversion of weapons-usable material—such as plutonium-239 or highly enriched uranium—remains unmitigated. Furthermore, the deployment of sealed-core SMRs, which eliminate the possibility of periodic fuel sampling, renders traditional safeguards approaches obsolete unless a non-invasive, continuous, and isotopically sensitive monitoring technique is developed. The present invention addresses these shortcomings by demonstrating, for the first time, that the ratio of neutron detection rate to reactor thermal power, measured externally at distances exceeding 50 meters from the core, is a direct and quantifiable function of the weighted sum of fissile isotope masses within the core. This relationship, grounded in nuclear physics and validated through extensive experimental measurement, enables the detection of kilogram-scale changes in fissile inventory within weeks, meeting or exceeding the IAEA’s timeliness goals for significant quantity detection, without requiring any modification to the reactor’s design or operational procedures. The invention further introduces a novel methodology for deploying multiple neutron detectors in a spatially distributed array, enabling the discrimination of localized interferences—such as shielding modifications or artificial neutron sources—from genuine changes in core composition, thereby providing a robust, tamper-resistant, and operationally practical solution for modern nuclear safeguards.

## SUMMARY

The invention comprises a system and method for verifying the isotopic composition of fissile material within a nuclear reactor core by detecting neutrons emitted from the reactor at stand-off distances beyond the primary shielding and correlating the detected neutron count rate with the reactor’s thermal power output. The system comprises an array of at least two large-area neutron detectors, each with a detection area of at least 0.1 square meters, positioned at distinct locations surrounding the reactor facility at distances ranging from 50 to 150 meters from the reactor core. Each detector is configured to measure thermalized neutrons resulting from the moderation of fast neutrons escaping the reactor shielding, and is coupled to a data acquisition system capable of recording time-stamped neutron events with a temporal resolution of less than one minute. The reactor’s thermal power is simultaneously measured using conventional reactor instrumentation, such as primary coolant flow rate and temperature differential, and the neutron count rate is normalized to the reactor power to produce a neutron-to-power ratio. This ratio is then compared over time to a reference baseline established during a period of known and stable isotopic composition, and deviations from this baseline are used to infer changes in the relative abundance of fissile isotopes within the core. The method is based on the physical principle that the neutron flux per unit power emitted from a reactor core is proportional to a weighted sum of the masses of fissile isotopes present, where the weighting factors are determined by the respective fission cross-sections and energy yields per fission event for each isotope, as defined by nuclear data tables for uranium-235, plutonium-239, and plutonium-241. The system is designed to detect changes in fissile inventory as small as one kilogram of uranium-235 or plutonium-239 within a monitoring period of less than 90 days, thereby satisfying the IAEA’s timeliness criteria for significant quantity detection. The array of detectors is strategically positioned to capture neutron leakage from multiple directions, enabling the system to distinguish between global changes in core composition and localized interferences caused by the placement of neutron moderators, shielding, or artificial neutron sources. By comparing the neutron-to-power ratios measured at multiple locations, the system identifies anomalies that are inconsistent with the reactor’s design and operational history, thereby flagging potential safeguards violations. The method further includes a data analytics component that applies statistical filtering, environmental correction algorithms, and machine learning techniques to reduce noise from background radiation, atmospheric variations, and operational transients, thereby improving the sensitivity and reliability of isotopic change detection. The system operates continuously and autonomously, requiring no physical interaction with the reactor core or fuel assemblies, and can be retrofitted to existing reactor facilities without modification to their structural or operational integrity. The invention provides a non-invasive, cost-effective, and tamper-resistant means of verifying nuclear fuel cycle activities, enabling international safeguards authorities to detect and deter the illicit diversion of fissile material from peaceful nuclear programs.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention is a comprehensive system and method for the continuous, non-invasive, and isotopically sensitive monitoring of nuclear reactor cores through the external detection of neutrons escaping the reactor shielding at stand-off distances. The fundamental premise of the invention is that the neutron emission rate from a reactor core, when normalized to the reactor’s thermal power output, serves as a direct and quantifiable indicator of the weighted isotopic composition of fissile material within the core. This relationship arises from the fact that different fissile isotopes—primarily uranium-235, plutonium-239, and plutonium-241—produce distinct numbers of neutrons per unit of energy released during fission, due to differences in their microscopic fission cross-sections and the average energy liberated per fission event. These nuclear properties are well-characterized and tabulated in international nuclear data libraries, and their combined influence on the neutron flux per unit power is mathematically deterministic. The invention exploits this deterministic relationship by measuring the external neutron count rate and reactor power simultaneously over extended periods, and by analyzing the resulting neutron-to-power ratio as a function of time. When the isotopic composition of the core changes—whether due to fuel burn-up, online refueling, or unauthorized material substitution—the neutron-to-power ratio shifts in a predictable manner, and this shift can be detected even when the total reactor power remains constant. The system is designed to detect these shifts with sufficient sensitivity to identify the addition or removal of one kilogram of uranium-235 or plutonium-239 within a monitoring window of less than 90 days, thereby meeting the International Atomic Energy Agency’s safeguards timeliness goals for the detection of a significant quantity of fissile material. The invention achieves this sensitivity not through the use of high-efficiency or exotic detector technologies, but through the strategic deployment of multiple large-area, commercially available neutron detectors operating in a coordinated network, combined with a rigorous analytical framework that isolates isotopic variation from confounding environmental and operational factors.

The system consists of an array of at least two neutron detectors, each with a detection area of no less than 0.1 square meters, positioned at distinct locations surrounding the reactor facility. These locations are chosen to be at stand-off distances ranging from 50 to 150 meters from the reactor core, ensuring that the detectors are situated beyond the primary neutron shielding and outside the reactor building envelope, thereby minimizing the risk of tampering and maximizing the ability to detect neutron leakage from multiple directions. Each detector is a thermal neutron detector, typically constructed using a boron-lined proportional counter or a helium-3 filled tube, both of which are commercially available and have been proven in industrial and research applications for their stability, reliability, and ability to operate continuously under ambient environmental conditions. The detectors are housed in weatherproof enclosures and are equipped with internal temperature and humidity sensors to monitor environmental conditions that may influence neutron moderation or detector response. The neutron detection signal is processed by a dedicated data acquisition system capable of recording individual neutron events in list mode with microsecond-level time resolution, allowing for the reconstruction of count rates at minute-by-minute intervals. The system is designed to operate autonomously, with data transmitted securely to a central monitoring station via encrypted communication channels, and with redundant power and data storage capabilities to ensure continuous operation even during temporary disruptions. The reactor’s thermal power is measured independently using conventional instrumentation, such as the differential temperature and flow rate of the primary coolant loop, which are standard parameters monitored by reactor operators for safety and efficiency purposes. These power measurements are synchronized with the neutron count rate data using a common time reference, typically derived from a GPS-synchronized clock, to ensure precise temporal alignment between the two datasets.

The core analytical method of the invention involves computing the neutron-to-power ratio for each time interval, typically one minute or longer, and then normalizing this ratio relative to a reference baseline established during a period of known and stable isotopic composition. This baseline is established during a period of routine reactor operation, such as during a sustained period of steady-state power production following a recent refueling cycle, when the isotopic inventory of the core is known with high confidence through reactor physics simulations and operational records. The reference baseline serves as the fiducial point against which all subsequent measurements are compared. Deviations from this baseline are then analyzed using statistical methods to determine whether they are consistent with normal operational fluctuations or indicative of a genuine change in fissile inventory. The statistical analysis accounts for both counting statistics and environmental noise, including variations in cosmic ray background, atmospheric pressure, humidity, and temperature, all of which can influence neutron moderation and detector response. The system employs a moving window algorithm that continuously updates the baseline as new data is acquired, allowing for adaptive tracking of slow, natural changes in isotopic composition due to burn-up, while maintaining sensitivity to abrupt, anomalous deviations that may signal illicit activity. The system further incorporates a multi-detector correlation algorithm that compares the neutron-to-power ratios measured at each detector location. Because the neutron leakage pattern from a reactor core is spatially dependent—neutrons escaping from the top of the core may be more readily detected by an elevated detector, while those escaping laterally may be more prominent at ground-level detectors—any attempt to mask a change in core composition by introducing a localized neutron moderator or an artificial neutron source will produce inconsistent responses across the detector array. For example, if an adversary were to place a thick layer of high-density polyethylene around one detector to artificially enhance its neutron detection rate in an attempt to mimic a higher fissile inventory, the other detectors, positioned at different angles and distances, would not exhibit the same increase, and the resulting discrepancy would be flagged as an anomaly. Similarly, if a neutron source were placed near a detector to simulate a reactor operating at higher power, the reactor’s actual power output would remain unchanged, and the neutron-to-power ratio at that detector would appear artificially elevated relative to the others. The system is designed to detect such inconsistencies with high confidence, thereby rendering attempts at deception both technically difficult and operationally detectable.

The sensitivity of the system to isotopic changes is derived from the physical relationship between the neutron flux per unit power and the mass of fissile isotopes in the core. This relationship is expressed mathematically as the inverse proportionality between the neutron-to-power ratio and the weighted sum of fissile isotope masses, where the weights are determined by the product of each isotope’s fission cross-section and energy yield per fission. For the three dominant fissile isotopes—uranium-235, plutonium-239, and plutonium-241—their respective contributions to the neutron-to-power ratio are approximately 1.0, 1.34, and 1.42 times that of uranium-235, based on thermal neutron data. This means that a substitution of uranium-235 with plutonium-239, even if the total fissile mass remains constant, will result in a measurable increase in the neutron-to-power ratio. Conversely, the burn-up of uranium-235 and the subsequent buildup of plutonium isotopes will cause a gradual, predictable increase in this ratio over time, which the system can track and differentiate from sudden, anomalous shifts. The system is calibrated using reactor physics simulations, such as those performed with the TRIAD code, to predict the expected evolution of the neutron-to-power ratio under normal operating conditions. These simulations incorporate the reactor’s geometry, fuel composition, burn-up history, and neutron leakage characteristics to generate a theoretical baseline that is then compared to the measured data. The agreement between simulated and measured values, typically within 5% over extended periods, validates the accuracy of the method and confirms that the observed variations are attributable to isotopic changes rather than measurement error. The system also includes a self-diagnostic module that monitors the stability of the detector response over time, identifying drifts in gain, background noise, or electronic performance that could compromise the integrity of the measurements. If a detector exhibits a sustained deviation from its expected response, the system triggers an alert and initiates a diagnostic sequence to determine whether the deviation is due to a hardware fault or a potential safeguards violation.

The invention further includes a data analytics engine that applies advanced filtering and machine learning techniques to enhance the detection capability of the system. Raw neutron count rate data, which may contain sporadic spikes from cosmic ray interactions or electronic noise, are subjected to a wavelet-based denoising algorithm that preserves the temporal structure of genuine neutron events while suppressing transient artifacts. Environmental variables such as atmospheric pressure, temperature, and humidity are correlated with the neutron count rate using multivariate regression models to quantify and remove their influence from the data. The system also employs a change-point detection algorithm that identifies statistically significant deviations in the neutron-to-power ratio over time, automatically flagging potential anomalies for further review. These algorithms are trained on historical data from reactor operations, including normal refueling cycles, power ramping, and shutdown events, to minimize false positives while maximizing sensitivity to genuine safeguards-relevant events. The system is capable of detecting not only the addition or removal of fissile material but also more subtle forms of misuse, such as the partial replacement of irradiated fuel with fresh fuel of unknown origin, the introduction of low-enriched uranium into a reactor designed for high-enriched fuel, or the clandestine operation of a reactor at elevated power levels for the purpose of producing weapons-grade plutonium. In each case, the isotopic signature of the neutron emission will deviate from the expected pattern, and the system will detect this deviation with high confidence.

The deployment of the system is designed to be minimally invasive and compatible with existing reactor infrastructure. The neutron detectors are mounted on portable, weather-resistant platforms that can be installed on the ground, on elevated platforms, or on the exterior walls of reactor buildings, without requiring any modification to the reactor itself. The detectors are powered by standard electrical sources or by solar panels with battery backup, and their data transmission is secured using end-to-end encryption and digital authentication protocols to prevent interception or spoofing. The system is designed to operate continuously for years without maintenance, and its components are selected for their robustness in harsh environmental conditions, including exposure to rain, snow, dust, and temperature extremes. The central monitoring station, which may be located on-site or remotely, receives and processes data from all detectors in real time, and generates daily, weekly, and monthly reports summarizing the neutron-to-power ratio trends, deviations from baseline, and any flagged anomalies. These reports are accessible to authorized safeguards inspectors and can be integrated into existing international safeguards databases, providing a verifiable, auditable record of reactor behavior over time. The system also supports remote calibration verification through periodic transmission of reference neutron sources or simulated neutron signatures, allowing inspectors to confirm the integrity of the detector response without requiring physical access to the detector locations.

The invention is particularly advantageous for safeguarding sealed-core small modular reactors, which are increasingly being developed for decentralized energy applications but pose significant challenges to traditional safeguards approaches. Because these reactors are designed to operate for years without refueling and are encased in hermetic containment, physical inspection of the fuel is impossible once the reactor is sealed. The present invention provides the only known method for continuously verifying the isotopic composition of the fuel within such reactors without requiring any penetration of the containment vessel. Similarly, for research reactors that employ highly enriched uranium and are frequently refueled for isotope production, the system enables the detection of unauthorized fuel diversion during refueling operations, even when those operations occur under the cover of normal maintenance. The system’s ability to detect the temporary blocking of neutron leakage during refueling—such as when a fuel handling flask is positioned above the reactor core—is a critical capability that has been demonstrated in experimental trials, where the neutron count rate at a detector located above the reactor dropped by 50% precisely during refueling events, while detectors at other locations remained unaffected. This spatial discrimination capability allows the system to distinguish between legitimate operational activities and potential attempts to conceal material movement, making it uniquely suited for monitoring facilities with complex fuel handling procedures.

The economic feasibility of the system is another key advantage. Each neutron detector, including its housing, data acquisition system, and power supply, can be manufactured and deployed for less than $50,000, and the cost of operating a network of four to six detectors for continuous monitoring is comparable to the cost of a single annual physical inspection by an IAEA safeguards inspector. The system requires no specialized training to operate, and its outputs are presented in a clear, intuitive format that can be interpreted by both technical and non-technical personnel. The data generated by the system is immutable and timestamped, providing a legally defensible record of reactor behavior that can be used in international verification proceedings. The system also supports long-term data archiving and retrospective analysis, enabling safeguards authorities to re-examine historical data in light of new information or emerging suspicions, thereby extending the temporal reach of verification beyond the immediate monitoring window.

In summary, the invention represents a paradigm shift in nuclear safeguards by transforming neutron detection from a tool for reactor power estimation into a precise, isotopically sensitive instrument for verifying the integrity of nuclear fuel cycles. It provides a means to detect the diversion of fissile material at the kilogram scale within weeks, without requiring physical access to the reactor core, without altering reactor operations, and without relying on the cooperation or transparency of the facility operator. The system’s ability to distinguish between normal operational variations and malicious interference through the coordinated use of multiple detectors, combined with its robust analytical framework and economic scalability, makes it uniquely suited for deployment in the next generation of nuclear facilities, including sealed-core SMRs, research reactors, and heavy water reactors, where traditional safeguards methods are no longer adequate. The invention fulfills a critical need identified by the International Atomic Energy Agency and provides a technically sound, operationally practical, and legally defensible solution for ensuring the peaceful use of nuclear energy in the 21st century.
