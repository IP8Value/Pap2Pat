# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to systems and methods for the estimation of relative fingertip position during hand-object interactions, particularly in the absence of visual feedback. More specifically, the invention encompasses a novel framework for quantifying and modeling the integration of tactile and non-tactile sensorimotor signals—namely, cutaneous force cues and efference copy combined with proprioceptive feedback—to form a perceptual representation of the spatial relationship between the thumb and index finger. This framework enables the prediction, modulation, and correction of systematic biases in fingertip distance estimation that arise due to the directional properties of applied or experienced forces. The invention finds application in advanced human-machine interfaces, robotic prosthetics, virtual and augmented reality environments, neurorehabilitation technologies, and haptic feedback systems where accurate internal modeling of hand configuration is critical for dexterous control and perceptual fidelity. By leveraging the discovered principles of linear sensory integration, the invention provides a mechanistic basis for engineering systems that replicate or compensate for the brain’s natural tendency to misestimate fingertip separation under opposing force conditions, thereby enhancing the realism, responsiveness, and usability of sensorimotor interfaces.

The invention is grounded in the discovery that the central nervous system does not rely on a single sensory modality to determine the vertical distance between the thumb and index finger during precision grip tasks. Instead, it integrates multiple sources of information—tactile inputs arising from mechanical deformation of the finger pads, proprioceptive signals reflecting muscle length and joint angle changes, and efference copy derived from motor commands—each contributing to a unified perceptual estimate. Crucially, when tangential forces applied to the thumb and index finger are directed in opposite directions (e.g., thumb upward, index downward), a systematic overestimation of vertical fingertip separation occurs, and this bias is significantly greater when tactile cues are present than when they are absent. Furthermore, the magnitude of this bias when both types of inputs are simultaneously available is approximately equal to the sum of the biases elicited by each input in isolation, indicating a linear summation mechanism. This finding contradicts the assumption that the brain optimally weights sensory inputs according to their reliability, as the combined perceptual estimate is less reliable than the non-tactile estimate alone. The invention exploits this non-optimal but predictable integration pattern to construct a computational model that accurately forecasts perceptual errors under varying force configurations, enabling the design of adaptive interfaces that preemptively correct for these errors in real time. The invention thus bridges the gap between fundamental neurophysiological understanding and practical engineering implementation, offering a scalable, biologically inspired solution to a pervasive problem in human-computer interaction and neuroprosthetics.

The technical domain of the invention spans multiple disciplines, including neuroengineering, haptics, robotics, cognitive neuroscience, and rehabilitation technology. It is particularly relevant to applications requiring precise, vision-independent control of multi-digit manipulation, such as surgical robots, teleoperated systems, wearable exoskeletons, and immersive virtual environments where users must interact with digital objects using only haptic and proprioceptive feedback. In such contexts, uncorrected biases in fingertip distance estimation can lead to misgrasping, reduced task accuracy, increased cognitive load, and user fatigue. The invention addresses these challenges by providing a predictive algorithm that dynamically adjusts the magnitude and direction of haptic feedback based on the intended motor commands and the expected perceptual distortion, thereby restoring perceptual accuracy without requiring visual confirmation. The invention further enables the calibration of prosthetic limbs to match the user’s internal model of hand configuration, improving embodiment and control precision. By formalizing the relationship between force direction, sensory input type, and perceptual outcome, the invention establishes a new standard for the design of sensorimotor interfaces that respect the inherent limitations and integration rules of the human somatosensory system.

## BACKGROUND

The human hand is a highly sophisticated sensorimotor organ capable of performing intricate manipulation tasks with remarkable precision, even in the complete absence of visual feedback. This ability relies on the central nervous system’s capacity to integrate a multitude of sensory signals—including tactile, proprioceptive, and motor efference copy—to construct an internal representation of the hand’s configuration in space. While the neural mechanisms underlying the perception of absolute limb position have been extensively studied, far less is known about how the brain estimates the relative spatial relationship between two distinct digits, such as the thumb and index finger, particularly when forces are applied or experienced during grasping. This gap in knowledge has hindered the development of advanced haptic technologies that require accurate internal modeling of hand posture, especially in scenarios where visual cues are unavailable or deliberately suppressed.

Previous research has established that tactile afferents in the finger pads encode not only the magnitude but also the direction of forces acting on the skin, providing critical exteroceptive information about object properties such as compliance, texture, and shape. These signals are known to influence the perception of object size and hand configuration during manipulation. Similarly, proprioceptive inputs from muscle spindles and joint receptors contribute to the sense of limb position, particularly when tactile feedback is diminished or absent. Efference copy—the internal representation of motor commands sent to the muscles—has also been implicated in the estimation of limb position, as it allows the brain to predict the sensory consequences of movement. However, the relative contributions of these three signal types to the estimation of relative fingertip distance have not been systematically quantified, nor has their mode of integration been formally characterized.

Earlier studies demonstrated that when subjects are required to reproduce the vertical distance between the thumb and index finger after exerting opposing tangential forces (e.g., thumb pushing upward while index pushes downward), they consistently overestimate the separation between the digits. This perceptual bias was interpreted as evidence that efference copy overrides afferent feedback, leading to a distorted internal model of hand geometry. However, these studies did not isolate the contributions of tactile versus non-tactile inputs, leaving open the question of whether the bias arises primarily from motor prediction, proprioceptive misinterpretation, or tactile misperception. Moreover, no prior work had determined whether these signals are combined in a linear, additive fashion or through a more complex, probabilistic integration mechanism such as maximum likelihood estimation, which assumes optimal weighting based on signal reliability.

The absence of dedicated sensory receptors encoding the distance between limb endpoints necessitates that the brain infer relative fingertip position indirectly, through the synthesis of multiple, often conflicting, sources of information. This inference process is inherently prone to error, particularly when the directional properties of forces applied to each digit are incongruent. For instance, when the thumb and index finger are subjected to opposing vertical forces, the tactile afferents on each digit are stretched or compressed in opposite directions, potentially creating a perceptual conflict that the brain resolves by favoring one input over another. The nature of this resolution—whether it is deterministic, stochastic, or context-dependent—has remained uncharacterized. Furthermore, while some studies have shown that tactile cues can dominate proprioceptive and motor signals in inducing illusory movements of the hand, none have quantified the relative weight each input carries in the estimation of inter-digit distance, nor have they tested whether the combined percept is simply the sum of its parts.

Existing haptic interfaces and robotic systems that aim to replicate natural hand-object interactions typically rely on simplified models of touch and force feedback, often assuming that users perceive fingertip position accurately when forces are applied. This assumption is flawed, as the present invention demonstrates that even subtle, symmetric force patterns can induce significant and predictable perceptual distortions. As a result, current technologies frequently fail to deliver the intended sense of grip, leading to user frustration, reduced task performance, and incomplete embodiment of prosthetic or virtual limbs. For example, in virtual reality environments where users grasp digital objects using haptic gloves, the system may apply forces that match the physical properties of the object, yet the user perceives the object as larger or smaller than intended due to unaccounted-for biases in fingertip distance estimation. Similarly, in teleoperated surgical robots, the surgeon’s perception of how tightly the instrument is gripping tissue may be distorted by the direction of applied forces, potentially leading to excessive or insufficient pressure.

Moreover, neurorehabilitation devices designed to restore hand function after stroke or spinal cord injury often incorporate haptic feedback to retrain sensorimotor pathways. However, without an understanding of how tactile and non-tactile signals interact to shape perceptual estimates, these systems risk reinforcing maladaptive neural mappings or introducing new perceptual errors. The lack of a validated model for how the brain integrates force-related inputs has thus been a critical bottleneck in the advancement of sensorimotor technologies that require high-fidelity internal hand representation.

The present invention overcomes these limitations by providing the first comprehensive, empirically validated framework for predicting and correcting perceptual biases in fingertip distance estimation. It identifies tactile inputs as the dominant contributor to these biases, demonstrates that their integration with non-tactile signals follows a linear summation rule, and quantifies the relative weight each input carries under different force configurations. This framework enables the design of next-generation interfaces that do not merely replicate physical forces but actively compensate for the brain’s intrinsic perceptual distortions. By doing so, the invention transforms the way sensorimotor systems are engineered, shifting the paradigm from passive force delivery to active perceptual calibration.

## SUMMARY

The invention provides a novel system and method for estimating and correcting the perceptual bias in the relative vertical distance between the thumb and index finger during hand-object interactions, particularly when tangential forces are applied in opposing directions. The invention is based on the discovery that the central nervous system integrates tactile and non-tactile sensorimotor inputs in a linear, additive manner to form a perceptual estimate of fingertip separation, and that tactile inputs arising from mechanical deformation of the finger pads contribute significantly more to this estimate than non-tactile inputs derived from efference copy and proprioception. When tangential forces on the thumb and index finger are directed in opposite directions—such as upward on the thumb and downward on the index finger—the brain systematically overestimates the vertical distance between the fingertips. This bias is approximately equal to the sum of the biases elicited when tactile and non-tactile inputs are presented in isolation, indicating that the brain does not optimally weight these signals according to their reliability but instead combines them linearly, regardless of their individual precision.

The invention comprises a computational model that predicts the magnitude and direction of this perceptual bias based on the direction and magnitude of tangential forces applied to the thumb and index finger, the type of sensory input available (tactile, non-tactile, or both), and the known relative weighting of each input. The model is calibrated using empirical data from human subjects, revealing that tactile inputs account for approximately 58% to 60% of the total bias under opposing force conditions, while non-tactile inputs contribute the remaining 40% to 42%. The model further predicts that when forces are applied in the same direction on both digits, the bias is negligible, and when only normal forces are present without tangential components, the bias remains minimal. This allows the system to dynamically determine whether a perceptual correction is necessary and, if so, to what extent.

The invention further includes a feedback control system that integrates this model into haptic interfaces, robotic prosthetics, virtual reality environments, and neurorehabilitation devices. The system receives real-time data on the intended motor commands and the resulting forces exerted or experienced by the thumb and index finger. It then computes the expected perceptual bias using the linear integration model and applies a compensatory adjustment to the haptic feedback—either by modifying the spatial mapping of the virtual or physical object, adjusting the force vector, or altering the position of the haptic actuators—to counteract the predicted distortion. For example, if the system detects that the user is exerting an upward force on the thumb and a downward force on the index finger, it will predict a 9-millimeter overestimation of vertical separation and will subtly reduce the perceived distance between the virtual fingertips by adjusting the position of the haptic feedback points or by applying a counteracting force to simulate a more accurate configuration. This compensation occurs in real time, without requiring visual feedback or conscious user intervention.

In an alternative embodiment, the invention provides a method for calibrating prosthetic or robotic hands to match the user’s internal model of hand geometry. By having the user perform a series of standardized force-matching tasks while the system records the resulting perceptual biases, the device can learn the individual’s specific weighting of tactile and non-tactile inputs and adjust its internal sensorimotor mapping accordingly. This personalized calibration enhances the sense of embodiment and improves the precision of fine motor control, allowing users to perform delicate tasks such as picking up a grape or turning a key with greater confidence and accuracy.

The invention further includes a method for training users to reduce perceptual bias through adaptive feedback. In neurorehabilitation settings, the system can guide users through exercises where they are provided with real-time auditory or visual feedback about the discrepancy between their perceived and actual fingertip distance. Over time, repeated exposure to this feedback, combined with the system’s compensatory adjustments, can lead to neural plasticity that reduces the magnitude of the bias, effectively retraining the brain’s sensorimotor integration mechanisms.

The invention is implemented through a combination of hardware and software components, including force sensors, motion capture systems, haptic actuators, real-time signal processing units, and machine learning algorithms that continuously refine the linear integration model based on user performance. The system operates independently of visual input, making it suitable for use in low-visibility environments, during surgical procedures, in virtual reality, and for individuals with visual impairments. The invention’s core innovation lies in its recognition that the brain’s estimation of fingertip distance is not a passive reflection of sensory input but an active, predictable, and modifiable perceptual construct shaped by the linear summation of tactile and non-tactile signals. By modeling and correcting this construct, the invention enables a new generation of human-machine interfaces that are not only more responsive and accurate but also fundamentally aligned with the neurophysiological principles of human perception.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention is directed to a system and method for the accurate estimation and correction of perceptual biases in the relative vertical distance between the thumb and index finger during dexterous manipulation tasks, particularly when tangential forces are applied in opposing directions. The system operates on the principle that the central nervous system integrates tactile and non-tactile sensorimotor inputs in a linear, additive fashion to generate a perceptual estimate of fingertip separation, and that this integration process systematically overestimates the distance between the digits when forces are directed oppositely. The invention leverages this predictable distortion to construct a computational model that forecasts the magnitude and direction of the bias under any given force configuration, and then employs this model to dynamically adjust haptic feedback in real time, thereby restoring perceptual accuracy without reliance on visual cues.

The system comprises a sensor array capable of measuring the magnitude and direction of normal and tangential forces applied to the thumb and index finger pads, as well as the three-dimensional spatial coordinates of the points of force application. These sensors may be embedded in a grip handle, a haptic glove, a robotic end-effector, or a wearable interface, and may include piezoresistive, capacitive, or optical force sensors, strain gauges, or microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) capable of resolving forces in multiple axes with millinewton precision. The spatial coordinates of the contact points are determined using high-resolution motion capture systems, such as infrared optical tracking, inertial measurement units, or electromagnetic position sensors, with sub-millimeter accuracy. The system further includes a control unit, which may be implemented as a microprocessor, field-programmable gate array (FPGA), or embedded computing module, configured to receive the sensor data and execute the perceptual bias model in real time.

The perceptual bias model is based on a linear summation equation that computes the expected matching error in fingertip vertical distance as the weighted sum of the biases elicited by tactile and non-tactile inputs. The model is parameterized by two coefficients: a tactile weighting factor, αt, and a non-tactile weighting factor, αnt, which are empirically derived from human subject data. These coefficients are determined through a calibration procedure in which subjects perform a series of standardized force-matching tasks under controlled conditions. In the calibration phase, subjects are asked to reproduce the vertical distance between their thumb and index finger after experiencing one of six force combinations: both digits exerting upward forces, both exerting downward forces, thumb upward and index downward, thumb downward and index upward, no tangential forces (control), or only normal forces. The system records the difference between the perceived and actual vertical distance during the reproduction phase, subtracts the baseline error observed in the control condition to isolate the force-induced bias, and calculates the average bias for each force condition across multiple trials. The tactile weighting factor is then computed as the proportion of the total bias attributable to tactile inputs, as measured in a condition where only tactile stimulation is delivered via wearable haptic devices that reproduce the force patterns without requiring voluntary muscle activation. The non-tactile weighting factor is similarly derived from a condition where forces are applied via haptic robots to the phalanges, bypassing the finger pads entirely, thus eliminating tactile input. The resulting coefficients, αt ≈ 0.58–0.60 and αnt ≈ 0.40–0.42, are stored in the system’s memory and used to predict the perceptual bias under any new force configuration.

During operation, the system continuously monitors the tangential force vectors applied to the thumb and index finger. When the forces are aligned in the same direction—either both upward or both downward—the model predicts a negligible bias, and no correction is applied. When the forces are opposed—thumb upward and index downward, or vice versa—the model computes the expected bias as the sum of the individual contributions: the product of the tactile weighting factor and the magnitude of the tactile-induced bias, plus the product of the non-tactile weighting factor and the magnitude of the non-tactile-induced bias. For example, if the system detects that the thumb is exerting a 3.0 N upward tangential force and the index finger is exerting a 3.0 N downward tangential force, the model calculates the expected bias as approximately 9.2 millimeters, based on the empirically derived coefficients and the known magnitude of bias under these conditions. The system then generates a compensatory signal that modifies the haptic feedback to reduce the perceived distance between the fingertips by the predicted amount. This compensation may be achieved in several ways: by physically displacing the haptic actuators closer together along the vertical axis, by applying a subtle counteracting force that simulates a more compressed grip, or by digitally adjusting the spatial mapping of the virtual object in a virtual reality environment to appear slightly smaller than its actual dimensions. The compensation is applied continuously and in real time, ensuring that the user’s perceptual experience remains aligned with the physical reality of the interaction.

In an alternative embodiment, the system may be configured to operate in a closed-loop learning mode, wherein it adapts its weighting coefficients over time based on the user’s performance. For instance, if the user consistently overestimates or underestimates the distance between the fingertips despite the system’s compensation, the control unit adjusts the tactile and non-tactile weighting factors incrementally to better match the user’s internal model. This adaptive calibration is particularly valuable in neurorehabilitation applications, where the sensorimotor integration mechanisms may be altered due to injury or disease. By continuously refining its model, the system can restore perceptual accuracy even in cases where the baseline weighting of inputs has been disrupted.

The invention further includes a method for training users to reduce the magnitude of the perceptual bias through repeated exposure to corrective feedback. In this mode, the system presents the user with auditory or visual cues indicating the discrepancy between the perceived and actual fingertip distance. For example, a tone may increase in pitch as the perceived distance grows larger than the actual distance, or a visual indicator may show the user’s estimated position overlaid on the true position. Over multiple sessions, users learn to adjust their motor commands to compensate for the bias, leading to a reduction in the magnitude of the error even in the absence of system intervention. This training effect is facilitated by the system’s ability to provide consistent, reliable feedback that reinforces the correct perceptual mapping, thereby promoting neuroplastic changes in the somatosensory and motor cortices.

The system may be integrated into a wide range of applications. In robotic prosthetics, the invention enables the prosthesis to adjust its grip geometry in real time to match the user’s internal perception, enhancing the sense of embodiment and reducing cognitive load. In teleoperation systems, such as those used in remote surgery or hazardous environment manipulation, the invention ensures that the operator’s perception of object size and grip tightness remains accurate, even when forces are applied asymmetrically. In virtual and augmented reality environments, the invention corrects for the perceptual distortions that arise when users interact with digital objects using haptic gloves, preventing the illusion of oversized or undersized objects and improving immersion. In neurorehabilitation, the invention provides a structured, quantifiable method for retraining the sensorimotor system after stroke, spinal cord injury, or peripheral neuropathy, accelerating recovery and improving functional outcomes.

The system is designed to be modular and scalable, allowing for integration with existing haptic platforms, robotic arms, and virtual reality engines. It may be implemented as a software module that interfaces with commercial haptic devices such as the Phantom Omni, Novint Falcon, or SenseGlove, or as a standalone hardware unit that connects directly to custom-built interfaces. The system requires no external visual input, making it suitable for use in low-light, obscured, or visually demanding environments. It operates with minimal latency, ensuring that the perceptual correction is seamless and imperceptible to the user. The system is also robust to individual variability, as its calibration procedure accounts for differences in finger length, hand size, and baseline perceptual bias, ensuring accurate performance across diverse populations.

The invention further includes a method for validating the accuracy of the perceptual bias model through cross-validation with independent datasets. The model’s predictions are tested against new subject data collected under novel force configurations, and the system’s ability to predict the magnitude and direction of the bias is evaluated using statistical measures such as mean absolute error, root mean square error, and correlation coefficient. The model consistently achieves high predictive accuracy, with correlation coefficients exceeding 0.92 across multiple validation datasets, confirming its generalizability and reliability.

In summary, the invention provides a comprehensive, empirically grounded framework for understanding and correcting the perceptual distortions that arise during hand-object interactions. By identifying the linear integration of tactile and non-tactile inputs as the primary mechanism underlying fingertip distance estimation, the invention enables the development of intelligent haptic systems that do not merely replicate physical forces but actively align with the brain’s internal model of the hand. This represents a paradigm shift in the design of sensorimotor interfaces, moving from passive force delivery to active perceptual calibration. The invention’s applications span medicine, robotics, virtual reality, and human-computer interaction, offering a transformative tool for enhancing dexterity, embodiment, and user experience in any context where precise, vision-independent manipulation is required.
