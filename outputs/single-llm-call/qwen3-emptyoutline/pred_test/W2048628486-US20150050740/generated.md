# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to a novel method for enabling efficient genetic transformation of bacterial strains that are refractory to conventional transformation techniques due to the presence of complex and multiple restriction-modification (R-M) systems. Specifically, the invention provides a mimicking-of-DNA-methylation-patterns (MoDMP) pipeline that utilizes a genetically engineered Escherichia coli host strain devoid of all known endogenous restriction-modification systems and orphan methyltransferases, in which methyltransferase genes derived from target bacterial strains are heterologously expressed to replicate the native DNA methylation signatures of those strains. Plasmid DNA prepared from this engineered host exhibits methylation patterns indistinguishable from those of the target organism, thereby evading recognition and degradation by the target’s restriction enzymes. This approach enables high-efficiency transformation of previously intractable bacterial species, including Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, chemoheterotrophs, and chemolithoautotrophs, regardless of the number or type of R-M systems they harbor. The method is broadly applicable to any bacterial strain with a sequenced genome and known or predicted methyltransferase genes, and it overcomes longstanding limitations in microbial genetics that have impeded functional studies, metabolic engineering, and industrial strain development in non-model organisms.

## BACKGROUND ART

For decades, the ability to genetically manipulate bacteria has been a cornerstone of molecular biology, microbiology, and biotechnology. The development of transformation protocols for model organisms such as Escherichia coli, Bacillus subtilis, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa has enabled groundbreaking advances in gene function analysis, synthetic biology, and industrial fermentation. However, a vast majority of bacterial species—particularly those isolated from environmental niches or with specialized metabolic capabilities—remain genetically intractable. This limitation stems primarily from the presence of restriction-modification systems, which serve as innate immune defenses against foreign DNA. These systems consist of restriction endonucleases that cleave unmethylated or aberrantly methylated DNA and cognate methyltransferases that protect the host genome by methylating specific nucleotide sequences. While these systems are evolutionarily conserved and widespread, their activity presents a formidable barrier to the introduction of exogenous plasmid DNA, especially when multiple systems are present within a single strain.

The problem is particularly acute in industrially and ecologically significant strains such as Nitrobacter hamburgensis X14, Bacillus cereus ATCC 10987, and Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TA208. Nitrobacter hamburgensis X14, a key player in the global nitrogen cycle due to its ability to oxidize nitrite to nitrate, has been studied for over a century but has never been successfully transformed using conventional methods despite genomic sequencing revealing eleven putative restriction-modification systems. Bacillus cereus ATCC 10987, a non-pathogenic strain closely related to Bacillus anthracis, has resisted routine genetic manipulation despite the availability of transformation protocols for other B. cereus strains, largely due to the presence of multiple restriction enzymes that degrade incoming plasmid DNA. Similarly, Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TA208, a high-yield industrial producer of guanosine, exhibits extremely low transformation efficiencies with plasmids prepared from standard E. coli hosts, suggesting the presence of a Type IV restriction system that recognizes methylated DNA with non-native patterns.

Prior attempts to overcome these barriers have included the use of E. coli strains lacking Dam and Dcm methyltransferases, which prevent methylation at GATC and CCWGG sites, respectively. While this approach has enabled transformation of some strains, such as certain Bacillus and Corynebacterium species, it fails in organisms that recognize methylation patterns beyond these two motifs or that possess Type IV restriction systems targeting methylated DNA regardless of its origin. Other strategies have involved the in vitro methylation of plasmid DNA using purified methyltransferases, heat inactivation of restriction enzymes, or co-transformation with restriction inhibitors such as the Ocr protein. However, these methods are labor-intensive, often yield inconsistent results, require prior knowledge of the specific restriction enzyme specificity, and are not scalable for high-throughput applications. Furthermore, the use of restriction-deficient mutants of the target organism is frequently impractical due to the essential roles of some R-M systems in cellular processes such as DNA replication, repair, and phage defense, making their deletion lethal or severely deleterious.

Recent advances in DNA sequencing and bioinformatics have allowed for the identification of putative methyltransferase genes in numerous bacterial genomes, yet the functional validation of these enzymes and their integration into transformation protocols has remained fragmented and inefficient. Moreover, the expression of foreign methyltransferases in E. coli has historically been problematic due to the presence of endogenous methyltransferases that alter plasmid methylation patterns and the presence of orphan restriction systems such as Mrr, McrA, and McrBC, which recognize and cleave DNA containing non-native methylation marks, leading to host cell death or poor expression. Consequently, no generalizable, robust, and scalable method has existed to overcome the restriction barrier in bacteria harboring multiple, diverse, and poorly characterized R-M systems. The field has thus been constrained to a small subset of genetically tractable organisms, leaving the vast majority of microbial diversity inaccessible to direct genetic interrogation.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and universally applicable method for overcoming the restriction barrier in bacterial strains that are refractory to genetic transformation due to the presence of multiple restriction-modification systems. The invention is based on the discovery that the DNA methylation patterns of a target bacterium can be faithfully replicated in a genetically engineered Escherichia coli host strain that has been systematically stripped of all known endogenous restriction-modification systems and orphan methyltransferases. By introducing and co-expressing multiple active methyltransferase genes from the target organism into this engineered E. coli host, the plasmid DNA produced therein acquires methylation signatures that are indistinguishable from those of the native host genome. When this DNA is introduced into the target bacterium via transformation, it is no longer recognized as foreign by the host’s restriction enzymes, thereby enabling high-efficiency genetic manipulation.

The invention comprises three essential components: (1) a genetically modified Escherichia coli strain, designated EC135, which lacks all six characterized endogenous restriction-modification systems—including Dam, Dcm, EcoKI, EcoRI, EcoB, and EcoC—and is further engineered to be deficient in the orphan restriction systems Mrr, McrA, and McrBC, thereby preventing self-degradation upon introduction of foreign methylation patterns; (2) a modular plasmid system capable of co-expressing multiple methyltransferase genes from a target bacterium, assembled rapidly and efficiently using yeast-based homologous recombination; and (3) a transformation protocol that utilizes plasmid DNA methylated in the engineered E. coli host to achieve transformation efficiencies orders of magnitude higher than those obtained with conventional methods.

The method enables the successful transformation of bacterial strains that were previously considered genetically intractable, including Nitrobacter hamburgensis X14, Bacillus cereus ATCC 10987, and Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TA208. In each case, plasmids prepared from the engineered E. coli host expressing the appropriate methyltransferase repertoire were transformed with efficiencies exceeding 10⁵ to 10⁷ colony-forming units per microgram of DNA, representing increases of up to 10⁴-fold compared to plasmids prepared from standard E. coli hosts. The method further enabled the first reported genetic transformation of Nitrobacter hamburgensis X14, the direct inactivation of chromosomal genes using non-replicative integration plasmids in Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TA208, and the expression of fluorescent reporters in all three strains, confirming stable maintenance and transcriptional activity of introduced genetic material.

The invention is not limited to the specific strains or methyltransferases described herein. It is broadly applicable to any bacterial species for which the genome sequence is known and for which putative methyltransferase genes can be identified, whether they belong to Type I, Type II, Type III, or Type IV restriction-modification systems. The method is rapid, scalable, and does not require prior knowledge of the exact restriction enzyme specificity, as the co-expression of all identified active methyltransferases ensures comprehensive methylation mimicry. Furthermore, the use of a single, standardized E. coli host strain eliminates the need for strain-specific optimization and enables the development of standardized plasmid libraries for high-throughput genetic manipulation of diverse bacterial species. This represents a paradigm shift in microbial genetics, transforming previously inaccessible organisms into tractable systems for functional genomics, metabolic engineering, and industrial biotechnology.

## SPECIFIC EMBODIMENTS

### Example 1

The first embodiment of the invention involves the construction of a genetically engineered Escherichia coli strain designated EC135, which serves as the foundational platform for the mimicking-of-DNA-methylation-patterns (MoDMP) pipeline. This strain is derived from the E. coli TOP10 background, a commonly used laboratory strain known for its high transformation efficiency and genetic stability. The construction of EC135 involved a series of precise, sequential genetic deletions to eliminate all known endogenous restriction-modification systems and orphan methyltransferases that could interfere with the faithful replication of foreign methylation patterns. Initially, the dam and dcm genes, encoding the N6-methyladenine and N4-methylcytosine methyltransferases responsible for methylation at GATC and CCWGG sequences, respectively, were deleted using homologous recombination techniques. To prevent the inviability associated with the dam recA double mutant phenotype, a wild-type recA allele was reintroduced into the chromosome prior to dam inactivation, thereby preserving DNA repair capacity without reintroducing methylation activity. Subsequently, the ecoKI, ecoRI, ecoB, and ecoC gene clusters, encoding the Type I restriction-modification systems, were excised from the genome using λ Red-mediated recombination and antibiotic selection. The mrr, mcrA, and mcrBC genes, which encode restriction enzymes that recognize and cleave DNA containing non-native methylation patterns, were also deleted to ensure that the host strain would not degrade its own chromosomal DNA upon expression of heterologous methyltransferases. The resulting strain, EC135, was validated through a combination of phenotypic assays, restriction digestion profiles, and whole-genome sequencing to confirm the absence of all targeted genes and the absence of compensatory mutations. The strain was shown to be viable, genetically stable over multiple generations, and incapable of methylating DNA at any of the known E. coli methylation sites. Importantly, when transformed with plasmids carrying methyltransferase genes from other organisms, EC135 exhibited no signs of growth inhibition or DNA degradation, in stark contrast to wild-type E. coli strains, which rapidly succumb to self-restriction when expressing foreign methyltransferases. This engineered host provides a clean, neutral platform for the expression of methyltransferases from any bacterial source, enabling the production of plasmid DNA with methylation patterns that are tailored to the target organism without interference from endogenous E. coli systems.

### Example 2

The second embodiment of the invention involves the identification, cloning, and functional characterization of methyltransferase genes from target bacterial strains that are refractory to genetic transformation. Using genomic sequence data from Nitrobacter hamburgensis X14, Bacillus cereus ATCC 10987, and Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TA208, a total of twenty-four putative methyltransferase genes were selected for analysis based on homology to known methyltransferase families and their genomic context within restriction-modification loci. These genes were amplified by polymerase chain reaction using gene-specific primers designed from annotated genome sequences and cloned into the pBAD43 expression vector under the control of an arabinose-inducible promoter. The resulting constructs were transformed into the EC135 host strain, and methyltransferase activity was assessed using a southwestern dot blot assay with antibodies specific for N6-methyladenine (m6A), N4-methylcytosine (m4C), and 5-methylcytosine (m5C). Thirteen of the twenty-four tested genes exhibited detectable methylation activity, as evidenced by the appearance of distinct immunoreactive signals corresponding to the presence of modified bases in the total genomic DNA of the expressing strains. For four of the weaker signals—BCE_0392, Nham_0582, Nham_0803, and Nham_3225—liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis was performed to detect the presence of N6-methyl-2′-deoxyadenosine (m6dA) in enzymatically digested DNA samples. This approach confirmed the activity of BCE_0392, Nham_0582, and Nham_3225 as adenine methyltransferases, with m6dA peaks observed at the expected retention time and mass-to-charge ratio. The activity of Nham_0803 remained ambiguous, as no m6dA was detected, but a slight but reproducible increase in dot blot signal suggested the possibility of a novel or low-abundance modification. Notably, the expression of Nham_0569 in EC135 resulted in severe growth retardation, with final biomass reduced by approximately 60% compared to controls, indicating potential toxicity due to methylation of endogenous E. coli sequences overlapping with Dam recognition sites. Subsequent restriction enzyme digestion assays confirmed that Nham_0569 modifies GATC sequences, consistent with its interference with replication and repair processes. All active methyltransferases were then subjected to specificity analysis using plasmid DNA challenged with a panel of restriction enzymes, revealing that several enzymes exhibited broad specificity, modifying multiple recognition sequences. For instance, BCE_0393 from B. cereus ATCC 10987 was found to protect DNA from cleavage by at least twelve different restriction enzymes, indicating its ability to methylate GCWGC, GGCC, CCGG, GGNNCC, GCGCGC, GGWCC, and CCWGG sequences. This multi-specificity renders BCE_0393 a central component in the MoDMP pipeline for B. cereus ATCC 10987. The functional characterization of these methyltransferases established a comprehensive catalog of methylation activities that could be harnessed for methylation pattern mimicry in the target strains.

### Example 3

The third embodiment of the invention involves the assembly of multi-gene operons containing multiple active methyltransferase genes from each target strain, enabling the simultaneous expression of all necessary methylation activities in the EC135 host strain. To achieve this, a yeast-based homologous recombination system was employed to rapidly and efficiently assemble up to eight methyltransferase genes into a single plasmid backbone. The pWYE724 vector was constructed by integrating the CEN6 replicon and TRP1 selection marker from Saccharomyces cerevisiae into the pBAD43 plasmid, allowing for replication and selection in yeast. PCR fragments encoding each active methyltransferase gene were amplified with 50-base overlapping ends complementary to adjacent genes and the linearized pWYE724 vector. These fragments were co-transformed into S. cerevisiae DAY414, where homologous recombination assembled the full operon in a single step. Transformants were selected on synthetic complete medium lacking tryptophan, and plasmids were rescued into E. coli TOP10 for amplification and verification by restriction digestion and DNA sequencing. Three distinct plasmids were generated: pM.Bam, containing the four active methyltransferases from B. amyloliquefaciens TA208; pM.Bce, containing five active methyltransferases from B. cereus ATCC 10987; and pM.Nham, containing four active methyltransferases from N. hamburgensis X14. Each plasmid was designed with optimized ribosome binding sites to ensure balanced expression of all components in E. coli. When introduced into the EC135 host strain and induced with arabinose, these plasmids conferred methylation patterns that were detectable by southwestern dot blot assays, showing the presence of m6A, m4C, and m5C modifications corresponding to the combined activities of the co-expressed methyltransferases. The methylation profiles of the plasmid DNA prepared from these strains were compared to those of the native target organisms, revealing a high degree of similarity in modification patterns, although the MoDMP hosts exhibited a broader range of methylation sites due to the constitutive overexpression of methyltransferases that are only partially expressed or regulated in their native hosts. This comprehensive mimicry was critical to achieving high transformation efficiencies, as plasmids prepared from the MoDMP hosts escaped restriction by all known and predicted restriction enzymes in the target strains. The use of yeast-based assembly allowed for rapid, modular construction of new MoDMP plasmids for any bacterial strain with a sequenced genome, making the pipeline highly adaptable and scalable for industrial and research applications.

### Example 4

The fourth embodiment of the invention involves the application of the MoDMP pipeline to achieve high-efficiency genetic transformation of previously intractable bacterial strains. Plasmids carrying selectable markers, replication origins, and reporter genes were transformed into the EC135 host strains harboring the pM.Bam, pM.Bce, or pM.Nham plasmids, and were allowed to undergo in vivo methylation under arabinose induction. The resulting methylated plasmids were then purified and introduced into B. amyloliquefaciens TA208, B. cereus ATCC 10987, and Nitrobacter hamburgensis X14 using electroporation protocols optimized for each strain. In B. amyloliquefaciens TA208, plasmids prepared from standard E. coli TOP10 or EC135 hosts failed to produce any transformants, confirming the presence of a Type IV restriction system that recognizes Dam and Dcm methylation. However, plasmids prepared from the MoDMP host expressing the four B. amyloliquefaciens methyltransferases achieved transformation efficiencies of up to 3×10⁶ colony-forming units per microgram of DNA, representing a 10⁴-fold increase over controls. Notably, two plasmids previously deemed untransformable—pAD123 and pDG148StuI—were successfully introduced at efficiencies exceeding 1×10⁵ CFU/µg DNA. In B. cereus ATCC 10987, transformation efficiencies with pMK4 and pMK3 plasmids increased by 10³- to 10⁴-fold when prepared from the MoDMP host, reaching up to 2×10⁷ CFU/µg DNA, demonstrating that the MoDMP pipeline effectively overcomes the restriction barrier even in strains where the native restriction systems are not fully characterized. In Nitrobacter hamburgensis X14, the MoDMP pipeline enabled the first successful genetic transformation of this strain, with plasmids carrying the gfpmut3a gene under the control of a native promoter being introduced at sufficient efficiency to yield detectable green fluorescent protein expression. The transformed cells were enriched through serial subculturing and confirmed by plasmid rescue into E. coli, restriction digestion, and fluorescence microscopy. Flow cytometry revealed that 50.37% of the population exhibited GFP fluorescence, indicating a high rate of successful transformation and stable plasmid maintenance. Furthermore, the MoDMP pipeline enabled the direct inactivation of the upp gene in B. amyloliquefaciens TA208 using a non-replicative integration plasmid, pWYE748, which recombined with the chromosomal locus at a frequency sufficient to yield chloramphenicol-resistant clones. These clones were verified by PCR and sequencing and demonstrated resistance to 5-fluorouracil, confirming the functional knockout of the upp gene. The success of this approach demonstrates that the MoDMP pipeline not only enables plasmid transformation but also facilitates chromosomal engineering, gene knockouts, and reporter expression in organisms previously considered genetically inaccessible. The versatility of this method across Gram-positive and Gram-negative, chemoheterotrophic and chemolithoautotrophic bacteria underscores its broad applicability and transformative potential for microbial genetics.
