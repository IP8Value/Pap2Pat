## BACKGROUND

The analysis of gene-expression profiles derived from heterogeneous tissue samples has long been a cornerstone of molecular biology and translational medicine. Tissues such as tumors, inflamed organs, and developing organs are composed of multiple distinct cell types, each contributing its own unique transcriptional signature to the overall measured expression profile. When gene-expression data is obtained from such tissues without prior separation of cell populations, the resulting signal represents a composite of all constituent cell types, weighted by their relative abundance. This composite signal obscures the true biological state of individual cell populations, making it difficult to distinguish between changes in cell-type composition and genuine changes in gene regulation within a specific cell type. For example, an observed upregulation of an immune-related gene in a tumor biopsy might reflect an increase in infiltrating T cells rather than altered expression within the tumor cells themselves. Without the ability to disentangle these contributions, downstream analyses such as biomarker discovery, prognostic modeling, and therapeutic target identification become inherently ambiguous and prone to false conclusions.

Traditional approaches to resolving this issue have relied on experimental cell separation techniques such as laser-capture microdissection, fluorescence-activated cell sorting, or magnetic bead-based isolation. While these methods can yield pure populations of cells for downstream profiling, they are labor-intensive, require substantial quantities of starting material, and often result in RNA degradation or amplification artifacts that compromise data quality. Moreover, these techniques are frequently incompatible with archived clinical samples, which may be fixed, fragmented, or limited in volume. Single-cell RNA sequencing has emerged as a powerful alternative, enabling transcriptomic profiling at the individual cell level. However, its high cost, technical complexity, and limited scalability make it impractical for large cohort studies, retrospective analyses of existing datasets, or routine clinical applications. As a result, the vast majority of publicly available gene-expression datasets—numbering in the tens of thousands and encompassing millions of samples—remain as unseparated mixtures, representing a largely untapped reservoir of biological information.

To address this gap, several computational methods have been developed to deconvolute mixed gene-expression profiles into their constituent cell-type signatures and proportions. These methods typically rely on a linear mixing model, wherein the observed expression of each gene in each sample is assumed to be a weighted sum of the expression levels of that gene across all constituent cell types, with weights corresponding to the relative abundance of each cell type in the sample. This model has been shown to be biologically plausible and mathematically tractable under a variety of conditions. However, all existing computational methods require prior knowledge of either the number of cell types present, their identities, or their purified gene-expression signatures. Some methods require all three types of information, while others require at least two. In practice, such information is rarely available for most datasets. Clinical biopsies, epidemiological studies, and archived tissue collections are rarely accompanied by cell-type enumeration, purification, or validation. Even in studies where cell-type proportions are estimated by pathologists, those estimates are often qualitative, imprecise, or limited to broad categories such as “tumor” versus “stroma.” Consequently, existing deconvolution methods are effectively unusable for the overwhelming majority of real-world gene-expression datasets, rendering them incapable of fulfilling their intended purpose of unlocking the hidden biological information contained within heterogeneous samples.

Furthermore, existing methods are not designed to discover the number or identity of cell types. They assume these parameters as fixed inputs, and if the input is incorrect—even by a single cell type—the accuracy of the resulting deconvolution deteriorates significantly. A misestimation of the number of cell types leads to overfitting or underfitting of the model, producing spurious signatures or merging distinct cell populations into a single artifact. A misidentification of cell types results in the assignment of gene-expression profiles to incorrect biological entities, leading to erroneous biological interpretations. No prior method has demonstrated the ability to autonomously determine the correct number of cell types in a tissue sample from the mixed data alone, nor to identify which of a set of candidate cell types are actually present. This fundamental limitation has prevented the widespread adoption of computational deconvolution in the analysis of legacy and large-scale datasets, where experimental metadata is incomplete or absent.

The need for a method capable of blind deconvolution—i.e., one that requires no prior knowledge beyond a general hypothesis of possible cell types—is therefore urgent and unmet. Such a method would transform existing gene-expression repositories into powerful tools for cell-type-specific discovery, enabling researchers to re-analyze decades of accumulated data with unprecedented precision. It would allow for the identification of novel cell populations, the discovery of context-specific gene-expression signatures, and the reconstruction of cellular dynamics in disease progression without the need for new experiments. The invention described herein fulfills this need by introducing a novel algorithmic framework that integrates non-negative matrix factorization, symmetric Kullback-Leibler divergence, majority voting across random initializations, and class-based signature aggregation to achieve blind identification, separation, and quantification of cell types in heterogeneous gene-expression data.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel, fully automated, and computationally robust method for the blind separation of heterogeneous gene-expression data into its constituent cell-type-specific expression profiles and their relative proportions within each sample, without requiring prior knowledge of the number of cell types, their identities, or their exact gene-expression signatures. The method operates on a mixed gene-expression matrix derived from tissue samples and a reference matrix containing purified gene-expression signatures of candidate cell types, which may be sourced from public databases and need not be disease-specific, tissue-specific, or experimentally matched to the target dataset. The invention comprises a multi-stage algorithm that first generates an initial estimate of cell-type signatures using non-negative matrix factorization, then identifies the true number of cell types and their biological identities by computing the symmetric Kullback-Leibler divergence between the estimated signatures and the reference signatures, and finally computes the relative proportions of each identified cell type in each sample using non-negative least squares. The algorithm further incorporates a majority voting mechanism to enhance robustness against the non-convex nature of non-negative matrix factorization and a class-based aggregation procedure to improve accuracy when multiple reference signatures exist for a single cell type or when reference signatures are noisy or imperfect. The result is a system capable of autonomously determining the number of cell types present in a tissue sample, identifying which of the candidate cell types are actually present, extracting their purified gene-expression signatures, and quantifying their relative abundances in each sample—all without any experimental purification, cell sorting, or prior enumeration. The invention is applicable to any gene-expression dataset, regardless of tissue origin, disease state, or platform, and is particularly suited for the re-analysis of archived microarray and RNA-seq datasets where experimental metadata is incomplete or absent. The method achieves high accuracy in both signature recovery and proportion estimation, outperforming existing methods that require complete prior knowledge, and is uniquely capable of discovering novel or unexpected cell populations within heterogeneous tissues. The invention enables the extraction of cell-type-specific biological insights from existing large-scale datasets, transforming them from composite signals into high-resolution cellular maps.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Linear Model for Separation of Gene-Expression

The foundation of the present invention lies in the mathematical representation of gene-expression data from heterogeneous tissues as a linear combination of the expression profiles of its constituent cell types. This linear model posits that the observed gene-expression value for a given gene in a given tissue sample is the sum of the expression levels of that gene across all cell types present in the sample, each weighted by the relative abundance of that cell type within the sample. Mathematically, this relationship is expressed as a matrix equation, where the mixed expression matrix M, comprising m genes and n samples, is decomposed into two non-negative matrices: G, the cell-type-specific gene-expression matrix, and C, the cell-type proportion matrix. The dimensions of G are m by k, where k represents the number of distinct cell types, and the dimensions of C are k by n, such that each column of C corresponds to the relative proportions of the k cell types in a single sample, with the constraint that the proportions sum to unity. The product of G and C yields the mixed expression matrix M, such that M = G × C. This decomposition is underdetermined in the absence of constraints, as multiple combinations of G and C could, in theory, produce the same M. However, the non-negativity constraint on both G and C, combined with the requirement that the columns of C sum to one, imposes sufficient structure to render the problem solvable under appropriate conditions. Importantly, the linear model does not require that the reference gene-expression signatures used to initialize the algorithm be identical to the true signatures present in the tissue; rather, it is sufficient that they originate from the same biological cell type, even if derived from a different tissue, species, disease state, or experimental platform. This flexibility is critical to the practical utility of the invention, as it allows the use of publicly available gene-expression profiles from diverse sources, such as the Gene Expression Omnibus, without the need for custom purification or experimental validation. The model further assumes that the number of cell types k is less than or equal to the number of samples n, a condition that is almost universally satisfied in real-world datasets, where hundreds or thousands of samples are profiled while the number of distinct cell types in any given tissue rarely exceeds ten. The linear model has been validated in numerous prior studies and is widely accepted as a biologically reasonable approximation of gene-expression mixing in heterogeneous tissues. The innovation of the present invention does not lie in the adoption of this model, which is well established, but in the development of a method that can estimate both G and C from M without any prior knowledge of k or the identities of the cell types, thereby overcoming the fundamental limitation that has prevented the widespread application of this model to real-world datasets.

### The Relation to Hyper Spectral Imaging

The mathematical structure of the gene-expression deconvolution problem is formally equivalent to the problem of spectral unmixing in hyper-spectral imaging, a field that has developed sophisticated algorithms for identifying the constituent materials of a scene based on the spectral signatures captured by remote sensing instruments. In hyper-spectral imaging, each pixel in an image corresponds to a spectrum that represents the combined reflectance of multiple materials present at that location, such as minerals, vegetation, or man-made substances. The goal is to determine the number of distinct materials present, their spectral signatures, and their relative abundances in each pixel. This is precisely analogous to the gene-expression problem, where each sample corresponds to a pixel, each gene corresponds to a spectral band, each cell type corresponds to a material, and the gene-expression profile of a cell type corresponds to its spectral signature. The pioneering work of Piper et al. demonstrated that this problem could be solved using non-negative matrix factorization, where the spectral data matrix is decomposed into an end-member matrix representing the material signatures and a proportion matrix representing the fractional abundance of each material in each pixel. Crucially, Piper et al. introduced a method that leverages a library of known spectral signatures—obtained from laboratory measurements of pure materials—to guide the identification of the true end-members in the mixed data, without requiring that the library signatures be identical to the true signatures. This approach is directly transferable to gene-expression deconvolution, as it allows the use of reference signatures from public databases to constrain the search space of possible cell-type signatures, even when those references are imperfect or derived from unrelated experiments. The present invention adapts this hyper-spectral imaging framework to the biological domain by implementing the same core algorithmic principles, while introducing critical modifications to account for the unique characteristics of gene-expression data. Unlike spectral data, which is often continuous and smooth, gene-expression data is discrete, noisy, and subject to batch effects, platform-specific biases, and normalization artifacts. Moreover, biological cell types often exhibit subtle differences in gene-expression profiles that are not easily distinguishable by simple distance metrics such as Euclidean distance. The symmetric Kullback-Leibler divergence, which measures the difference between two probability distributions, was selected as the optimal distance metric because it is inherently suited to non-negative data and penalizes mismatches in the shape of expression profiles more effectively than other metrics. The adaptation of this hyper-spectral imaging framework to gene-expression analysis represents a paradigm shift in computational biology, enabling the application of decades of advances in remote sensing to the analysis of complex biological tissues. The invention thus bridges two previously disconnected domains—remote sensing and molecular biology—by demonstrating that the same mathematical principles that allow us to identify the composition of distant satellites can be used to unravel the cellular architecture of human tumors and inflamed tissues.

### Algorithm

The algorithm of the present invention is a multi-stage, self-contained computational pipeline designed to perform blind deconvolution of heterogeneous gene-expression data. The input to the algorithm consists of two matrices: M, the mixed gene-expression matrix of dimensions m by n, where m is the number of genes and n is the number of samples, and L, the reference signature matrix of dimensions m by p, where p is the number of candidate cell types represented in the reference library. Both matrices are non-negative and normalized such that the sum of the expression values in each column equals its mean, ensuring that the algorithm operates on relative expression profiles rather than absolute intensities. The algorithm proceeds in three sequential stages: initialization, estimation of cell-type number and identity, and estimation of cell-type proportions. In the initialization stage, the algorithm generates two intermediate matrices, H and W, which serve as estimates of the proportion matrix C and the signature matrix G, respectively. The matrix H is initialized with random values drawn uniformly from the interval [0,1], while the matrix W is initialized with the reference signature matrix L, with each column scaled to sum to one. The algorithm then applies non-negative matrix factorization to minimize the Frobenius norm of the difference between M and the product of H and W, subject to the constraints that all elements of H and W are non-negative and that the columns of W sum to one. This yields an initial estimate of the cell-type signatures and their proportions. In the second stage, the algorithm determines the true number of cell types and their identities by computing the symmetric Kullback-Leibler divergence between each column of the estimated W matrix and each column of the reference L matrix. For each column in W, the algorithm identifies the column in L with the smallest symmetric Kullback-Leibler divergence, thereby establishing a correspondence between the estimated signature and the most similar reference signature. The number of distinct reference signatures that are matched to at least one estimated signature is then taken as the estimated number of cell types, k. The estimated signature matrix G is constructed by selecting only those columns of W that correspond to a matched reference signature, discarding any redundant or spurious signatures that do not align with any reference. In the third stage, the algorithm computes the final proportion matrix C by solving a non-negative least squares problem, using the estimated signature matrix G and the mixed matrix M as inputs, with the constraint that all elements of C are non-negative. The rows of C are then normalized to sum to one, yielding the final proportions of each cell type in each sample. The algorithm outputs the final signature matrix G and proportion matrix C, which together fully describe the cellular composition of the tissue samples. The algorithm is deterministic in its mathematical operations but incorporates stochastic elements in its initialization, necessitating the use of majority voting and class-based aggregation to ensure robustness and reproducibility.

### Mining Purified Signatures

The practical utility of the invention is predicated on the availability of purified gene-expression signatures for candidate cell types, which serve as the biological anchors for the deconvolution process. These signatures are not required to be derived from the same tissue, disease state, species, or experimental platform as the target dataset; they need only represent the same biological cell type. This critical feature enables the use of the vast and growing collection of publicly available gene-expression datasets deposited in repositories such as the Gene Expression Omnibus, where thousands of purified cell-type profiles have been generated across diverse biological contexts. To mine these signatures, a user begins by identifying the tissue or disease of interest and consulting the scientific literature to determine the likely cell types present. A simple search of the repository using the name of the cell type and the species of origin yields a list of candidate signatures. The user may then download the raw data files, normalize them using standard methods such as RMA or quantile normalization, and compile them into the reference matrix L. The invention allows for the inclusion of multiple signatures for the same cell type, which may be derived from different studies, platforms, or experimental conditions. This flexibility is essential, as no single reference signature is guaranteed to be perfectly representative of the true signature in the target dataset. By including multiple signatures, the algorithm gains resilience to noise, platform-specific biases, and biological variability. Furthermore, the invention permits the user to over-guess the number of candidate cell types, including signatures for cell types that may not be present in the tissue. This is not a limitation but a feature, as the algorithm is designed to discard spurious signatures during the symmetric Kullback-Leibler divergence step. The only constraint on the size of the reference matrix is that the number of candidate cell types p must not exceed the number of samples n, as dictated by the mathematical properties of the linear model. In practice, this constraint is rarely binding, as most datasets contain hundreds or thousands of samples while the number of candidate cell types rarely exceeds fifty. The invention thus transforms public gene-expression databases from passive archives into active tools for deconvolution, enabling any researcher to apply the method to any dataset without the need for new experiments or specialized expertise in cell isolation.

### Example 1

The invention was applied to a controlled dataset consisting of mixed samples of rat liver, brain, and lung tissue, each with known proportions of the three cell types. The mixed expression data was obtained from a publicly available dataset, while reference signatures for liver, brain, lung, intestine, heart, and granulosa cells were mined from the Gene Expression Omnibus, all derived from the same microarray platform. The reference matrix L included six signatures, even though only three cell types were present in the mixture. The algorithm was executed with ten random initializations and a majority voting threshold of seventy percent. The resulting estimated signature matrix G contained three columns, each corresponding to one of the three true cell types: liver, brain, and lung. The symmetric Kullback-Leibler divergence between each estimated signature and its corresponding reference signature was consistently the smallest among all possible pairings, confirming accurate identification. The correlation between the estimated and true gene-expression profiles exceeded 0.95 for all three cell types, surpassing the correlations achieved by prior methods that were provided with the exact number and identities of the cell types. The estimated proportions of each cell type in each sample were highly correlated with the known proportions, with an average absolute error of 3.4 percent. The algorithm correctly discarded the three irrelevant reference signatures (intestine, heart, and granulosa) as spurious, demonstrating its ability to distinguish true from false candidates. Notably, the estimated signature for lung tissue exhibited a higher correlation with the true signature than the input reference signature, indicating that the algorithm did not merely reproduce the input but refined it to better match the true biological profile. This result demonstrates that the invention not only identifies the correct cell types but also improves the quality of the gene-expression signatures through the deconvolution process.

### Example 2

The invention was applied to a human dataset consisting of mixed samples of heart and brain tissue, with reference signatures for myocardial cells, brain cortex, brain grey matter, oocytes, and hepatocytes. Two reference signatures for heart tissue and two for brain tissue were included, representing different studies and tissue subregions. The algorithm was executed with ten random initializations and a majority voting threshold of seventy percent, and the two heart signatures were grouped into a single class labeled “heart,” as were the two brain signatures into a class labeled “brain.” The algorithm identified two distinct cell types, corresponding to the “heart” and “brain” classes, and computed a single averaged signature for each class. The estimated signature for heart tissue was highly correlated with both input reference signatures, as was the signature for brain tissue. The symmetric Kullback-Leibler divergence confirmed that the brain cortex signature was more strongly matched than the grey matter signature, suggesting that the mixed samples were enriched for cortical cells. The estimated proportions of heart and brain cells were highly accurate, with an average absolute error of 1.7 percent. The algorithm successfully aggregated the multiple reference signatures into coherent classes, demonstrating its ability to handle biological variability and noise. The resulting signatures were more representative of the true underlying biology than any single input signature, illustrating the power of class-based aggregation to enhance robustness. This example confirms that the invention can operate effectively even when the reference signatures are imperfect, incomplete, or derived from heterogeneous sources.

### Example 3

The invention was applied to a complex dataset of immune cell mixtures containing T cells, two distinct B cell lines, and monocytes. The reference signatures included T cells, B cells, monocytes, natural killer cells, and epithelial cells, with two signatures for each B cell line and two for T cells. The algorithm was executed with twenty random initializations and a majority voting threshold of seventy percent. The algorithm identified four distinct cell types: T cells, two distinct B cell lines, and monocytes. The two B cell lines were correctly separated despite their nearly identical gene-expression profiles, a feat that is impossible for methods that require prior knowledge of cell-type identity. The symmetric Kullback-Leibler divergence confirmed that each estimated signature was most similar to one of the input signatures, with no spurious matches to natural killer or epithelial cells. The estimated proportions were highly accurate, with an average absolute error of 5.7 percent. When the two B cell signatures were grouped into a single class labeled “B cells,” the algorithm correctly identified only three cell types—T cells, B cells, and monocytes—with an even lower error of 3.7 percent, demonstrating that the class-based aggregation can be used to tailor the resolution of the analysis to the biological question. This example demonstrates the invention’s ability to resolve subtle differences between closely related cell types and to adapt its output to the user’s analytical goals, whether high-resolution separation or broad classification.

### Example 4

The invention was applied to a semi-controlled dataset of prostate cancer biopsies, where pathologists had estimated the proportion of tumor cells in each sample. Reference signatures were obtained for prostate tumor cell lines, benign epithelial cells, normal epithelial cells, stromal cells surrounding tumors, and normal stroma. The six tumor cell lines were grouped into a single class labeled “tumor,” and the epithelial and stromal signatures were grouped into a single class labeled “other.” The algorithm was executed with ten random initializations and a majority voting threshold of seventy percent. The algorithm identified two cell types: “tumor” and “other,” with estimated proportions that were highly correlated with the pathologist’s estimates, achieving an average absolute error of 12.4 percent. This error rate was comparable to the minimum error reported in the original study, despite the fact that the algorithm used only general reference signatures and no information about the specific subtype of tumor or stroma. The estimated signature for the “tumor” class was highly enriched for known oncogenic pathways and epithelial-mesenchymal transition markers, while the “other” class was enriched for extracellular matrix and immune cell markers. This result demonstrates that the invention can extract biologically meaningful signatures even when the reference signatures are not disease-specific, and that the estimated proportions are sufficiently accurate to be useful in clinical contexts. The invention thus enables the re-analysis of clinical datasets that were previously considered intractable due to incomplete metadata.

### Example 5

The invention was applied to a synthetic dataset generated by mixing known proportions of purified gene-expression profiles from five distinct cell types, with added Gaussian noise to simulate experimental variability. The reference matrix included ten signatures, five of which corresponded to the true cell types and five of which were unrelated. The algorithm was executed with thirty random initializations and a majority voting threshold of sixty percent. The algorithm correctly identified all five true cell types and discarded all five unrelated signatures. The estimated signatures were more accurate than the input signatures, with correlations exceeding 0.93 across all five cell types. The estimated proportions had an average absolute error of 2.1 percent, demonstrating the algorithm’s robustness to noise and its ability to recover true biological signals even under suboptimal conditions. This example confirms that the invention is not dependent on the quality of the reference signatures and can operate effectively even when the reference library is noisy or contains irrelevant signatures. The algorithm’s performance in this controlled setting establishes its theoretical validity and provides a benchmark for future applications.

## Discussion

The invention represents a fundamental advancement in the field of computational biology by enabling the blind deconvolution of heterogeneous gene-expression data without requiring any prior knowledge of the number of cell types, their identities, or their exact gene-expression profiles. Unlike all existing methods, which are constrained by their dependence on complete and accurate prior information, the invention operates autonomously, using only a general hypothesis of possible cell types and their reference signatures to extract the true cellular composition of a tissue. This capability transforms the analysis of archived gene-expression datasets from a speculative exercise into a precise and reproducible science. The invention’s ability to identify the correct number of cell types, even when the initial guess is incorrect, eliminates the most common source of error in existing methods and ensures that the results are biologically meaningful. The use of symmetric Kullback-Leibler divergence as a distance metric is critical to this success, as it captures the shape of gene-expression profiles more accurately than Euclidean distance or correlation, especially in the presence of noise and non-linearities. The majority voting mechanism ensures robustness against the non-convex nature of non-negative matrix factorization, while the class-based aggregation allows the algorithm to handle biological variability and imperfect references. The invention’s performance across multiple datasets, including controlled, semi-controlled, and synthetic examples, demonstrates its generalizability and reliability. The fact that the estimated signatures are often more accurate than the input signatures suggests that the algorithm does not merely match existing data but actively refines it to reveal the true underlying biology. This has profound implications for the re-analysis of legacy datasets, where the original experiments may have used outdated platforms, small sample sizes, or incomplete annotations. The invention allows researchers to revisit these datasets and extract new biological insights that were previously inaccessible. Furthermore, the method is scalable and computationally efficient, making it suitable for high-throughput analysis of thousands of samples. The invention also opens new avenues for discovery, as it can identify novel or unexpected cell types that were not included in the reference library, provided that their gene-expression profiles are sufficiently distinct from those of known cell types. This capability transforms the invention from a deconvolution tool into a discovery engine, capable of uncovering new cellular states in disease, development, and aging. The invention thus represents not merely an incremental improvement but a paradigm shift in the analysis of heterogeneous tissues, with the potential to revolutionize precision medicine, cancer biology, immunology, and developmental biology.

## Example 7

### Application to Breast Cancer Microarrays

The invention was applied to a large-scale dataset of breast cancer tissue microarrays, comprising over five hundred patient samples profiled on the Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 platform. The dataset included tumor samples from multiple subtypes, including luminal A, luminal B, HER2-enriched, basal-like, and normal-like, as well as adjacent normal tissue. No prior information regarding cell-type composition was available, and no cell sorting or purification had been performed. Reference signatures were mined from the Gene Expression Omnibus and included purified signatures for epithelial cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, macrophages, B cells, T cells, natural killer cells, plasma cells, adipocytes, and myoepithelial cells, all derived from unrelated tissues and platforms. The algorithm was executed with fifty random initializations and a majority voting threshold of sixty-five percent, with all epithelial signatures grouped into a single “epithelial” class and all immune signatures grouped into a single “immune” class. The algorithm identified six distinct cell types: epithelial, fibroblast, endothelial, macrophage, lymphocyte, and adipocyte. The estimated signature for the epithelial class was highly enriched for keratin genes and epithelial adhesion molecules, consistent with tumor epithelial cells. The fibroblast signature was enriched for collagen and extracellular matrix genes, while the endothelial signature was enriched for vascular markers such as CD31 and von Willebrand factor. The macrophage signature was enriched for CD68, CD163, and IL10, while the lymphocyte signature was enriched for CD3, CD19, and CD8, indicating a mixed population of T and B cells. The adipocyte signature was enriched for lipid metabolism genes. The estimated proportions of each cell type varied significantly across samples, with epithelial cells dominating in tumor samples and fibroblasts and immune cells enriched in stromal-rich regions. The algorithm detected a subset of samples with unusually high proportions of adipocytes, which were later confirmed to be from fatty breast tissue. The invention also identified a small subset of samples with a distinct lymphocyte signature that was enriched for interferon-response genes, suggesting the presence of an activated immune microenvironment. These findings were validated by immunohistochemistry performed on a subset of samples, which confirmed the presence and relative abundance of the predicted cell types. The invention thus enabled the reconstruction of the cellular architecture of breast cancer tissues without any prior knowledge, revealing subtype-specific differences in stromal and immune composition that were previously hidden in the composite signal. This application demonstrates the invention’s power to uncover novel biological insights from large-scale clinical datasets, transforming them into high-resolution cellular atlases that can inform prognosis, treatment selection, and drug development.
