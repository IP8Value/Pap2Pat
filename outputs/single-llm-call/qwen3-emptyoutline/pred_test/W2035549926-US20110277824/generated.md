# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The performance of photovoltaic devices, particularly those based on multi-crystalline silicon, has long been constrained by the fundamental limitations of light management within the semiconductor substrate. Incident solar radiation, when striking a flat silicon surface, is subject to significant losses due to reflection and transmission, with a substantial fraction of photons escaping the active region without contributing to electron-hole pair generation. These optical losses are especially pronounced in the shorter wavelength regions of the solar spectrum, where silicon exhibits lower absorption coefficients and where the penetration depth of photons is shallow. Consequently, even modest improvements in light trapping can yield disproportionately large gains in short-circuit current density and overall power conversion efficiency. Historically, surface texturing has emerged as the most widely adopted strategy to mitigate these losses by introducing micro- or nano-scale topographical features that promote multiple internal reflections, thereby increasing the effective optical path length within the silicon wafer. Among the earliest and most industrially implemented approaches, isotropic wet chemical etching using acidic or alkaline solutions has been employed to generate random, bowl-like textures on silicon surfaces. These textures, while cost-effective and compatible with large-scale manufacturing, are inherently limited in their ability to achieve ultra-low reflectance, particularly in the blue and ultraviolet spectral range, where the quantum efficiency of silicon solar cells is most sensitive to surface conditions. The resulting reflectance values typically remain above 10% across the visible spectrum, leaving room for substantial optical enhancement.

In parallel with the development of wet etching techniques, plasma-based etching methods, particularly reactive ion etching (RIE), have been explored for their capacity to generate highly anisotropic, nanostructured surfaces characterized by dense arrays of needle-like or cone-shaped protrusions. These structures, formed through the synergistic action of chemically reactive plasma species and energetic ion bombardment, offer the potential to achieve reflectance values below 1% over broad wavelength ranges, a level of optical performance previously unattainable with conventional wet texturing. The mechanism underlying this dramatic reduction in reflectance lies in the graded refractive index transition from air to silicon, achieved through the sub-wavelength spacing and high aspect ratio of the etched features. This gradient effectively minimizes the abrupt dielectric discontinuity that causes Fresnel reflection at planar interfaces. However, the very process that enables such superior optical properties also introduces a critical trade-off: the high-energy ion bombardment inherent to RIE induces lattice damage in the near-surface region of the silicon substrate. This damage manifests as a high concentration of crystallographic defects, dangling bonds, and dislocations within the top few hundred nanometers of the material. These defects act as recombination centers for photogenerated charge carriers, significantly increasing the surface recombination velocity and thereby degrading the open-circuit voltage and fill factor of the resulting solar cell. The consequence is a paradoxical outcome: while RIE-textured surfaces capture more photons, the carriers generated near the surface are more likely to recombine before being collected at the p-n junction, leading to a net reduction in cell efficiency despite superior light absorption.

Previous attempts to mitigate this issue have focused on post-etch surface treatments designed to remove or passivate the damaged layer. These include thermal annealing, chemical polishing, and acid-based etching processes using mixtures of hydrofluoric acid and nitric acid. While some of these methods have demonstrated partial success in restoring carrier lifetime and improving voltage characteristics, they often come at the cost of smoothing the nanostructured surface, thereby partially or fully reversing the optical benefits that motivated the use of RIE in the first place. The challenge, therefore, is not merely to remove damage, but to do so in a manner that preserves the essential light-trapping geometry while eliminating the electronic defects that compromise carrier collection. Furthermore, the integration of RIE texturing into industrial manufacturing processes has been hindered by the lack of a standardized, scalable, and reproducible protocol that balances optical performance with electrical integrity. Most reported studies have been conducted on laboratory-scale devices using homogeneous emitter structures, which do not reflect the complexity of modern industrial solar cell architectures. The emergence of selective emitter technology, which combines regions of high dopant concentration beneath the metal contacts with lightly doped regions between them, has further complicated the optimization landscape. Selective emitters are known to reduce series resistance and improve carrier collection efficiency, but their performance is highly sensitive to the underlying surface morphology and the quality of the silicon interface. The interaction between RIE-induced surface damage, the subsequent chemical etching step, and the diffusion profile of the selective emitter has not been systematically investigated in a manner suitable for industrial implementation.

Moreover, the characterization of surface morphology and its correlation with electrical performance has often relied on qualitative assessments using scanning electron microscopy, without sufficient quantitative linkage to optical and electrical metrics such as external quantum efficiency, surface recombination velocity, and minority carrier lifetime. There exists a critical gap in the literature between the ability to fabricate nanostructured surfaces with exceptional optical properties and the ability to do so while maintaining or enhancing the electrical quality of the silicon. The prevailing paradigm has treated optical and electrical optimization as separate, sequential steps, rather than as interdependent parameters that must be co-optimized. As a result, many proposed RIE-based texturing schemes, while scientifically intriguing, have failed to translate into commercially viable solar cell technologies due to their inability to simultaneously deliver high short-circuit current, high open-circuit voltage, and high fill factor. The industrial demand for higher efficiency, lower cost-per-watt photovoltaic modules has intensified the need for a robust, manufacturable process that overcomes these longstanding limitations. This invention addresses this unmet need by introducing a novel, integrated fabrication protocol that combines reactive ion etching with a precisely controlled, time-dependent dilute acid etch to achieve a surface morphology that simultaneously minimizes optical reflectance, eliminates plasma-induced lattice damage, and maintains compatibility with high-throughput screen printing and selective emitter formation. The invention does not merely improve upon existing techniques—it redefines the relationship between surface texture and electronic quality, enabling a new class of high-efficiency, industrially scalable silicon solar cells that achieve performance levels previously thought incompatible with nanostructured surfaces.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method for fabricating high-efficiency multi-crystalline silicon solar cells by integrating reactive ion etching with a precisely controlled dilute acid etch to simultaneously achieve ultra-low optical reflectance and minimal surface recombination, thereby enabling significant improvements in short-circuit current density without compromising open-circuit voltage or fill factor. Unlike prior approaches that treat optical texturing and surface damage removal as separate, often conflicting, steps, this invention introduces a synergistic sequence in which the nanostructured surface generated by reactive ion etching is subjected to a carefully calibrated chemical etching treatment using a mixture of dilute nitric acid and hydrofluoric acid, such that the damaged silicon layer is selectively removed while preserving the essential light-trapping geometry. The duration of this dilute acid etch is optimized to a range between 15 and 35 seconds, during which the surface morphology evolves from a densely packed array of nanoscale protrusions with high aspect ratios to a smoother, yet still highly textured, surface that retains sub-wavelength features capable of suppressing reflection across the solar spectrum, particularly in the critical short-wavelength region below 600 nanometers. This precise control over etch time ensures that the surface recombination velocity is reduced to levels comparable to or lower than those achieved with conventional wet-chemically textured surfaces, while the average reflectance remains below 8% across the 300 to 1100 nanometer wavelength range, with values as low as 4% at 400 nanometers.

The invention further integrates this optimized texturing process with a selective emitter structure formed through a two-step phosphorus diffusion process, wherein a silicon dioxide layer is deposited as a diffusion mask, patterned using an etch paste to define regions of heavy doping beneath the future metal contact fingers, and subsequently subjected to phosphorus oxychloride diffusion in a conventional tube furnace. Following the first diffusion, the phosphorus silicate glass and residual silicon dioxide are removed using a 10% hydrofluoric acid solution, after which a second, shallower diffusion is performed to create a lightly doped region between the contact fingers, resulting in a sheet resistance of approximately 20 ohms per square under the fingers and 80 ohms per square between them. This selective emitter configuration, when combined with the optimized RIE-dilute acid textured surface, enables a substantial reduction in series resistance and an enhancement in carrier collection efficiency, particularly in regions where the metal contacts are in close proximity to the emitter. The final device structure is completed by the deposition of a silicon nitride anti-reflection coating via plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition, with a refractive index of 2.06 and a thickness of 85 nanometers, followed by screen printing of silver front and aluminum back contacts and co-firing in a lamp-heated belt furnace. The resulting solar cells exhibit a conversion efficiency exceeding 17.5% when fabricated on industrial-grade, 156 mm by 156 mm, boron-doped, p-type multi-crystalline silicon wafers with a resistivity of 1.9 ohm-centimeters and a thickness of 200 micrometers, representing an absolute gain in short-circuit current density of over 200 milliamperes compared to conventionally acid-textured cells with homogeneous emitters, while maintaining open-circuit voltage and fill factor at levels indistinguishable from those of the reference devices.

The invention is distinguished from prior art by its recognition that the detrimental effects of reactive ion etching are not an inherent limitation of the technique, but rather a consequence of improper post-processing. By introducing a controlled, time-dependent dilute acid etch immediately following RIE, the invention achieves a previously unattainable balance between optical and electrical performance. The dilute acid etch does not simply smooth the surface; it selectively etches away the amorphous, defect-rich layer formed during ion bombardment while leaving behind the crystalline silicon framework that constitutes the light-trapping structure. This results in a surface that is both optically superior and electronically pristine. The external quantum efficiency of devices fabricated using this method demonstrates a marked improvement in the 300 to 600 nanometer range, confirming that the enhanced photon capture is directly translated into increased carrier generation without the accompanying recombination losses that have historically plagued RIE-textured cells. Furthermore, the process is fully compatible with existing industrial manufacturing infrastructure, requiring only minor modifications to standard wet chemistry and diffusion lines, and does not necessitate the use of expensive or exotic materials, complex alignment procedures, or vacuum-based post-treatment steps. The invention thus provides a manufacturable, scalable, and economically viable pathway to high-efficiency silicon photovoltaics that overcomes the longstanding trade-off between optical texturing and electrical quality, enabling a new generation of multi-crystalline silicon solar cells with performance characteristics previously reserved for single-crystalline or epitaxial technologies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EXEMPLARY EMBODIMENTS

The exemplary embodiment of the invention begins with the selection of a standard industrial-grade multi-crystalline silicon wafer, fabricated from a cast ingot and characterized by a p-type doping profile due to boron incorporation during crystal growth. The wafer dimensions are standardized at 156 millimeters by 156 millimeters, with a thickness of 200 micrometers and a bulk resistivity of 1.9 ohm-centimeters, consistent with the typical specifications used in commercial photovoltaic production lines. Prior to any texturing or doping steps, the wafer undergoes a preliminary cleaning sequence to remove organic contaminants, metallic impurities, and native oxide layers. This is accomplished through a series of rinses in deionized water, followed by immersion in an acetone bath for five minutes at ambient temperature, then a subsequent rinse in isopropyl alcohol for an additional five minutes, and finally a final rinse in deionized water under ultrasonic agitation to ensure complete removal of residual solvents. The cleaned wafers are dried using a nitrogen gas stream and immediately transferred to a wet chemical texturing station for isotropic surface etching. In this step, the wafers are immersed in a heated solution of hydrofluoric acid and nitric acid, maintained at a temperature of 70 degrees Celsius, for a duration of 120 seconds. This acidic etch generates a uniform, bowl-like texture across the entire surface, characterized by micro-scale depressions with radii on the order of several micrometers and depths of approximately 1 to 2 micrometers. The resulting surface exhibits a matte appearance and a reflectance of approximately 12% averaged across the solar spectrum, which is typical for conventionally textured multi-crystalline silicon wafers. This isotropic texture serves as a foundational topography upon which the subsequent reactive ion etching step will build, providing a stable, reproducible substrate that enhances the uniformity and reproducibility of the nanostructured features formed in the next stage.

Following the isotropic texturing, the wafers are transferred to a reactive ion etching system, which consists of a capacitively coupled plasma chamber equipped with independently controllable radio frequency power supplies for generating the plasma and biasing the substrate holder. The chamber is evacuated to a base pressure of less than 1 × 10⁻⁶ torr, after which a gas mixture of sulfur hexafluoride and oxygen is introduced at a flow rate of 40 standard cubic centimeters per minute and 10 standard cubic centimeters per minute, respectively. The chamber pressure is stabilized at 20 millitorr, and the plasma is ignited using a 13.56 MHz radio frequency source operating at a power of 300 watts. Simultaneously, a substrate bias of 150 watts is applied to accelerate ions vertically toward the wafer surface. The etching duration is maintained at 90 seconds, during which time the surface morphology evolves from the pre-existing bowl-like features into a dense array of nanoscale protrusions, each with a height of 300 to 500 nanometers and a base diameter of 50 to 100 nanometers. These features are highly anisotropic, with aspect ratios exceeding five, and are distributed with a mean inter-feature spacing of approximately 150 nanometers, well below the wavelength of visible light. The resulting surface appears visually black under ambient illumination, indicative of extreme light trapping. Reflectance measurements confirm a dramatic reduction in optical reflectance, with values falling below 5% across the 400 to 1000 nanometer range and reaching as low as 1.2% at 450 nanometers. However, scanning electron microscopy reveals a thin, amorphous layer, approximately 10 to 20 nanometers in thickness, covering the surfaces of these nanostructures. This layer is composed of damaged silicon, silicon fluoride compounds, and embedded oxygen species introduced during the ion bombardment process. This damaged layer is electrically active and functions as a high-density recombination center, leading to a measurable degradation in open-circuit voltage and fill factor when the wafer is processed into a complete solar cell without further treatment.

To address this critical limitation, the invention introduces a post-RIE dilute acid etch, which is the central innovation of the present disclosure. Immediately following RIE, the wafers are removed from the plasma chamber and transferred to a dedicated etching station containing a freshly prepared solution of 0.5 molar nitric acid and 1.0 molar hydrofluoric acid, maintained at a temperature of 25 degrees Celsius. The etch time is precisely controlled and varied across a range of 15 to 35 seconds in a series of experimental runs, with each time point corresponding to a distinct batch of wafers. During this etch, the dilute acid mixture selectively dissolves the amorphous, damaged layer while leaving the underlying crystalline silicon nanostructures largely intact. The nitric acid acts as an oxidizing agent, converting silicon into soluble silicon dioxide, while the hydrofluoric acid rapidly etches away the oxide, creating a self-limiting, cyclic dissolution mechanism that preferentially targets defect-rich regions. The etch rate is calibrated such that the damaged layer is completely removed within 20 seconds, as confirmed by cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy and secondary ion mass spectrometry. At 15 seconds, the damaged layer is only partially removed, and the open-circuit voltage remains slightly depressed. At 20 seconds, the damaged layer is fully eliminated, and the surface recombination velocity drops to 80 centimeters per second, a value comparable to that of conventionally textured surfaces. At 30 seconds, the nanostructures begin to show signs of rounding at their tips, and the reflectance increases slightly, particularly in the 400 to 500 nanometer range, indicating the onset of optical degradation. The optimal etch time is therefore determined to be 20 seconds, at which point the surface morphology retains its high aspect ratio, the reflectance remains below 5% across the visible spectrum, and the electrical quality of the surface is restored to its maximum potential. This precise temporal control is essential; deviations of more than ±5 seconds result in measurable losses in either optical performance or electrical efficiency, demonstrating the narrow window of operational parameters that define the invention.

Following the dilute acid etch, the wafers are rinsed thoroughly in deionized water and dried under a nitrogen stream. A thin layer of silicon dioxide, approximately 100 nanometers in thickness, is then deposited across the entire surface using plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition with tetraethylorthosilicate and oxygen as precursor gases. This silicon dioxide layer functions as a diffusion mask for the subsequent formation of the selective emitter. The mask is patterned using a screen-printed etch paste composed of a suspension of silicon dioxide particles in a viscous organic binder, with a mesh size of 325 lines per inch. The paste is applied to the front surface of the wafer in a grid pattern corresponding to the intended location of the front metal contacts, with finger widths of 80 micrometers and a spacing of 2.5 millimeters. The wafer is then subjected to a low-temperature bake at 150 degrees Celsius for 10 minutes to remove volatile components and enhance adhesion. The patterned wafer is then loaded into a conventional horizontal tube furnace, where it undergoes a two-step phosphorus diffusion process. In the first step, the wafer is exposed to phosphorus oxychloride vapor at a temperature of 850 degrees Celsius for 30 minutes in a nitrogen atmosphere, resulting in heavy phosphorus doping beneath the open areas of the mask, where the silicon dioxide has been removed by the etch paste. After this first diffusion, the wafer is cooled to room temperature and immersed in a 10% aqueous hydrofluoric acid solution for 60 seconds to remove the phosphorus silicate glass and the remaining silicon dioxide layer. The wafer is then rinsed and dried, and a second, shallower diffusion is performed under identical conditions but for a reduced duration of 15 minutes at a slightly lower temperature of 820 degrees Celsius. This second diffusion creates a lightly doped region in the areas between the contact fingers, resulting in a sheet resistance of 20 ohms per square under the fingers and 80 ohms per square between them. The resulting selective emitter structure reduces the series resistance of the cell by minimizing the lateral current flow through the lightly doped regions, while simultaneously reducing recombination losses at the metal-semiconductor interface by ensuring that the high doping concentration is localized directly beneath the contact points.

After the selective emitter formation, the wafer is once again cleaned in a 10% hydrofluoric acid solution to remove any residual phosphorus silicate glass, followed by a final rinse in deionized water and drying. A silicon nitride anti-reflection coating is then deposited using plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition with silane and ammonia as precursor gases. The deposition parameters are carefully controlled to produce a film with a refractive index of 2.06 and a thickness of 85 nanometers, which is optimized for the spectral response of the multi-crystalline silicon substrate and the underlying surface texture. This coating serves a dual purpose: it further reduces optical reflectance through interference effects and provides a passivating layer that reduces surface recombination at the silicon-air interface. The refractive index is chosen to match the effective medium approximation of the textured surface, ensuring minimal reflection at the coating-substrate interface. The thickness is selected to provide quarter-wavelength optical interference for the peak of the solar spectrum around 600 nanometers, thereby maximizing the constructive interference of transmitted light within the silicon. Following deposition, the wafer is transferred to a screen printing station, where front silver and back aluminum contacts are printed using standard paste formulations. The front contacts are printed using a high-resolution stencil with a line width of 80 micrometers and a height of 15 micrometers, while the back contacts are printed with a wider, lower-profile pattern to maximize coverage and minimize shunting. The printed wafers are then co-fired in a lamp-heated belt furnace, with a peak temperature of 800 degrees Celsius and a dwell time of 10 seconds, to sinter the metal contacts and form low-resistance ohmic contacts with the silicon. The firing profile is optimized to ensure good contact formation without inducing excessive diffusion of the phosphorus dopant or damaging the underlying silicon nitride layer.

The completed solar cells are isolated using a pulsed laser cutting system that precisely scribes the wafer along predefined scribe lines, creating individual cells with minimal kerf loss. Each cell is then electrically characterized under standard test conditions using a solar simulator calibrated to AM1.5G irradiance at 25 degrees Celsius. The results demonstrate a consistent and significant improvement in performance metrics compared to control devices fabricated using conventional isotropic acid texturing and homogeneous emitters. The average short-circuit current density of the inventive devices exceeds 37.5 milliamperes per square centimeter, representing an absolute gain of over 200 milliamperes for a 156 mm by 156 mm cell. The open-circuit voltage is maintained at 635 millivolts, identical to that of the reference devices, and the fill factor remains above 78%, indicating no degradation in carrier collection or series resistance. The resulting average conversion efficiency reaches 17.6%, a value that exceeds the performance of any previously reported multi-crystalline silicon solar cell fabricated using industrial screen printing techniques. External quantum efficiency measurements confirm that the gain in short-circuit current is primarily attributable to enhanced photon absorption in the 300 to 600 nanometer range, where the RIE-dilute acid textured surface exhibits significantly lower reflectance than the conventional acid-textured surface. The quantum efficiency at 450 nanometers increases by over 15 percentage points, directly correlating with the preserved nanostructure and the absence of surface recombination. Furthermore, minority carrier lifetime measurements using microwave photoconductance decay reveal that the effective lifetime of carriers in the inventive devices exceeds 1.2 microseconds, compared to less than 0.8 microseconds in devices without the dilute acid etch step, confirming that the damage introduced by RIE has been effectively eliminated.

The invention is further validated through a series of comparative experiments in which the etch time of the dilute acid step is varied outside the optimal range. Wafers subjected to a 10-second etch exhibit a 5% reduction in open-circuit voltage and a 3% reduction in fill factor, attributable to residual surface damage. Wafers subjected to a 40-second etch exhibit a 2% increase in reflectance at 450 nanometers and a corresponding 1.2% reduction in short-circuit current density, demonstrating that the nanostructure begins to degrade beyond the optimal window. The performance of the inventive devices remains stable across multiple production batches, with a standard deviation in efficiency of less than 0.3%, indicating excellent process repeatability. The method is fully compatible with existing industrial equipment, requiring no new capital investment beyond the addition of a dilute acid etch station and precise timing controls. The process flow is seamlessly integrated into standard photovoltaic manufacturing lines, with no additional cleaning, alignment, or vacuum steps required. The use of common chemicals—nitric acid, hydrofluoric acid, phosphorus oxychloride, and silicon nitride precursors—ensures that the method is scalable, cost-effective, and environmentally manageable within existing industrial safety and waste disposal protocols. The resulting solar cells exhibit no degradation in long-term stability under damp heat or thermal cycling tests, confirming that the surface morphology and passivation remain intact under operational conditions. This invention, therefore, represents a transformative advancement in silicon photovoltaics, enabling a new class of high-efficiency, industrially scalable solar cells that overcome the historical limitations of nanostructured surfaces and deliver performance levels previously unattainable with multi-crystalline silicon.
