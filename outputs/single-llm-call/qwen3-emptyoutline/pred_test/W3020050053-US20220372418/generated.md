# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### Field of the Invention

The present invention relates to microfluidic cell culture systems, and more specifically to pumpless organ-on-chip devices capable of sustaining continuous, controlled perfusion of cell culture medium without the need for external pumps, tubing, or power sources. The invention provides a novel method and apparatus for driving fluid flow through microfluidic channels by exploiting the capillary wicking properties of hydrophilic threads placed at the outlet of the device, coupled with the controlled evaporation of the culture medium within a standard cell culture incubator. This approach enables autonomous, long-term perfusion of cellular monolayers under physiologically relevant fluid shear stress conditions, thereby facilitating the cultivation of tissue-like structures in vitro with high reproducibility, minimal contamination risk, and reduced technical complexity. The invention is particularly suited for applications in drug screening, toxicology testing, disease modeling, and personalized medicine, where the faithful recapitulation of tissue microenvironments is critical, and where access to specialized microfluidic infrastructure is limited.

### Description of Related Art

Microfluidic organ-on-chip technologies have emerged as transformative platforms for in vitro cell culture, offering unprecedented control over the biochemical and biophysical microenvironment of living cells. Unlike conventional static culture systems, these devices enable the application of dynamic flow conditions that mimic the mechanical forces experienced by cells in vivo, such as fluid shear stress, cyclic strain, and interstitial flow. These forces are now well established as critical regulators of cell phenotype, function, and organization, particularly in endothelial, epithelial, and barrier-forming tissues. For instance, fluid shear stress has been shown to modulate the expression of tight junction proteins, induce cytoskeletal reorganization, enhance barrier integrity, and trigger epithelial-mesenchymal transitions in a dose-dependent manner. As a result, organs-on-chips incorporating perfusion have become indispensable tools for studying intestinal absorption, pulmonary responses to airborne agents, hepatic metabolism, and vascular inflammation, among other physiological processes.

Despite their scientific promise, the widespread adoption of organ-on-chip technologies has been hindered by significant practical limitations inherent in conventional perfusion systems. The vast majority of existing platforms rely on external peristaltic, syringe, or pressure-driven pumps connected to the microfluidic device via flexible tubing. These systems introduce multiple points of failure: air bubbles can form within the tubing or at the interface between the pump and the device, leading to intermittent or complete flow cessation; leaks may develop at connection points, resulting in loss of sterility, contamination, or loss of culture volume; and the complexity of setup and maintenance demands specialized training, time-intensive calibration, and expensive equipment. Furthermore, the use of external pumps and tubing renders these systems incompatible with standard biosafety cabinets and sterile incubators, as the protruding tubing compromises the integrity of the enclosed environment and increases the risk of cross-contamination, particularly when handling pathogenic agents or patient-derived samples. The capital cost of high-precision pumps, flow controllers, and associated instrumentation also creates a substantial barrier to entry for laboratories without dedicated microfluidics expertise or funding.

Several alternative approaches have been proposed to circumvent these challenges by eliminating the need for external pumps. One such strategy involves the use of hydrostatic pressure gradients generated by height differences between inlet and outlet reservoirs, often combined with flow resistors to regulate flow rate. While conceptually simple, this method offers limited flexibility in adjusting flow conditions during an experiment, and the flow rate is highly sensitive to minor variations in reservoir height, temperature, or medium viscosity. Another approach employs rocking platforms to induce bidirectional flow via gravity-driven oscillations. Although this method avoids tubing and pumps, it generates complex, non-uniform flow profiles with fluctuating shear stress magnitudes and directions, which are unsuitable for studies requiring stable, directional flow. Moreover, the mechanical motion of the platform introduces vibration and thermal fluctuations that can interfere with sensitive cellular processes and imaging modalities. A third method utilizes modified intravenous infusion sets to create passive siphon-driven flow. While this approach reduces equipment requirements, it is inherently unreliable due to the susceptibility of siphon systems to air entrapment, clogging, and spontaneous interruption, particularly when the medium contains proteins, salts, or particulates that can precipitate or adhere to the inner surfaces of the tubing.

More recently, capillary-driven systems have been explored in the context of lateral flow assays and point-of-care diagnostics, where wicking materials such as nitrocellulose membranes or paper-based substrates are used to transport fluids through porous networks via capillary action alone. However, these systems are typically designed for single-use, short-duration assays and lack the structural integrity, dimensional precision, and compatibility with cell culture conditions required for long-term organ-on-chip applications. Furthermore, the flow rates generated by these materials are often too slow or too uncontrolled to support the physiological shear stress ranges necessary for epithelial differentiation or barrier formation. To date, no system has successfully integrated the principles of capillary wicking with the sustained evaporation-driven flow needed to maintain continuous, tunable, and stable perfusion over multiple days in a sealed, sterile, incubator-compatible microfluidic device.

The present invention addresses these longstanding limitations by introducing a novel and elegantly simple design that leverages the intrinsic capillary properties of commercially available hydrophilic threads—specifically, spunlace non-woven textile fibers—as microfluidic conduits positioned at the outlet of a sealed microfluidic chamber. Unlike prior capillary systems, this invention exploits the controlled evaporation of the culture medium within the humidified environment of a standard cell culture incubator (37 °C, 5% CO₂, 90% relative humidity) as the primary driving force for continuous fluid flow. The thread, inserted into a precisely sized outlet port in the device, wicks the medium from the microfluidic channel toward its dry end, where evaporation occurs at a predictable and sustained rate. The resulting pressure gradient between the reservoir and the evaporating thread end generates a steady, unidirectional flow through the channel. Crucially, the flow rate can be precisely tuned by adjusting the length and diameter of the thread, enabling the application of defined shear stress regimes tailored to specific cell types without requiring any external instrumentation. The entire device, including the inlet reservoir and outlet thread, remains contained within a sterile Petri dish, eliminating the need for tubing, minimizing contamination risk, and enabling seamless integration into routine cell culture workflows. This innovation transforms organ-on-chip technology from a specialized, equipment-dependent methodology into an accessible, robust, and user-friendly platform suitable for laboratories across diverse scientific disciplines.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a pumpless organ-on-chip device and method for the autonomous, long-term perfusion of cellular monolayers under controlled fluid shear stress conditions, without the use of external pumps, tubing, or power sources. The device comprises a microfluidic chamber fabricated from a biocompatible polymer such as polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), bonded to a transparent substrate such as a glass coverslip, and includes an inlet reservoir for holding cell culture medium and an outlet port designed to receive a hydrophilic thread. The hydrophilic thread, composed of a non-woven, capillary-active material such as spunlace fabric, is inserted into the outlet port such that one end is positioned within the microfluidic channel and the other end extends outward into the ambient environment of a cell culture incubator. The microfluidic channel is sealed at the inlet by a lid or cover to prevent evaporation from the medium reservoir, ensuring that fluid loss occurs exclusively through evaporation at the distal end of the thread. The controlled evaporation of the culture medium from the thread, driven by the temperature and humidity conditions of the incubator, generates a sustained capillary pressure gradient that draws medium continuously through the channel, thereby maintaining a steady, unidirectional flow over periods exceeding 24 hours and up to five days or longer, depending on the configuration.

The flow rate within the microfluidic channel is directly modulated by the physical dimensions of the hydrophilic thread, specifically its length and diameter. Longer threads increase the total surface area available for evaporation, thereby enhancing the driving force for flow, while larger diameter threads increase the volume of medium that can be wicked per unit time, thereby increasing the flow rate. By selecting threads of specific dimensions, the shear stress experienced by the cellular monolayer can be precisely calibrated to match physiological ranges relevant to specific tissues—for example, low shear stress (0.002–0.025 dyn/cm²) for intestinal epithelial cells or moderate shear stress (0.03–0.15 dyn/cm²) for lung epithelial cells undergoing epithelial-mesenchymal transition. The device is compatible with standard cell culture protocols, including pre-coating of the channel surface with extracellular matrix components such as Matrigel, seeding of cells at defined densities, and periodic replenishment of the inlet reservoir with fresh medium. The entire system remains contained within a sterile Petri dish, eliminating the need for external connections and minimizing the risk of microbial contamination. The simplicity of the design allows for rapid, low-cost fabrication using standard soft lithography techniques, and the hydrophilic threads are commercially available, inexpensive, and disposable, making the system scalable and suitable for high-throughput applications.

The invention further encompasses a multiplexed variant of the device, wherein multiple parallel microfluidic channels are fed from a common inlet reservoir but are each connected to a distinct hydrophilic thread of varying length or diameter. This configuration enables the simultaneous exposure of identical cell populations to different shear stress conditions within a single chip, facilitating high-resolution, within-experiment comparisons of cellular responses to mechanical stimuli. The device is compatible with live-cell imaging, immunofluorescence staining, and molecular analyses, and the absence of tubing or pumps eliminates vibration, electromagnetic interference, and optical obstructions that may compromise high-resolution microscopy. The invention enables the cultivation of functional epithelial barriers, such as Caco-2 monolayers exhibiting dense F-actin networks, high ZO-1 expression, and mature tight junctions, as well as the induction of phenotypic transitions in A549 cells, including vimentin upregulation and morphological changes consistent with epithelial-mesenchymal transition, under precisely controlled shear stress conditions. The system is robust, reproducible, and requires no specialized training to operate, thereby democratizing access to physiologically relevant in vitro models for researchers in pharmacology, toxicology, regenerative medicine, and clinical diagnostics.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention described herein is a pumpless organ-on-chip device designed to sustain continuous, controlled perfusion of cell culture medium through a microfluidic channel without the use of external pumps, tubing, or electrical power. The device is constructed from a transparent, gas-permeable, and biocompatible substrate, typically a glass coverslip, bonded to a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) slab that defines the fluidic architecture. The PDMS slab is fabricated using standard soft lithography techniques, wherein a negative photoresist such as SU-8 is patterned on a silicon wafer to create a master mold with features corresponding to the desired microfluidic geometry. The PDMS mixture, composed of a base polymer and curing agent in a 10:1 weight ratio, is poured onto the master mold and cured at elevated temperature to form a rigid yet flexible replica. After curing, the PDMS slab is peeled from the mold and punched to create two openings: a larger inlet port, typically 8 mm in diameter, to serve as a reservoir for cell culture medium, and a smaller outlet port, approximately 1.5 mm in diameter, designed to receive and securely retain a hydrophilic thread. The outlet port is sized such that the thread is held in place by friction and capillary forces, ensuring that the thread does not fully penetrate the channel and contact the underlying glass substrate, thereby preventing premature drainage of the medium and maintaining a stable fluid interface within the channel.

The hydrophilic thread used in the invention is composed of a non-woven textile material, specifically a spunlace fabric composed of a blend of rayon and polyester fibers, with a diameter ranging from 0.5 mm to 2 mm. The fibers within the thread exhibit smooth surfaces and diameters of approximately 15 micrometers, as confirmed by scanning electron microscopy, and are arranged in a twisted, entangled configuration that forms a network of interconnected capillary channels. These capillary pores are inherently hydrophilic due to the presence of hydroxyl groups in the rayon component, but may be further enhanced through surface treatments such as plasma oxidation or mercerization to remove wax residues and increase wettability. The thread is cut to a predetermined length, typically between 1 mm and 28 mm, and inserted into the outlet port such that a portion of the thread extends into the microfluidic channel while the majority of its length protrudes outward into the incubator environment. The thread is positioned such that its distal end remains exposed to the ambient air within the incubator, where the temperature is maintained at 37 °C, the CO₂ concentration at 5%, and the relative humidity at approximately 90%. These conditions are critical to the function of the device, as they ensure a consistent rate of evaporation from the distal end of the thread, which in turn generates a sustained capillary pressure gradient that drives fluid flow from the inlet reservoir, through the channel, and toward the evaporating end of the thread.

The inlet reservoir is sealed with a custom-fitted lid or cover, typically made of a thin, gas-permeable polymer such as polyethylene or a perforated silicone membrane, to prevent evaporation from the medium reservoir itself. This sealing is essential to ensure that fluid loss occurs exclusively through the thread, thereby maintaining a stable and predictable flow rate. The device is then placed within a sterile Petri dish, which serves as a containment vessel to maintain sterility and prevent desiccation of the thread’s proximal end. The entire assembly is transferred to a standard cell culture incubator, where the evaporation of the culture medium from the thread’s distal end creates a continuous suction force that draws fresh medium from the reservoir through the channel. The flow rate is determined by the balance between the rate of evaporation at the thread’s end and the rate of capillary uptake through the fiber network, both of which are influenced by the thread’s length and diameter. Longer threads increase the total evaporative surface area, thereby enhancing the driving force for flow, while thicker threads increase the volume of medium that can be wicked per unit time, thereby increasing the volumetric flow rate. The relationship between thread dimensions and flow rate has been empirically characterized, demonstrating that flow rates can be tuned from as low as 0.15 µL/min, achieved with a 0.5 mm diameter thread of 1 mm length, to as high as 6 µL/min, achieved with a 2 mm diameter thread of 28 mm length. This range of flow rates corresponds to fluid shear stresses experienced by the cellular monolayer ranging from 0.002 to 0.15 dyn/cm², which encompasses the physiological ranges observed in the human intestine, lung, and other epithelial tissues.

The device is compatible with a wide variety of cell types commonly used in organ-on-chip applications, including but not limited to Caco-2 human intestinal epithelial cells, A549 human lung adenocarcinoma cells, primary human hepatocytes, endothelial cells derived from umbilical veins, and patient-derived organoids. Prior to cell seeding, the microfluidic channel is sterilized by flushing with 70% ethanol followed by phosphate-buffered saline, and the glass substrate is coated with an extracellular matrix protein such as Matrigel to promote cell adhesion and polarization. Cells are introduced into the device via the outlet port, using a pipette to gently aspirate and dispense the cell suspension, thereby minimizing cell loss in the inlet reservoir. After allowing sufficient time for cell attachment—typically one hour—the cell culture medium is added to the inlet reservoir, and the hydrophilic thread is inserted into the outlet port. The medium is then allowed to flow through the channel under the influence of the capillary-evaporation-driven gradient. The flow rate is maintained continuously for the duration of the experiment, which may extend up to five days or longer, depending on the cell type and experimental objectives. During this period, the inlet reservoir is replenished daily with fresh medium, typically 200 µL per day, to compensate for the volume lost through evaporation and to ensure the continuous supply of nutrients and growth factors. The replenishment process is performed aseptically within a biosafety cabinet, and the lid is replaced immediately after to maintain the sealed environment.

The absence of tubing and external pumps confers several critical advantages over conventional systems. First, the entire device remains contained within a single sterile Petri dish, eliminating the risk of microbial contamination introduced by open connections or external equipment. Second, the system generates no vibration, electromagnetic interference, or thermal fluctuations, making it ideal for high-resolution live-cell imaging using confocal microscopy, fluorescence time-lapse, or super-resolution techniques. Third, the simplicity of the design allows for rapid, low-cost fabrication and deployment, enabling high-throughput screening of multiple conditions within a single incubator. Fourth, the system requires no specialized training to operate, making it accessible to researchers without prior experience in microfluidics, including clinicians, pharmacologists, and biologists working in resource-limited settings. Fifth, the disposable nature of the hydrophilic thread ensures that each experiment is conducted with a fresh, uncontaminated flow system, eliminating carryover effects and improving reproducibility. Finally, the system is inherently scalable: multiple devices can be operated simultaneously within a single incubator, and multiplexed variants can be fabricated to expose different regions of a single chip to distinct shear stress conditions, enabling within-experiment comparisons of cellular responses.

The multiplexed embodiment of the invention comprises a single PDMS slab containing five or more parallel microfluidic channels, each with identical width and height but connected to a separate hydrophilic thread of varying length or diameter. All channels are fed from a single, large rectangular inlet reservoir, ensuring that the same cell culture medium is delivered to each channel. However, because each thread is configured differently, the flow rate—and thus the shear stress—experienced by the cells in each channel is uniquely determined by the thread’s physical properties. This design allows for the simultaneous testing of multiple shear stress regimes within a single experiment, eliminating inter-experimental variability and enabling direct, quantitative comparisons of cellular responses. For example, in a study of A549 cells, five parallel channels may be configured to expose cells to shear stresses ranging from 0.008 to 0.135 dyn/cm², allowing researchers to identify the threshold at which epithelial-mesenchymal transition is initiated. The flow rates in each channel are independently verified by tracking the motion of fluorescent polystyrene nanoparticles, confirming that the system generates stable, reproducible, and non-interfering flow profiles across all channels. This multiplexed architecture is particularly valuable for drug screening, where the dose-response relationship to a compound may be influenced by mechanical stimuli, or for personalized medicine, where patient-derived cells may respond differently to varying levels of shear stress.

The performance of the device has been validated through extensive characterization of both fluid dynamics and cellular responses. Flow rates have been quantified by injecting 200 nm fluorescent polystyrene beads into the channel and tracking their displacement over time using inverted fluorescence microscopy and automated image analysis software. The average velocity of the particles, measured across multiple regions of the channel, is used to calculate the volumetric flow rate using the cross-sectional area of the channel and the relationship Q = A × V, where Q is the flow rate, A is the cross-sectional area, and V is the average particle velocity. These measurements confirm that the flow rate remains constant over periods exceeding 24 hours, with negligible drift or fluctuation, even as the medium composition changes due to cellular metabolism. The shear stress experienced by the cells is calculated using the Hele-Shaw approximation for laminar flow in a rectangular channel, τ = 6μQ/(wh²), where μ is the dynamic viscosity of the medium, Q is the flow rate, w is the channel width, and h is the channel height. The resulting shear stress values are consistent with those reported in the literature for physiologically relevant conditions in the human intestine and lung.

Cellular responses have been evaluated using a range of functional and structural assays. In Caco-2 monolayers cultured for five days under a shear stress of 0.02 dyn/cm², immunofluorescence staining revealed a dense, organized F-actin cytoskeleton, high expression of the tight junction protein ZO-1, and the presence of mature mucin-secreting goblet cells, all of which are hallmarks of a fully differentiated intestinal barrier. These characteristics were indistinguishable from those observed in Caco-2 monolayers cultured under identical shear stress conditions using a conventional peristaltic pump. Similarly, A549 cells expressing a vimentin-red fluorescent protein fusion were cultured under shear stress conditions ranging from 0.008 to 0.135 dyn/cm², and a clear, dose-dependent decrease in vimentin expression was observed as shear stress decreased. Cells exposed to shear stress above 0.03 dyn/cm² exhibited a marked increase in vimentin fluorescence, elongated spindle-shaped morphology, reduced cell-cell adhesion, and loss of cytokeratin expression—all consistent with the acquisition of a mesenchymal phenotype. These phenotypic changes were uniform along the length of the channel, confirming that the flow conditions were stable and homogeneous. In contrast, cells cultured under uniform high shear stress in a constant-width channel exhibited consistent vimentin expression throughout the entire length of the channel, demonstrating that the system is capable of generating spatially uniform mechanical stimuli.

The device has also been tested under conditions of medium replenishment, temperature variation, and medium composition change. Daily replenishment of the inlet reservoir with fresh medium did not disrupt the flow profile or introduce air bubbles, and the thread continued to function without clogging or degradation. The system remained functional even when the medium contained serum, growth factors, and antibiotics, and no evidence of thread degradation, fiber shedding, or leaching of synthetic components into the medium was observed. The device was compatible with standard fixation and staining protocols, including formaldehyde fixation, Triton X-100 permeabilization, and antibody labeling, and no artifacts attributable to the thread material were detected in confocal or fluorescence microscopy. The thread was easily removed after the experiment without damaging the PDMS or glass substrate, and the device could be reused for subsequent experiments after thorough cleaning and plasma treatment.

The invention represents a paradigm shift in the field of organ-on-chip technology by eliminating the most significant barriers to adoption: complexity, cost, contamination risk, and technical expertise. It transforms the organ-on-chip from a specialized, equipment-dependent instrument into a simple, disposable, and robust platform that can be deployed in any laboratory with access to a standard incubator. The system is compatible with high-throughput screening, multiplexed experimentation, and long-term culture, and its simplicity enables rapid iteration and customization. The invention is not limited to the specific materials or dimensions described herein; variations in the type of hydrophilic thread, the geometry of the microfluidic channel, the composition of the culture medium, or the incubation conditions may be adapted to suit the needs of specific applications, including the culture of primary cells, organoids, or co-cultures of multiple cell types. The invention provides a foundational platform upon which future innovations in tissue engineering, drug discovery, and precision medicine can be built, offering a pathway to democratize access to physiologically relevant in vitro models for researchers worldwide.

### Examples

The invention has been successfully implemented in multiple configurations to demonstrate its versatility, robustness, and biological relevance across a range of cell types and experimental paradigms. In a first example, a constant-width microfluidic channel device was fabricated with a width of 1 mm and a height of 150 µm, yielding a cross-sectional area of 0.15 mm². The inlet reservoir was formed by a punched hole 8 mm in diameter, and the outlet port was a 1.5 mm hole designed to receive a hydrophilic thread. A spunlace thread with a diameter of 1.2 mm and a length of 12 mm was inserted into the outlet port, with approximately 2 mm of the thread extending into the channel and the remainder protruding into the incubator environment. The device was sealed with a gas-permeable lid, placed in a sterile Petri dish, and transferred to a cell culture incubator maintained at 37 °C, 5% CO₂, and 90% relative humidity. Caco-2 cells, seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells/cm², were allowed to adhere for one hour before the channel was flushed with culture medium and the thread was activated. The flow rate was measured by tracking the displacement of 200 nm fluorescent polystyrene beads using time-lapse microscopy and was found to be 0.22 µL/min, corresponding to a shear stress of 0.02 dyn/cm². Over five days, the cells formed a confluent monolayer exhibiting dense F-actin stress fibers, continuous ZO-1 staining at cell-cell junctions, and the presence of Muc2-positive goblet cells, all indicative of a mature, differentiated intestinal epithelium. These characteristics were statistically indistinguishable from those observed in Caco-2 monolayers cultured under identical shear stress conditions using a peristaltic pump, confirming the biological equivalence of the pumpless system.

In a second example, a Hele-Shaw device was fabricated with a converging channel geometry, wherein the width decreased linearly from 3 mm at the inlet to 0.5 mm at the outlet, creating a gradient of shear stress along the length of the channel. The channel height remained constant at 150 µm. The inlet reservoir was again 8 mm in diameter, and a hydrophilic thread of 1.8 mm diameter and 20 mm length was inserted into the outlet. The device was sealed, placed in a Petri dish, and incubated under standard conditions. A549 VIM RFP cells, expressing vimentin fused to a red fluorescent protein, were seeded at the same density and allowed to adhere for one hour. The flow rate was measured at five distinct sections along the channel, corresponding to shear stresses ranging from 0.008 to 0.135 dyn/cm². Fluorescence intensity of vimentin was quantified at each section using confocal microscopy and normalized to the maximum intensity observed. A statistically significant decrease in vimentin expression was observed as shear stress decreased (p = 0.02), with cells in the high-shear region exhibiting elongated, spindle-shaped morphologies and reduced cell-cell adhesion, while cells in the low-shear region retained a more cobblestone-like epithelial morphology. These findings demonstrate that the device can generate spatially resolved mechanical stimuli within a single chip, enabling the identification of threshold shear stress values that trigger phenotypic transitions.

In a third example, a multiplexed device was fabricated containing five parallel microfluidic channels, each with a width of 1 mm and a height of 150 µm, fed from a single rectangular inlet reservoir measuring 10 mm by 20 mm. Each channel was connected to a unique hydrophilic thread with a fixed diameter of 1.5 mm but varying lengths of 5 mm, 10 mm, 15 mm, 20 mm, and 25 mm. The device was sealed, placed in a Petri dish, and incubated under standard conditions. Fluorescent beads were introduced into each channel, and their velocities were measured independently. The resulting flow rates were 0.18 µL/min, 0.31 µL/min, 0.45 µL/min, 0.62 µL/min, and 0.85 µL/min, respectively, corresponding to shear stresses of 0.024, 0.041, 0.060, 0.083, and 0.113 dyn/cm². Caco-2 cells were seeded simultaneously into all five channels and cultured for five days. At the end of the experiment, transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurements were performed using a voltmeter and electrodes inserted into the inlet and outlet reservoirs. TEER values increased progressively with shear stress, with the highest resistance observed in the channel exposed to the greatest shear stress, demonstrating that the device can be used to systematically investigate the relationship between mechanical stimulation and barrier function in a high-throughput manner.

In a fourth example, the device was used to culture primary human intestinal organoids derived from patient biopsies. The organoids were dissociated into single cells and seeded into a constant-width channel with a 1.2 mm diameter, 15 mm long thread. The flow rate was set to 0.15 µL/min, corresponding to a shear stress of 0.015 dyn/cm². Over seven days, the organoids formed a polarized epithelial layer with crypt-like structures, expressed Lgr5 and Ki67 as markers of stemness and proliferation, and secreted mucin and defensins. No signs of cell death, detachment, or abnormal morphology were observed, and the organoids remained viable and functional throughout the culture period. This example demonstrates that the device is compatible with delicate, patient-derived cell populations that are typically difficult to culture under conventional perfusion systems due to their sensitivity to flow fluctuations and contamination.

In a fifth example, the device was modified to accommodate a two-layer culture system, wherein a porous membrane was inserted between the glass substrate and the PDMS slab to allow for the co-culture of epithelial and endothelial cells. The membrane was coated with collagen and seeded with human umbilical vein endothelial cells on the lower side and Caco-2 cells on the upper side. The hydrophilic thread was inserted into the outlet port as before, and the flow rate was maintained at 0.2 µL/min. Over five days, the endothelial cells formed a continuous monolayer with VE-cadherin expression, while the Caco-2 cells developed tight junctions and microvilli. The system was then challenged with lipopolysaccharide, and the translocation of inflammatory cytokines from the apical to the basolateral compartment was quantified using ELISA. The results demonstrated a dose-dependent increase in IL-8 secretion, consistent with a functional barrier model. This example illustrates the adaptability of the invention to complex, multi-cellular architectures.

In a sixth example, the device was used to test the effect of drug exposure under controlled shear stress conditions. A549 cells were cultured in a constant-width channel with a 2 mm diameter, 28 mm long thread, generating a shear stress of 0.15 dyn/cm². After three days, the medium was replaced with medium containing 10 µM of a known EMT-inducing agent, TGF-β1. The flow was maintained continuously, and vimentin expression was monitored over the next 48 hours. A significant increase in vimentin fluorescence was observed compared to control cells cultured under the same shear stress but without TGF-β1, confirming that the device can be used to study the interaction between mechanical and biochemical stimuli in a controlled, reproducible manner.

In a seventh example, the device was operated continuously for 12 days without interruption. The inlet reservoir was replenished daily with fresh medium, and the thread was not replaced. Flow rate was measured every 24 hours using fluorescent bead tracking, and no significant decline in flow rate was observed over the entire period. Cell viability, as assessed by live/dead staining, remained above 95% throughout the experiment. This example demonstrates the long-term stability and reliability of the system, even under extended culture conditions.

In an eighth example, the device was sterilized using UV irradiation for 30 minutes prior to use and then operated under aseptic conditions in a biosafety cabinet. No microbial contamination was detected in any of the cultures, even when the device was operated in close proximity to other cell culture systems. This confirms that the sealed, tubing-free design effectively prevents cross-contamination.

In a ninth example, the device was used to culture cells under hypoxic conditions. The incubator was modified to maintain an oxygen concentration of 1% instead of the standard 21%. The flow rate remained stable, and the cells exhibited expected hypoxic responses, including upregulation of HIF-1α and increased expression of vascular endothelial growth factor. This demonstrates that the device is compatible with environmental perturbations beyond standard culture conditions.

In a tenth example, the device was fabricated using alternative materials, including cyclic olefin copolymer (COC) and polycarbonate, in place of PDMS. The hydrophilic thread was replaced with a commercially available wicking material composed of cellulose acetate. The system functioned identically, with comparable flow rates and cellular responses, demonstrating that the invention is not limited to PDMS or spunlace threads and can be adapted to a variety of materials and manufacturing processes.

These examples collectively demonstrate that the invention provides a robust, versatile, and biologically faithful platform for long-term, pumpless perfusion of cellular monolayers. The system is simple to fabricate, easy to operate, and compatible with a wide range of experimental conditions, cell types, and analytical techniques. It eliminates the need for expensive and complex equipment, reduces contamination risk, and enables high-throughput, multiplexed experimentation. The invention represents a transformative advancement in the field of organ-on-chip technology, making physiologically relevant in vitro models accessible to researchers across all levels of expertise and institutional resources.
