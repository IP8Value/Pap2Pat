# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of computational genetics and bioinformatics, specifically to the accelerated processing of pedigree data structures for the estimation of genetic probabilities, identity by descent (IBD), inbreeding coefficients, and haplotype reconstructions in diploid organisms. The invention provides a novel hardware architecture implemented on Field Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGAs) that enables the simultaneous, clock-synchronized simulation of meiotic allele transmission across all individuals in a pedigree structure, thereby achieving a level of computational parallelism unattainable by conventional sequential algorithms running on general-purpose central processing units. This architecture leverages the inherent hierarchical and modular nature of pedigrees—where each individual inherits exactly two alleles, one from each parent, and where the genetic state of any individual is determined solely by the meiotic events along the paths connecting it to its founders—to map each individual in the pedigree to a dedicated, reconfigurable logic module within the FPGA. Each such module operates in lockstep with all others, processing a single sample of allele inheritance per clock cycle, with the entire pedigree completing a full genetic simulation in a time proportional only to the number of generations, not the total number of individuals or loci. This approach fundamentally transforms the computational complexity of pedigree analysis from an exponential or combinatorial burden into a linear, pipelined operation, enabling real-time or near-real-time probabilistic inference for pedigrees containing thousands of individuals and multiple linked genetic markers. The invention is particularly applicable to applications in human medical genetics, livestock breeding, conservation biology, and population genetics, where the accurate and rapid estimation of carrier probabilities, disease risk, ancestral haplotypes, and relatedness coefficients is critical for decision-making, selection, and risk assessment. The system is designed to be self-contained, requiring no external computational resources during the core simulation phase, and is capable of operating with deterministic, pseudo-random, or enumerated allele and meiosis inputs, making it suitable for both approximate sampling and exact enumeration tasks. The architecture is scalable, modular, and compatible with hybrid computing environments that couple FPGA processing with general-purpose CPUs for post-processing, parameter optimization, or handling pedigrees too large to fit entirely within a single device.

## BACKGROUND ART

Prior methods for analyzing pedigree data have relied almost exclusively on sequential computational algorithms executed on general-purpose processors, which process individuals or meiotic pathways one at a time, often through nested loops that iterate over all possible combinations of allele inheritance patterns. These algorithms, while mathematically rigorous, suffer from exponential growth in computational demand as pedigree size, number of loci, or complexity of inheritance patterns increases. For example, the estimation of identity by descent probabilities or genotype likelihoods in pedigrees with inbreeding loops or multiple generations requires evaluating every possible combination of meiosis indicators—binary variables indicating which parental allele is transmitted at each meiosis event—leading to a combinatorial explosion that renders exact maximum likelihood estimation infeasible for even moderately sized pedigrees. To mitigate this, researchers have developed approximate sampling methods such as gene dropping, in which alleles are stochastically transmitted from founders through the pedigree according to Mendelian rules, and samples consistent with observed genotype data are retained to estimate probabilities. While these methods reduce the burden of exhaustive enumeration, they remain computationally intensive, requiring millions or billions of independent samples to achieve statistically reliable estimates, particularly when the proportion of observed genotypes is low or when multiple linked loci are involved. The time required to generate each sample scales linearly with the number of individuals and the depth of the pedigree, and since each sample must be processed sequentially, the total runtime grows proportionally with both the number of samples and the size of the pedigree. Parallelization efforts have been attempted using clusters of general-purpose processors, where different processors handle different subsets of the outermost sampling loop. However, such approaches yield only marginal speedups—typically no greater than the number of processors employed—because the underlying algorithm remains sequential within each processor, and communication overhead, load balancing, and synchronization between nodes introduce significant inefficiencies. Moreover, these methods do not exploit the intrinsic spatial and temporal regularity of pedigree structures, where the genetic state of any individual depends only on its direct ancestors and not on unrelated branches of the pedigree. This structural property, which permits independent and concurrent simulation of unrelated lineages, has remained untapped in conventional software implementations. Alternative hardware accelerators, such as application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs), have been considered but rejected due to their inflexibility and prohibitive development costs, especially given the wide variability in pedigree structures across different species and studies. Field Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGAs), while recognized for their potential in bioinformatics due to their reconfigurable logic fabric and fine-grained parallelism, have not previously been successfully applied to the problem of pedigree simulation. Prior FPGA applications in biology have focused on molecular dynamics, sequence alignment, or protein folding—domains characterized by regular but fundamentally different data structures. No prior art describes a method for mapping the hierarchical, tree-like, generationally layered structure of a pedigree onto a reconfigurable logic array in such a way that each individual is represented by a dedicated, self-contained processing module, synchronized across all layers, and capable of transmitting alleles and evaluating inheritance consistency in a single clock cycle. Furthermore, no prior system has achieved the critical innovation of pipelining: the ability to initiate a new sample for the entire pedigree at every clock cycle, such that multiple samples propagate simultaneously through different generations, thereby achieving a throughput of one complete pedigree sample per clock cycle regardless of pedigree size. Existing FPGA-based genetic algorithms have been limited to single-locus, single-individual, or non-pedigree contexts, and none have addressed the challenge of maintaining allele coherence across multiple generations, managing memory for founder alleles, handling holder modules for individuals with multiple offspring, or synchronizing the validation of samples only after full transmission through the terminal generation. The absence of such a system has left a critical gap in the field: the inability to perform high-throughput, high-precision genetic inference on large, complex pedigrees in a time frame that is practically useful for real-world applications in breeding programs, clinical diagnostics, or population studies. The present invention fills this gap by introducing a novel, fully integrated hardware architecture that redefines the computational paradigm for pedigree analysis, transforming it from a serial, iterative process into a massively parallel, pipelined, and deterministic simulation engine.

## DISCLOSURE OF INVENTION

The present invention discloses a novel hardware-based system for the rapid simulation of genetic inheritance in pedigrees, implemented on a Field Programmable Gate Array (FPGA), wherein each individual in the pedigree is represented by a dedicated, reconfigurable logic module arranged in a layered architecture corresponding to generations. The system operates by initializing a set of founder individuals in layer zero, each assigned a pair of alleles drawn from a specified distribution, and then, at each subsequent clock cycle, propagating those alleles through the pedigree according to Mendelian inheritance rules, with each descendant module receiving one allele from each parent, or both alleles from a single parent in the case of a holder module. The key innovation lies in the architectural mapping of the pedigree structure onto the FPGA fabric such that every meiosis event, allele transmission, and genotype validation occurs in parallel across all individuals simultaneously, with all operations completed within a single clock cycle. This is achieved by designing each individual module to contain memory registers for storing incoming alleles, a comparator to determine identity by descent, a counter to accumulate valid samples, and logic gates to transmit the appropriate allele to offspring, all implemented in combinational and sequential digital logic. Holder modules, which serve as memory buffers for individuals with multiple offspring but only one parent, are integrated into the architecture to maintain temporal synchronization across the pedigree, ensuring that alleles transmitted from generation to generation remain aligned with the clock cycle of the system. The system is pipelined such that, while one sample is being completed in the terminal generation, a new sample is simultaneously initiated in the founder layer, allowing for a continuous throughput of one complete pedigree sample per clock cycle, regardless of the number of individuals or the depth of the pedigree. The architecture supports both stochastic and deterministic sampling: founder alleles may be generated randomly from a multinomial distribution based on population allele frequencies, or they may be systematically enumerated to produce exact solutions; meiosis indicators may be pseudo-randomly generated using cellular automata or other FPGA-compatible random number generators, or they may be iterated through all possible binary combinations for exhaustive enumeration. The system further includes a terminal validation layer, positioned below the final generation of individuals, which collects and evaluates the consistency of all observed genotypes across the pedigree. Only when all observed genotypes are matched by the simulated alleles is a master validity signal asserted, enabling the accumulation of counts for genotypes, haplotypes, or IBD states in dedicated counters. This design eliminates the need for post-processing or backtracking, as validation occurs in real time during the simulation. The invention further encompasses the use of modular, scalable logic blocks that can be replicated across the FPGA to accommodate pedigrees of varying size, with the only constraint being the available logic resources on the device. For multi-locus analyses, the system extends to haplotype tracking, where linked alleles on the same chromosome are treated as a single unit, and recombination events between loci are simulated using binomially distributed indicators, with each recombination event occurring independently and in parallel across all individuals. The system avoids floating-point arithmetic entirely, relying instead on integer counters and bit-level operations, which are efficiently implemented in FPGA logic and do not require clock speed reduction. The invention further includes an interface module, implemented using a soft-core processor such as Picoblaze, which provides communication between the FPGA and an external general-purpose computer, enabling the loading of pedigree structures, configuration of allele distributions, initiation of sampling runs, and retrieval of accumulated results. The entire system is programmable in a hardware description language such as VHDL, allowing the pedigree structure to be pre-processed and mapped onto the FPGA fabric prior to operation, with logic optimized to minimize resource usage and maximize clock frequency. The invention enables the simulation of pedigrees containing hundreds to thousands of individuals at speeds orders of magnitude faster than any sequential algorithm running on a general-purpose processor, with speedup ratios exceeding 10,000-fold for large pedigrees and approaching 100,000-fold when scaled to high-performance FPGAs. The architecture is inherently deterministic, reproducible, and free from the convergence issues inherent in Monte Carlo methods, and it enables exact enumeration of all possible inheritance paths when computational resources permit. The invention further enables the integration of the system into hybrid computing platforms, where the FPGA performs the high-throughput sampling while a coupled CPU performs parameter optimization, likelihood weighting, or statistical aggregation, thereby extending the utility of the system to complex inference models such as Metropolis-Hastings or importance sampling. The invention represents a paradigm shift in pedigree analysis, replacing iterative, sequential computation with a massively parallel, hardware-accelerated simulation engine that exploits the intrinsic structure of genetic inheritance to achieve unprecedented computational efficiency.

## BEST MODE FOR CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION

The best mode for carrying out the invention involves the construction of a complete, self-contained hardware system implemented on a Field Programmable Gate Array (FPGA), wherein the pedigree structure is first parsed and converted into a layered topology of interconnected logic modules, each representing an individual in the pedigree. The system is initialized by loading the pedigree structure into a pre-processing module, which identifies the parent-offspring relationships, determines the generation layer of each individual, flags individuals with known genotypes, and designates those without known parents as founders. The resulting topology is then mapped onto the FPGA fabric using a hardware description language such as VHDL, with each individual assigned a dedicated logic module that occupies a fixed region of the FPGA’s configurable logic blocks, memory elements, and interconnect resources. Founder modules are configured with internal registers to store two alleles per locus, which may be fixed, randomly sampled from a multinomial distribution, or systematically enumerated depending on the analysis objective. These modules do not receive input from parents, as they have none, and instead generate their alleles at the start of each clock cycle. Descendant modules, which represent individuals with known parents, are constructed with two input ports for receiving alleles from their respective parents, a comparator to determine whether the two received alleles are identical by descent, and output ports to transmit one of the two received alleles to each of their offspring, with the selection governed by a meiosis indicator—a single-bit signal generated at each clock cycle that determines which parental allele is passed on. In the case of individuals with only one known parent, a holder module is instantiated, which contains memory registers to store both alleles received from the single parent and to retain them across multiple clock cycles, thereby enabling the same pair of alleles to be transmitted to multiple offspring in subsequent generations without requiring redundant computation. The holder module is synchronized with the global clock and ensures that the alleles it transmits to its progeny are those received in the previous cycle, thereby preserving the temporal integrity of the simulation. All modules, whether founder, descendant, or holder, operate in lockstep with the system clock, such that every allele transmission, meiosis decision, and genotype comparison occurs simultaneously across the entire pedigree during a single clock cycle. The system is structured into horizontal layers, with layer zero containing all founders, layer one containing their direct offspring, layer two containing grandchildren, and so forth, with each layer representing one generation. The depth of the system corresponds to the maximum number of generations separating any individual from its founders. At each clock cycle, the founder modules generate new alleles, which propagate downward through the pedigree, with each descendant module receiving its parental alleles and producing its own transmitted alleles for the next generation. After a number of clock cycles equal to the depth of the pedigree, the alleles reach the terminal generation, where they are compared against any observed genotype data associated with those individuals. A validation network, composed of a tree of AND gates and comparators, evaluates whether the simulated genotypes match the observed genotypes for all individuals with known data. If all observed genotypes are consistent with the simulated ones, a global validity signal is asserted, triggering the incrementation of counters that track the frequency of specific genotypes, haplotypes, or IBD states for each individual. These counters are implemented as integer registers, each associated with a particular genotype or haplotype combination, and are updated only when the validity signal is high, ensuring that only samples consistent with the observed data contribute to the final probability estimates. For multi-locus analyses, the system is extended to track haplotypes rather than individual alleles, with each locus on a chromosome treated as a contiguous unit. Recombination events between adjacent loci are simulated using independent binomial indicators generated for each meiosis event, with the probability of recombination determined by a pre-defined rate. These indicators are incorporated into the logic of each descendant module, such that when a recombination event occurs, the haplotype transmitted to offspring is a mosaic of the two parental haplotypes, with the crossover point determined by the recombination indicator. The system maintains separate counters for each possible haplotype combination at each locus, enabling the estimation of linkage disequilibrium and recombination rates directly from the accumulated samples. The entire architecture is designed to operate at a high clock frequency, typically in the range of 50 to 200 MHz, depending on the complexity of the logic and the FPGA technology used, with each clock cycle producing one complete sample of the entire pedigree. The system does not require any external memory access during the core simulation phase, as all data is stored locally within the FPGA’s embedded block RAM and registers, minimizing latency and maximizing throughput. The interface to the external computing system is provided by a soft-core processor such as Picoblaze, which is embedded within the FPGA alongside the pedigree modules. This processor handles the initialization of the system, the loading of pedigree metadata, the configuration of allele frequency distributions, the triggering of sampling runs, and the retrieval of accumulated counter values after each simulation batch. The processor communicates with the host computer via a standard interface such as USB or PCIe, enabling the user to specify parameters, initiate runs, and download results without requiring specialized knowledge of hardware design. The system is designed to be scalable: for larger pedigrees, the FPGA’s logic resources are partitioned to accommodate additional modules, with the only limitation being the total number of configurable logic elements available on the device. For pedigrees exceeding the capacity of a single FPGA, multiple devices may be interconnected, with synchronization maintained across devices through a shared clock signal and coordinated data transfer protocols. The system is also designed for power efficiency, as all operations are performed in digital logic without floating-point computations, and the FPGA consumes significantly less power than an equivalent general-purpose processor performing the same task. The entire system, including the FPGA, interface processor, and supporting circuitry, may be mounted on a single printed circuit board, with power and data connections to a standard computer, forming a plug-and-play hardware accelerator for pedigree analysis. In operation, the user uploads the pedigree structure and desired analysis parameters to the system via the host computer. The system then configures the FPGA according to the specified topology, initializes the founder alleles, and begins the simulation. At each clock cycle, a new sample is generated, and if the sample is valid, the corresponding genotype or haplotype counts are incremented. After a predetermined number of samples have been collected, the system halts, and the accumulated counts are transferred back to the host computer for statistical analysis. The speed of the system is independent of pedigree size, as long as the pedigree fits within the FPGA’s resources, and is determined solely by the clock frequency and the number of generations. For a pedigree of 1,000 individuals with 10 generations, the system can generate 100 million samples per second on a 100 MHz FPGA, whereas a conventional sequential algorithm on a 3 GHz processor might generate only 1,000 samples per second, yielding a speedup of 100,000-fold. The system achieves this extraordinary performance not by brute-force parallelism, but by exploiting the inherent structure of genetic inheritance to create a deterministic, pipelined, and fully parallel simulation engine that mirrors the biological process itself. The invention thus represents a fundamental rethinking of how pedigree data is processed, replacing software iteration with hardware synchronization, and transforming what was once an intractable computational problem into a routine, real-time operation.
