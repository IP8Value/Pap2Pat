# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to systems and methods for modeling the progression of chronic diseases using longitudinal observational data, particularly through the application of a Continuous-Time Hidden Markov Model (CTHMM) to infer latent disease states and their temporal transitions across multiple clinical domains. More specifically, the invention provides a computational framework capable of identifying and quantifying subtle, multidimensional changes in disease status prior to and following clinically recognized milestones—such as motor onset in neurodegenerative disorders—by integrating heterogeneous, irregularly sampled clinical measurements from large-scale observational cohorts. The system is particularly suited for diseases lacking well-defined biomarkers or staging systems, including rare and under-studied conditions such as Huntington’s Disease, where traditional clinical metrics are insufficient to capture the full spectrum of progression. The invention enables the construction of data-driven, probabilistic disease progression models that account for continuous-time dynamics, missing observations, and multi-symptom interdependencies, thereby offering a more granular, individualized, and temporally accurate representation of disease evolution than existing clinical staging systems. The resulting models can be deployed in clinical decision support systems, stratified patient recruitment for clinical trials, prognostic counseling, and longitudinal monitoring platforms to enhance personalized care and accelerate therapeutic development.

## SUMMARY

The invention discloses a novel computational framework for modeling the progression of chronic diseases by inferring a sequence of latent, clinically meaningful disease states from longitudinal, multi-domain observational data collected in real-world settings. The framework operates through two principal phases: first, a data-driven determination of the optimal number of disease states via model selection based on cross-validated log-likelihood scores; and second, the estimation of a continuous-time hidden Markov model that captures the probabilistic transitions between these states and the statistical relationships between observed clinical measurements and unobserved disease states. The model assumes that disease progression unfolds as a continuous-time Markov process governed by a transition rate matrix, wherein each state corresponds to a distinct configuration of symptom severity across multiple clinical domains—motor, cognitive, and functional—represented by latent factor scores derived from Bayesian latent variable analysis. The observed clinical features are modeled as conditionally independent Gaussian distributions given the underlying disease state, allowing for robust handling of heterogeneous, sparse, and irregularly sampled data. The model is trained using an expectation-maximization algorithm that iteratively updates the transition rates, initial state probabilities, and observation parameters while estimating the most probable sequence of hidden states for each individual. A critical innovation lies in the imposition of domain-specific structural constraints on the transition matrix—such as a second-order forward-chain structure for Huntington’s Disease—ensuring biological plausibility while preserving flexibility to capture non-linear progression patterns, including state skipping. The framework further enables the calculation of expected durations for each disease state, the prediction of future state trajectories for individual patients, and the generation of personalized disease staging that extends beyond traditional clinical benchmarks. Unlike existing staging systems that rely on single-domain, post-onset criteria, the invention provides a unified, longitudinal, and multidimensional characterization of disease evolution from pre-symptomatic to terminal phases, revealing previously undetected subphenotypes and progression pathways. The model has been validated on an integrated dataset comprising over 55,000 clinical assessments from more than 16,000 genetically confirmed Huntington’s Disease gene expansion carriers, demonstrating superior resolution in capturing pre-motor progression, identifying transition phases with distinct symptom trajectories, and aligning with known clinical phenomena such as the late attenuation of chorea and the early decline in occupational function. The resulting Integrated Huntington’s Disease Progression Model (IHDPM), comprising nine discrete disease states with empirically derived transition dynamics and expected durations spanning decades, serves as a prototype for the application of the framework to other chronic conditions lacking robust staging systems. The invention thus provides a generalizable, statistically rigorous, and clinically interpretable methodology for transforming sparse, noisy, real-world observational data into a dynamic, probabilistic map of disease progression, enabling precision medicine approaches previously unattainable with conventional clinical metrics.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive, computationally rigorous, and clinically actionable framework for modeling the progression of chronic diseases through the integration of longitudinal observational data collected across multiple clinical domains. The framework is designed to overcome the fundamental limitations of existing disease staging systems, which are typically derived from cross-sectional clinical knowledge, rely on single-domain biomarkers, and fail to capture the continuous, multidimensional nature of disease evolution. In particular, the invention addresses the challenges posed by sparse, irregularly sampled observations, the absence of well-defined clinical milestones for many rare or under-studied conditions, and the inability of traditional models to simultaneously account for progression across motor, cognitive, and functional domains. The core innovation lies in the application of a Continuous-Time Hidden Markov Model (CTHMM) to infer a sequence of latent disease states that represent distinct, empirically derived configurations of symptom burden, each characterized by its own statistical profile of clinical manifestations and its own probabilistic transition dynamics to subsequent states. The model is not constrained by predefined clinical thresholds or expert-defined staging criteria; rather, it derives its structure entirely from the data, using statistical inference to uncover the underlying temporal architecture of disease progression.

The framework begins with the preprocessing and dimensionality reduction of high-dimensional, heterogeneous clinical measurements collected from large observational cohorts. Clinical assessments, which may include motor scores, cognitive test results, functional capacity ratings, behavioral questionnaires, and imaging biomarkers, are often collected at irregular intervals, with significant missingness and variability in measurement protocols across sites and time points. To address this, the invention employs a Bayesian latent variable analysis technique to extract low-dimensional, domain-specific latent factor scores that capture the underlying progression trajectories within each clinical domain—motor, cognitive, and functional—while minimizing noise and redundancy. The number of latent factors retained in each domain is kept equal to ensure balanced representation and prevent bias toward any single domain. These latent factor scores, derived from the integrated dataset of thousands of patients, serve as the observed variables in the subsequent CTHMM. This step is critical, as it transforms raw, heterogeneous clinical measurements into a unified, interpretable, and statistically robust feature space that reflects the unobserved disease process with greater sensitivity and fidelity than any single clinical score.

Once the latent feature space is established, the invention proceeds to determine the optimal number of disease states that best explain the observed longitudinal patterns. This is accomplished through a data-driven model selection procedure in which a series of CTHMMs, each with a different number of states ranging from six to twelve, are trained on a training subset of the data. Each model is then evaluated on a held-out testing subset by computing the log-likelihood of the observed data under the fitted model. The number of states yielding the highest log-likelihood is selected as the optimal configuration, ensuring that the model achieves the best balance between complexity and explanatory power. This approach is particularly powerful for diseases such as Huntington’s Disease, where no universally accepted staging system exists for the pre-motor phase and where clinical knowledge alone is insufficient to define discrete progression milestones. The selection of nine states as optimal for Huntington’s Disease, for instance, was not based on prior clinical assumptions but emerged from the statistical fit of the data, revealing a previously unrecognized granularity in the progression trajectory that spans decades.

With the number of disease states determined, the invention constructs the full CTHMM by estimating the parameters of the model using an expectation-maximization algorithm. The underlying disease progression is modeled as a continuous-time Markov process, characterized by an M × M transition generator matrix Q, where M is the number of disease states. Each off-diagonal element Q(i,j) represents the instantaneous rate at which a patient transitions from state i to state j, while the diagonal elements are constrained such that the row sums equal zero, ensuring the conservation of probability. The structure of Q is not left unconstrained; instead, it is informed by clinical knowledge and biological plausibility. For Huntington’s Disease, a second-order forward-chain progression model is imposed, meaning that a patient in state i may remain in state i, transition to state i+1, or transition directly to state i+2, but cannot regress to a prior state or skip more than two states at once. This constraint reflects the known slow, progressive, and irreversible nature of the disease, and it significantly reduces the parameter space, improving model identifiability and interpretability. The last state is designated as an absorbing state, representing the terminal phase of the disease, from which no further transitions occur. The initial state distribution π, which represents the probability of a patient entering the disease process in each of the M states, is also estimated from the data. The observation model assumes that each clinical feature, given the underlying disease state, follows a univariate Gaussian distribution with state-specific mean and variance parameters. This assumption, while simplifying the model, is both statistically tractable and empirically valid for the latent factor scores used in this framework, and it allows for straightforward parameter updates during the expectation-maximization procedure.

The expectation-maximization algorithm alternates between two steps: the expectation step, in which the posterior probabilities of the hidden state sequences are computed for each patient using the forward-backward algorithm, and the maximization step, in which the model parameters—Q, π, and the Gaussian observation parameters—are updated to maximize the expected log-likelihood of the complete data. The transition rates are updated by computing the expected number of transitions between each pair of states over all time intervals, normalized by the expected total time spent in the source state. The initial state probabilities are updated based on the proportion of patients whose first observation is assigned to each state. The means and variances of the Gaussian observation distributions are updated using weighted averages of the observed latent factor scores, where the weights are the posterior probabilities of the corresponding state at each time point. The Viterbi algorithm is employed during the expectation step to compute the most likely sequence of hidden states for each individual patient, enabling the generation of personalized disease trajectories. The algorithm is iterated until convergence, at which point the model parameters stabilize and the inferred state sequences provide the most probable explanation of the observed data under the assumed model structure.

The resulting model, referred to as the Integrated Huntington’s Disease Progression Model (IHDPM), comprises nine distinct disease states that collectively span the entire progression pathway from pre-symptomatic genetic carrier status to terminal disease. These states are not defined by arbitrary clinical thresholds but emerge from the statistical structure of the data itself. The model reveals that the progression of Huntington’s Disease can be meaningfully segmented into three broad phases: Prodromal, Transition, and Manifest. States 1 and 2 correspond to the Prodromal phase, during which the majority of patients have not yet reached the clinically defined motor onset, as indicated by Diagnostic Confidence Level scores below 4. States 3 through 5 constitute the Transition phase, during which motor onset occurs in the majority of patients, and the model captures subtle but critical shifts in symptom profiles that precede and accompany this milestone. States 6 through 9 constitute the Manifest phase, during which patients exhibit overt clinical signs of the disease and progress toward functional and cognitive decline. The model demonstrates that the transition from Prodromal to Transition states is not abrupt but gradual, with measurable changes in cognitive and motor latent factors occurring years before motor onset is clinically recognized. This finding contradicts the conventional clinical paradigm, which treats motor onset as the primary event in disease progression, and instead positions it as a late manifestation within a much longer, more complex trajectory.

The transition dynamics between states are captured in the transition probability matrix A(δ), computed as the matrix exponential of δQ, where δ represents a time interval—in this case, one year. The matrix reveals that the majority of patients remain in the same state over a one-year period, reflecting the slow progression of the disease. However, non-zero probabilities of transitioning to states two steps ahead—specifically from state 3 to state 5 and from state 4 to state 6—suggest the existence of multiple progression pathways during the Transition phase. These “skipping” transitions indicate that some patients experience rapid deterioration across multiple domains simultaneously, while others progress more gradually. This heterogeneity is invisible to traditional staging systems, which assign patients to a single stage based on a single functional score. The IHDPM, by contrast, reveals that the period surrounding motor onset is not a uniform transition but a bifurcating pathway with distinct subphenotypes, each with its own rate of progression and symptom profile. This insight has profound implications for clinical trial design, as it suggests that subcohorts of patients following different progression trajectories may respond differently to therapeutic interventions, and that stratification based on latent state trajectories—not just motor onset—could improve trial sensitivity and efficiency.

The model also provides quantitative estimates of the expected duration of each disease state, derived from the transition rates and the structure of the Markov process. The Prodromal phase, encompassing states 1 and 2, has an expected cumulative duration of nearly 20 years, consistent with epidemiological observations that subtle changes in cognition, behavior, and fine motor control can precede motor diagnosis by decades. The Transition phase, comprising states 3 through 5, lasts approximately 12 years in total, with state 4 exhibiting the longest expected duration, suggesting that this state represents a prolonged period of clinical ambiguity during which patients are approaching but have not yet crossed the threshold of motor diagnosis. The Manifest phase, covering states 6 through 8, has a cumulative expected duration of approximately 10 years, aligning closely with the known life expectancy following motor diagnosis. These durations are not arbitrary but are derived from the empirical transition rates observed across the entire cohort, making them statistically grounded and generalizable. The final state, state 9, is absorbing, and no duration is assigned to it, as it represents the terminal phase of the disease.

The IHDPM model is further validated by its ability to reproduce known clinical phenomena that are not explicitly encoded into the model. For example, the model correctly captures the late peak and subsequent attenuation of chorea scores, which rise during the Transition phase and reach maximum severity in state 7 before plateauing or declining in the later Manifest states. This pattern, which is well-documented in clinical literature but not modeled in traditional staging systems, emerges naturally from the data-driven estimation of state-specific means for motor latent factors. Similarly, the model identifies the early decline in occupational function—represented by the Occupation score—as one of the first measurable indicators of functional impairment, occurring as early as state 2, long before motor onset. This finding challenges the assumption that functional decline is a consequence of motor deterioration and instead suggests that it may be an independent, early manifestation of the underlying neurodegenerative process. The Mini-Mental State Examination score, which remains relatively stable until late in the Manifest phase, is also accurately captured, reinforcing the model’s ability to distinguish between domains with different temporal profiles of decline.

The individual-level state sequences generated by the Viterbi algorithm provide a powerful tool for personalized clinical management. Unlike the binary distinction between “premanifest” and “manifest” used in traditional staging, the IHDPM assigns each patient a continuous, granular progression trajectory that reflects their unique symptom burden and rate of change. For example, a patient may remain in state 2 for several years, indicating stable pre-motor status, before transitioning to state 3, signaling the onset of a more active progression phase. Subsequent transitions to states 4 and 5 indicate increasing risk of imminent motor diagnosis, enabling clinicians to anticipate clinical milestones and tailor counseling, monitoring, and intervention strategies accordingly. The model can also be used to predict future states and latent factor values for individual patients, providing probabilistic forecasts of disease trajectory that can inform care planning and patient decision-making. These predictions are not deterministic but are expressed as probability distributions over future states, reflecting the inherent uncertainty in disease progression and the variability between individuals.

The framework is not limited to Huntington’s Disease and is designed to be generalizable to other chronic, progressive conditions. The methodology can be applied to any disease for which longitudinal observational data are available across multiple clinical domains, including Parkinson’s Disease, Alzheimer’s Disease, multiple sclerosis, chronic kidney disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and rare genetic disorders. The key requirements for application are the availability of longitudinal clinical assessments, the existence of multiple symptom domains that evolve over time, and the absence of a well-established, validated staging system. The structural constraints on the transition matrix—such as forward-only progression, order of progression, or the inclusion of absorbing states—can be adapted based on domain-specific knowledge. For example, in a disease with potential for partial recovery, the transition matrix could be modified to allow for backward transitions. In a disease with multiple distinct subtypes, the model could be extended to include mixture components or covariates that condition the transition rates on genetic, demographic, or environmental factors. The Gaussian observation model can be replaced with other distributions from the exponential family, such as Poisson for count data or Bernoulli for binary outcomes, without altering the core algorithmic structure. The expectation-maximization framework remains unchanged, and the model selection procedure based on cross-validated log-likelihood remains applicable.

The invention further enables the integration of the model into clinical decision support systems, electronic health record platforms, and remote monitoring applications. The inferred state sequences and transition probabilities can be automatically updated as new clinical data become available, allowing for real-time re-staging of patients and dynamic adjustment of care plans. For example, a patient undergoing annual assessments could receive a quarterly update on their projected disease trajectory, with alerts triggered if their progression rate accelerates beyond expected norms. In clinical trials, the model can be used to identify patients who are likely to transition to the next disease state within a specified time window, enabling more efficient recruitment of participants for disease-modifying therapies. The model can also be used to define novel endpoints for clinical trials—such as the time to transition from state 3 to state 4—that are more sensitive and biologically meaningful than traditional functional scores.

The statistical robustness of the model is further enhanced by its ability to handle missing data, irregular sampling intervals, and heterogeneous measurement protocols. Unlike models that require complete, regularly spaced observations, the CTHMM framework explicitly accounts for the timing and structure of each patient’s observation schedule. The transition probabilities are computed as functions of the actual time elapsed between visits, not as fixed intervals, ensuring that the model remains accurate even when patients are assessed every six months, every two years, or at irregular intervals due to clinical or logistical constraints. The use of latent factor scores, derived from Bayesian factor analysis, further mitigates the impact of missing individual assessments, as the model infers the underlying progression trajectory even when some clinical measures are unavailable at certain time points.

The invention represents a paradigm shift in disease progression modeling. Rather than imposing preconceived clinical categories onto heterogeneous data, the framework allows the data to reveal its own structure, uncovering latent phases, transition dynamics, and progression pathways that were previously invisible. The resulting model is not a static staging system but a dynamic, probabilistic map of disease evolution that can be continuously refined as more data become available. It transforms the way clinicians and researchers conceptualize chronic disease—from a series of discrete stages defined by arbitrary thresholds to a continuous, multidimensional journey shaped by individual biology and time.

The model’s outputs are interpretable, actionable, and scalable. Each disease state is accompanied by a profile of expected symptom severity across all domains, enabling clinicians to understand not just where a patient is in their disease course, but what symptoms they are likely to experience next. The expected durations provide a quantitative basis for prognostic counseling, allowing patients and families to plan for future care needs with greater accuracy. The individual state sequences offer a personalized roadmap of progression that can guide treatment decisions, clinical monitoring schedules, and enrollment in targeted interventions. The model’s ability to detect early changes in functional capacity before motor onset opens new avenues for preventive strategies and early intervention trials.

In summary, the invention provides a novel, statistically rigorous, and clinically grounded framework for modeling the progression of chronic diseases using real-world observational data. By combining continuous-time Markov processes, latent variable modeling, and data-driven model selection, the framework overcomes the limitations of traditional staging systems and delivers a comprehensive, individualized, and dynamic representation of disease evolution. The resulting Integrated Huntington’s Disease Progression Model exemplifies the power of this approach, revealing a decade-long pre-motor phase, multiple progression pathways around motor onset, and a nuanced, multidimensional trajectory that aligns with clinical knowledge while uncovering new insights. The framework is generalizable, adaptable, and scalable, making it applicable to a wide range of chronic conditions and poised to transform the future of precision medicine in neurodegenerative and other progressive diseases.
