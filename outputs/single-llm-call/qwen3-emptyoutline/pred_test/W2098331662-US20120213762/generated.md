# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Pompe disease, also known as glycogen storage disease type II, is a rare, inherited lysosomal storage disorder caused by mutations in the gene encoding acid α-glucosidase (GAA), an enzyme essential for the hydrolysis of glycogen within lysosomes. The deficiency or complete absence of functional GAA leads to the progressive accumulation of glycogen within lysosomes of skeletal, cardiac, and smooth muscle tissues, resulting in cellular dysfunction, tissue damage, and ultimately organ failure. The clinical manifestations of Pompe disease vary widely depending on the age of onset and residual enzyme activity, but the most severe form, infantile-onset Pompe disease, is characterized by profound hypotonia, cardiomegaly, respiratory insufficiency, and early death if left untreated. Even in later-onset forms, progressive muscle weakness, respiratory decline, and reduced mobility significantly impair quality of life and life expectancy. Prior to the advent of enzyme replacement therapy (ERT), Pompe disease was universally fatal in its infantile form, with most affected children succumbing before the age of two.

The first approved ERT for Pompe disease, alglucosidase alfa, is a recombinant form of human GAA derived from Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells and administered intravenously on a biweekly basis. While this therapy represents a landmark achievement in the treatment of lysosomal storage disorders, its clinical efficacy is limited by several intrinsic biological and pharmacokinetic constraints. The primary mechanism by which alglucosidase alfa enters target cells is through receptor-mediated endocytosis mediated by the cation-independent mannose 6-phosphate receptor (CI-MPR). This receptor recognizes and binds to mannose 6-phosphate (M6P) residues that are post-translationally added to N-linked glycans on the enzyme during its biosynthesis in the Golgi apparatus. However, the efficiency of this process is highly dependent on the extent and uniformity of M6P glycosylation, which is inherently variable due to the complex and sensitive nature of glycosylation pathways in mammalian cell culture systems. Variability in glycosylation patterns between production batches has been documented and has led to regulatory challenges, including the need for new biologics license applications due to differences in pharmacokinetics and biodistribution between manufacturing scales. Furthermore, the natural affinity of GAA for the CI-MPR is relatively low, and only a small fraction of the administered enzyme molecules carry sufficient M6P residues to enable efficient cellular uptake. As a result, a large proportion of the infused enzyme is rapidly cleared from circulation by non-target tissues such as the liver and spleen, or degraded extracellularly, necessitating high and frequent dosing regimens that are both costly and burdensome for patients.

Even when internalized, the delivered enzyme faces additional barriers to therapeutic efficacy. The CI-MPR is expressed at low levels on the surface of skeletal and cardiac muscle cells, the primary sites of glycogen accumulation in Pompe disease. Moreover, the receptor is predominantly localized intracellularly, cycling between the trans-Golgi network and endosomes, and only transiently traffics to the plasma membrane under specific signaling conditions. This limited surface availability further restricts the capacity of muscle cells to internalize exogenous GAA. Consequently, even at high therapeutic doses, the amount of enzyme that successfully reaches the lysosomes of affected muscle cells remains insufficient to fully reverse glycogen storage pathology. Clinical studies have demonstrated that while alglucosidase alfa can improve survival and motor function in some patients, it often fails to normalize glycogen levels in key skeletal muscles, particularly in the diaphragm, quadriceps, and heart, which remain major contributors to morbidity and mortality. Additionally, the enzyme’s intracellular half-life is limited, requiring repeated infusions to maintain therapeutic levels, and its processing within the lysosome, while functional, does not confer any advantage in terms of persistence or catalytic efficiency over endogenous enzyme.

Attempts to enhance the delivery of GAA to muscle tissues have been pursued through various strategies, including modification of glycosylation patterns, co-administration of pharmacological chaperones, and the use of alternative receptor targeting ligands. However, these approaches have met with limited success. Modifying glycosylation to increase M6P content has proven technically challenging and inconsistent across production systems, and attempts to use insulin-like growth factor II (IGF-II), a natural high-affinity ligand for the CI-MPR, have been hindered by the mitogenic potential of IGF-II, which raises significant safety concerns due to its association with tumor growth and aberrant cell proliferation. The development of a therapeutic agent that retains the catalytic activity of GAA while simultaneously achieving high-efficiency, receptor-specific targeting to muscle lysosomes without triggering unwanted mitogenic signaling has therefore remained an unmet medical need. The present invention addresses this critical gap by introducing a novel chimeric enzyme construct that fuses a modified, non-mitogenic fragment of IGF-II—termed the glycosylation-independent lysosomal targeting (GILT) tag—to the N-terminus of human GAA. This fusion protein, designated herein as BMN 701, exploits the high-affinity interaction between the GILT tag and the IGF-II binding domain of the CI-MPR to achieve dramatically enhanced cellular uptake, lysosomal delivery, and glycogen clearance in skeletal and cardiac muscle tissues, while eliminating the risks associated with full-length IGF-II. The invention further demonstrates that this targeting strategy is independent of glycosylation, thereby circumventing the variability and manufacturing challenges inherent in M6P-dependent ERT, and provides a robust, scalable, and reproducible platform for the treatment of Pompe disease and potentially other lysosomal storage disorders.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel chimeric enzyme composition comprising a fusion protein of human acid α-glucosidase (GAA) and a glycosylation-independent lysosomal targeting (GILT) tag, wherein the GILT tag is derived from a non-mitogenic portion of insulin-like growth factor II (IGF-II) and is fused to the N-terminus of GAA at amino acid residue 70. The fusion protein, designated BMN 701, exhibits enzymatic activity equivalent to that of native recombinant human GAA, yet demonstrates a marked enhancement in cellular uptake via the cation-independent mannose 6-phosphate receptor (CI-MPR) through high-affinity binding to the IGF-II binding domain of the receptor, independent of mannose 6-phosphate recognition. This binding results in a 26-fold increase in uptake efficiency into muscle cells compared to conventional recombinant GAA, leading to significantly improved delivery of the enzyme to lysosomes and more effective clearance of accumulated glycogen in skeletal and cardiac muscle tissues. The GILT tag is designed to be proteolytically cleaved and degraded upon entry into the lysosome, ensuring that the mature, active form of GAA is indistinguishable from the native enzyme in structure and function, while the targeting moiety is eliminated to prevent any potential interference with lysosomal enzymatic activity or unintended signaling. The fusion protein is produced using recombinant expression systems, including stable Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cell lines, and exhibits biochemical properties such as specific activity, Michaelis-Menten kinetics, and intracellular half-life that are comparable to those of native GAA, confirming that the fusion does not impair the catalytic integrity of the enzyme. The invention further encompasses a method of treating Pompe disease by administering a therapeutically effective amount of the fusion protein to a subject in need thereof, wherein the fusion protein is administered intravenously and achieves superior glycogen reduction in muscle tissues at lower doses than conventional enzyme replacement therapies. The fusion protein is produced without reliance on M6P glycosylation, rendering its production more consistent, scalable, and less susceptible to batch-to-batch variability, thereby overcoming a major limitation of existing therapies. The invention further provides a pharmaceutical composition comprising the fusion protein and a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, wherein the composition is formulated for intravenous infusion and is stable under standard storage conditions. The fusion protein demonstrates a binding affinity for the IGF-II binding domain of the CI-MPR that is within six-fold of native IGF-II, as measured by surface plasmon resonance, and its uptake into muscle cells is competitively inhibited by soluble receptor fragments containing the IGF-II binding domain but not by mannose 6-phosphate, confirming the specificity and mechanism of action. The invention further includes a method of producing the fusion protein by transfecting a host cell with an expression vector encoding the fusion construct, culturing the cell under conditions permitting expression and secretion of the fusion protein, and purifying the protein from the culture supernatant using a series of chromatographic steps including phenyl-Sepharose hydrophobic interaction chromatography and anion exchange chromatography. The purified fusion protein is free of detectable IGF-II mitogenic activity, as confirmed by cell proliferation assays, and exhibits no evidence of immunogenicity in preclinical models. The invention further provides a method of diagnosing or monitoring the efficacy of treatment in a subject with Pompe disease by measuring glycogen levels in muscle tissue biopsies or serum biomarkers following administration of the fusion protein, wherein a reduction in glycogen content correlates with clinical improvement. The fusion protein is shown to be more effective than conventional GAA at equivalent or lower doses in reducing glycogen accumulation in multiple skeletal muscle types, including the diaphragm, quadriceps, gastrocnemius, soleus, tibialis anterior, extensor digitorum longus, and heart, and in some tissues, a dose of 5 mg/kg of the fusion protein achieves greater glycogen clearance than a 20 mg/kg dose of conventional GAA. The invention further provides a method of enhancing lysosomal delivery of enzymes to muscle cells by fusing a GILT tag to the N-terminus of a lysosomal enzyme, wherein the GILT tag comprises amino acids 1 and 8–67 of mature human IGF-II, a three-amino acid spacer, and is fused to the enzyme at a position that preserves enzymatic activity. The invention further encompasses a method of improving the pharmacokinetic profile of a lysosomal enzyme by replacing its native signal peptide with the IGF-II signal peptide, thereby altering its trafficking pathway and reducing M6P-dependent uptake while enhancing IGF-II receptor-mediated uptake. The fusion protein of the invention is suitable for long-term, repeated administration and demonstrates no evidence of immune sensitization or neutralizing antibody formation in preclinical models. The invention thus provides a transformative therapeutic approach for Pompe disease that overcomes the limitations of current enzyme replacement therapies by enabling efficient, glycosylation-independent, high-affinity delivery of GAA to the lysosomes of affected muscle cells, resulting in superior clinical outcomes with reduced dosing frequency and improved manufacturing consistency.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Lysosomal Enzymes

Lysosomal enzymes constitute a diverse class of hydrolytic proteins responsible for the degradation of macromolecules within the acidic environment of the lysosome, including proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and complex carbohydrates such as glycogen. These enzymes are synthesized in the endoplasmic reticulum as precursor polypeptides, undergo post-translational modifications in the Golgi apparatus, and are ultimately sorted to the lysosome through a highly regulated trafficking pathway. The majority of lysosomal enzymes are targeted to the lysosome via the addition of mannose 6-phosphate (M6P) residues to their N-linked glycans, a modification catalyzed by two enzymes: N-acetylglucosamine-1-phosphotransferase and N-acetylglucosamine-1-phosphodiester α-N-acetylglucosaminidase. The resulting M6P-tagged enzymes are recognized by either the cation-dependent or cation-independent mannose 6-phosphate receptors, which mediate their transport from the trans-Golgi network to endosomes, where the acidic pH triggers dissociation of the enzyme from the receptor. The receptor is then recycled back to the Golgi, while the enzyme is delivered to the lysosome, where it is activated by proteolytic cleavage and begins its degradative function. This M6P-dependent pathway is the primary mechanism for lysosomal targeting in mammalian cells and is essential for the proper function of over 60 known lysosomal enzymes. However, this system is inherently inefficient for therapeutic applications, as the addition of M6P is stochastic and subject to variability based on cellular metabolic state, culture conditions, and genetic background. Consequently, only a fraction of the recombinant enzyme produced in cell culture systems bears sufficient M6P residues to enable efficient receptor binding, and the remainder is either secreted extracellularly or taken up by non-target cells such as hepatocytes, which express high levels of the receptor but are not the primary site of pathology in many lysosomal storage diseases. Furthermore, the M6P modification is susceptible to enzymatic removal by phosphatases in serum or extracellular fluids, further diminishing the bioavailability of the enzyme. The invention circumvents these limitations by replacing the M6P-dependent targeting mechanism with a high-affinity, receptor-specific ligand that does not rely on glycosylation. The fusion protein disclosed herein retains full enzymatic activity while achieving targeted delivery through a fundamentally different mechanism, thereby overcoming the inherent inefficiencies of the natural M6P pathway. This approach not only enhances delivery to the intended target tissues but also enables consistent, reproducible manufacturing in a broader range of expression systems, including those incapable of M6P glycosylation, such as yeast or insect cells, thereby expanding the potential for scalable, cost-effective production. The fusion protein is designed to be processed identically to native GAA within the lysosome, ensuring that the mature enzyme is structurally and functionally equivalent, while the targeting moiety is rapidly degraded, eliminating any risk of interference with lysosomal substrate degradation or unintended signaling.

### Pompe Disease

Pompe disease is a progressive, autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorder caused by mutations in the GAA gene located on chromosome 17q25.3, leading to a deficiency or complete absence of acid α-glucosidase activity. The clinical spectrum of Pompe disease is broad, ranging from a rapidly progressive infantile-onset form characterized by severe cardiomyopathy, generalized muscle weakness, and respiratory failure, to a slower-progressing late-onset form marked by progressive limb-girdle and respiratory muscle weakness without cardiac involvement. The underlying pathophysiology involves the accumulation of glycogen within lysosomes of skeletal, cardiac, and smooth muscle cells, resulting in lysosomal distension, disruption of cellular architecture, impaired contractility, and eventual cell death. In the infantile form, glycogen accumulation begins in utero and leads to massive cardiac enlargement, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and early death, typically before the age of two. In late-onset forms, glycogen accumulation is more gradual and primarily affects skeletal muscle, leading to progressive weakness, difficulty climbing stairs, respiratory insufficiency, and ultimately respiratory failure. The disease is often misdiagnosed due to its heterogeneous presentation and overlap with other neuromuscular disorders, resulting in significant delays in treatment initiation. Prior to the development of enzyme replacement therapy, management was largely supportive, involving mechanical ventilation, nutritional support, and physical therapy, with no disease-modifying interventions available. The introduction of recombinant human GAA (alglucosidase alfa) marked a paradigm shift in the treatment of Pompe disease, demonstrating the feasibility of replacing a deficient lysosomal enzyme to ameliorate disease pathology. However, despite its life-saving potential, alglucosidase alfa has significant limitations, including suboptimal delivery to skeletal and cardiac muscle, high dosing requirements, variable efficacy between patients, and manufacturing inconsistencies due to glycosylation heterogeneity. Clinical studies have shown that even with high-dose, biweekly infusions, glycogen levels in key muscle tissues such as the diaphragm and quadriceps remain elevated, and many patients continue to experience progressive functional decline. The inability of conventional ERT to fully normalize glycogen storage in muscle tissues is attributed to the low expression of the CI-MPR on muscle cell surfaces, the low percentage of M6P-modified enzyme molecules in each batch, and the rapid clearance of unbound enzyme by the liver. Furthermore, the development of neutralizing antibodies against the recombinant enzyme in some patients further compromises therapeutic efficacy. The invention disclosed herein provides a solution to these persistent challenges by engineering a fusion protein that bypasses the M6P-dependent targeting system entirely, enabling direct, high-affinity binding to the CI-MPR via the IGF-II binding site, which is more abundantly accessible on muscle cells, and achieving superior lysosomal delivery and glycogen clearance at significantly lower doses. This approach represents a fundamental advancement in the treatment of Pompe disease, offering the potential for improved clinical outcomes, reduced treatment burden, and broader accessibility to patients worldwide.

### Enzyme Replacement Therapy

Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) is a well-established treatment modality for lysosomal storage disorders, wherein a recombinant form of the deficient enzyme is administered intravenously to restore lysosomal function and reduce substrate accumulation. The principle of ERT relies on the ability of the exogenous enzyme to be taken up by cells via receptor-mediated endocytosis and trafficked to the lysosome, where it can degrade stored substrates. For Pompe disease, ERT has been implemented using recombinant human acid α-glucosidase, which is produced in mammalian cell lines and purified to homogeneity. However, the success of ERT is contingent upon the enzyme’s ability to reach its target tissues in sufficient quantities and to be efficiently internalized by the affected cells. The current standard of care, alglucosidase alfa, depends on the presence of mannose 6-phosphate (M6P) residues on its glycan chains to bind the cation-independent mannose 6-phosphate receptor (CI-MPR), which facilitates uptake into lysosomes. This mechanism, while biologically elegant, is fraught with practical limitations. The glycosylation process is highly sensitive to cell culture conditions, including pH, temperature, nutrient availability, and oxygen tension, leading to batch-to-batch variability in M6P content and, consequently, in therapeutic efficacy. Regulatory agencies have previously required new biologics license applications due to changes in glycosylation patterns between manufacturing scales, underscoring the fragility of this approach. Moreover, the CI-MPR is expressed at low levels on the surface of skeletal and cardiac muscle cells, the primary sites of pathology in Pompe disease, and is predominantly localized intracellularly, cycling between the trans-Golgi network and endosomes. This limits the window of opportunity for the enzyme to bind and be internalized. Additionally, the enzyme’s affinity for the receptor is relatively weak, requiring high plasma concentrations to achieve meaningful uptake, which in turn leads to rapid clearance by the liver and spleen, where the receptor is more abundantly expressed. As a result, only a small fraction of the infused enzyme reaches the target muscle tissues, and much of the dose is wasted. The invention overcomes these limitations by replacing the M6P-dependent targeting mechanism with a high-affinity, glycosylation-independent ligand derived from insulin-like growth factor II (IGF-II). This ligand, termed the GILT tag, binds with high specificity and affinity to the IGF-II binding domain of the CI-MPR, which is structurally distinct from the M6P binding sites and is more accessible on muscle cell surfaces. Unlike M6P, the GILT tag does not require post-translational modification and is genetically encoded as part of the fusion protein, ensuring consistent expression and function across all molecules. The fusion protein is produced using stable cell lines, yielding a homogeneous product with predictable pharmacokinetics and biodistribution. The resulting enzyme is taken up 26-fold more efficiently than conventional GAA, leading to significantly greater delivery to lysosomes in skeletal and cardiac muscle tissues. This enhanced delivery translates into superior glycogen clearance at lower doses, reducing the frequency and volume of infusions required, lowering treatment costs, and improving patient compliance. Furthermore, because the GILT tag is degraded upon lysosomal entry, the mature enzyme is identical to native GAA, preserving its catalytic function and minimizing the risk of immunogenicity. The invention thus transforms ERT from a fragile, variable, and inefficient therapy into a robust, reproducible, and highly effective treatment modality for Pompe disease.

### Glycosylation Independent Lysosomal Targeting

The concept of glycosylation-independent lysosomal targeting (GILT) represents a paradigm shift in the design of enzyme replacement therapies for lysosomal storage disorders. Traditional approaches rely on the addition of mannose 6-phosphate (M6P) residues to glycoproteins to facilitate receptor-mediated uptake via the cation-independent mannose 6-phosphate receptor (CI-MPR). However, this strategy is inherently limited by the variability of glycosylation, the low efficiency of M6P addition, and the susceptibility of M6P to enzymatic dephosphorylation in circulation. The GILT strategy circumvents these constraints by replacing the M6P-dependent targeting mechanism with a genetically encoded peptide ligand that binds directly to a high-affinity site on the CI-MPR, independent of glycosylation. The GILT tag used in this invention is derived from a non-mitogenic portion of insulin-like growth factor II (IGF-II), specifically residues 1 and 8–67 of the mature protein, which retain the ability to bind the IGF-II binding domain of the CI-MPR (domains 10–13) without triggering downstream proliferative signaling. This domain has been previously shown to bind the receptor with high affinity, comparable to full-length IGF-II, but lacks the C-terminal region responsible for interaction with the IGF-I receptor and subsequent activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway. By fusing this fragment to the N-terminus of acid α-glucosidase (GAA) at residue 70, the invention creates a chimeric enzyme that is efficiently internalized by muscle cells through receptor-mediated endocytosis, trafficked to the lysosome, and processed into its mature, catalytically active form. The fusion does not interfere with the enzymatic function of GAA, as confirmed by kinetic assays, substrate specificity, and intracellular processing profiles. Moreover, the GILT tag is rapidly degraded within the lysosome, ensuring that the mature enzyme is structurally and functionally indistinguishable from native GAA. This design eliminates the need for complex glycosylation engineering, enables consistent production in any expression system, and allows for scalable manufacturing without the regulatory hurdles associated with glycosylation variability. The GILT tag is not merely a passive targeting moiety; it actively enhances the pharmacokinetic and biodistribution profile of the enzyme by increasing its affinity for the CI-MPR and reducing its clearance by non-target tissues. The invention demonstrates that the GILT-tagged enzyme achieves uptake saturation at levels twice as high as conventional GAA, suggesting a potential secondary mechanism involving receptor redistribution to the plasma membrane, possibly mediated by low-level signaling through the IGF-I receptor. This phenomenon, while not fully elucidated, contributes to the enhanced therapeutic efficacy of the fusion protein. The GILT strategy is broadly applicable to other lysosomal enzymes and represents a platform technology for the treatment of a wide range of lysosomal storage disorders, including Fabry disease, Gaucher disease, and mucopolysaccharidoses, where inefficient lysosomal delivery remains a major therapeutic barrier.

### Furin-Resistant GILT Tag

The GILT tag used in this invention is engineered to be resistant to proteolytic cleavage by furin and other proprotein convertases that are active in the trans-Golgi network and secretory pathway. Furin is a ubiquitously expressed serine endoprotease that recognizes and cleaves consensus motifs containing the sequence Arg-X-Lys/Arg-Arg, commonly found in precursor proteins such as IGF-II. Full-length IGF-II contains a furin cleavage site at its N-terminus, which, if present in the fusion protein, would result in premature cleavage of the GILT tag prior to secretion, thereby abolishing its targeting function. To prevent this, the GILT tag sequence was modified to eliminate any furin recognition sites while preserving the receptor-binding interface. The sequence encoding the GILT tag was derived from the mature form of IGF-II, excluding the signal peptide and the furin cleavage site, and was fused directly to the N-terminus of GAA at residue 70, which is located downstream of the native GAA signal peptide cleavage site. This design ensures that the fusion protein is secreted as an intact molecule, with the GILT tag fully exposed and functional. The absence of furin cleavage sites within the GILT tag was confirmed by mass spectrometry analysis of the purified protein, which showed no evidence of N-terminal truncation or processing. Furthermore, the fusion protein was expressed in multiple cell lines, including HEK293 and CHO cells, and in all cases, the intact fusion protein was recovered from the culture supernatant, demonstrating the stability of the construct under physiological conditions. The furin-resistant design is critical to the success of the invention, as any cleavage of the GILT tag prior to secretion would render the enzyme indistinguishable from conventional GAA in terms of uptake efficiency. The engineered tag is stable in circulation, allowing it to bind the CI-MPR with high affinity and mediate efficient internalization. Once internalized, the tag is cleaved and degraded within the lysosome by lysosomal proteases such as cathepsins, which recognize different cleavage motifs than furin, ensuring that the mature GAA enzyme is liberated without residual peptide fragments. This dual design—resistance to extracellular proteases and susceptibility to intracellular lysosomal degradation—ensures optimal targeting and functional release, maximizing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing potential side effects. The furin-resistant GILT tag thus represents a critical innovation that enables the reliable, reproducible production of a high-affinity lysosomal targeting fusion protein, overcoming a major technical obstacle that has hindered previous attempts to use IGF-II-derived ligands in therapeutic applications.

### Binding Affinity for the Insulin Receptor

The binding affinity of the GILT-tagged fusion protein for the insulin receptor was rigorously evaluated to ensure that the therapeutic agent does not trigger unintended mitogenic signaling, which could lead to tumorigenesis or other pathological consequences. Although the GILT tag is derived from IGF-II, which is known to bind both the IGF-I receptor and the insulin receptor with high affinity, the specific fragment used in this invention was selected and engineered to retain binding to the CI-MPR while minimizing interaction with the insulin receptor family. Surface plasmon resonance analysis was performed using purified insulin receptor extracellular domains immobilized on sensor chips, and the fusion protein was passed over the surface at multiple concentrations ranging from 0.1 nM to 100 nM. No measurable binding was detected at any concentration, with response units remaining at baseline levels throughout the injection and dissociation phases. In contrast, full-length IGF-II produced a robust, concentration-dependent binding signal with a dissociation constant (Kd) of approximately 0.5 nM, confirming the sensitivity of the assay. Additional binding assays were conducted using radiolabeled insulin and insulin receptor-expressing cell lines, where the fusion protein failed to compete with insulin for receptor binding, even at concentrations 1,000-fold higher than the physiological concentration of insulin. Cell proliferation assays were performed using human fibroblasts and myoblasts, which express high levels of the insulin receptor, and exposure to the fusion protein for up to 72 hours resulted in no increase in DNA synthesis, cell number, or phosphorylation of Akt and ERK, key downstream mediators of insulin and IGF-I signaling. In contrast, exposure to IGF-II under identical conditions resulted in a 3-fold increase in proliferation and strong phosphorylation of Akt. These findings confirm that the GILT tag, as engineered in this invention, is functionally inert with respect to the insulin receptor and does not activate mitogenic pathways. This is a critical safety feature, as previous attempts to use IGF-II-based targeting strategies were abandoned due to concerns over oncogenic potential. The invention thus provides a high-affinity lysosomal targeting ligand that is both potent and safe, enabling efficient delivery to muscle cells without the risk of promoting abnormal cell growth. The structural basis for this selectivity lies in the precise deletion of the C-terminal region of IGF-II that mediates interaction with the IGF-I receptor, while preserving the N-terminal helical domain responsible for CI-MPR binding. This refined molecular design ensures that the fusion protein engages only the intended receptor, maximizing therapeutic specificity and minimizing off-target effects.

### Mutagenesis

The design of the GILT tag and its fusion to GAA involved a series of targeted mutagenesis experiments to optimize binding affinity, stability, and expression yield while eliminating unwanted biological activities. Initial constructs attempted to fuse the entire mature IGF-II sequence to the N-terminus of GAA, but these resulted in poor secretion, aggregation, and low enzymatic activity, likely due to steric hindrance and improper folding. To address this, a series of N-terminal truncations were generated, systematically removing residues from the C-terminus of IGF-II while retaining the core receptor-binding domain. The most effective construct was found to include residues 1 and 8–67 of mature IGF-II, which retained high-affinity binding to the CI-MPR while eliminating the C-terminal region responsible for IGF-I receptor interaction. Further mutagenesis was performed to eliminate potential furin cleavage sites within the GILT tag sequence. The native IGF-II sequence contains a furin recognition motif at residues 15–18 (Arg-Lys-Arg-Arg), which was mutated to Ala-Ala-Ala-Ala to prevent premature cleavage in the secretory pathway. This mutation was confirmed by mass spectrometry and functional assays, which showed complete retention of the intact fusion protein in culture supernatants. Additional point mutations were introduced into the linker region between the GILT tag and GAA to optimize spacing and flexibility. A three-amino acid spacer (Gly-Gly-Gly) was inserted between the GILT tag and residue 70 of GAA to prevent steric interference between the targeting moiety and the catalytic domain. This spacer was chosen based on its known ability to act as a flexible hinge in fusion proteins, allowing independent folding and function of both domains. The spacer sequence was also designed to be proteolytically inert, ensuring that it would not be cleaved by endogenous proteases during secretion or circulation. Mutagenesis was also performed to introduce a diagnostic SpeI restriction site into the CI-MPR receptor construct used for binding assays, which allowed for rapid verification of the I1572T mutation known to reduce IGF-II binding affinity. This mutation was confirmed by sequencing and functional assays, and served as a critical negative control in competition experiments. All mutations were introduced using site-directed mutagenesis techniques, and the resulting constructs were verified by DNA sequencing and functional characterization. The final fusion protein, designated BMN 701, contains no extraneous residues, no cleavage sites, and no mitogenic sequences, representing a precisely engineered molecule optimized for therapeutic efficacy and safety.

### Spacer

The spacer sequence employed in the fusion protein is a critical structural element that ensures the proper folding, stability, and function of both the GILT tag and the GAA enzyme. The spacer is a short, flexible peptide linker composed of three amino acids—glycine, glycine, and glycine—inserted between the C-terminus of the GILT tag and the N-terminus of GAA at residue 70. This tri-glycine sequence was selected for its well-documented properties in protein engineering: it is non-reactive, non-immunogenic, and provides maximal conformational flexibility, allowing the GILT tag to adopt an orientation independent of the GAA domain. The absence of bulky or charged residues in the spacer minimizes the potential for steric clashes or electrostatic repulsion that could interfere with receptor binding or enzymatic activity. Structural modeling and molecular dynamics simulations predicted that a longer or more rigid linker would constrain the mobility of the GILT tag, potentially reducing its accessibility to the CI-MPR binding pocket, while a shorter linker might cause folding interference between the two domains. The three-amino acid spacer was empirically validated through a series of comparative constructs, wherein variants with longer linkers (e.g., Gly-Gly-Gly-Gly, Gly-Gly-Gly-Ser) or more rigid linkers (e.g., Pro-Pro-Pro) were tested for secretion efficiency, binding affinity, and enzymatic activity. The tri-glycine spacer consistently outperformed all alternatives, yielding the highest levels of secreted fusion protein, the greatest binding affinity for the CI-MPR, and the most consistent enzymatic activity. This spacer also proved to be resistant to proteolytic cleavage in serum and within lysosomes, ensuring that the fusion protein remains intact during circulation and that the GILT tag is only removed upon lysosomal delivery. The spacer’s role is not merely structural; it contributes to the overall pharmacokinetic profile by preventing aggregation and enhancing solubility, as evidenced by the high recovery yield during purification and the absence of visible precipitates in storage solutions. The use of a minimal, non-functional spacer ensures that the fusion protein is as close as possible to a native protein in structure and behavior, minimizing the risk of immunogenicity and maximizing biocompatibility. The spacer thus represents a subtle but essential innovation that enables the successful integration of two distinct functional domains into a single, therapeutically viable molecule.

### Cells

The production and functional characterization of the fusion protein were conducted using a variety of mammalian cell lines, each selected for its ability to support high-level expression, proper protein folding, and post-translational modifications consistent with human physiology. The initial proof-of-concept experiments were performed using transiently transfected human embryonic kidney (HEK293) cells, which are widely used for rapid protein expression and secretion due to their high transfection efficiency and robust secretory machinery. These cells were employed to produce the fusion protein for in vitro uptake assays, enzymatic characterization, and binding studies, as they provide a reliable source of homogeneous protein for preliminary evaluation. Subsequently, a stable Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cell line was developed to enable scalable, reproducible manufacturing of the fusion protein for preclinical and clinical applications. The CHO cell line, designated sCHO-S-GAA 701-R35, was generated using the GPEx retrovector expression system, which allows for stable integration of the fusion gene into the host genome under the control of a strong, constitutive promoter. This system was chosen for its proven track record in the production of therapeutic glycoproteins, including alglucosidase alfa, and its compatibility with large-scale bioreactor culture. The resulting cell line produced fusion protein at titers exceeding 250 mg/L in 10-liter Wave bioreactors, demonstrating the feasibility of industrial-scale manufacturing. The CHO-derived protein exhibited identical biochemical properties to the HEK293-derived protein, including molecular weight, enzymatic activity, binding affinity, and intracellular processing, confirming the consistency of the product across expression systems. Additional cell lines, including rat L6 myoblasts and mouse C2C12 myoblasts, were used for functional uptake and trafficking studies. These muscle cell lines were selected because they express the CI-MPR and are representative of the primary target tissues in Pompe disease. Uptake assays performed in these cells demonstrated that the fusion protein was internalized with 26-fold greater efficiency than conventional GAA, and immunofluorescence studies confirmed its co-localization with the lysosomal marker LAMP-1, validating its delivery to the correct intracellular compartment. The use of multiple cell lines ensured that the findings were reproducible across species and cell types, strengthening the translational relevance of the invention. The fusion protein was not tested in non-mammalian systems such as yeast or insect cells, as these systems do not naturally produce the complex glycosylation patterns required for M6P-dependent targeting, but the glycosylation-independent nature of the GILT tag renders them viable candidates for future production platforms. The choice of mammalian cells for manufacturing ensures that the fusion protein is properly folded, glycosylated (if at all), and secreted in a form that is biologically active and suitable for human administration.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The fusion protein comprising the GILT tag and human acid α-glucosidase (GAA) was produced by recombinant expression in transiently transfected HEK293 cells. A DNA cassette encoding the full-length human GAA sequence (amino acids 1–952) was modified to include an N-terminal fusion of the GILT tag, which consists of the IGF-II signal peptide, residues 1 and 8–67 of mature human IGF-II, a three-amino acid glycine spacer, and the GAA sequence beginning at residue 70. This cassette was cloned into the expression vector pCEP4 under the control of the cytomegalovirus immediate-early promoter and the Epstein-Barr virus nuclear antigen 1 (EBNA1) origin of replication, which enables episomal maintenance and high-level expression in HEK293 cells. The plasmid was transfected into suspension-adapted FreeStyle 293-F cells using 293Fectin transfection reagent at a ratio of 1:1:1 (cells:gDNA:reagent). Transfected cells were cultured in Opti-MEM I medium supplemented with glutamine and HEPES buffer at 37°C in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere on an orbital shaker. Culture supernatants were harvested between days 5 and 10 post-transfection, centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 20 minutes to remove cellular debris, and filtered through a 0.8/0.2 μm AcroPak 500 capsule. The clarified supernatant was concentrated using tangential flow filtration with a 30,000 MWCO PLTK membrane, and sodium acetate was added to a final concentration of 0.1 M to adjust the pH to 4.6. The concentrated supernatant was then subjected to a two-step chromatographic purification protocol. First, ammonium sulfate was added to a final concentration of 0.75 M, and the sample was loaded onto a phenyl-Sepharose 6 Fast Flow (low sub) column equilibrated with HIC load buffer (50 mM sodium acetate, pH 4.6, 0.75 M ammonium sulfate). The column was washed with 10 column volumes of HIC wash buffer (50 mM sodium acetate, pH 5.3, 0.75 M ammonium sulfate) to remove non-specifically bound contaminants, and the fusion protein was eluted with 5 column volumes of HIC elution buffer (50 mM sodium acetate, pH 5.3, 20 mM ammonium sulfate). Pooled fractions were dialyzed extensively against 20 mM histidine buffer, pH 6.5, containing 50 mM NaCl, and then loaded onto a Q Sepharose XL anion exchange column. The column was washed with 10 column volumes of QXL load buffer and eluted with 10 column volumes of QXL elution buffer (20 mM histidine, pH 6.5, 150 mM NaCl). Fractions containing the fusion protein were concentrated by ultrafiltration, buffer-exchanged into phosphate-buffered saline, pH 6.2, and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. The purified protein was analyzed by SDS-PAGE under reducing conditions and silver staining, revealing a single band at approximately 115 kDa, consistent with the expected molecular weight of the fusion protein. Western blot analysis confirmed the presence of GAA using monoclonal antibody 3A6-1F2 and the presence of the GILT tag using a polyclonal anti-IGF-II antibody. Enzymatic activity was measured using a para-nitrophenyl α-glucoside substrate, yielding a specific activity of 140,000 units per milligram of protein, which was comparable to that of recombinant human GAA produced under identical conditions. The Michaelis-Menten constant (Km) for the substrate was determined to be 5.9 mM, identical to that of native GAA, confirming that the fusion did not alter the catalytic properties of the enzyme. These results demonstrate the successful production of a biochemically authentic fusion protein with enhanced targeting potential.

### Example 2

To evaluate the cellular uptake efficiency of the fusion protein compared to conventional recombinant human GAA, rat L6 myoblasts were seeded into 24-well plates and allowed to reach 90% confluency. Cells were washed with uptake medium containing DMEM, high glucose, bovine serum albumin, L-glutamine, and HEPES, pH 7.2, and incubated for 18 hours with increasing concentrations of either the GILT-tagged fusion protein or recombinant human GAA, ranging from 0.1 nM to 100 nM. After incubation, cells were washed four times with DPBS to remove unbound protein, lysed with CelLytic M reagent, and lysates were centrifuged to remove debris. GAA enzymatic activity in the lysates was quantified using a 4-methylumbelliferyl α-D-glucoside substrate, and activity was normalized to total protein concentration as determined by bicinchoninic acid assay. Saturation curves were generated by plotting enzyme activity against the concentration of added protein, and the half-maximal uptake concentration (Kuptake) was calculated using nonlinear regression analysis. The Kuptake for the fusion protein was determined to be 5.4 nM, whereas the Kuptake for recombinant human GAA was 141 nM, indicating a 26-fold increase in uptake efficiency. To confirm the mechanism of uptake, competition assays were performed by co-incubating cells with either 5 mM mannose 6-phosphate or 2.4 mM IGF-II. Mannose 6-phosphate completely inhibited the uptake of recombinant human GAA but had no effect on the uptake of the fusion protein. In contrast, IGF-II completely blocked the uptake of the fusion protein but only partially inhibited the uptake of recombinant human GAA, consistent with previously reported steric interference effects. These results demonstrate that the fusion protein is internalized exclusively through the IGF-II binding site of the CI-MPR and does not rely on M6P recognition. The specificity of uptake was further confirmed using soluble receptor fragments. A recombinant construct containing domains 10–13 of the CI-MPR, which bind IGF-II but not M6P, was added to the uptake medium at a concentration of 100 nM. This construct potently inhibited the uptake of the fusion protein but had no effect on recombinant human GAA. A mutant version of the same construct, containing a single amino acid substitution (I1572T) known to reduce IGF-II binding affinity, failed to inhibit uptake of the fusion protein. These findings provide definitive evidence that the fusion protein engages the CI-MPR through the IGF-II binding domain and that its uptake is both specific and high-affinity.

### Example 3

To determine whether the fusion protein is properly processed within the lysosome, L6 myoblasts were incubated with either the fusion protein or recombinant human GAA for 18 hours, after which the cells were washed and returned to fresh medium without enzyme. At time points ranging from 0 to 14 days, cells were lysed, and protein samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and Western blot using monoclonal antibody 3A6-1F2, which recognizes an epitope within residues 204–482 of GAA. Both the fusion protein and recombinant human GAA were initially detected as a 110-kDa precursor form. Within 24 hours, both proteins were processed into intermediate 95-kDa and 75-kDa forms, and by day 2, the mature 76-kDa and 70-kDa forms predominated. The decay of these mature forms over time was nearly identical between the two proteins, with half-lives of 9.0 days for the fusion protein and 8.3 days for recombinant human GAA. Western blot analysis using a polyclonal anti-IGF-II antibody confirmed the presence of the GILT tag in the fusion protein immediately after uptake but revealed no detectable signal at any time point thereafter, indicating that the tag is rapidly degraded upon lysosomal delivery. This degradation is consistent with the expected behavior of a targeting moiety designed to be cleaved by lysosomal proteases, ensuring that the mature enzyme is structurally identical to native GAA. Immunofluorescence staining of C2C12 myoblasts treated with the fusion protein for 18 hours and then fixed and permeabilized revealed a punctate intracellular staining pattern that co-localized precisely with the lysosomal marker LAMP-1, as visualized using a FITC-conjugated anti-LAMP-1 antibody. No such staining was observed in untreated cells or in cells treated with recombinant human GAA under the same conditions. These results demonstrate that the fusion protein is not only internalized with high efficiency but is also correctly trafficked to the lysosome, where it is processed into its mature, active form without any residual targeting moiety.

### Example 4

To assess the therapeutic efficacy of the fusion protein in vivo, a murine model of Pompe disease was used. Twelve-week-old 129-GAA−/− mice, which lack endogenous acid α-glucosidase and accumulate glycogen in skeletal and cardiac muscle tissues, were treated with four weekly intravenous injections of either the fusion protein or recombinant human GAA at doses of 5 mg/kg or 20 mg/kg. Control animals received saline injections. One week after the final injection, mice were euthanized, and tissues including the diaphragm, heart, soleus, quadriceps, gastrocnemius, tibialis anterior, extensor digitorum longus, and tongue were harvested. Glycogen content was measured using the Amplex Red glucose assay kit, and results were normalized to tissue protein content. Statistical analysis was performed using analysis of variance with two-sided t-tests to compare treatment groups. In all tissues examined, the fusion protein at 5 mg/kg achieved significantly greater glycogen reduction than recombinant human GAA at 20 mg/kg, with p-values less than 0.05 in the diaphragm, heart, soleus, quadriceps, gastrocnemius, and tibialis anterior. In the extensor digitorum longus, the 5 mg/kg dose of the fusion protein was also significantly superior to the 20 mg/kg dose of recombinant human GAA. The 20 mg/kg dose of the fusion protein resulted in near-complete glycogen clearance in all tissues, with levels approaching those of wild-type mice. In contrast, the 20 mg/kg dose of recombinant human GAA achieved only partial clearance, with glycogen levels remaining elevated in all tissues. No significant difference in glycogen clearance was observed between the 5 mg/kg and 20 mg/kg doses of recombinant human GAA, indicating a plateau in efficacy at higher doses. In contrast, the fusion protein demonstrated a clear dose-response relationship, with the 20 mg/kg dose achieving significantly greater clearance than the 5 mg/kg dose. These results demonstrate that the fusion protein is not only more potent than conventional enzyme replacement therapy but also more efficacious at lower doses, suggesting a wider therapeutic window and reduced treatment burden. GAA enzyme activity in tissue homogenates was measured and found to correlate poorly with glycogen clearance, consistent with prior observations that enzyme accumulation in capillary endothelial cells may confound activity measurements. These findings confirm that the enhanced delivery of the fusion protein to muscle lysosomes translates directly into superior clinical outcomes.

### Example 5

To evaluate the manufacturability and stability of the fusion protein, a stable Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cell line was generated using the GPEx retrovector expression system. The coding sequence for the fusion protein was cloned into the retrovector under the control of a strong constitutive promoter and transfected into CHO cells. Stable clones were selected using antibiotic resistance and screened for high-level secretion of the fusion protein. The top-producing clone, designated sCHO-S-GAA 701-R35, was expanded in 5-liter cultures in PF-CHO LS medium supplemented with R15.4, and cultures were shifted from 37°C to 31°C on day 5 to enhance protein folding and secretion. Cultures were harvested on day 11, and the fusion protein was purified using the same two-step chromatography protocol as described in Example 1. The purified protein exhibited a specific activity of 145,000 units per milligram, a Km of 5.8 mM, and a molecular weight of 115 kDa on SDS-PAGE, identical to the HEK293-derived protein. Western blot analysis confirmed the presence of both GAA and GILT tag epitopes, and no degradation products were observed. The protein was stable in phosphate-buffered saline at 4°C for at least six months, with no loss of enzymatic activity or aggregation. The production titer in the CHO cell line averaged 250 mg/L, with a range of 230–270 mg/L across multiple batches, demonstrating exceptional consistency. The fusion protein was formulated into a sterile, endotoxin-free solution suitable for intravenous infusion and showed no signs of precipitation, opalescence, or viscosity changes upon storage. These results confirm that the fusion protein can be manufactured at scale using a well-established, regulatory-compliant cell line, yielding a product with consistent quality, high potency, and excellent stability, making it suitable for global clinical development and commercialization.
