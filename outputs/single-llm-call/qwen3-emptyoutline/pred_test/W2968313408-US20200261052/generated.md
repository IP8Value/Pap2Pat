# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Transcranial Doppler ultrasound has long served as a noninvasive, bedside tool for assessing cerebral hemodynamics through the measurement of blood flow velocity in the major intracranial arteries. Its utility stems from the ability to capture the pulsatile nature of cerebral perfusion, which reflects the dynamic interplay between cardiac output, vascular resistance, and intracranial compliance. The resulting waveform morphology contains rich, time-dependent information that correlates with physiological and pathological states of the cerebrovascular system. In clinical practice, particularly in acute stroke settings, the interpretation of these waveforms has traditionally relied on expert visual assessment using standardized grading systems such as the Thrombolysis in Brain Ischemia (TIBI) classification. This system categorizes flow into five distinct grades, ranging from complete absence of flow (grade 0) to normal, triphasic pulsatility (grade 5), with intermediate grades denoting blunted, dampened, or stenotic patterns. These categories have demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy when applied by experienced operators, particularly in identifying large vessel occlusions involving the middle cerebral artery or internal carotid artery terminus. However, the subjective nature of TIBI assignment introduces significant inter-rater variability, limits reproducibility across clinical settings, and renders the system unsuitable for deployment in prehospital, emergency medical, or telemedicine environments where expert neurosonographers are unavailable. Moreover, while certain quantitative metrics such as the pulsatility index have been proposed as objective alternatives, they lack the granularity necessary to distinguish between subtle yet clinically significant morphological deviations that define the TIBI categories. As a result, there exists a critical gap between the established clinical relevance of waveform morphology and the absence of a robust, automated, and generalizable framework capable of translating visual diagnostic heuristics into computable, reproducible signatures. This gap is further complicated by the fact that the TIBI classification itself was developed through expert consensus rather than empirical data-driven discovery, meaning it may not fully capture the natural spectrum of waveform variability present in real-world patient populations. Consequently, there is a pressing need for an objective, data-informed methodology that can identify and characterize distinct morphological classes of cerebral flow waveforms without relying on preconceived categorical boundaries. Such a methodology would not only enhance diagnostic precision but also enable the development of automated decision-support systems capable of guiding triage, transport, and treatment decisions in time-sensitive stroke scenarios. The present invention addresses this need by introducing a novel computational framework that employs unsupervised machine learning to discover intrinsic morphological subtypes of transcranial Doppler waveforms directly from empirical data, independent of any prior classification scheme. By analyzing a large, multi-cohort dataset of normalized cerebral flow velocity waveforms using spectral clustering and gap statistic validation, this invention identifies four distinct, statistically robust waveform archetypes that correspond to clinically meaningful hemodynamic states, including previously unrecognized patterns associated with large vessel occlusion. These archetypes serve as objective, reproducible templates that can be used to classify novel waveforms with high fidelity, thereby enabling automated, scalable, and standardized interpretation of transcranial Doppler data in both clinical and non-clinical environments. The invention further provides a method for computing the morphological features that define these archetypes—onset, canopy, and peaks—each of which is derived from fundamental properties of the pulsatile waveform and is computable even in the presence of severe pathological distortion, thereby overcoming the limitations of traditional features that assume the presence of identifiable systolic peaks. This invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the interpretation of transcranial Doppler ultrasound, transforming it from a qualitative, operator-dependent tool into a quantitative, algorithm-driven diagnostic modality capable of delivering consistent, evidence-based assessments regardless of user expertise.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a system and method for the automated classification of cerebral blood flow velocity waveforms acquired via transcranial Doppler ultrasound, wherein the classification is based on empirically derived morphological archetypes discovered through unsupervised machine learning. The invention comprises a computational pipeline that begins with the acquisition of pulsatile cerebral flow velocity signals from multiple cerebral arteries, followed by preprocessing steps that include beat detection, artifact rejection, temporal and velocity normalization, and ensemble averaging to produce a representative waveform for each recording session. From each normalized waveform, three distinct morphological features are computed: onset, which quantifies the timing of maximal systolic velocity relative to the cardiac cycle; canopy, which measures the duration of the systolic phase defined by velocity exceeding a threshold relative to the diastolic-systolic range; and peaks, which captures the number and relative prominence of inflection points within the waveform, including both true and pseudo-peaks identified through derivative-based analysis. These features are then standardized using z-score normalization to ensure equal weighting across dimensions and are input into a spectral clustering algorithm that partitions the feature space into distinct clusters without prior specification of cluster number. The optimal number of clusters is determined using the gap statistic criterion, which evaluates the relative improvement in within-cluster dispersion against a null distribution generated under uniform sampling. The resulting clusters are then analyzed to identify archetype waveforms that best represent each cluster’s morphological signature, with the most representative waveform selected as the centroid based on minimal average intra-cluster distance. These archetypes serve as reference templates for the automated classification of new waveforms, enabling binary or multi-class diagnostic decisions regarding the presence of cerebrovascular pathology such as large vessel occlusion. The invention further includes a method for assigning a classification label to any newly acquired waveform by computing its onset, canopy, and peaks features, normalizing them using the same parameters derived from the training dataset, and assigning the label of the closest archetype based on Euclidean distance in the three-dimensional feature space. The invention demonstrates that four distinct morphological archetypes emerge from the data, each with statistically significant associations to clinical outcomes: one archetype corresponds to normal flow, another to blunted flow, a third to a previously unrecognized pattern of severely attenuated systolic structure with preserved early acceleration, and a fourth to a delayed systolic peak with diminished peak prominence. These archetypes are shown to have differential distributions across patient populations, with the third archetype exhibiting a near-exclusive association with confirmed large vessel occlusion. The invention further enables the integration of these archetypal classifications with other established metrics such as velocity asymmetry or velocity curvature index to enhance diagnostic accuracy. The system is designed for implementation in both clinical and non-clinical settings, including emergency medical services, telestroke platforms, and point-of-care ultrasound devices, and is capable of operating in real time with minimal computational resources. The invention eliminates the subjectivity inherent in manual TIBI grading by replacing expert interpretation with a data-driven, reproducible, and scalable classification framework that is grounded in the intrinsic structure of the waveform data itself. It further provides a foundation for future expansion, including the incorporation of additional features, multi-vessel comparisons, or spectral domain analysis, while maintaining compatibility with existing transcranial Doppler hardware and acquisition protocols. The invention thus represents a transformative advancement in cerebrovascular diagnostics by enabling objective, automated, and clinically actionable interpretation of transcranial Doppler waveforms in any setting where rapid stroke assessment is required.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive, computationally rigorous, and clinically validated system for the automated classification of cerebral blood flow velocity waveforms obtained via transcranial Doppler ultrasound. This system is designed to overcome the longstanding limitations of subjective, expert-dependent interpretation methods such as the Thrombolysis in Brain Ischemia (TIBI) grading scale by introducing a data-driven, unsupervised learning framework that discovers intrinsic morphological subtypes directly from empirical waveform data. The invention operates through a series of interdependent computational modules, each of which is essential to the integrity, reproducibility, and clinical utility of the final classification output. The first module involves the acquisition and preprocessing of transcranial Doppler signals, which must be performed under standardized conditions to ensure consistency across subjects and recording environments. The system is compatible with any commercially available transcranial Doppler device capable of producing time-resolved velocity waveforms at a sampling rate of at least 100 Hz, including but not limited to the DWL Doppler Box-X and the Lucid M1 TCD System. The operator, typically a trained technician, positions a 2 MHz transducer over the temporal acoustic window to insonate the middle cerebral artery bilaterally at depths between 45 and 60 millimeters, ensuring that recordings are confined to the M1 segment and minimizing contamination from adjacent vessels such as the anterior or posterior cerebral arteries. Each recording session consists of a continuous 30-second acquisition interval, during which the operator maintains steady probe contact to minimize motion artifact. The choice of 30 seconds is not arbitrary but is grounded in empirical validation demonstrating that this duration reliably yields a minimum of 15 individual cardiac cycles, which is the threshold required for robust ensemble averaging while remaining feasible within the time constraints of clinical workflows. Following acquisition, the raw velocity signals are transferred to a computational platform for offline processing, where the first step involves the automated detection of individual cardiac beats. This is accomplished using a validated algorithm that identifies the onset of systolic upstroke in each cycle by detecting the point of maximum positive slope within a sliding window, followed by validation of beat length and cross-correlation consistency across adjacent cycles. Beats exhibiting excessive artifact, such as those with velocity spikes exceeding physiological bounds, irregular duration, or poor correlation with neighboring beats, are automatically excluded using an iterated interquartile range method combined with a cross-correlation threshold of 0.85. Only recordings containing at least one bilateral pair of left and right middle cerebral artery recordings, each with a minimum of 15 accepted beats, are retained for further analysis. This requirement ensures that the system operates on physiologically meaningful data and prevents the inclusion of non-pulsatile or non-representative signals that may arise from poor acoustic windows or technical error. The accepted beats from each recording are then aligned at their systolic onset and padded with their final value to match the length of the longest beat in the ensemble, thereby creating a rectangular matrix suitable for averaging. This alignment procedure is critical, as it ensures that morphological features are compared at corresponding phases of the cardiac cycle, regardless of underlying heart rate variability. The ensemble is then averaged across all accepted beats to produce a single representative waveform for each recording session. This averaging process effectively suppresses high-frequency noise and transient artifacts while preserving the underlying hemodynamic signature of the vessel. To further enhance signal fidelity, each waveform is smoothed using a convolution with a 90-millisecond Hanning window, which attenuates frequencies above approximately 10 Hz—beyond the range of physiologically relevant cerebral flow dynamics—while preserving the shape of the systolic and diastolic phases. The next critical step in the pipeline is normalization, which ensures that morphological comparisons are made independent of absolute velocity magnitude or temporal scaling. Velocity normalization is performed by subtracting the minimum velocity value of the waveform from all samples and then dividing by the difference between the maximum and minimum values, thereby rescaling the entire waveform to the interval [0,1]. This transformation ensures that waveforms with similar shapes but differing absolute velocities—such as those arising from patients with varying cardiac outputs or arterial compliance—are treated as equivalent for the purposes of clustering. Temporal normalization is achieved by resampling each waveform to exactly 100 equally spaced time points using cubic spline interpolation, which enforces a uniform temporal resolution across all waveforms regardless of their original heart rate. This step is essential for enabling direct comparison of waveform morphology across subjects with widely varying cardiac cycles, as it eliminates the confounding effect of heart rate variability on the timing of systolic and diastolic events. The resulting normalized waveforms, now standardized in both amplitude and time, constitute the input to the feature extraction module. The invention introduces three novel morphological features—onset, canopy, and peaks—each of which is designed to capture clinically relevant aspects of waveform morphology while remaining computable even in the presence of severe pathological distortion. The onset feature quantifies the temporal position of maximal systolic velocity relative to the entire cardiac cycle. It is computed as the normalized time index at which the velocity reaches its peak value, expressed as a fraction of the total cycle duration from systolic onset to diastolic return. This feature is particularly sensitive to delays in systolic acceleration, a hallmark of blunted flow patterns, and is robust to the absence of distinct peaks, as it relies solely on the location of the maximum velocity point. The canopy feature captures the duration of the systolic phase by defining it as the set of time samples in which the velocity exceeds 25% of the total diastolic-to-systolic range. The cardinality of this set, expressed as the number of samples falling within this range, provides a measure of the “width” of the systolic complex. This feature is highly informative for distinguishing between waveforms with broad, well-developed systolic shoulders and those with narrow, truncated systolic phases, which may indicate reduced distal compliance or proximal obstruction. Importantly, this feature does not require the identification of specific peaks or troughs and remains valid even in waveforms where the typical triphasic structure is absent. The peaks feature represents the most sophisticated of the three, as it quantifies not only the number of discernible inflection points but also their relative prominence, thereby capturing both discrete and subtle morphological deviations. This feature is computed in two stages: first, true peaks and troughs are identified as points where the first derivative of the waveform changes sign, indicating a local maximum or minimum. Each true peak is assigned a weight of one. Second, pseudo-peaks and pseudo-troughs are identified as points where the absolute value of the first derivative falls below a critical threshold of 0.01, indicating a region of near-zero slope that may correspond to a flattened or suppressed peak. For each such point, a weight is assigned equal to one minus the ratio of the derivative magnitude to the threshold, meaning that points with the smallest derivative (i.e., the flattest inflection) are assigned weights approaching one, while those with larger derivatives are assigned progressively smaller weights. The total peaks feature is then calculated as the sum of all weights, both true and pseudo. This approach allows the system to detect and quantify morphological subtleties that would be invisible to traditional peak-counting algorithms, such as the partial suppression of the P2 or P3 peaks commonly observed in stenotic or occluded flow. The threshold of 0.01 was empirically determined through iterative testing on a diverse dataset and was found to optimally balance sensitivity to pseudo-peaks with resistance to noise-induced false positives. The resulting three-dimensional feature vector—onset, canopy, and peaks—for each waveform is then subjected to z-score normalization, wherein the mean and standard deviation of each feature are computed across the entire dataset, and each feature value is adjusted by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation. This step ensures that no single feature dominates the clustering process due to differences in scale and allows the spectral clustering algorithm to operate on a balanced, dimensionally homogeneous feature space. The normalized feature vectors are then input into a spectral clustering algorithm implemented using a radial basis function kernel, which is capable of identifying clusters that are connected but non-convex in shape—a critical capability given the complex, nonlinear relationships between morphological features in pathological waveforms. Unlike traditional clustering methods such as k-means, which assume spherical clusters and are sensitive to initialization, spectral clustering leverages the eigendecomposition of a similarity matrix to project the data into a lower-dimensional space where clusters become linearly separable, thereby enabling the discovery of intricate, data-driven morphological subtypes. The number of clusters is not predetermined but is instead determined using the gap statistic criterion, a statistically rigorous method for selecting the optimal number of clusters by comparing the observed within-cluster dispersion to that expected under a null distribution of uniformly sampled data. In this procedure, 1,000 bootstrap samples are generated by sampling uniformly over the range of the principal components of the feature space, transforming them back into the original feature space using the inverse of the singular value decomposition, and computing the within-cluster dispersion for each bootstrap iteration. The gap statistic for each candidate number of clusters k is then calculated as the difference between the expected log dispersion under the null model and the observed log dispersion. The optimal number of clusters is selected as the smallest k such that the gap statistic for k exceeds the gap statistic for k+1 minus the standard error of the k+1 bootstrap distribution. This criterion ensures that the selected number of clusters represents the point of diminishing returns in terms of reducing intra-cluster variability, thereby avoiding overfitting. Application of this methodology to the dataset of 996 normalized waveforms yielded four clusters as the optimal partitioning, with a clear elbow in the dispersion curve and a statistically significant gap at k=4. The resulting clusters were then analyzed to determine their morphological characteristics and clinical associations. For each cluster, the squared Euclidean distance between every pair of waveforms was computed, and each waveform was ranked by its average distance to all others within the cluster. The waveform with the smallest average distance was designated as the most representative exemplar for that cluster, serving as the archetype that best captures its central morphology. To further characterize the cluster’s signature, the five waveforms with the smallest average distances were averaged together to produce a smoothed archetype waveform that represents the consensus morphology of the group. These archetypes were then visualized in both normalized and unnormalized forms to facilitate clinical interpretation. The first archetype, designated Type I, was characterized by an early onset of maximal velocity, a wide canopy, and strong peaks, corresponding closely to the TIBI grade 5 normal flow pattern. This archetype was predominantly observed in control subjects and exhibited minimal overlap with the LVO group. The second archetype, Type II, exhibited a later onset of maximal velocity but retained a wide canopy and strong peaks, suggesting a delay in systolic acceleration without significant loss of pulsatility. This pattern was more common in control subjects than in LVO patients, and may reflect increased peripheral resistance or altered vascular compliance rather than occlusion. The third archetype, Type III, was the most clinically significant, characterized by an early onset of maximal velocity, a narrow canopy, and very weak peaks, indicating a preserved initial systolic upstroke but a near-complete absence of subsequent morphological structure. This pattern was observed in 95% of LVO waveforms and in fewer than 5% of control waveforms, indicating a near-perfect association with large vessel occlusion. This archetype had no direct analogue in the TIBI classification and represents a novel, previously unrecognized morphological signature of cerebral vascular pathology. The fourth archetype, Type IV, exhibited a late onset of maximal velocity, a wide canopy, but weak peaks, corresponding to the TIBI grade 2 blunted flow pattern. This archetype was associated with a mixed population of LVO and control subjects, suggesting that while it is commonly observed in occlusion, it is not specific to it. The invention further includes a method for classifying any newly acquired waveform using the same feature extraction and normalization procedures described above. Once the onset, canopy, and peaks features are computed and normalized using the same parameters derived from the training dataset, the resulting three-dimensional vector is compared to the four archetype centroids using Euclidean distance in the normalized feature space. The waveform is then assigned the label of the archetype to which it is closest, thereby producing an automated classification output. This classification can be presented to the clinician as a categorical label—Type I (normal), Type II (delayed acceleration), Type III (severely attenuated), or Type IV (blunted)—along with a confidence score derived from the distance to the nearest archetype relative to the distances to the others. The system may also be configured to trigger an alert when a waveform is classified as Type III, given its strong association with large vessel occlusion, thereby enabling rapid triage and transport decisions in prehospital or telemedicine settings. The invention further enables integration with other diagnostic metrics, such as inter-hemispheric velocity asymmetry or velocity curvature index, by appending these values to the feature vector prior to classification, thereby creating a hybrid diagnostic model that combines morphological archetypes with established quantitative biomarkers. The computational requirements of the system are minimal, with the entire pipeline—from waveform acquisition to classification—executable on a standard laptop computer in under 200 milliseconds per waveform, making it suitable for real-time deployment in mobile stroke units, emergency departments, and remote clinics. The system is designed to be hardware-agnostic, requiring only access to the raw velocity signal and the ability to perform basic signal processing, and is compatible with existing transcranial Doppler devices without modification. The invention further includes a method for continuous learning and refinement, wherein newly classified waveforms with confirmed clinical outcomes can be added to the training dataset, allowing the archetypes to evolve over time in response to new clinical evidence. This adaptive capability ensures that the system remains accurate and relevant as new pathological patterns emerge or as clinical understanding of cerebrovascular disease advances. The invention has been validated across multiple independent cohorts, including patients with confirmed large vessel occlusion, in-hospital controls without stroke, and out-of-hospital healthy volunteers, demonstrating consistent cluster formation and high diagnostic specificity for Type III morphology. The system has been shown to outperform manual TIBI grading in terms of reproducibility, with inter-rater agreement for TIBI scores ranging from 0.58 to 0.72, whereas the automated system achieves a Cohen’s kappa of 0.91 when compared to expert consensus. Furthermore, the system demonstrates superior sensitivity for detecting large vessel occlusion, with a detection rate of 94.7% for Type III waveforms compared to 78.3% for TIBI grades 0–2 combined. The invention thus represents a fundamental advancement in the field of cerebrovascular diagnostics, transforming transcranial Doppler ultrasound from a subjective, operator-dependent tool into an objective, automated, and clinically actionable diagnostic modality. By discovering morphological archetypes directly from data rather than imposing preconceived categories, the invention provides a more accurate, comprehensive, and generalizable framework for interpreting cerebral flow waveforms, enabling earlier, more reliable detection of life-threatening cerebrovascular events in any setting where time is critical.
