# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of vaccine development, specifically to improved methods for the production of virus-like particles (VLPs) using recombinant expression systems, with particular emphasis on yeast-based platforms. More specifically, the invention concerns a novel bioprocessing strategy for enhancing the structural integrity, antigenic fidelity, and immunogenic potency of virus-like particles derived from human papillomavirus type 16 L1 protein through the controlled modulation of carbon source concentration during cell culture. The invention further encompasses the resulting virus-like particles, their purified formulations, and their use as immunogens in prophylactic and therapeutic vaccine compositions. The technical domain encompasses molecular biology, bioprocess engineering, immunology, and pharmaceutical formulation, with direct applicability to the manufacture of subunit vaccines against oncogenic pathogens, particularly those requiring conformationally accurate antigen presentation for elicitation of neutralizing antibody responses.

## BACKGROUND ART

Virus-like particles have emerged as one of the most promising platforms for the development of safe and highly immunogenic subunit vaccines. Unlike traditional inactivated or live-attenuated vaccines, VLPs mimic the native structure of viral capsids without containing any genetic material, thereby eliminating the risk of reversion to virulence or integration into host genomes. Their repetitive, high-density surface architecture closely resembles that of infectious virions, enabling efficient recognition and uptake by antigen-presenting cells, particularly dendritic cells, through receptor-mediated endocytosis. This structural mimicry triggers robust B-cell activation and T-cell-dependent immune responses, leading to the production of high-affinity, neutralizing antibodies that confer long-lasting protection. The success of licensed VLP-based vaccines against hepatitis B virus and human papillomavirus underscores the clinical viability of this approach.

Despite their proven efficacy, the production of high-quality VLPs remains a significant technical challenge. Many VLPs, particularly those derived from papillomaviruses, require precise folding, multimerization, and stabilization of capsid proteins to maintain critical conformational epitopes necessary for neutralization. In the yeast expression system, Saccharomyces cerevisiae has been widely adopted due to its well-characterized genetics, scalability, low cost, and capacity for post-translational modifications. The HPV L1 protein, when expressed in yeast, spontaneously assembles into VLPs under appropriate conditions, making it an ideal model system. However, VLPs produced under conventional fermentation conditions—typically involving carbon source concentrations of 2–4% glucose or mixed sugars—often exhibit structural heterogeneity, reduced stability, and diminished immunogenicity. These limitations are frequently attributed to suboptimal intracellular folding environments, premature protein aggregation, insufficient assembly kinetics, or the accumulation of misfolded or incompletely assembled intermediates.

Previous efforts to enhance VLP quality have focused primarily on post-expression interventions, including redox refolding, salt-induced maturation, detergent treatments, and chromatographic purification under denaturing or non-denaturing conditions. While some of these methods have yielded modest improvements in antigenicity, they are often labor-intensive, costly, and may introduce structural artifacts or residual contaminants that compromise vaccine safety. Moreover, such approaches address the consequences of poor assembly rather than preventing the underlying causes during biosynthesis. There has been limited investigation into how the physiological state of the host cell during expression—particularly nutrient availability, osmotic pressure, metabolic flux, and intracellular redox balance—directly influences the fidelity of VLP assembly. Conventional wisdom in yeast bioprocessing has long held that elevated carbon source concentrations above 4% induce osmotic stress, ethanol accumulation, and premature entry into stationary phase, thereby reducing overall protein yield and cell viability. As a result, industrial protocols have been optimized for rapid biomass accumulation and short fermentation cycles, typically lasting 48 to 72 hours, with little consideration for the extended time required for complex protein folding and multimeric assembly.

Recent studies have suggested that prolonged culture durations may improve the quality of HPV L1 VLPs, but these observations were not systematically linked to nutrient availability or metabolic adaptation. Furthermore, the relationship between carbon source concentration, intracellular redox state, soluble protein fraction, and VLP structural homogeneity has not been previously established. The prevailing assumption has been that higher yields of recombinant protein equate to better vaccine candidates, without rigorous evaluation of the functional quality of the assembled particles. Consequently, many VLP preparations, even those with high protein titers, fail to elicit potent neutralizing antibody responses in vivo due to the presence of non-native conformers, incomplete capsid structures, or exposed non-neutralizing epitopes that divert the immune response away from protective targets. There exists, therefore, a critical unmet need in the field for a production method that enhances the intrinsic quality of VLPs during biosynthesis, rather than relying on post-hoc corrections, thereby enabling the consistent manufacture of highly immunogenic, structurally defined vaccine antigens with minimal downstream processing.

## DISCLOSURE

### Technical Problem

The technical problem addressed by the present invention is the persistent inability of conventional yeast-based expression systems to consistently produce human papillomavirus type 16 L1 virus-like particles with optimal structural integrity, conformational epitope presentation, and immunogenic potency. Despite high yields of recombinant L1 protein under standard fermentation conditions (2–4% carbon source), the resulting VLPs frequently exhibit heterogeneous size distributions, reduced thermal stability, diminished binding affinity for neutralizing monoclonal antibodies, and suboptimal induction of protective immune responses in vivo. This deficiency arises not from limitations in protein expression per se, but from an inadequate intracellular environment that fails to support the slow, multi-step folding and assembly processes required for the formation of native-like capsids. Specifically, the problem lies in the mismatch between the temporal requirements of VLP assembly—which necessitates extended periods of protein synthesis, chaperone-assisted folding, and multimeric organization—and the short, high-glucose fermentation cycles traditionally employed, which prioritize rapid biomass growth over protein quality. Additionally, the metabolic byproducts generated under standard conditions, including ethanol and reactive oxygen species, contribute to oxidative stress that disrupts disulfide bond formation and promotes aberrant protein aggregation. The consequence is a population of VLPs that, while detectable and quantifiable, lack the precise three-dimensional architecture necessary to engage B-cell receptors with high specificity and affinity. This results in immune responses that are either weak, non-neutralizing, or directed against non-protective epitopes, thereby compromising vaccine efficacy and necessitating higher antigen doses or adjuvants to achieve protection. There is, therefore, a clear and urgent need for a method that resolves this fundamental disconnect between production efficiency and antigenic quality by modulating the culture environment to promote the in vivo formation of structurally authentic, immunologically superior VLPs.

### Technical Solution

The technical solution provided by the present invention resides in the novel discovery that the concentration of carbon source in the culture medium during the expression of HPV16 L1 protein in Saccharomyces cerevisiae directly and profoundly influences the structural fidelity and immunogenic potential of the resulting virus-like particles. Contrary to conventional bioprocessing paradigms, which limit carbon source concentrations to 2–4% to avoid osmotic stress and metabolic toxicity, the present invention demonstrates that culturing yeast cells at carbon source concentrations of 6% or 8%—comprising a 1:1 molar ratio of glucose and galactose—induces a physiological state that is uniquely conducive to the correct folding, stabilization, and multimeric assembly of HPV16 L1 into homogeneous, high-quality VLPs. This elevated carbon concentration extends the duration of the productive phase of fermentation beyond the typical 72-hour window, enabling a prolonged stationary phase during which intracellular protein synthesis continues without significant cell lysis or metabolic collapse. The sustained availability of energy substrates supports enhanced expression levels of molecular chaperones, redox-regulating enzymes, and protein-folding machinery, which collectively facilitate the formation of properly folded L1 monomers and their subsequent assembly into VLPs with native-like topology. Furthermore, the altered metabolic flux under these conditions shifts the intracellular redox balance toward a more reduced state, as evidenced by a significant increase in the reduced glutathione (GSH) to oxidized glutathione (GSSG) ratio, which promotes the formation of correct disulfide bonds critical for capsid stability. The resulting VLPs exhibit a narrower hydrodynamic size distribution, increased resistance to denaturation, and enhanced binding affinity for conformation-dependent neutralizing monoclonal antibodies targeting key epitopes in the FG and HI loops of the L1 protein. Importantly, this improvement in antigenic quality is achieved without the need for post-expression refolding, chemical maturation, or complex purification protocols, thereby simplifying manufacturing and reducing costs. The invention thus provides a process for producing HPV16 L1 VLPs wherein the carbon source concentration in the yeast culture medium is maintained at 6% or 8% throughout a fermentation period of at least 144 hours, resulting in VLPs with superior structural homogeneity and immunogenic potency compared to those produced under standard conditions.

### Advantageous Effects

The advantageous effects of the present invention are manifold and represent a transformative advancement in the field of VLP vaccine production. First and foremost, the VLPs produced under the claimed culture conditions exhibit a marked increase in structural homogeneity, as demonstrated by dynamic light scattering analyses showing a significant reduction in polydispersity index and a narrower size distribution centered around the native 55–60 nm diameter. This structural precision directly correlates with enhanced binding to neutralizing monoclonal antibodies H16.V5 and H16.E70, which recognize conformational epitopes essential for viral entry inhibition. The improved antigenic fidelity translates into a substantially greater capacity to elicit high-titer, high-avidity neutralizing antibodies in vivo, as evidenced by murine immunization studies showing up to ninefold higher neutralizing antibody titers compared to VLPs produced under conventional 2% carbon source conditions. Notably, this enhanced immunogenicity is achieved even at antigen doses as low as 10 ng per immunization, a magnitude lower than the doses typically required for commercially available VLP vaccines. This represents a profound reduction in antigen burden, which has significant implications for vaccine cost, scalability, and safety profile. Additionally, the VLPs produced under the claimed conditions demonstrate greater thermal and chemical stability, resisting aggregation and denaturation under conditions that cause structural collapse in conventionally produced particles. This intrinsic stability reduces the need for cryogenic storage, simplifies formulation, and extends shelf life, thereby improving logistical feasibility in resource-limited settings. The method also eliminates the necessity for post-expression refolding or maturation steps, which are often variable, expensive, and introduce batch-to-batch inconsistencies. By optimizing the in vivo assembly environment, the invention enables a more robust, reproducible, and scalable manufacturing process that yields a product with consistent quality attributes. Furthermore, the extended fermentation period under elevated carbon source conditions does not compromise cell viability or lead to excessive ethanol accumulation, contrary to prior assumptions, suggesting that yeast metabolism can be effectively tuned to support complex protein assembly without triggering stress-induced collapse. The resulting VLPs are also produced with higher purity, as evidenced by SDS-PAGE and Western blot analyses showing a greater proportion of L1 protein relative to host cell contaminants, thereby reducing downstream purification burden. Collectively, these advantages enable the production of a next-generation HPV vaccine that is not only more potent and stable but also more economical and manufacturable at scale, offering a superior alternative to existing VLP-based products.

## MODES OF THE INVENTION

The present invention encompasses a method for producing human papillomavirus type 16 L1 virus-like particles with enhanced structural and immunological properties through the controlled modulation of carbon source concentration during fermentation in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. The method comprises the steps of transforming a yeast host strain with a recombinant expression vector encoding a codon-optimized HPV16 L1 gene, cultivating the transformed yeast cells in a liquid culture medium containing a carbon source at a concentration of 6% or 8% by weight, maintaining the culture at a temperature of approximately 30°C with continuous agitation for a period of at least 144 hours, and subsequently harvesting and purifying the expressed L1 protein to isolate virus-like particles. The carbon source is preferably a mixture of glucose and galactose in a 1:1 molar ratio, with each sugar present at 3% or 4% respectively, to ensure balanced metabolic utilization and to prevent premature catabolite repression. The cultivation medium may further comprise yeast extract, peptone, and essential salts, but is free from inducers such as galactose alone or methanol, which could trigger uncontrolled expression or stress responses. The fermentation is conducted in a controlled bioreactor or shake flask system under aerobic conditions with agitation sufficient to maintain oxygen saturation and prevent sedimentation. The extended duration of culture—ranging from 144 to 168 hours—is critical to allow for the slow, sequential folding and assembly of L1 monomers into pentamers and subsequently into complete capsids, a process that is significantly delayed under standard fermentation conditions. The harvested cells are lysed using mechanical disruption methods such as bead beating, followed by ammonium sulfate precipitation to enrich for L1 protein, and subsequent purification by cation-exchange chromatography on phosphocellulose resin, with elution performed using a linear or stepwise gradient of sodium chloride. The purified VLPs are then concentrated by ultrafiltration and dialyzed into a pharmaceutically acceptable storage buffer comprising potassium and sodium phosphate salts, sodium chloride, and a non-ionic surfactant such as Tween 80 to maintain colloidal stability. The resulting VLP preparation is characterized by a hydrodynamic diameter of 50 to 65 nm, a polydispersity index below 0.2, and a binding affinity for neutralizing monoclonal antibodies H16.V5 and H16.E70 that exceeds 80% of the maximum binding observed for authentic virions. The invention further includes pharmaceutical compositions comprising these VLPs in combination with an immunologically acceptable adjuvant, such as aluminum hydroxide, for use in vaccination regimens. The VLPs may be formulated as lyophilized powders or liquid suspensions for parenteral administration, and may be used in multi-valent formulations to target multiple HPV serotypes. The invention further contemplates the use of the method for producing VLPs of other papillomavirus types, including HPV18, HPV31, HPV45, and HPV58, by applying analogous carbon source concentration parameters to their respective L1 expression systems. The method is scalable to industrial bioreactor volumes exceeding 1000 liters and is compatible with current Good Manufacturing Practices, enabling the consistent production of vaccine-grade VLPs with minimal batch variability.

## EXAMPLES

The following examples illustrate the practice of the invention and demonstrate the superior quality and immunogenicity of HPV16 L1 virus-like particles produced under the claimed culture conditions. These examples are provided for illustrative purposes and are not intended to limit the scope of the invention.

Example 1: Construction of Recombinant Yeast Expression System

A synthetic gene encoding the HPV16 L1 protein was codon-optimized for expression in Saccharomyces cerevisiae to reduce mRNA secondary structure and enhance translational efficiency. The optimized sequence was cloned into the YEGα-MCS expression vector under the control of the constitutive glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase promoter and the α-factor secretion signal. The resulting plasmid, designated YEGα-MCS-HPV16 L1 gene-opt, was transformed into the yeast strain Y2805 by lithium acetate-mediated transformation. Transformants were selected on synthetic dropout medium lacking uracil (SD-ura) and verified by colony PCR and sequencing. A single clone exhibiting stable expression and no plasmid loss over multiple generations was selected for all subsequent experiments.

Example 2: Fermentation Under Varying Carbon Source Concentrations

Four independent cultures of the transformed yeast strain were established in 150 mL of YPDG medium containing 1% yeast extract and 2% peptone, with carbon source concentrations of 2%, 4%, 6%, and 8% (w/v), each comprising a 1:1 molar ratio of glucose and galactose. Cultures were inoculated at an initial optical density of 0.1 at 600 nm and incubated at 30°C with shaking at 230 rpm for a total duration of 144 hours. Cell density was monitored daily by spectrophotometry, and viable cell counts were determined by trypan blue exclusion. At the end of the fermentation period, cells were harvested by centrifugation and processed for protein extraction. The specific yield of HPV16 L1 protein per milligram of total cellular protein was determined by Western blot analysis and densitometry, revealing a progressive increase in L1 expression with increasing carbon source concentration, with the 8% condition yielding 7.6-fold more L1 than the 2% condition.

Example 3: Purification and Characterization of HPV16 L1 VLPs

Harvested cells were disrupted using glass beads in a vortex mixer, and cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 10 minutes. The supernatant was subjected to ammonium sulfate precipitation at 40% saturation, and the resulting pellet was resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.15 M NaCl and 0.01% Tween 80. The solution was dialyzed against the same buffer for 4 hours at room temperature, followed by a 16-hour precipitation step at 4°C to remove contaminating proteins. The supernatant was then dialyzed against cation-exchange binding buffer and loaded onto a P-11 phosphocellulose column. Bound proteins were eluted using a stepwise gradient of sodium chloride (0.6 M to 1.0 M), and fractions containing HPV16 L1 were identified by SDS-PAGE and pooled. The purified protein was concentrated using an Amicon Ultra-4 centrifugal filter and dialyzed into storage buffer. Protein concentration was determined by Bradford assay, and purity was assessed by SDS-PAGE, revealing that preparations from 6% and 8% carbon source cultures contained over 95% L1 protein, whereas the 2% preparation contained approximately 80% L1, necessitating a 1.2-fold higher protein load in subsequent analyses.

Example 4: Structural Analysis by Transmission Electron Microscopy and Dynamic Light Scattering

Purified VLPs were adsorbed onto carbon-coated grids, negatively stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid, and imaged using a transmission electron microscope operating at 200 kV. Images revealed that VLPs from 6% and 8% cultures exhibited uniform, spherical morphology with diameters ranging from 52 to 58 nm, closely resembling the size and shape of native HPV virions. In contrast, VLPs from 2% and 4% cultures displayed irregular shapes, heterogeneous sizes (ranging from 30 to 90 nm), and frequent aggregation. Dynamic light scattering analysis confirmed these observations, showing a mean hydrodynamic diameter of 56.3 ± 2.1 nm for VLPs from 8% culture versus 72.5 ± 15.4 nm for those from 2% culture, with polydispersity indices of 0.15 and 0.41, respectively. These data demonstrate a significant improvement in structural homogeneity under elevated carbon source conditions.

Example 5: Antigenicity Assessment via Neutralizing Monoclonal Antibody Binding

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays were performed using purified VLPs coated onto microtiter plates and incubated with neutralizing monoclonal antibodies H16.V5 and H16.E70. Binding was detected using horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies and quantified by absorbance at 492 nm. VLPs derived from 6% and 8% carbon source cultures showed 2.8- and 3.1-fold higher binding to H16.V5, and 2.5- and 2.9-fold higher binding to H16.E70, respectively, compared to VLPs from 2% culture. The 4% condition showed intermediate binding, indicating a dose-dependent relationship between carbon source concentration and epitope exposure. These results confirm that the structural integrity of key neutralizing epitopes is preserved and enhanced under the claimed conditions.

Example 6: Immunogenicity Evaluation in Murine Model

Six-week-old female BALB/c mice were divided into five groups (n=8–10 per group) and immunized subcutaneously three times at two-week intervals with 10 ng or 1000 ng of purified VLPs formulated with 200 μg of aluminum hydroxide adjuvant. Control mice received PBS. Serum samples were collected ten days after the third immunization. Anti-HPV16 L1 IgG titers were measured by ELISA using VLP-coated plates and showed that VLPs from 6% and 8% cultures induced IgG titers that were 5.7- and 6.2-fold higher than those induced by 2% VLPs at the 10 ng dose. At the 1000 ng dose, the difference was less pronounced but still significant, with 8% VLPs inducing a 2.3-fold higher titer than 2% VLPs. Neutralization assays using HPV16 pseudoviruses demonstrated that sera from mice immunized with 6% and 8% VLPs neutralized 89% and 92% of pseudovirus infectivity, respectively, compared to 41% and 53% for 2% and 4% VLPs. The median neutralizing antibody titer for 8% VLPs was 9-fold higher than for 2% VLPs, confirming the superior protective capacity of VLPs produced under elevated carbon source conditions.

Example 7: Redox State Analysis of Intracellular Environment

Intracellular levels of reduced glutathione (GSH) and oxidized glutathione (GSSG) were quantified in yeast cells harvested at 144 hours using a colorimetric assay based on the reaction of thiol groups with 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid). The GSH/GSSG ratio in cells cultured at 8% carbon source was 4.7-fold higher than in cells cultured at 2% carbon source, indicating a significantly more reduced intracellular environment. This shift correlated with increased expression of glutathione reductase and thioredoxin pathways, suggesting that the elevated carbon source promotes a redox state favorable for disulfide bond formation and protein folding.

Example 8: Stability and Shelf-Life Assessment

Purified VLPs from 2% and 8% carbon source cultures were stored at 4°C, 25°C, and 37°C for 30 days, and their structural integrity was assessed by dynamic light scattering and ELISA binding to H16.V5. VLPs from 8% culture retained 94% of their original size distribution and 91% of their antibody binding capacity at 37°C after 30 days, whereas VLPs from 2% culture showed a 42% increase in polydispersity and a 68% loss in binding under the same conditions. These results demonstrate that VLPs produced under the claimed conditions possess superior thermodynamic stability, rendering them more suitable for storage and distribution in regions lacking cold-chain infrastructure.

Example 9: Scalability and Reproducibility

The fermentation process was scaled to a 5-liter bioreactor under controlled pH (5.5), dissolved oxygen (30%), and temperature (30°C) conditions, with carbon source maintained at 8% throughout a 168-hour fermentation. VLPs were purified using the same protocol and analyzed by TEM, DLS, and ELISA. The resulting VLPs exhibited identical structural and antigenic properties to those produced in shake flasks, confirming the robustness and scalability of the method. Three independent production runs yielded VLPs with coefficient of variation below 5% for size, binding affinity, and immunogenicity in mice, demonstrating exceptional batch-to-batch consistency.

## INDUSTRIAL APPLICABILITY

The invention possesses significant industrial applicability across multiple sectors of the biopharmaceutical industry, particularly in the manufacturing of prophylactic vaccines against oncogenic human papillomaviruses. The method described herein enables the consistent, scalable, and cost-effective production of high-quality HPV16 L1 virus-like particles with superior structural homogeneity, enhanced immunogenic potency, and improved stability compared to existing commercial products. By eliminating the need for complex post-expression refolding or maturation steps, the invention streamlines the manufacturing process, reduces production time, minimizes reagent consumption, and lowers the risk of contamination or batch failure. The ability to achieve potent immune responses at antigen doses as low as 10 ng per immunization translates directly into reduced material costs, increased vaccine supply per production run, and the potential for dose-sparing strategies in global immunization programs. The method is fully compatible with existing yeast fermentation infrastructure, requiring only a modification of carbon source concentration and culture duration, both of which are easily implemented in standard bioreactor control systems. The resulting VLPs are suitable for formulation into multi-valent vaccines targeting multiple HPV serotypes, including HPV18, HPV31, HPV45, and HPV58, by applying the same culture parameters to their respective L1 expression systems. The enhanced thermal stability of the VLPs permits storage at refrigerated or even ambient temperatures, reducing dependency on cold-chain logistics and expanding access in low-resource settings. The invention further enables the development of next-generation HPV vaccines with improved safety profiles, reduced side effects, and longer shelf lives, thereby increasing patient compliance and vaccination coverage. Regulatory agencies, including the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency, have increasingly emphasized the importance of structural characterization and functional immunogenicity in vaccine approval, and the methods and products described herein meet these stringent criteria. The invention is therefore directly applicable to commercial vaccine manufacturing by leading biotechnology and pharmaceutical companies, and represents a transformative advancement in the field of recombinant subunit vaccine production.
