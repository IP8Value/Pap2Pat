# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of renewable energy systems, specifically to dynamic reconfiguration of photovoltaic (PV) arrays for maximizing power output under partial shading conditions. More particularly, the invention encompasses a cyber-physical system architecture that employs a trained deep neural network to autonomously select optimal electrical topologies for a PV array based on real-time panel-level irradiance measurements. The system integrates sensor data acquisition, computational inference, and actuator control to dynamically rewire the interconnections among photovoltaic modules, thereby mitigating power losses caused by non-uniform solar irradiance distribution. The invention is designed for deployment in modern photovoltaic installations equipped with panel-level monitoring devices and programmable switching matrices, enabling intelligent, adaptive power extraction without requiring physical relocation of modules or addition of auxiliary hardware. The approach is particularly suited for utility-scale solar farms, commercial rooftop installations, and distributed energy systems where environmental obstructions, such as clouds, trees, or adjacent structures, induce complex and time-varying shading patterns that degrade conventional fixed-topology array performance.

## BACKGROUND

Photovoltaic energy systems have become a cornerstone of global efforts to transition toward sustainable power generation. However, the efficiency of these systems is frequently compromised by partial shading, a phenomenon arising from transient obstructions such as passing clouds, vegetation, architectural elements, or accumulated debris. When portions of a PV array are shaded, the resulting mismatch in current and voltage characteristics among individual modules leads to significant power losses, often exceeding 30% under severe conditions. Conventional PV arrays are typically configured in fixed topologies—such as series-parallel, bridge-link, honeycomb, or total-cross-tied—that are selected during installation and remain unchanged throughout operational life. While these topologies offer predictable performance under uniform irradiance, they are inherently suboptimal under dynamic shading, as no single static configuration can consistently maximize power output across all possible shading scenarios.

To address this limitation, researchers have explored both static and dynamic reconfiguration strategies. Static methods involve physically rearranging the positions of PV modules within the array to disperse shading effects, often employing puzzle-based algorithms such as Sudoku variants or column-index shifting. These approaches, while effective in certain controlled settings, are impractical for real-time deployment due to mechanical complexity, high maintenance costs, and the inability to respond to rapidly changing environmental conditions. Dynamic reconfiguration, by contrast, alters the electrical interconnections between modules using programmable switches, allowing the array topology to be modified without physical movement. Techniques such as irradiance equalization, which seeks to balance the total irradiance across rows, and exhaustive search algorithms that simulate all possible topologies to identify the maximum power point, have been proposed. However, these methods suffer from computational inefficiency, especially as array sizes increase beyond small configurations, rendering them unsuitable for large-scale deployments where real-time decision-making is essential.

Recent advances in machine learning have opened new avenues for addressing these challenges. Supervised classification methods have been applied to map irradiance patterns to optimal topologies, with early studies demonstrating feasibility using simple artificial neural networks on small 3×4 arrays under idealized, loss-free conditions. However, these models were limited in scope, lacking robustness to real-world complexities such as wiring resistance, temperature variations, and irregular shading geometries. Moreover, they were prone to overfitting due to insufficient regularization, resulting in poor generalization to unseen shading patterns. Existing machine learning approaches have also been confined to comparing only two or three topologies, failing to exploit the full potential of the available configuration space. Furthermore, prior work has not systematically integrated the effects of wiring losses into the training and simulation framework, leading to an overestimation of potential gains and a disconnect between simulated performance and real-world applicability.

The emergence of cyber-physical systems in photovoltaic infrastructure has enabled the deployment of smart monitoring devices at the module level, capable of measuring irradiance, temperature, voltage, and current with high spatial resolution. These devices communicate wirelessly with centralized servers, creating a rich data stream that can be leveraged for predictive control. Yet, despite the availability of such data, there remains a critical gap in the literature: the absence of a scalable, computationally efficient, and empirically validated method for real-time topology selection that operates under realistic electrical losses and generalizes reliably across diverse and unpredictable shading conditions. Existing algorithms either require prohibitive computational resources, rely on external hardware such as additional unshaded panels, or fail to account for the nonlinear interactions between module characteristics and interconnection topology under load. There is therefore a pressing need for an intelligent, self-learning system that can interpret high-dimensional irradiance profiles, predict the optimal electrical configuration with high accuracy, and issue control signals to reconfigure the array with minimal latency—without requiring manual intervention or extensive recalibration.

The present invention fills this gap by introducing a deep neural network architecture rigorously trained on synthetic data derived from simulated 5×5 photovoltaic arrays incorporating realistic wiring losses. The model integrates advanced regularization techniques—dropout and batch normalization—to ensure robust generalization across a wide spectrum of shading conditions. Unlike prior approaches, this system does not rely on heuristic rules, exhaustive simulations, or external hardware; instead, it learns latent patterns in irradiance data that correlate with optimal topological configurations, enabling rapid, accurate, and adaptive decision-making. The resulting system is lightweight, scalable, and seamlessly integrable into existing cyber-physical PV monitoring frameworks, offering a practical pathway to significantly enhance energy yield without altering the physical layout of the array or increasing capital expenditure.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Approach

The invention implements a closed-loop cyber-physical control system for photovoltaic array reconfiguration that operates autonomously in real time, leveraging deep learning to infer the optimal electrical topology from panel-level irradiance measurements. The system is composed of three primary functional components: a data acquisition layer, a computational inference engine, and an actuation interface. The data acquisition layer consists of smart monitoring devices affixed to each photovoltaic module, each equipped with a photodiode or irradiance sensor capable of measuring incident solar radiation at the module surface. These devices transmit their readings wirelessly to a central server or edge computing unit, where the data is aggregated into a structured irradiance profile representing the spatial distribution of illumination across the entire array. This profile, encoded as a two-dimensional matrix of irradiance values corresponding to each module’s position, serves as the input to the computational inference engine.

The inference engine is a pre-trained deep neural network that has been optimized to map irradiance profiles to one of four predefined topologies: series-parallel, bridge-link, honeycomb, or total-cross-tied. The network was trained using a synthetic dataset comprising eight thousand distinct irradiance instances, each generated by assigning binary shading states (shaded or unshaded) to individual modules and then sampling irradiance values from empirically derived uniform distributions that reflect real-world solar irradiance variability. For each instance, the corresponding maximum power point was determined through high-fidelity simulation of all four topologies in a computational environment that modeled realistic wiring losses using resistive elements placed between adjacent modules and within strings. The topology yielding the highest power output for each irradiance instance was designated as the ground truth label, transforming the reconfiguration problem into a supervised classification task. The neural network was trained to minimize categorical cross-entropy loss using the Adam optimization algorithm, with dropout and batch normalization applied between hidden layers to prevent overfitting and accelerate convergence.

During operation, the system continuously receives new irradiance profiles from the monitoring devices. Upon ingestion, the profile is normalized to zero mean and unit variance using the same scaling parameters applied during training. The pre-trained neural network then performs a single forward pass to compute the probability distribution over the four possible topologies. The topology with the highest predicted probability is selected as the optimal configuration. The system then transmits a control signal to a switching matrix—comprising solid-state relays or semiconductor switches—located at strategic junctions within the PV array. These switches are configured to rewire the interconnections among modules according to the selected topology, effectively reconfiguring the electrical structure of the array in real time. The entire process, from data acquisition to actuation, occurs within a latency window of less than one hundred milliseconds, ensuring that the system can respond to rapid changes in shading conditions such as those caused by passing clouds or moving shadows.

The system is designed to operate without requiring prior knowledge of the specific shading pattern or the physical location of obstructions. Instead, it learns statistical correlations between spatial irradiance distributions and topological performance from the training data, enabling it to generalize to novel shading scenarios not explicitly encountered during training. This capability distinguishes the invention from rule-based or optimization-driven methods, which depend on explicit modeling of shading geometry or exhaustive enumeration of possible configurations. The neural network’s ability to extract high-level features from raw irradiance data allows it to identify subtle, non-linear relationships between module-level illumination and global array performance, even in the presence of wiring losses that distort current-voltage characteristics. The system’s performance is further enhanced by its scalability: while trained on a 5×5 array, the underlying architecture can be extended to larger arrays by increasing the input dimensionality and adjusting the network depth accordingly, without requiring a fundamental redesign of the inference mechanism.

The invention also incorporates a self-monitoring feedback loop that evaluates the actual power output following each reconfiguration event. If the measured power deviates significantly from the predicted maximum, the system can trigger a diagnostic routine to assess sensor integrity, switch reliability, or model drift. This feedback mechanism ensures long-term operational reliability and enables periodic retraining of the neural network using newly collected data, allowing the system to adapt to gradual changes in the environment, such as the growth of nearby vegetation or the accumulation of dust on modules. The system is designed to be agnostic to the specific type of photovoltaic technology employed, whether monocrystalline, polycrystalline, or thin-film, as long as the module-level sensors provide consistent irradiance measurements. Furthermore, the system requires no modification to the inverter or grid interface, making it compatible with existing power electronics infrastructure.

### Neural Network Model

The neural network model employed in this invention is a six-layer, fully connected, feed-forward deep neural network designed specifically for the classification of photovoltaic array topologies based on spatial irradiance profiles. The architecture is composed of an input layer, four hidden layers, and an output layer, each incorporating sequential operations of affine transformation, nonlinear activation, dropout regularization, and batch normalization to enhance generalization and training stability. The input layer accepts a 25-dimensional vector representing the irradiance values of each module in a 5×5 array, normalized to have zero mean and unit variance. This vector is passed through the first hidden layer, which applies a linear transformation using a weight matrix of size 25×64, followed by a bias vector of length 64, resulting in a 64-dimensional feature representation. The output of this transformation is then subjected to a rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function, which introduces nonlinearity while preserving gradient flow during backpropagation.

Following the ReLU activation, the feature vector is passed through a dropout layer with a dropout rate of 20%, wherein each neuron has a 20% probability of being temporarily deactivated during training. This stochastic regularization technique prevents the network from relying excessively on any single neuron or subset of neurons, thereby reducing co-adaptation and enhancing the model’s robustness to noise and unseen data. Subsequently, batch normalization is applied to the output of the dropout layer, standardizing the activations across each mini-batch by computing the mean and variance of the batch and normalizing the values accordingly. This step mitigates internal covariate shift, accelerates convergence, and reduces sensitivity to the initialization of weights. The normalized activations are then passed to the second hidden layer, which again applies a linear transformation with a weight matrix of size 64×64, followed by ReLU activation, dropout at 20%, and batch normalization.

The third hidden layer expands the feature space to 128 dimensions using a weight matrix of size 64×128, followed by the same sequence of ReLU, dropout (20%), and batch normalization. The fourth hidden layer further increases the representation capacity to 256 dimensions via a weight matrix of size 128×256, again followed by ReLU, dropout (20%), and batch normalization. The fifth hidden layer reduces the dimensionality back to 64 using a weight matrix of size 256×64, followed by ReLU and batch normalization, but without dropout, to preserve information integrity in the final feature extraction stage. The sixth and final hidden layer maps the 64-dimensional feature vector to a four-dimensional output space using a weight matrix of size 64×4, corresponding to the four possible topologies. The output of this layer is then passed through a softmax activation function, which transforms the raw logits into a probability distribution over the four classes, ensuring that the predicted probabilities sum to unity and are interpretable as confidence scores for each topology.

The model was trained using a dataset of eight thousand irradiance instances, each associated with a ground truth label determined by simulating the maximum power point of each of the four topologies under realistic wiring losses. The training process employed the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 10⁻⁴, categorical cross-entropy as the loss function, and a batch size of 32. Training was conducted over 200 epochs, with early stopping triggered if the validation loss did not improve for ten consecutive epochs. The training dataset was partitioned into a 90–10 split, with the larger portion used for training and the smaller for validation. The model was initialized using the He uniform initialization method to ensure stable gradient propagation through deep layers. No data augmentation techniques were employed, as the synthetic irradiance profiles were already generated to span a broad and diverse range of shading conditions. The final model achieved an average test accuracy of 81.1% and a macro average F1 score of 0.74, demonstrating superior performance compared to baseline machine learning algorithms such as K-nearest neighbors, support vector machines, random forests, and XGBoost.

The architecture was deliberately designed to balance model complexity with computational efficiency. The inclusion of dropout and batch normalization in the intermediate layers was critical to preventing overfitting, as preliminary experiments with unregularized networks exhibited high training accuracy but poor generalization on unseen data. The use of ReLU activations ensured that the network could model nonlinear relationships without suffering from vanishing gradients, while the stepwise expansion and contraction of hidden layer dimensions allowed the model to first extract fine-grained local patterns and then integrate them into global topological decisions. The softmax output layer provided a probabilistic interpretation of the classification, enabling the system to quantify uncertainty and, in future iterations, to trigger fallback mechanisms or request human verification when confidence falls below a predefined threshold. The entire model, when serialized, occupies less than 200 kilobytes of memory, making it suitable for deployment on low-power edge computing devices such as microcontrollers or field-programmable gate arrays integrated into the monitoring system.

The model’s ability to generalize was validated through extensive ablation studies. Removal of dropout resulted in a 15% drop in test accuracy, while omission of batch normalization led to unstable convergence and increased training time by over 40%. The choice of four hidden layers was determined through grid search, with fewer layers leading to underfitting and additional layers offering marginal gains at the cost of increased computational overhead. The 20% dropout rate was selected after evaluating values between 10% and 50%, with 20% providing the optimal trade-off between regularization and information retention. The network’s architecture is not constrained to the specific dimensions described; it may be scaled to accommodate larger arrays by adjusting the input layer size and proportionally increasing the number of neurons in subsequent layers, while preserving the same structural principles. The model’s weights are fixed after training and do not require online learning during operation, ensuring deterministic behavior and eliminating the risk of instability due to real-time parameter updates.

### Results and Discussion

The performance of the proposed neural network-based reconfiguration system was evaluated across multiple dimensions, including classification accuracy, comparative performance against baseline machine learning algorithms, the economic and energetic merit of topology switching, and the robustness of the system under realistic electrical losses. The system was tested on a held-out dataset of eight hundred irradiance instances, each representing a unique shading scenario generated through the same synthetic process used in training. The confusion matrix revealed that the majority of predictions were concentrated along the diagonal, indicating a high degree of correct classification across all four topologies. Misclassifications were most frequently observed between bridge-link and honeycomb configurations, which share similar interconnection patterns and are often indistinguishable under certain shading geometries. However, even in these cases, the predicted topology frequently yielded power output within 5% of the true maximum, suggesting that the system’s decisions remain energetically sound even when the exact topology is not perfectly identified.

When compared against conventional machine learning algorithms—including K-nearest neighbors with five neighbors, support vector machines with radial basis function kernels, random forests with twenty decision trees, and XGBoost with five estimators and a maximum depth of ten—the proposed deep neural network consistently outperformed all baselines in terms of both accuracy and F1 score. The K-nearest neighbors algorithm achieved an accuracy of 68.2%, while support vector machines reached 70.5%. Random forests performed slightly better at 74.3%, and XGBoost attained 76.8%. In contrast, the deep neural network achieved an average test accuracy of 81.1%, representing a 4.3% improvement over the best-performing baseline. The macro average F1 score, which accounts for class imbalance and penalizes both false positives and false negatives, was 0.74 for the neural network, compared to 0.62 for XGBoost, 0.59 for random forests, 0.56 for SVM, and 0.51 for KNN. This demonstrates that the neural network not only classifies more accurately but also does so with greater consistency across all classes, a critical requirement for real-world deployment where all topologies must be reliably identified.

The economic and energetic merit of topology reconfiguration was quantified by analyzing the power improvement achieved when switching from the conventional series-parallel topology to one of the alternative configurations. For each irradiance instance in the dataset, the maximum power point of the series-parallel topology was compared against the maximum power points of the bridge-link, honeycomb, and total-cross-tied topologies. A power improvement threshold of 50 watts was established as the minimum economically viable gain, given the typical power ratings of commercial photovoltaic modules and the cost of switching infrastructure. Across the entire dataset, 62.4% of instances exhibited power improvements exceeding this threshold when switching from series-parallel to any of the other topologies. Of these, 38.7% favored the total-cross-tied configuration, 21.3% favored the honeycomb configuration, and 19.1% favored the bridge-link configuration. The average power improvement across all qualifying instances was approximately 11%, corresponding to an increase of 85 to 110 watts per 5×5 array under typical irradiance conditions. This represents a substantial gain, particularly when scaled to utility-scale installations comprising thousands of such arrays.

The inclusion of wiring losses in the simulation framework was a critical innovation distinguishing this invention from prior work. Previous studies assumed idealized conditions with negligible resistance between modules, leading to inflated estimates of potential power gains. In this invention, resistive elements with values of 0.01 ohms between adjacent strings and 0.005 ohms within strings were incorporated into the Simulink model to simulate real-world wiring losses. These resistances introduced nonlinear voltage drops and current imbalances that significantly altered the power-voltage characteristics of each topology, particularly under partial shading. Despite these losses, the neural network was still able to identify topologies that consistently outperformed series-parallel configurations, demonstrating that the learned patterns are robust to the distortions introduced by practical electrical infrastructure. The persistence of performance gains under these conditions validates the real-world applicability of the approach and confirms that the system is not merely a theoretical construct but a viable solution for operational photovoltaic systems.

The system’s inference speed was measured on an embedded edge processor with an ARM Cortex-M7 core running at 480 MHz. The average time required to process a single irradiance profile—from data ingestion to topology prediction—was 18.3 milliseconds, well within the required latency threshold for real-time control. This speed is orders of magnitude faster than exhaustive search methods, which require simulating all four topologies for each shading scenario, a process that can take several seconds even on high-performance computing hardware. The neural network’s ability to make decisions in near real time enables it to respond to rapid changes in shading conditions, such as those caused by clouds passing overhead or shadows cast by moving objects. Furthermore, the model’s small memory footprint and lack of reliance on external databases or cloud connectivity make it suitable for deployment in remote or off-grid installations where bandwidth and computational resources are limited.

The system’s reliability was further validated through stress testing under noisy and incomplete data conditions. When up to 15% of the irradiance sensors were randomly disabled or reported erroneous values, the network’s accuracy degraded by only 3.2%, demonstrating resilience to sensor failure—a common occurrence in field deployments. The model’s robustness was also tested under temperature variations, with module temperatures ranging from 15°C to 65°C. Although the training data was generated at a fixed temperature of 25°C, the network maintained an accuracy of over 78% across the temperature range, suggesting that the irradiance patterns contain sufficient discriminative information to compensate for thermal effects. Future iterations of the system may incorporate temperature as an additional input feature to further enhance performance.

The invention’s integration into a cyber-physical system is seamless and non-intrusive. The neural network model can be deployed on a microcontroller embedded within the central monitoring unit, communicating with the smart monitoring devices via wireless protocols such as Zigbee, LoRa, or Wi-Fi. The control signals to the switching matrix are transmitted over low-voltage digital lines, compatible with existing relay systems. No modifications to the photovoltaic modules, inverters, or grid interface are required, making the system retrofittable to existing installations. The system’s operation is transparent to end users and grid operators, as it functions autonomously without requiring manual intervention or configuration. The ability to learn from historical data and adapt to new shading patterns without reprogramming makes the system self-sustaining over its operational lifetime.

In summary, the results demonstrate that the proposed system achieves superior classification accuracy, outperforms all conventional machine learning methods, delivers significant and consistent power gains under realistic electrical losses, operates with minimal latency, and integrates effortlessly into existing photovoltaic infrastructure. The system’s success lies in its ability to transform a complex, high-dimensional control problem into a tractable classification task, leveraging the representational power of deep neural networks to uncover hidden relationships between irradiance patterns and topological performance. This approach represents a paradigm shift from rule-based and optimization-driven methods to data-driven, learning-based control, setting a new standard for intelligent reconfiguration in photovoltaic systems.

## Computing Device

The computing device central to the operation of this invention is a dedicated embedded processing unit designed for real-time inference of photovoltaic array topology selection. This device is physically integrated into the monitoring and control infrastructure of the photovoltaic system, typically located at a central hub or within a field-mounted enclosure that communicates wirelessly with the smart monitoring devices affixed to each photovoltaic module. The device is constructed using a low-power, high-efficiency microcontroller or system-on-chip architecture, optimized for deterministic execution of neural network inference with minimal latency and energy consumption. The processor core is based on an ARM Cortex-M7 or equivalent architecture, operating at a clock frequency of 480 MHz, with sufficient on-chip memory to store the trained neural network weights, biases, and intermediate activation buffers without requiring external RAM.

The computing device is equipped with a non-volatile memory subsystem comprising flash memory and electrically erasable programmable read-only memory to permanently store the trained neural network model, calibration parameters, and operational logs. The model, serialized in a compact binary format, occupies less than 200 kilobytes of storage space, allowing for rapid loading and execution. The device includes a real-time clock to timestamp incoming irradiance data and coordinate the timing of topology switching events, ensuring synchronization with the sampling rate of the monitoring devices. A communication interface, implemented via a wireless transceiver supporting protocols such as Zigbee, LoRaWAN, or Wi-Fi 6, enables bidirectional data exchange with the smart monitoring devices distributed across the array. The device receives irradiance profiles at a configurable interval, typically every 10 to 30 seconds, depending on the rate of environmental change and the desired responsiveness of the system.

The device’s computational pipeline is structured as a single-threaded, lock-free inference engine that executes the neural network model in a deterministic sequence. Upon receipt of a new irradiance profile, the device first applies a linear transformation to normalize the input vector using precomputed mean and standard deviation values derived during training. This normalization step ensures consistency between training and inference conditions, preserving the model’s predictive accuracy. The normalized vector is then passed through the layers of the neural network in sequence, with each layer performing its respective affine transformation, activation function, and normalization operation. The dropout and batch normalization layers are executed in inference mode, with dropout disabled and batch normalization parameters fixed to their trained values. The output layer computes the softmax probabilities for each of the four topologies, and the topology with the highest probability is selected as the optimal configuration.

The device includes a control logic unit that translates the selected topology into a sequence of digital signals corresponding to the state of each relay in the switching matrix. These signals are transmitted over low-voltage digital output lines to the actuation layer, which physically reconfigures the electrical connections among the photovoltaic modules. The device also incorporates a watchdog timer and fault detection mechanism to monitor the integrity of the switching process. If a relay fails to respond within a predefined timeout window, the device logs the event, initiates a diagnostic routine, and reverts to the previous topology to maintain system stability. The device is powered by a low-voltage DC supply derived from the photovoltaic array itself, ensuring continuous operation even during grid outages.

The computing device is designed to operate autonomously under a wide range of environmental conditions, including temperatures from -20°C to 60°C and relative humidity levels up to 95%. It is housed in an IP67-rated enclosure to protect against dust, moisture, and mechanical stress. The device supports over-the-air firmware updates, allowing for remote deployment of improved models or bug fixes without requiring physical access to the unit. The software stack is built on a real-time operating system with minimal overhead, ensuring predictable execution timing and deterministic response to incoming data. The device does not require internet connectivity for normal operation, making it suitable for deployment in remote or off-grid installations where network infrastructure is unreliable or nonexistent.

The computing device is designed with modularity and scalability in mind. Multiple instances of the device may be deployed across large photovoltaic installations, each managing a subset of the array to reduce communication latency and computational load. The neural network architecture is compatible with distributed inference, enabling hierarchical decision-making where local devices handle regional shading patterns and a central coordinator resolves conflicts or aggregates data for global optimization. The device’s firmware is written in C++ and compiled using a cross-platform toolchain to ensure compatibility across different hardware platforms. The entire system is designed to operate with a power consumption of less than 2 watts during active inference, making it energy-neutral within the context of a typical photovoltaic installation.

## Methods

The methods employed by this invention encompass a comprehensive pipeline for the autonomous reconfiguration of photovoltaic arrays through deep learning-based topology selection. The process begins with the acquisition of panel-level irradiance data from an array of smart monitoring devices, each affixed to an individual photovoltaic module and calibrated to measure incident solar radiation with high spatial and temporal resolution. These devices transmit their measurements wirelessly to a central computing unit, where the data is aggregated into a structured irradiance profile corresponding to the two-dimensional geometry of the array. The profile is then normalized using a linear transformation that subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation of the training dataset, ensuring that the input to the neural network conforms to the statistical distribution encountered during training.

Once normalized, the irradiance profile is fed as input to a pre-trained deep neural network that has been optimized for the classification of photovoltaic topologies. The network, comprising six fully connected layers with ReLU activations, dropout regularization, and batch normalization, performs a single forward pass to compute the probability distribution over the four possible configurations: series-parallel, bridge-link, honeycomb, and total-cross-tied. The output of the network is a four-element vector of probabilities, each representing the likelihood that the corresponding topology will yield the maximum power output under the given irradiance conditions. The topology with the highest probability is selected as the optimal configuration, and the corresponding control signal is generated by the computing device.

The control signal is transmitted to a switching matrix composed of solid-state relays or semiconductor switches positioned at strategic junctions within the photovoltaic array. These switches are arranged in a configurable grid that allows for the reconfiguration of electrical connections between modules, enabling the array to adopt any of the four target topologies. The switching matrix is controlled by a digital interface that maps the selected topology to a set of binary switch states, each corresponding to the closure or opening of a specific interconnection path. The switching event is executed within a latency window of less than 20 milliseconds, ensuring that the array can respond to rapid changes in shading conditions without significant power loss.

Following the reconfiguration, the system enters a verification phase in which the actual power output of the array is measured and compared against the predicted maximum power point. If the measured power deviates from the prediction by more than a predefined tolerance threshold, the system initiates a diagnostic protocol to assess the integrity of the sensors, switches, and network model. This feedback loop enables the system to detect and compensate for hardware degradation, sensor drift, or model misalignment over time. Periodically, the system may initiate a retraining cycle in which newly collected data is used to update the neural network model. This retraining is performed offline using a secure connection to a cloud-based training server, where the updated model is validated and then deployed back to the computing device via over-the-air update.

The training process for the neural network begins with the generation of a synthetic dataset comprising eight thousand irradiance instances, each representing a unique shading scenario. Each instance is generated by assigning a binary shading state (shaded or unshaded) to each of the 25 modules in a 5×5 array, followed by sampling irradiance values from uniform distributions calibrated to reflect real-world solar irradiance variability. For each instance, the maximum power point of each of the four topologies is computed using a high-fidelity simulation environment that incorporates realistic wiring losses, bypass diode characteristics, and module-level electrical parameters. The topology yielding the highest power output is designated as the ground truth label, transforming the problem into a supervised classification task.

The neural network is trained using the Adam optimization algorithm with a learning rate of 10⁻⁴, categorical cross-entropy as the loss function, and a batch size of 32. Dropout with a rate of 20% is applied to the second through fifth hidden layers, and batch normalization is applied to the same layers to stabilize training and improve generalization. The model is trained for 200 epochs, with early stopping triggered if the validation loss does not improve for ten consecutive epochs. The training dataset is partitioned into a 90–10 split, with the larger portion used for training and the smaller for validation. The model is initialized using He uniform initialization to ensure stable gradient propagation. After training, the model is evaluated on a held-out test set to confirm its performance metrics, including accuracy, F1 score, and confusion matrix distribution.

The methods further include a systematic analysis of the economic and energetic merit of topology reconfiguration. For each irradiance instance in the dataset, the power improvement resulting from switching from the conventional series-parallel topology to any of the other topologies is calculated. Instances where the improvement exceeds a threshold of 50 watts are identified as economically viable, and the percentage of such instances is computed across the dataset. The average power gain across all qualifying instances is determined to be approximately 11%, validating the practical value of the reconfiguration strategy. This analysis is repeated under varying conditions, including different temperatures, irradiance levels, and wiring resistances, to establish the robustness of the system.

The methods are designed to be reproducible and scalable. The synthetic data generation process, neural network architecture, training protocol, and simulation parameters are documented in sufficient detail to enable replication by a person skilled in the art. The system may be extended to larger arrays by increasing the input dimensionality and proportionally adjusting the hidden layer sizes, while preserving the same structural principles. The methods do not require proprietary hardware, specialized sensors, or external computational resources beyond those commonly available in modern photovoltaic installations. The entire system operates autonomously, requiring no manual intervention, and is designed to function reliably over the operational lifetime of the photovoltaic array.
