## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to brain-machine interfaces and, more specifically, to a novel class of recurrent neural network decoders designed to maintain high-performance cursor control in the presence of significant and unpredictable variations in neural signal recordings over time. The invention provides a method and system for decoding motor intent from chronic intracortical neural recordings using a multi-day recurrent neural network architecture that is trained on extensive, temporally distributed datasets spanning months of neural activity. The decoder is engineered to be inherently robust to abrupt changes in electrode performance, gradual drift in neural tuning, and other nonstationarities commonly encountered in long-term neural recording experiments, without requiring real-time adaptation, recalibration, or retraining during closed-loop operation. This technology is particularly suited for clinical applications in neuroprosthetics, where consistent and reliable control of external devices by individuals with paralysis or limb loss is critically dependent on decoder stability across days, weeks, and months of use.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Brain-machine interfaces have emerged as a promising therapeutic modality for restoring motor function to individuals with severe motor impairments. These systems translate neural activity recorded from the cerebral cortex into control signals for external devices such as computer cursors, robotic arms, or exoskeletons. Despite significant advances in sensor technology, signal processing, and decoding algorithms, one of the most persistent challenges to the clinical translation of BMI systems remains the instability of neural recordings over time. Chronic intracortical electrode arrays, while capable of recording from hundreds of neurons simultaneously, are subject to a variety of physiological and mechanical changes that alter the relationship between neural activity and intended movement. These include micro-movements of the electrode array relative to cortical tissue, glial scarring, changes in neuronal excitability, variations in recording impedance, and loss of individual neurons or electrode connections. As a result, decoders trained on neural data collected on one day often perform poorly when deployed on subsequent days, necessitating frequent and burdensome recalibration sessions that interrupt device use and reduce user autonomy.

Current state-of-the-art decoders, such as the linear Kalman filter, are typically trained using a small dataset collected during a single calibration session immediately preceding use. These models assume that the mapping between neural activity and kinematic output remains constant over short time intervals and rely on the assumption that recent neural recordings are the best predictors of current intent. However, this assumption breaks down when recording conditions change unpredictably or when the most recent calibration data are unavailable due to technical failure, user fatigue, or biological variability. While adaptive decoding strategies have been proposed to update decoder parameters in real time based on feedback from user performance, these approaches require a period of stable, interpretable control to function effectively. In cases where the decoder degrades rapidly—such as following the sudden loss of a highly informative electrode—the user may be unable to generate sufficient reliable feedback to enable adaptation, resulting in complete loss of control.

Previous attempts to improve decoder robustness have focused on improving sensor longevity, using alternative neural signals such as local field potentials or multiunit activity, or incorporating physiological models of neural drift. While these approaches have yielded incremental improvements, they do not fundamentally address the core problem: that a decoder trained on a narrow, static dataset lacks the capacity to generalize across the wide spectrum of neural states that occur naturally over time. Moreover, many of these methods are incompatible with the high-dimensional, nonlinear relationships that characterize motor cortical activity, limiting their ability to capture the full complexity of movement intent. There remains a critical need for a decoding architecture that can learn from a vast repository of past neural recordings, extract invariant features across diverse recording conditions, and maintain high-fidelity control even when confronted with novel or previously unseen patterns of neural activity.

The present invention addresses this unmet need by introducing a multi-day recurrent neural network decoder that is trained on hundreds of days of neural data collected over many months, enabling it to internalize a rich representation of the space of possible neural-to-kinematic mappings. Unlike conventional decoders that rely on linear assumptions and single-session calibration, this system leverages the representational power of recurrent neural networks to model complex temporal dependencies and nonlinear interactions between neural inputs and motor outputs. Crucially, the decoder is not adapted during closed-loop operation; instead, its robustness is embedded during training through a combination of extensive data exposure and deliberate perturbation of neural inputs. This design eliminates the need for real-time recalibration and enables continuous, uninterrupted use of the BMI system even under conditions that would render traditional decoders unusable.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is a brain-machine interface decoder system that enables robust, high-performance cursor control in the presence of substantial and unpredictable variations in chronic neural recordings. The system comprises a recurrent neural network architecture configured to receive as input a time series of binned spike counts from an array of intracortical electrodes and to generate as output a continuous estimate of cursor position and velocity in two spatial dimensions. The decoder is trained offline using a large corpus of neural and kinematic data collected across hundreds of recording sessions spanning many months, allowing it to learn a generalized mapping between neural activity and movement intent that is invariant to many common sources of recording variability. During training, the neural input data are intentionally perturbed using a stochastic augmentation procedure that simulates a broad range of recording condition changes, including global shifts in firing rates, electrode-specific gain changes, and partial or complete loss of input signals. The network is optimized to minimize the error between predicted and actual kinematic trajectories across all training data, with the constraint that the perturbations are applied anew on each training iteration, ensuring that the learned representation is not overfit to any specific recording session or noise profile.

The recurrent neural network architecture is defined by a set of parameters that include a tensor of recurrent weights modulated multiplicatively by the input spike counts, a set of linear projection matrices that factorize the input-to-hidden and hidden-to-output connections, and bias terms that stabilize the network dynamics. The network is trained using a second-order optimization algorithm that back-propagates error through time, enabling precise adjustment of the network’s internal parameters. The training procedure is designed to sample data from many different recording days within each minibatch, ensuring that the network is exposed to a diverse array of neural activity patterns. The resulting decoder, once trained, operates in closed-loop without any parameter updates, relying solely on its pre-trained weights to interpret incoming neural signals. This fixed-parameter design distinguishes the invention from adaptive decoders that continuously update their internal models during use.

The invention demonstrates superior performance compared to conventional decoders in two critical scenarios: first, when a subset of the most informative electrodes is suddenly disabled, simulating a hardware failure; and second, when the decoder is trained on data collected months prior to use, simulating a scenario in which recent calibration data are unavailable. In both cases, the invention maintains high cursor control accuracy and target acquisition rates, whereas conventional decoders fail completely or exhibit erratic, unreliable performance. The invention does not sacrifice peak performance under ideal conditions; in fact, it consistently outperforms same-day trained decoders in terms of speed and accuracy of target acquisition. The system is capable of operating on both radial and random target tasks, demonstrating generalization beyond the specific movement patterns used during training. Importantly, the invention requires no user intervention, no real-time recalibration, and no manual selection of decoder parameters during use, making it uniquely suited for long-term, unattended clinical deployment.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### MRNN Definition

The multi-day recurrent neural network, or MRNN, is a nonlinear dynamical system designed to map high-dimensional, time-varying neural spike count inputs to continuous, two-dimensional cursor velocity and position outputs. The architecture is defined by a set of differential equations that govern the evolution of a hidden state vector over time, with the input spike counts directly modulating the recurrent connectivity structure of the network. Unlike conventional recurrent neural networks in which the input acts as a static bias term added to the hidden state dynamics, the MRNN employs a multiplicative interaction in which the input vector parameterizes the entire recurrent weight matrix through a low-rank factorization. This allows the network to dynamically reconfigure its internal dynamics based on the statistical properties of the incoming neural signal, effectively selecting an appropriate internal decoder for the current recording condition without requiring external adaptation.

The network operates in continuous time and is described by a set of state variables representing the activation levels of N recurrently connected units. The activation vector, denoted x(t), evolves according to a differential equation that incorporates both recurrent feedback and multiplicative input modulation. The firing rate of each unit is computed as the hyperbolic tangent of its activation, yielding a smooth, bounded output that is biologically plausible and numerically stable. The recurrent connectivity is represented by a three-dimensional tensor Ju(t), which is constructed as a product of three lower-dimensional matrices: Jxf, Jfu, and Jfx. The matrix Jxf maps the hidden state to an intermediate factor space, Jfu maps the input spike counts to the same factor space, and Jfx maps the factor space back to the hidden state. This factorization reduces the number of parameters required to represent the full tensor from O(N²E) to O(NF + FE + FN), where E is the number of electrodes and F is the number of factors, making the model computationally tractable while preserving its representational capacity.

The input to the network, u(t), is a vector of binned spike counts collected from each electrode at discrete time intervals, typically 20 to 25 milliseconds. These spike counts are derived from threshold crossings of neural waveforms recorded via chronically implanted Utah arrays, and they represent the aggregate activity of multiple neurons in the vicinity of each electrode. The network does not require spike sorting or single-unit isolation, as it operates on population-level signals that are more stable over time than isolated single-unit activity. The output of the network, z(t), is a two-dimensional vector representing the desired cursor velocity in the horizontal and vertical dimensions. This output is generated by a linear readout layer that projects the network’s firing rates onto the output space, followed by a bias term that accounts for baseline offsets in the kinematic signal. The entire system is governed by a single time constant τ, which determines the temporal integration window of the network and is set to a physiologically plausible value on the order of several hundred milliseconds to match the natural timescales of motor planning and execution.

The architecture is designed to be invariant to the absolute scale of neural activity, as the multiplicative interaction between input and recurrent weights allows the network to compensate for global increases or decreases in firing rates. This property is critical for robustness, as it enables the decoder to function even when the overall level of neural activity fluctuates due to changes in arousal, attention, or electrode impedance. The network is also capable of handling missing or corrupted inputs, as the factorized structure allows it to infer plausible hidden states even when some electrodes are silent. The recurrent connections enable the network to maintain an internal memory of past inputs, allowing it to resolve ambiguities in the current signal by referencing prior patterns of activity. This temporal integration is essential for generating smooth, continuous cursor trajectories that mimic natural human movement.

### MRNN Output Definition

The output of the MRNN is defined as a continuous, two-dimensional vector z(t) that represents the instantaneous velocity of the cursor in the fronto-parallel plane. This output is derived from a linear transformation of the network’s firing rates, which are themselves a nonlinear function of the hidden state activations. The firing rate of each recurrent unit is computed as the hyperbolic tangent of its corresponding activation, ensuring that the output remains bounded between -1 and 1, which facilitates numerical stability during training and inference. The linear readout matrix Wo, which maps the N-dimensional firing rate vector to the two-dimensional output space, is trained alongside the recurrent weights to minimize the mean squared error between the predicted velocity and the true hand velocity measured during training.

In addition to the velocity output, a separate MRNN is trained to predict the cursor position, yielding a second output vector that represents the cumulative displacement of the cursor over time. These two decoders operate in parallel and are combined during closed-loop operation to generate a final cursor position estimate. The position decoder receives the same neural input as the velocity decoder but is trained to minimize the error between predicted and actual hand position, rather than velocity. The outputs of the two decoders are blended using a weighted sum, with the position signal contributing a small stabilizing component to counteract drift that might otherwise accumulate from velocity integration alone. The blending parameter β, which determines the relative contribution of position versus velocity, is set to a value close to 1, ensuring that the cursor movement is predominantly driven by velocity while retaining sufficient positional feedback to prevent unbounded drift.

The velocity output is normalized to a range that corresponds to the physical workspace of the virtual cursor, typically spanning a 20 cm × 20 cm area. This normalization is achieved by scaling the raw network output using empirically determined gain factors γv and γp, which convert the dimensionless network outputs into physical units of distance per unit time or distance. These scaling factors are determined during the training phase by correlating the network’s predicted velocity with the true hand velocity across a large set of training trials and adjusting the gains to minimize the residual error. The resulting output is then used to update the cursor position at each time step according to a discrete-time integration rule that preserves the temporal continuity of the movement.

The output of the MRNN is designed to be smooth and continuous, reflecting the natural dynamics of human motor control. Unlike decoders that produce discrete or jerky movements due to abrupt changes in neural tuning, the MRNN generates trajectories that are temporally coherent and exhibit the characteristic acceleration and deceleration profiles of voluntary reaching movements. This is achieved through the combination of the recurrent dynamics, which enforce temporal dependencies between successive time steps, and the hyperbolic tangent nonlinearity, which prevents the network from producing extreme or unphysical outputs. The absence of abrupt transitions or discontinuities in the output is critical for user comfort and task performance, as users are highly sensitive to delays, jitter, or instability in cursor motion.

During offline evaluation, the output of the MRNN is compared to the true hand velocity using the square of the Pearson correlation coefficient, which provides a robust measure of the linear relationship between predicted and actual kinematics. This metric is used to quantify decoder accuracy and to compare performance across different training regimes and experimental conditions. In closed-loop operation, the output is evaluated using more ecologically valid metrics such as target acquisition rate and time to target, which reflect the user’s ability to successfully complete tasks in real time. The MRNN consistently outperforms conventional decoders on both metrics, demonstrating that its output is not only statistically accurate but also behaviorally effective.

### Network Construction for Cursor BMI Decoder

The network construction for the cursor BMI decoder is a carefully engineered system that integrates multiple components into a unified architecture capable of robust, high-fidelity motor decoding. The system consists of two parallel MRNNs: one trained to decode cursor velocity and the other trained to decode cursor position. Each network is constructed with identical recurrent architecture but distinct output targets, allowing the system to simultaneously estimate both the instantaneous movement direction and the accumulated position of the cursor. The input to each network is the same: a time series of binned spike counts from all electrodes in the array, sampled at a rate of 40 to 50 Hz. The spike counts are collected from threshold crossings of neural waveforms, with no attempt made to isolate individual neurons, thereby preserving the population-level signal that is most stable over time.

Each MRNN is initialized with a fixed number of recurrent units, typically 50 to 100, depending on the number of electrodes and the complexity of the neural signal. The number of factors F used in the tensor factorization is set equal to the number of recurrent units, ensuring that the network has sufficient capacity to model complex interactions between inputs and hidden states. The recurrent weight tensor Ju(t) is constructed from three matrices: Jxf, which maps the hidden state to the factor space; Jfu, which maps the input spike counts to the factor space; and Jfx, which maps the factor space back to the hidden state. The elements of these matrices are initialized from a Gaussian distribution with zero mean and variance chosen to ensure stable network dynamics. The bias vectors bx and bz are initialized to zero, and the readout matrix Wo is initialized to a small random perturbation to break symmetry and facilitate learning.

The network is trained using a second-order optimization algorithm known as Hessian-Free optimization, which is adapted to the continuous-time dynamics of the MRNN. This algorithm computes the gradient of the mean squared error between predicted and actual kinematic outputs with respect to all network parameters, using back-propagation through time to account for the temporal dependencies in the data. The optimization is performed over minibatches of training data, each of which is constructed by concatenating five consecutive real reaching trials into a single training sequence. The first two trials in each sequence are used to seed the hidden state of the network, while the final three are used for learning. This approach ensures that the network is trained on realistic temporal patterns of neural activity, rather than isolated, artificial segments of data.

The training procedure is designed to be computationally intensive but highly effective. The optimization runs for hundreds of iterations, with snapshots of the network saved at regular intervals. The final network is selected based on its performance on a held-out validation set, ensuring that the chosen model generalizes well to unseen data. The entire training process is performed offline, using data collected over many months of behavioral experiments, and does not require any online adjustment during closed-loop use. Once trained, the network is compiled into a real-time operating system and deployed for cursor control, where it operates at a fixed sampling rate with minimal computational latency.

The system is designed to be modular and scalable. The number of recurrent units, factors, and electrodes can be adjusted to accommodate different array configurations, recording densities, or computational constraints. The architecture is also compatible with mixed signal types, including single-unit activity, multiunit activity, and local field potentials, although the present implementation uses threshold crossings as the primary input. The two parallel decoders can be independently trained, allowing for separate optimization of position and velocity outputs, which may be beneficial in tasks requiring fine positional control versus rapid velocity modulation. The entire system is implemented in software and can be deployed on standard computing hardware, with potential for acceleration using graphics processing units or specialized neuromorphic hardware in future implementations.

### MRNN Initialization

The initialization of the MRNN is a critical step that ensures the network begins training from a stable, well-conditioned state that facilitates convergence to a high-quality solution. The network parameters are initialized using a principled approach that balances the need for sufficient variability to break symmetry with the requirement for stable dynamics during early training. The recurrent weight matrices Jxf, Jfu, and Jfx are initialized with elements drawn independently from a zero-mean Gaussian distribution, with variances chosen to maintain the network in a regime of balanced excitation and inhibition. Specifically, the variance of Jxf is set to gxf/F, where gxf is a scaling factor equal to 1.0, and F is the number of factors. The variance of Jfu is set to gfu/E, where gfu is also 1.0 and E is the number of electrodes. The variance of Jfx is set to gfx/N, where gfx is 1.0 and N is the number of recurrent units. These choices ensure that the magnitude of the recurrent inputs to each unit is neither too large, which could lead to explosive dynamics, nor too small, which could cause the network to remain inactive.

The readout matrix Wo is initialized to zero, which ensures that the initial output of the network is neutral and does not bias the cursor in any particular direction. The bias vectors bx and bz are also initialized to zero, eliminating any default offset in the hidden state or output space. This initialization strategy is chosen to avoid introducing artificial biases into the learning process and to allow the network to discover the appropriate scale and offset of the neural-to-kinematic mapping entirely from the data. The absence of prior assumptions about the mean firing rate or kinematic output allows the decoder to adapt to the natural variability of neural activity across different recording sessions.

The network is initialized with a fixed number of recurrent units, typically 50 for monkeys with a single electrode array and 100 for those with two arrays. The number of factors F is set equal to the number of recurrent units, ensuring that the factorized representation has sufficient capacity to model the full complexity of the input-output relationship. The time constant τ, which governs the temporal integration of the network, is set to a physiologically relevant value of approximately 100 milliseconds, matching the timescale of motor planning and execution observed in primates. This value is fixed across all training and testing conditions and is not optimized during training, as it is assumed to reflect a fundamental property of the neural system rather than a tunable parameter of the decoder.

The initialization procedure is consistent across all training runs and is applied identically for each new training session, ensuring that differences in decoder performance are attributable to the training data and augmentation strategy rather than to variations in initial conditions. The use of a fixed initialization protocol also facilitates reproducibility and comparison across experiments. Once initialized, the network parameters are updated using a second-order optimization algorithm that does not rely on gradient descent alone but instead incorporates curvature information from the Hessian matrix to navigate the loss landscape more efficiently. This approach reduces the likelihood of converging to poor local minima and enables the network to achieve high decoding accuracy even when trained on highly heterogeneous data spanning many months of recording.

### Concatenating Neural Trials for Seeding the MRNN During Training

The training of the MRNN relies on a novel method of organizing neural data into extended sequences that preserve the temporal structure of natural reaching behavior. Rather than training the network on isolated, single-trial segments of neural activity, the system concatenates five consecutive real reaching trials into a single training sequence, with the first two trials serving as a seeding period and the final three used for learning. This approach ensures that the network is exposed to realistic patterns of neural dynamics that occur during the transition between movement initiation, execution, and termination. The seeding trials are not used to update the network parameters; instead, they serve to establish an appropriate initial state for the hidden units, allowing the network to transition smoothly into the learning phase.

The concatenation process begins by selecting a set of trials from a single recording session, ensuring that the neural and kinematic data are temporally aligned and collected under consistent recording conditions. Five consecutive trials are then extracted from the dataset and concatenated end-to-end, forming a single continuous sequence of neural inputs and corresponding kinematic outputs. The spike counts from each electrode are binned at a fixed interval of 20 to 25 milliseconds, and the hand position and velocity are computed from the infrared tracking system at a matching sampling rate. The resulting sequence spans approximately 10 to 15 seconds, depending on the duration of the individual trials, and contains multiple instances of movement initiation, target acquisition, and return to the center target.

The first two trials in this sequence are used exclusively to initialize the hidden state of the network. During this seeding period, the network receives the neural input and evolves its internal state according to the recurrent dynamics, but no parameter updates are performed. This allows the hidden state to settle into a configuration that is consistent with the ongoing neural activity, effectively simulating the natural transition from rest to movement that occurs in the brain. The final three trials are then used to compute the error between the predicted and actual cursor velocity, and the network parameters are updated using back-propagation through time to minimize this error. This approach ensures that the network learns to predict movement not from an arbitrary or reset state, but from a state that reflects the ongoing neural context, which is critical for generalization to real-world use.

The concatenation procedure is applied to all training data, with the exception that the trials are selected in a sliding window fashion, such that every possible consecutive set of five trials is used for training. This results in an enormous number of overlapping training sequences, each of which provides a unique perspective on the relationship between neural activity and movement. The use of overlapping sequences ensures that the network is exposed to a wide variety of movement patterns, including different directions, speeds, and trajectories, without requiring explicit labeling or segmentation of the data. The training procedure is further enhanced by sampling data from multiple recording days within each minibatch, ensuring that the network is not biased toward any single session or recording condition.

This method of trial concatenation and seeding is a key innovation that distinguishes the MRNN from conventional decoders, which typically treat each trial as an independent, isolated event. By preserving the temporal continuity of neural activity across trials, the MRNN learns to model the natural dynamics of motor behavior, including the gradual build-up of preparatory activity, the smooth execution of movement, and the controlled return to rest. This results in a decoder that generates more naturalistic, fluid cursor trajectories and is less prone to abrupt or erratic movements that can occur when the network is reset between trials.

### Perturbing the Neural Input During Training

A critical component of the MRNN’s robustness is the intentional perturbation of the neural input during training, a technique known in machine learning as data augmentation. Unlike conventional decoders that are trained on raw, unaltered neural data, the MRNN is exposed to a wide range of artificially modified neural signals that simulate the types of recording condition changes commonly encountered during long-term BMI use. These perturbations are applied stochastically on every iteration of the optimization algorithm, ensuring that the network learns to generalize across a broad spectrum of possible signal variations rather than memorizing the specific characteristics of any single recording session.

The perturbation procedure operates on the binned spike counts of each electrode in each training trial. For each electrode c in trial j, the observed number of spikes is multiplied by a multiplicative factor η that is drawn from a Gaussian distribution with a mean of one and a standard deviation that is separately controlled for trial-level and electrode-level variations. The trial-level perturbation ηj models global changes in neural activity, such as those caused by fluctuations in arousal, attention, or array movement relative to the cortex. The electrode-level perturbation ηc models localized changes, such as electrode dropping, changes in baseline firing rates, or variations in signal amplitude due to tissue encapsulation. The product of these two factors, ηj × ηc, determines the final perturbed spike count for each electrode in each trial.

To ensure that the perturbations remain physiologically plausible, the perturbed spike count is constrained to remain within a biologically reasonable range. If the perturbed value is less than zero, it is resampled until a non-negative value is obtained. If the perturbed value exceeds the maximum number of spikes observed in the original data, additional spikes are randomly inserted into time bins that already contained spikes, preserving the temporal structure of the signal. If the perturbed value is less than the original count, spikes are randomly removed from time bins that contained spikes, again preserving the overall temporal pattern. This procedure ensures that the perturbed data retain the statistical properties of the original recordings while introducing controlled variability that mimics real-world recording instabilities.

The perturbation is applied independently for each training iteration, meaning that the same neural trial is presented to the network with a different set of perturbations on each pass through the training data. This forces the network to learn a representation that is invariant to these variations, rather than overfitting to the specific noise profile of any single recording session. The standard deviations of the perturbation distributions, σtrial and σelectrode, are tuned empirically to maximize decoder robustness without degrading peak performance. The resulting training regime is equivalent to exposing the network to thousands of synthetic recording conditions, each of which corresponds to a different combination of global and local neural signal changes.

This data augmentation strategy is distinct from traditional dropout methods, which randomly deactivate units during training, as it is specifically tailored to the nonstationarities inherent in neural recording systems. It does not rely on the assumption that neural activity is independent across electrodes or time, but instead preserves the correlations and temporal structure of the original data while introducing realistic variability. The effectiveness of this approach is demonstrated by the fact that the MRNN trained with perturbations outperforms both the same decoder trained without perturbations and conventional decoders trained on unmodified data, even when the perturbations do not exactly match the types of changes encountered during closed-loop use. This suggests that the network learns a generalizable form of robustness that extends beyond the specific perturbations applied during training.

### Using Many Days Training Data

The MRNN decoder achieves its remarkable robustness and high performance by being trained on an extensive corpus of neural data collected over hundreds of days and spanning many months of behavioral experimentation. Unlike conventional decoders that rely on calibration data collected immediately before use, the MRNN is trained on a heterogeneous dataset that includes recordings from dozens to hundreds of distinct experimental sessions, each representing a unique combination of neural activity patterns, behavioral states, and recording conditions. This approach is based on the observation that, despite day-to-day variability in neural recordings, there are consistent statistical relationships between neural activity and movement intent that persist across time.

The training dataset is constructed by compiling all available neural and kinematic data from every recording session in which the animal performed a point-to-point reaching task. Each session contributes a set of trials that are concatenated into training sequences as described previously. The data are sampled in a balanced manner, with a small number of trials randomly selected from each day to ensure that no single recording session dominates the training process. This sampling strategy ensures that the network is exposed to the full diversity of neural activity patterns that occur across the animal’s research career, including periods of high and low signal quality, changes in electrode performance, and variations in behavioral performance.

The use of such a large and temporally distributed dataset enables the MRNN to learn a generalized mapping between neural activity and movement that is not tied to any specific recording session. The network learns to identify invariant features of the neural signal that are predictive of movement intent, regardless of the absolute firing rates, electrode gain, or signal-to-noise ratio. This is in stark contrast to conventional decoders, which are highly sensitive to the specific characteristics of the calibration data and fail when those characteristics change. The MRNN, by contrast, develops an internal representation that is robust to a wide range of signal variations, including those that were never explicitly encountered during training.

The effectiveness of this approach is demonstrated by the fact that the decoder’s performance continues to improve as more days of data are added to the training set, up to the limit of the available data. This improvement is not merely incremental but substantial, with offline decoding accuracy increasing significantly as the number of training days grows from a handful to over 150. The decoder trained on the full dataset achieves decoding accuracies that far exceed those of decoders trained on single-day data, even when those decoders are optimized using the same algorithm and training procedure. This suggests that the additional data are not simply providing more examples of the same patterns, but are instead revealing new, previously unseen relationships between neural activity and movement that are critical for robust decoding.

The use of many days of training data also enables the MRNN to learn from the subtle, long-term trends in neural activity that are often overlooked in conventional approaches. For example, the network may learn that certain patterns of neural activity that were previously associated with one movement direction are now associated with a different direction due to gradual drift in electrode placement. By observing these changes across time, the network can infer the underlying structure of the neural-to-kinematic mapping and develop a representation that is stable over time. This ability to learn from the evolution of neural signals over months is a key advantage of the MRNN and is not possible with decoders that rely on short-term calibration.

### Network Output

The output of the MRNN is a continuous, two-dimensional vector that represents the desired velocity and position of the cursor in the virtual workspace. This output is generated by two separate but parallel decoders: one trained to predict velocity and the other trained to predict position. The velocity decoder produces a signal that directly drives the movement of the cursor, while the position decoder provides a stabilizing feedback signal that prevents drift and ensures that the cursor returns to a neutral position when no movement is intended. The two outputs are combined using a weighted sum, with the velocity signal contributing the majority of the control signal and the position signal providing a small but critical corrective component.

The velocity output is derived from a linear readout of the network’s firing rates, which are themselves a nonlinear transformation of the hidden state activations. The readout matrix is trained to minimize the mean squared error between the predicted velocity and the true hand velocity measured during training. The resulting output is scaled to match the physical dimensions of the virtual workspace, ensuring that the cursor moves at a natural speed and with appropriate acceleration profiles. The position output is generated in a similar manner, but is trained to minimize the error between predicted and actual hand position. This output is then integrated over time to generate a cumulative displacement, which is combined with the velocity signal to produce the final cursor position.

The blending of velocity and position signals is governed by a parameter β, which determines the relative weight of each component. In the present implementation, β is set to 0.99, meaning that 99% of the cursor movement is driven by velocity and only 1% by position. This choice ensures that the cursor responds rapidly to changes in neural intent while maintaining sufficient positional feedback to prevent unbounded drift. The small position component is critical for long-term stability, as even minor errors in velocity estimation can accumulate over time and lead to large deviations in cursor position. The inclusion of this component allows the system to maintain accurate control over extended periods of use without requiring recalibration.

The output of the MRNN is designed to be smooth, continuous, and free of abrupt transitions or jitter. This is achieved through the recurrent dynamics of the network, which enforce temporal dependencies between successive time steps, and the use of the hyperbolic tangent nonlinearity, which bounds the output within a physiologically plausible range. The resulting cursor trajectories are indistinguishable from those produced by natural arm movements, exhibiting the characteristic acceleration and deceleration profiles of voluntary reaching. This level of naturalism is critical for user acceptance and performance, as users are highly sensitive to delays, instability, or unnatural motion in brain-machine interfaces.

The output is also designed to be invariant to the absolute scale of neural activity. The multiplicative interaction between input and recurrent weights allows the network to compensate for global increases or decreases in firing rates, ensuring that the decoder functions equally well regardless of whether the neural signal is strong or weak. This property is essential for robustness, as it allows the system to continue operating even when electrode performance degrades over time or when the animal’s arousal level fluctuates. The output is further normalized to ensure consistency across different animals and recording configurations, making the system adaptable to a wide range of clinical and experimental settings.

### Training and Running the Networks

The training and operation of the MRNN decoders are implemented as two distinct phases: an offline training phase and an online closed-loop operation phase. During the training phase, the network is exposed to a large and diverse dataset of neural and kinematic recordings collected over many months of behavioral experimentation. The data are preprocessed to extract binned spike counts and corresponding hand velocities, and then organized into concatenated sequences of five trials, with the first two used for seeding and the final three for learning. The network parameters are optimized using a second-order Hessian-Free optimization algorithm that computes the gradient of the mean squared error with respect to all parameters using back-propagation through time. The optimization is performed over hundreds of iterations, with snapshots of the network saved at regular intervals and the best-performing model selected based on its performance on a held-out validation set.

Once training is complete, the network is compiled into a real-time operating system and deployed for closed-loop cursor control. During operation, the network receives a continuous stream of binned spike counts from the implanted electrode array, sampled at a rate of 40 to 50 Hz. These inputs are processed by the pre-trained network to generate a two-dimensional output representing the desired cursor velocity and position. The output is then scaled and blended using the previously determined parameters to produce a smooth, continuous cursor trajectory that is displayed on a computer monitor. The cursor position is updated at each time step according to a discrete-time integration rule that preserves the temporal continuity of the movement.

The network operates without any parameter updates during closed-loop use, meaning that its weights remain fixed throughout the entire period of operation. This distinguishes the MRNN from adaptive decoders, which continuously update their internal models based on feedback from user performance. The fixed-parameter design ensures that the decoder is not susceptible to instability or runaway adaptation, which can occur when the feedback signal is noisy or unreliable. Instead, the decoder relies entirely on its pre-trained representation to interpret the incoming neural signal, making it robust to sudden changes in recording conditions that would render adaptive decoders ineffective.

The system is designed to be highly reliable and minimally invasive. The training process, while computationally intensive, is performed offline and does not interfere with the animal’s daily activities. Once trained, the decoder requires no recalibration, no user intervention, and no manual adjustment of parameters. The entire system can be deployed on standard computing hardware, with potential for acceleration using graphics processing units or specialized neuromorphic hardware in future implementations. The decoder is compatible with a wide range of electrode arrays and neural signal types, making it adaptable to different experimental and clinical settings.

The combination of extensive training data, deliberate input perturbations, and a powerful recurrent architecture enables the MRNN to achieve a level of robustness and performance that is unattainable with conventional decoders. The system consistently outperforms state-of-the-art Kalman filter decoders in both offline and closed-loop evaluations, demonstrating superior target acquisition rates, faster movement times, and greater resilience to electrode failure and recording drift. The MRNN represents a fundamental shift in the design philosophy of brain-machine interfaces, moving away from the paradigm of frequent recalibration and toward a model of inherent, built-in robustness that enables continuous, unattended use over extended periods of time.
