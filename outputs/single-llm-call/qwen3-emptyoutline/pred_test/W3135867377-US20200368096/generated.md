# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The human sensorimotor system relies on a continuous and dynamic integration of proprioceptive feedback to guide voluntary movement, maintain postural stability, and coordinate complex motor behaviors. Proprioception—the sense of body position and movement derived from mechanoreceptors in muscles, tendons, and joints—is fundamental to the brain’s ability to construct an internal representation of limb state in space. This internal model enables precise motor control without constant reliance on visual or auditory cues, allowing for smooth, efficient, and adaptive movement even in the absence of external feedback. Deficits in proprioceptive function are commonly observed following neurological injury, including stroke, traumatic brain injury, Parkinson’s disease, and peripheral neuropathies. These deficits manifest as impaired coordination, reduced movement accuracy, increased postural instability, and diminished motor learning capacity, all of which significantly compromise functional independence and quality of life. Traditional rehabilitation strategies have primarily focused on strengthening musculature, improving range of motion, or enhancing visual-motor coordination, often overlooking the critical role of somatosensory retraining in restoring sensorimotor integration. While some interventions have incorporated passive joint movement or tactile stimulation to stimulate proprioceptive afferents, few have systematically targeted the neural mechanisms underlying proprioceptive acuity through task-specific, active sensorimotor engagement that challenges the precision of position discrimination and error correction.

Recent advances in robotics and neurophysiological measurement have enabled the development of highly controlled, reproducible paradigms for quantifying and modulating proprioceptive function. Robotic exoskeletons, in particular, offer the unique capability to deliver precise, repeatable passive movements to a joint while simultaneously recording kinematic data with sub-degree spatial and millisecond temporal resolution. These devices can be seamlessly integrated with virtual reality environments to create immersive, goal-directed tasks that require participants to make rapid, small-amplitude corrections to maintain a virtual object within a target zone. Such tasks are designed not merely to move the limb, but to force the nervous system to resolve subtle discrepancies between intended and actual limb position—thereby engaging the proprioceptive system at a perceptual level. Prior research has demonstrated that brief, single-session interventions using such robotic-aided visuomotor tasks lead to measurable improvements in proprioceptive acuity, as indexed by reductions in the Just-Noticeable Difference (JND) threshold for joint position discrimination. Importantly, these sensory improvements are accompanied by enhancements in untrained motor tasks, such as goal-directed pointing, suggesting that the training does not merely alter perceptual sensitivity but also induces functional plasticity within sensorimotor networks. These findings challenge the classical dichotomy between sensory and motor learning, revealing instead a tightly coupled, bidirectional relationship in which changes in one domain directly influence the other.

Despite these advances, a critical gap remains in our understanding of whether such sensorimotor learning is confined to the trained limb or whether it can generalize to the contralateral, homologous limb. The phenomenon of interlimb transfer—where training one limb leads to performance improvements in the opposite, untrained limb—has been well-documented in the motor domain, particularly in studies involving force field adaptation, motor sequence learning, and visuomotor rotation tasks. In these paradigms, participants exhibit reduced errors, faster movement times, or improved coordination in the untrained limb, even without direct practice. The neural basis for this transfer is widely attributed to interhemispheric communication via the corpus callosum, which connects homologous regions of the primary motor cortex, premotor areas, and supplementary motor areas across the two cerebral hemispheres. However, while motor transfer has been extensively studied, the question of whether proprioceptive learning—defined as an improvement in the ability to discriminate between two distinct joint positions—can similarly transfer to the contralateral limb has remained unanswered. This is a profound omission, given that proprioception is not a peripheral sensation but a centrally processed percept, dependent on cortical integration of afferent signals from both limbs. If proprioceptive learning can indeed transfer, it would imply that the neural representations of joint position are not strictly lateralized but are instead shared, modifiable, and capable of bilateral plasticity following unilateral training.

The implications of such a finding are far-reaching, particularly for clinical populations. In patients with unilateral neurological injury—such as stroke survivors with hemiparesis—rehabilitation efforts are often hampered by severe motor and sensory impairments on the affected side. Many patients are unable to actively engage in intensive, high-fidelity proprioceptive training due to weakness, spasticity, neglect, or lack of voluntary control. If training the unaffected, less impaired limb could induce measurable improvements in proprioceptive acuity and motor control on the affected side, it would revolutionize rehabilitation paradigms. Such an approach would circumvent the limitations of direct training on the impaired limb, enabling therapists to leverage the intact sensorimotor system to drive recovery in the compromised one. This strategy would not only reduce patient fatigue and frustration but also allow for earlier and more intensive intervention during the critical post-injury window when neuroplastic potential is highest. Moreover, if the effects of such training are retained beyond the immediate post-training period, it would suggest the formation of durable, consolidated memory traces within bilateral sensorimotor networks, further enhancing the clinical viability of the approach.

To date, no study has systematically evaluated whether a brief, single-session visuomotor training task designed to challenge proprioceptive discrimination can induce contralateral transfer of both sensory and motor learning. While tactile perceptual learning has been shown to transfer to homologous fingers on the opposite hand, proprioception involves a more complex integration of joint angle, velocity, and force signals across multiple muscle groups and afferent pathways. The question of whether such learning is transferable to the contralateral wrist—a joint with distinct biomechanical properties and cortical representation—remains unaddressed. Furthermore, the temporal dynamics of such transfer are unknown: does sensory transfer occur immediately after training, or does it require consolidation over time? Does motor transfer follow a similar timeline, or is it delayed, as is often observed in motor learning paradigms? Answering these questions requires a tightly controlled experimental design that isolates proprioceptive acuity and motor performance bilaterally, employs objective, robotic measures of both sensory and motor outcomes, and evaluates retention at multiple time points to distinguish immediate effects from consolidated learning.

This invention addresses these critical gaps by introducing a novel, non-invasive, robotics-based method for inducing and measuring contralateral transfer of proprioceptive and motor learning following unilateral visuomotor training. The method leverages a precisely calibrated robotic exoskeleton to deliver a standardized, adaptive visuomotor task that challenges the participant’s ability to discriminate and correct small deviations in wrist position under visual occlusion. The same device is then used to quantify proprioceptive acuity via a psychophysical forced-choice paradigm and to assess untrained motor performance via a discrete, vision-deprived pointing task. Critically, both the trained and untrained limbs are assessed before, immediately after, and 24 hours after a single training session, enabling a direct comparison of the time course and magnitude of learning transfer. The invention is distinguished from prior approaches by its explicit focus on proprioceptive acuity as the primary outcome, its use of a single, unified robotic platform to deliver both training and assessment, and its demonstration that improvements in the untrained limb are not merely coincidental but are predictable based on the magnitude of learning in the trained limb. This invention thus provides a scientifically grounded, clinically translatable framework for harnessing interlimb sensorimotor plasticity to enhance recovery in neurological populations, offering a paradigm shift from unilateral to bilateral rehabilitation strategies.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a novel method for enhancing proprioceptive acuity and motor performance in a first limb by training a contralateral, homologous second limb through a robotics-based visuomotor task that challenges the precision of joint position discrimination. The method comprises the steps of: (a) positioning a subject such that a first limb, selected from the group consisting of a wrist, ankle, elbow, or knee, is immobilized in a robotic exoskeleton device capable of delivering passive motion and measuring joint angle with sub-degree spatial resolution and millisecond temporal precision; (b) presenting a virtual reality environment to the subject in which a virtual object is displayed on a tiltable surface, wherein the orientation of the surface is controlled by the angular position of the first limb; (c) instructing the subject to maintain the virtual object within a target zone by making small, rapid, online corrections to the position of the first limb, wherein the task requires precise proprioceptive feedback to execute successfully and wherein visual feedback is provided only during the training phase; (d) dynamically increasing the difficulty of the task by altering virtual mechanical properties of the system, including increasing the mass of the virtual object, decreasing the friction coefficient of the virtual surface, and increasing the gain of velocity response to limb movement, such that the subject must make progressively smaller and more accurate corrections to maintain the object within the target zone; (e) continuing the training until the subject completes a predetermined number of successful trials or until a maximum time duration has elapsed, wherein the training is performed exclusively on the first limb; (f) after completion of the training, measuring the proprioceptive acuity of the second limb, contralateral and homologous to the first limb, using a psychophysical forced-choice paradigm in which the second limb is passively moved by the robotic device to two distinct joint positions in succession, and the subject is required to verbally indicate which position was farther from a reference position, with the Just-Noticeable Difference (JND) threshold being calculated from the subject’s responses using a logistic Weibull function fitted to the psychometric data; (g) after completion of the training, measuring the motor performance of the second limb using a discrete, vision-deprived pointing task in which the robotic device passively moves the second limb to a fixed target position, holds it briefly, and then prompts the subject to actively move the limb to the perceived target position, with the Movement Accuracy Error (MAE) being computed as the mean absolute angular deviation between the target and the final position across multiple trials; and (h) determining that the proprioceptive acuity and/or motor performance of the second limb has improved relative to baseline measurements obtained prior to training, thereby demonstrating contralateral transfer of sensorimotor learning.

In a preferred embodiment, the first limb is the right wrist and the second limb is the left wrist, with the subject being right-handed. In another preferred embodiment, the robotic exoskeleton is configured to allow full range of motion in flexion/extension, adduction/abduction, and forearm rotation, and is calibrated to provide haptic feedback with a spatial resolution of less than 0.01 degrees and a sampling rate of at least 200 Hz. The virtual reality environment is rendered on a high-resolution display positioned in front of the subject, and the visual feedback is removed during all assessment phases to prevent visual compensation. The training task is adaptive, such that difficulty increases only after the subject successfully completes at least one trial at each of three predefined joint angles, and the virtual mechanical parameters are adjusted in a predetermined sequence to ensure consistent challenge across subjects. The psychophysical assessment for proprioceptive acuity employs a psi-marginal algorithm to dynamically adjust the angular difference between standard and comparison stimuli based on the subject’s prior responses, ensuring efficient convergence on the JND threshold within a fixed number of trials. The motor performance assessment involves ten trials per limb, with each trial separated by a rest interval to prevent fatigue, and the MAE is calculated as the mean absolute error across all trials, normalized to the target position. The method further includes a retention assessment conducted 24 hours after training, wherein the same proprioceptive and motor assessments are repeated to determine whether the observed improvements are consolidated into long-term memory.

The invention further encompasses a system for implementing the method, comprising a robotic exoskeleton device with integrated position encoders and torque motors, a computer-controlled virtual reality environment, a data acquisition system synchronized with the robotic device, and software configured to execute the adaptive training protocol, administer the psychophysical assessments, calculate the JND and MAE metrics, and generate reports indicating the magnitude and statistical significance of contralateral transfer. The system may be integrated into a clinical rehabilitation setting, wherein the training is administered by a therapist on the unaffected limb of a patient with unilateral neurological impairment, and the resulting improvements in the affected limb are monitored over multiple sessions to guide therapeutic progression. The method is non-invasive, requires no pharmacological intervention, and can be completed in under one hour, making it suitable for use in outpatient clinics, home-based rehabilitation, or intensive care units. The invention demonstrates that proprioceptive learning is not confined to the trained limb but can be transferred to the contralateral homologous joint through bilateral sensorimotor network plasticity, thereby providing a novel therapeutic strategy for enhancing recovery in patients with stroke, Parkinson’s disease, traumatic brain injury, or other conditions characterized by unilateral proprioceptive deficits.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention describes a method and system for inducing contralateral transfer of proprioceptive and motor learning through unilateral visuomotor training, leveraging robotic technology to precisely control and measure sensorimotor performance. The invention is grounded in the discovery that a brief, single-session training task performed on one limb can lead to measurable and statistically significant improvements in both proprioceptive acuity and motor performance in the contralateral, homologous limb, even in the absence of direct practice. This transfer effect is not attributable to general fatigue, practice effects, or visual compensation, as all assessments are conducted under conditions of visual and auditory occlusion, and baseline performance is rigorously controlled. The method is implemented using a robotic exoskeleton system that is capable of delivering passive, precisely controlled movements to a joint while simultaneously acquiring high-fidelity kinematic data. The system is integrated with a virtual reality environment that presents a task requiring the subject to make continuous, fine-grained corrections to maintain a virtual object within a target zone, thereby engaging the proprioceptive system at a perceptual level. The training is performed exclusively on one limb, while the contralateral limb remains untrained, and both limbs are assessed before, immediately after, and 24 hours after training to evaluate the time course and persistence of learning transfer.

The robotic exoskeleton employed in the invention is a three-degree-of-freedom device designed to replicate the natural biomechanics of the human wrist, allowing for unrestricted motion in flexion/extension, adduction/abduction, and forearm rotation. The device is constructed from lightweight, high-strength materials and is fully backdrivable, meaning that it can be moved passively by the robot without resistance while also being capable of applying controlled torque to guide the limb through specific trajectories. The exoskeleton is equipped with high-resolution optical encoders that measure joint angle with a spatial precision of 0.0075 degrees and a sampling frequency of 200 Hz, ensuring that even the smallest deviations in limb position are captured with high fidelity. The device is mounted on a stable platform that supports the subject’s forearm in a neutral, comfortable position, with a custom-molded splint that minimizes movement of the forearm and ensures that all motion is isolated to the wrist joint. The exoskeleton is interfaced with a high-performance computer system running custom software that controls the delivery of training stimuli, records kinematic data, and synchronizes the virtual reality environment with the physical movement of the limb. The software is designed to ensure that the virtual representation of the wrist position is mapped linearly and without delay to the orientation of the virtual table on which the ball is displayed, eliminating any perceptual lag that might interfere with the subject’s ability to make accurate corrections.

During the training phase, the subject is seated in front of a high-resolution monitor displaying a virtual environment in which a spherical object, representing a ball, is shown rolling on a flat, tiltable surface. The orientation of the surface is determined in real time by the angular position of the subject’s trained wrist: flexion of the wrist tilts the surface to the left, while extension tilts it to the right. The ball is subject to virtual physical forces, including gravity, inertia, friction, and damping, which are programmable and adjustable according to the difficulty level of the task. The subject’s goal is to maintain the ball within a circular target zone, which is displayed as a static region on the screen. To achieve this, the subject must make small, rapid, and precise adjustments to the position of the wrist, using proprioceptive feedback alone to determine the direction and magnitude of the required movement. Visual feedback is provided throughout the training phase, allowing the subject to see the position of the ball and the orientation of the surface, but no explicit instructions are given regarding how to move the wrist. The task is inherently challenging because the ball’s response to wrist movement is nonlinear and becomes increasingly sensitive as the difficulty level increases. The difficulty is dynamically adjusted based on the subject’s performance: after the subject successfully completes at least one trial at each of three predefined wrist angles (10°, 15°, and 20° of flexion), the virtual mass of the ball is increased, the friction coefficient of the surface is decreased, and the gain of the velocity response to wrist movement is amplified. These changes make the ball more difficult to control, requiring the subject to make smaller, more precise corrections to keep it within the target zone. The task is designed to be self-paced, with each trial lasting up to 60 seconds, and the subject is allowed to take a two-minute break after every 30 trials to prevent fatigue. The training session is limited to a maximum of 90 trials or 45 minutes, ensuring consistency across participants while allowing sufficient time for learning to occur.

The training protocol is intentionally designed to emphasize proprioceptive feedback over visual feedback. Although visual cues are available during training, the nature of the task—requiring continuous, millisecond-scale corrections to a rapidly moving object—forces the subject to rely on internal estimates of limb position rather than visual tracking. This is supported by prior research demonstrating that tasks involving rapid, small-amplitude corrections selectively engage proprioceptive pathways and suppress reliance on visual feedback. The adaptive nature of the task ensures that each subject is challenged at their individual performance threshold, preventing ceiling or floor effects and maximizing the potential for learning. The cumulative spatial error (CSE) and movement time (MT) are recorded for each trial as objective indices of motor learning, with CSE representing the total lateral deviation of the wrist from the neutral position over the course of the trial and MT representing the time taken to complete the trial successfully. Both metrics show a consistent decline across trials, indicating that the subject is learning to execute the task with greater efficiency and precision. Importantly, this learning is task-specific and does not require explicit instruction or reinforcement, suggesting that it arises from implicit sensorimotor adaptation rather than cognitive strategy.

Following the training session, the subject undergoes a series of assessments to quantify changes in proprioceptive acuity and motor performance in both the trained and untrained limbs. These assessments are conducted under conditions of complete sensory deprivation: the subject wears opaque goggles that block all visual input and listens to white noise through headphones to eliminate auditory cues. The robotic exoskeleton is then used to administer two distinct psychophysical tests: one for proprioceptive acuity and one for motor performance. The proprioceptive acuity test employs a forced-choice paradigm in which the subject is passively moved to two distinct joint positions in succession: a standard position (15° of flexion) and a comparison position (slightly greater than 15°). The order of presentation is randomized, and the subject is instructed to verbally indicate which position was farther from the neutral starting position. The angular difference between the standard and comparison positions is adjusted in real time using a psi-marginal algorithm, which adapts the stimulus difference based on the subject’s prior responses to efficiently converge on the Just-Noticeable Difference (JND) threshold. The JND is defined as the smallest angular difference that the subject can reliably discriminate with a probability of 75% correct responses. This threshold is calculated by fitting the subject’s responses to a logistic Weibull function, which models the relationship between stimulus difference and response probability, while accounting for potential lapses in attention or response errors. The resulting JND value serves as a quantitative index of proprioceptive acuity, with lower values indicating greater sensitivity to joint position changes.

The motor performance test is a discrete, vision-deprived pointing task designed to assess the subject’s ability to reproduce a target joint position without visual feedback. In this task, the robotic device first passively moves the wrist to the target position (15° of flexion), holds it for two seconds to allow the subject to form a proprioceptive memory of the position, and then returns the wrist to the neutral position. The subject is then instructed to actively move the wrist to the perceived target position and hold it there for two seconds. The robotic device records the final angular position of the wrist during this hold period, and the absolute angular error between the target and the achieved position is calculated. This procedure is repeated for ten trials per limb, with a 10-second rest interval between trials to prevent fatigue. The mean absolute error across all trials is computed as the Movement Accuracy Error (MAE), which serves as a quantitative measure of motor performance. Lower MAE values indicate greater accuracy in reproducing the target position, reflecting improved internal representation of limb state. Importantly, no feedback is provided during this task to prevent learning from occurring during the assessment itself, ensuring that the MAE reflects the subject’s pre-existing sensorimotor state at the time of testing.

All assessments are conducted in a standardized sequence: baseline measurements are obtained on day one, training is administered on day two, and post-training and retention assessments are conducted immediately after training and again 24 hours later, respectively. This design allows for the separation of immediate training effects from consolidated learning and enables the detection of any decay or persistence of the observed improvements. The data collected from each assessment are analyzed using parametric statistical methods, including paired t-tests to compare pretest, posttest, and retention values, and multiple linear regression to determine whether the magnitude of learning in the trained limb predicts the magnitude of transfer to the untrained limb. The results demonstrate a significant reduction in JND and MAE in the untrained limb following training of the contralateral limb, with the proprioceptive improvement being immediate and robust, while the motor improvement shows a delayed but persistent pattern, becoming significant only at the 24-hour retention assessment. This temporal dissociation suggests that proprioceptive learning and motor learning are mediated by distinct neural mechanisms, with proprioceptive changes occurring rapidly through short-term plasticity in somatosensory networks and motor changes requiring time-dependent consolidation processes involving interhemispheric communication and synaptic stabilization.

The invention further includes a software system that automates the entire protocol, from stimulus delivery and data acquisition to statistical analysis and report generation. The software is designed to be user-friendly and requires minimal operator training, making it suitable for deployment in clinical settings. The system logs all kinematic data, timestamps all events, and generates a comprehensive report that includes the JND and MAE values for each limb at each time point, statistical comparisons between conditions, effect sizes, and graphical representations of learning trajectories. The software also includes built-in quality control features, such as automatic detection of outliers, validation of baseline stability, and alerts for protocol deviations, ensuring the integrity of the data collected. The system can be integrated with electronic health records and tele-rehabilitation platforms, enabling remote monitoring and longitudinal tracking of patient progress. In clinical applications, the invention is particularly valuable for patients with unilateral neurological impairments, such as stroke survivors with hemiparesis. In these cases, the unaffected limb is trained using the protocol, and the resulting improvements in proprioceptive acuity and motor performance are measured in the affected limb. The non-invasive, low-effort nature of the intervention makes it ideal for use in the acute and subacute phases of recovery, when patients may be unable to tolerate prolonged or intensive therapy. The fact that improvements are retained for at least 24 hours suggests that repeated sessions could lead to cumulative gains, offering a scalable and sustainable approach to rehabilitation.

The neural mechanisms underlying the observed contralateral transfer are consistent with current models of sensorimotor integration and interhemispheric communication. The primary somatosensory cortex (S1) and secondary somatosensory cortex (S2) receive bilateral input from the body, and the corpus callosum provides direct anatomical pathways between homologous regions of the left and right hemispheres. Training the right wrist likely induces plastic changes in the left S1 and S2, which are then transmitted via callosal fibers to the right hemisphere, enhancing the neural representation of the left wrist. This enhanced representation may then influence motor output through projections from S1 to the primary motor cortex (M1), premotor cortex, and supplementary motor area, leading to improved motor performance. The delayed nature of the motor transfer suggests that this process involves synaptic consolidation, possibly mediated by long-term potentiation and structural changes in white matter tracts, whereas the immediate sensory transfer reflects transient increases in neural excitability or gain modulation within existing circuits. The fact that baseline performance in both limbs predicts the magnitude of transfer further supports the idea that the transfer is not random but is constrained by the initial state of the sensorimotor system, suggesting that individuals with higher baseline proprioceptive acuity or greater interhemispheric connectivity may benefit more from the intervention.

The invention is distinguished from prior art in several key ways. First, it is the first method to demonstrate contralateral transfer of proprioceptive learning, a phenomenon that has been assumed but never empirically verified. Second, it uses a unified robotic platform to deliver both training and assessment, eliminating variability introduced by using different devices or paradigms. Third, it employs a psychophysical gold standard (JND) to quantify proprioceptive acuity, rather than relying on indirect behavioral proxies. Fourth, it includes a retention assessment to distinguish transient effects from consolidated learning. Fifth, it demonstrates that the magnitude of transfer is predictable based on learning in the trained limb, providing a mechanistic link between the two limbs. Sixth, it is applicable to a wide range of joints, not just the wrist, and can be adapted for the ankle, elbow, or knee with minimal modification. Seventh, it requires no pharmacological intervention, invasive procedures, or specialized personnel, making it highly scalable and cost-effective. Finally, it is grounded in a clear theoretical framework linking sensory and motor learning through bilateral cortical plasticity, providing a strong scientific foundation for clinical translation.

The method described herein is not limited to healthy individuals and is particularly suited for use in clinical populations with unilateral sensorimotor deficits. In patients with stroke, for example, the unaffected limb can be trained to induce improvements in the affected limb, bypassing the limitations of direct training on the impaired side. In patients with Parkinson’s disease, where proprioceptive deficits contribute to postural instability and freezing of gait, training the less affected limb may improve balance and gait symmetry. In patients with peripheral neuropathy, where sensory loss is progressive, repeated sessions of contralateral training may help maintain or even restore proprioceptive function in the affected limb. The invention can also be used in sports medicine to enhance bilateral coordination in athletes, in occupational therapy to improve fine motor control in workers with repetitive strain injuries, and in geriatric rehabilitation to counteract age-related declines in proprioception. The system is portable, easy to use, and requires minimal space, making it suitable for home-based use with remote monitoring by clinicians. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in rehabilitation, moving from unilateral, impairment-focused interventions to bilateral, plasticity-driven strategies that harness the inherent symmetry and interconnectedness of the human sensorimotor system.

### Examples

In a first example, a 58-year-old male patient with a right-hemispheric stroke resulting in left-sided hemiparesis and severe proprioceptive deficits in the left wrist is enrolled in a rehabilitation program. The patient is unable to voluntarily move the left wrist beyond 10° of flexion and cannot accurately perceive joint position without visual cues. A baseline assessment is performed using the robotic exoskeleton system, revealing a JND of 3.2° and an MAE of 5.8° on the left wrist, indicating profound proprioceptive and motor impairment. On the right wrist, the JND is 1.1° and the MAE is 1.4°, indicating intact sensorimotor function. The patient then undergoes a single 40-minute training session in which the right wrist is trained using the visuomotor task described above. The task is adapted to the patient’s capabilities by reducing the initial difficulty level and allowing more time per trial. After training, the patient immediately undergoes posttest assessment on the left wrist. The JND on the left wrist decreases to 2.1°, representing a 34% improvement, and the MAE decreases to 3.9°, representing a 33% improvement. These changes are statistically significant (p < 0.01) and occur without any direct training on the affected limb. A retention assessment is conducted 24 hours later, and the JND remains at 2.3° (a 28% improvement from baseline), while the MAE remains at 4.1° (a 30% improvement). The patient reports improved awareness of wrist position during daily activities, such as holding a cup or turning a doorknob, and demonstrates greater stability during seated tasks. The patient undergoes five additional training sessions over two weeks, with each session followed by assessment of the left wrist. Each session results in incremental improvements in JND and MAE, with cumulative gains of 52% and 48%, respectively, by the end of the program. The patient is discharged with a home-based protocol using a simplified version of the robotic device, and follow-up assessments at six weeks show sustained improvements in proprioceptive acuity and motor control, with no regression to baseline levels.

In a second example, a 32-year-old professional pianist with a history of repetitive strain injury in the right wrist undergoes the training protocol as part of a performance enhancement program. The pianist has normal proprioceptive acuity on both wrists, with a baseline JND of 0.9° and MAE of 1.2° on the right wrist and 0.8° and 1.1° on the left wrist. The training session is performed on the right wrist, which is the dominant limb used for fine motor control. After training, the JND on the left wrist decreases to 0.6° (a 25% improvement), and the MAE decreases to 0.8° (a 27% improvement). These changes are statistically significant (p < 0.05) and occur without any direct training on the left hand. The pianist reports improved finger independence and reduced tension during complex passages, particularly those requiring rapid alternation between hands. A retention assessment 24 hours later confirms that the improvements are maintained, with JND at 0.65° and MAE at 0.85°. The pianist continues to use the protocol once per week as part of a maintenance regimen and reports sustained benefits in performance quality and reduced fatigue during extended practice sessions.

In a third example, a 65-year-old female with idiopathic Parkinson’s disease and bilateral but asymmetric proprioceptive deficits is enrolled in a clinical trial. The right wrist has a baseline JND of 2.5° and MAE of 4.1°, while the left wrist has a JND of 3.0° and MAE of 4.8°. The training protocol is administered on the right wrist, which is less affected. After a single session, the JND on the left wrist decreases to 2.2° (a 27% improvement), and the MAE decreases to 3.9° (a 19% improvement). The changes are statistically significant (p < 0.05), and the patient reports improved balance during standing and reduced freezing episodes during turning. A retention assessment 24 hours later shows that the JND remains at 2.3° and the MAE at 4.0°, indicating consolidation of the effect. The patient undergoes three training sessions per week for four weeks, and by the end of the program, the JND on the left wrist has improved by 41% and the MAE by 36%. The patient’s gait velocity and stride symmetry improve significantly, and her scores on the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) show marked improvement in the subscale for proprioceptive awareness and postural control. The patient continues to use the system at home and reports sustained benefits six months after the end of the trial.

In a fourth example, a healthy 22-year-old male athlete with no neurological history undergoes the training protocol as part of a research study. The training is performed on the right wrist, and the JND and MAE are measured bilaterally. The baseline JND is 1.0° on the right and 0.9° on the left, and the MAE is 1.3° and 1.2°, respectively. After training, the JND on the left wrist decreases to 0.6° (a 33% improvement), and the MAE decreases to 0.9° (a 25% improvement). The changes are statistically significant (p < 0.01), and the athlete reports improved coordination during unilateral tasks, such as throwing a ball with the left hand. A retention assessment 24 hours later confirms that the improvements are maintained. The athlete undergoes a second training session one week later, and the JND on the left wrist further decreases to 0.5°, indicating cumulative learning. These results demonstrate that the method is not only effective in clinical populations but also enhances sensorimotor performance in healthy individuals, suggesting broad applicability across domains.

In a fifth example, the system is integrated into a tele-rehabilitation platform, allowing patients to perform the training at home under remote supervision. The robotic device is connected to a secure cloud-based server that transmits real-time data to the clinician’s dashboard, including JND and MAE values, training completion rates, and adherence metrics. The software automatically adjusts the difficulty of the training task based on the patient’s performance, ensuring optimal challenge. The clinician receives alerts if the patient’s performance declines or if there are signs of fatigue or non-compliance. Patients are instructed to perform one session per day, five days per week, for four weeks. Data from 50 patients with stroke are analyzed, and the average improvement in JND on the affected limb is 38%, with 76% of patients showing clinically meaningful gains (defined as >20% improvement). The average improvement in MAE is 32%, with 71% of patients showing clinically meaningful gains. The system is found to be highly acceptable to patients, with 94% reporting ease of use and 89% expressing willingness to continue use after the trial. These results demonstrate the scalability, feasibility, and clinical impact of the invention in real-world settings.
