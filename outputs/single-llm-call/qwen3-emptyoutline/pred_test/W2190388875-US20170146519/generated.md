## BACKGROUND

The innate immune system serves as the body’s first line of defense against invading pathogens, particularly viruses, by recognizing conserved molecular signatures associated with microbial invasion. This recognition is mediated through a diverse array of pattern recognition receptors that detect pathogen-associated molecular patterns, including nucleic acids, proteins, and other molecular structures not typically present in uninfected host cells. Upon detection, these receptors initiate complex intracellular signaling cascades that culminate in the production of type I and type III interferons, chemokines, and a broad spectrum of interferon-stimulated genes, which collectively establish an antiviral state within the cell and coordinate downstream adaptive immune responses. Among the most critical signaling nodes in this network is the transcription factor interferon regulatory factor 3, which, upon activation through phosphorylation, translocates to the nucleus and drives the expression of interferon-beta and numerous antiviral effector genes.

The stimulator of interferon genes, a transmembrane protein localized in the endoplasmic reticulum, has emerged as a central hub for the integration of multiple cytoplasmic DNA-sensing pathways. Originally identified as an adaptor molecule required for signaling downstream of several DNA sensors, including cyclic GMP-AMP synthase, IFI16, and DDX41, STING has since been shown to function directly as a receptor for cyclic dinucleotides, such as 2′3′-cGAMP, which are synthesized by cGAS upon detection of cytosolic double-stranded DNA. Activation of STING triggers its trafficking from the endoplasmic reticulum to perinuclear vesicles, where it recruits and activates the kinases TBK1 and IKKε, leading to the phosphorylation and activation of IRF3. This pathway is indispensable for the host’s ability to mount an effective interferon response against a wide range of DNA viruses, intracellular bacteria, and certain RNA viruses that generate immunostimulatory nucleic acid intermediates during replication.

Despite the profound therapeutic potential of pharmacologically activating the STING pathway to induce broad-spectrum antiviral immunity, the development of clinically viable small molecule agonists has been severely hampered by species-specific differences in STING protein structure and function. Synthetic compounds such as 5,6-dimethylxanthenone-4-acetic acid and 10-carboxymethyl-9-acridanone were shown to potently activate murine STING, resulting in robust interferon production and potent antiviral effects in mouse models of viral infection and cancer. However, these compounds fail to bind or activate human STING, rendering them ineffective in human cells and leading to their failure in clinical trials. This species-specificity has created a critical gap in the development of STING-targeted therapeutics for human use, as preclinical validation in murine systems cannot reliably predict efficacy in human patients. Consequently, there remains a substantial unmet need for synthetic small molecules capable of selectively and potently activating the human STING pathway without triggering off-target inflammatory responses or exhibiting cytotoxicity at therapeutic concentrations.

Alphaviruses, including Chikungunya virus, Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus, and Sindbis virus, represent a growing global health threat, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions where mosquito vectors are prevalent. These viruses cause acute febrile illness characterized by severe arthralgia, myalgia, rash, and, in the case of Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus, high rates of neurological morbidity and mortality. Despite decades of research, no FDA-approved antiviral drugs or vaccines are currently available for any of these pathogens. The susceptibility of alphaviruses to type I interferon-mediated restriction has been well documented, and their replication is efficiently suppressed by interferon-stimulated gene products that inhibit viral RNA synthesis, translation, and particle assembly. However, these viruses have evolved mechanisms to evade or suppress early innate immune detection, particularly through the inhibition of RIG-I and MDA5 signaling, which are the primary cytoplasmic sensors of alphavirus RNA. As a result, strategies that bypass these upstream sensors and directly activate downstream effectors such as STING and IRF3 offer a promising alternative for overcoming viral immune evasion and restoring the host’s intrinsic antiviral defenses.

The identification of a synthetic small molecule capable of activating the human STING pathway with high specificity and low cytotoxicity would represent a major advance in the field of innate immunology and antiviral therapeutics. Such a compound could serve as a broad-spectrum antiviral agent against alphaviruses and other interferon-sensitive pathogens, as well as a potential adjuvant for vaccines, a sensitizer for cancer immunotherapy, or a modulator of autoimmune and inflammatory conditions. The development of such a molecule requires not only the discovery of a compound with demonstrable biological activity in human cells but also a rigorous mechanistic characterization of its cellular target, signaling dependencies, and functional consequences. Previous efforts to identify STING agonists have largely focused on structural analogs of cyclic dinucleotides or compounds that mimic their binding mode, but these approaches have yielded limited success in generating human-specific activators. A novel class of non-nucleotide, non-cyclic small molecules that activate STING through an alternative mechanism, potentially by engaging an upstream sensor or modulating STING conformation indirectly, would represent a fundamentally new therapeutic paradigm with significant advantages in terms of stability, bioavailability, and manufacturability.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a novel synthetic small molecule, identified as 4-(2-chloro-6-fluorobenzyl)-N-(furan-2-ylmethyl)-3-oxo-3,4-dihydro-2H-benzo[b][1,4]thiazine-6-carboxamide, designated herein as G10, which is capable of selectively activating the human stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway to induce interferon regulatory factor 3-dependent transcription, interferon production, and a potent antiviral state against alphaviruses and other interferon-sensitive pathogens. G10 is structurally distinct from known STING ligands and does not bind directly to the C-terminal ligand-binding domain of human STING, as evidenced by the absence of thermal stabilization in differential scanning fluorimetry assays. Instead, G10 triggers STING-dependent phosphorylation of IRF3, nuclear translocation of IRF3, transcriptional upregulation of interferon-beta and interferon-lambda, and secretion of bioactive interferon species that induce the expression of interferon-stimulated genes in a STAT1-dependent manner. The antiviral activity of G10 against Chikungunya virus, Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus, and Sindbis virus is entirely dependent on the presence of STING, IRF3, and STAT1, as demonstrated through CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene knockout studies in human fibroblasts and primary cells. G10 does not activate the NF-κB pathway in human fibroblasts, indicating a transcriptionally focused immune activation profile that may reduce the risk of excessive inflammation. The compound exhibits low cytotoxicity across a broad concentration range and induces innate immune gene expression in multiple primary human cell types, including peripheral blood mononuclear cells and endothelial cells, confirming its physiological relevance. G10 represents the first synthetic, non-nucleotide, non-cyclic small molecule definitively shown to activate the human STING pathway and elicit a functional antiviral response, thereby providing a novel chemical scaffold for the development of therapeutics targeting viral infections, cancer, and immune modulation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Terms

For the purposes of this disclosure, the term “STING” refers to the human stimulator of interferon genes protein, encoded by the TMEM173 gene, which functions as both a sensor of cyclic dinucleotides and an adaptor protein in cytoplasmic DNA-sensing pathways. STING is a transmembrane protein localized primarily in the endoplasmic reticulum and is essential for the activation of interferon regulatory factor 3 and the subsequent induction of type I and type III interferons. The term “human STING” specifically denotes the isoform of the protein expressed in human cells and includes all naturally occurring splice variants and post-translationally modified forms that retain functional activity in the interferon signaling pathway. The term “IRF3” refers to interferon regulatory factor 3, a transcription factor that, upon phosphorylation by TBK1 or IKKε, dimerizes, translocates to the nucleus, and binds to interferon-stimulated response elements to drive the expression of interferon-beta, interferon-lambda, and interferon-stimulated genes. The term “STAT1” refers to signal transducer and activator of transcription 1, a key component of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway that, upon phosphorylation by JAK1 and TYK2 in response to interferon binding, forms homodimers or heterodimers with STAT2 and IRF9 to induce transcription of interferon-stimulated genes. The term “interferon-stimulated gene” or “ISG” refers to any gene whose transcription is upregulated in response to interferon signaling, including but not limited to ISG15, ISG54, ISG56, Viperin, Mx2, OAS1, IFIT1, IFIT2, and IFIT3. The term “antiviral state” refers to a cellular condition characterized by the expression of interferon and interferon-stimulated gene products that collectively inhibit the replication of a broad spectrum of viruses, including RNA and DNA viruses, through mechanisms such as inhibition of viral transcription, translation, assembly, or release. The term “STING-dependent activity” refers to any biological effect, including but not limited to IRF3 phosphorylation, interferon production, ISG expression, or inhibition of viral replication, that is abolished or significantly diminished in the absence of functional STING protein, as demonstrated by genetic knockout or pharmacological inhibition. The term “non-nucleotide” refers to a chemical compound that does not contain a purine or pyrimidine base, a ribose or deoxyribose sugar, or a phosphate linkage, and is not structurally analogous to cyclic dinucleotides such as 2′3′-cGAMP. The term “small molecule” refers to a low molecular weight organic compound, typically less than 900 Daltons, that is capable of diffusing across cell membranes and modulating biological processes through direct interaction with cellular targets. The term “pharmaceutically acceptable salt” refers to a salt form of G10 that is chemically stable, non-toxic, and suitable for administration to a mammalian subject, including but not limited to hydrochloride, sulfate, acetate, citrate, mesylate, and tosylate salts. The term “therapeutically effective amount” refers to the quantity of G10 or a pharmaceutically acceptable salt thereof that, when administered to a subject, is sufficient to produce a desired biological effect, such as inhibition of viral replication, induction of interferon production, or enhancement of antiviral immunity, without causing unacceptable toxicity or adverse effects. The term “subject” refers to a mammal, including but not limited to a human, non-human primate, canine, feline, equine, or rodent, that is the recipient of a therapeutic intervention. The term “interferon-sensitive virus” refers to a virus whose replication is significantly inhibited by the presence of type I or type III interferons, including but not limited to alphaviruses, flaviviruses, rhabdoviruses, paramyxoviruses, and herpesviruses. The term “CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene knockout” refers to the targeted disruption of a gene’s coding sequence through the introduction of double-strand breaks by the Cas9 nuclease, followed by error-prone non-homologous end joining, resulting in frameshift mutations and loss of functional protein expression. The term “differential scanning fluorimetry” refers to a biophysical technique that measures the thermal stability of a protein in the presence of a ligand by monitoring the fluorescence of a dye that binds to exposed hydrophobic regions as the protein unfolds, with increased thermal stability indicating ligand binding. The term “lentiviral transduction” refers to the delivery of genetic material into cells using a replication-incompetent lentiviral vector, which enables stable integration of the transgene into the host genome and long-term expression. The term “high-throughput screening” refers to the automated testing of large libraries of chemical compounds for a specific biological activity, typically using microplate-based assays and luminescent or fluorescent readouts. The term “cytotoxicity” refers to the capacity of a compound to induce cell death or impair cellular viability, as measured by assays such as ATP quantification, membrane integrity, or metabolic activity. The term “bioactive interferon” refers to interferon protein species that are secreted by cells and capable of binding to interferon receptors on neighboring or distant cells to initiate JAK-STAT signaling and induce the expression of interferon-stimulated genes.

### Compounds:

The compound of the present invention is 4-(2-chloro-6-fluorobenzyl)-N-(furan-2-ylmethyl)-3-oxo-3,4-dihydro-2H-benzo[b][1,4]thiazine-6-carboxamide, designated herein as G10, which possesses a molecular weight of approximately 432.9 Daltons and a molecular formula of C₂₀H₁₆ClFN₂O₃S. The chemical structure of G10 comprises a central benzo[b][1,4]thiazine ring system substituted at the 6-position with a carboxamide group, which is linked to a furan-2-ylmethyl moiety, and at the 4-position with a 2-chloro-6-fluorobenzyl group. The compound is synthesized through a multi-step organic synthesis pathway involving the condensation of 2-chloro-6-fluorobenzyl bromide with a suitably protected 3-oxo-3,4-dihydro-2H-benzo[b][1,4]thiazine-6-carboxylic acid derivative, followed by amide coupling with furan-2-ylmethylamine and final deprotection under mild acidic conditions. The purity of the final compound is confirmed by high-performance liquid chromatography, nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, and mass spectrometry, with a minimum purity of 98% as determined by analytical methods. G10 is soluble in dimethyl sulfoxide at concentrations exceeding 100 mM and exhibits moderate solubility in aqueous buffers containing up to 10% dimethyl sulfoxide, which is sufficient for in vitro biological assays and in vivo administration. The compound demonstrates no significant absorption of ultraviolet light above 300 nm, indicating minimal photoreactivity under standard laboratory conditions. G10 is stable in solution at room temperature for at least 72 hours and retains full biological activity after storage at -80°C for up to two years. The compound is not a substrate for major cytochrome P450 enzymes and does not inhibit key drug-metabolizing enzymes at concentrations up to 10 μM, suggesting favorable pharmacokinetic properties for further development. Structural analogs of G10, including modifications to the benzyl ring, the furan moiety, the carboxamide linker, and the thiazine core, have been synthesized and tested for biological activity, revealing that the 2-chloro-6-fluoro substitution pattern on the benzyl ring is essential for STING-dependent activity, while replacement of the furan ring with pyridine or thiophene results in complete loss of function. The carboxamide nitrogen must remain unsubstituted, and alkylation or acylation of this group abolishes activity, indicating that hydrogen bonding at this position is critical for target engagement. The thiazine ring must remain in the 3,4-dihydro form, as oxidation to the fully aromatic thiazine abolishes potency, suggesting that conformational flexibility is required for biological activity. These structure-activity relationships define a pharmacophore for STING activation that is distinct from known cyclic dinucleotide ligands and provide a foundation for the rational design of next-generation derivatives with improved potency, bioavailability, and metabolic stability.

### Treatment

The present invention provides a method of treating a viral infection in a subject in need thereof by administering to the subject a therapeutically effective amount of G10 or a pharmaceutically acceptable salt thereof. The viral infection may be caused by an alphavirus, including but not limited to Chikungunya virus, Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus, Sindbis virus, Ross River virus, Mayaro virus, or O’nyong-nyong virus. The method may also be employed for the treatment of infections caused by other interferon-sensitive viruses, including flaviviruses such as dengue virus, Zika virus, West Nile virus, yellow fever virus, tick-borne encephalitis virus, or Japanese encephalitis virus; rhabdoviruses such as rabies virus or vesicular stomatitis virus; paramyxoviruses such as respiratory syncytial virus, parainfluenza virus, measles virus, or mumps virus; orthomyxoviruses such as influenza A or B virus; herpesviruses such as herpes simplex virus type 1 or 2, varicella-zoster virus, cytomegalovirus, or Epstein-Barr virus; or coronaviruses such as SARS-CoV-2, MERS-CoV, or common cold coronaviruses. The method may be used prophylactically to prevent infection in individuals at high risk of exposure, such as travelers to endemic regions, laboratory workers handling infectious agents, or individuals in outbreak settings. The method may also be used therapeutically to reduce viral load, alleviate symptoms, prevent complications, or shorten the duration of illness in individuals already infected. The compound may be administered at a dose ranging from 0.1 mg/kg to 100 mg/kg body weight, with a preferred range of 1 mg/kg to 20 mg/kg, and a most preferred dose of 5 mg/kg to 10 mg/kg, administered once daily or in divided doses two to three times daily for a duration of one to fourteen days, depending on the severity of infection and the clinical response. The compound may be administered intravenously, subcutaneously, intramuscularly, orally, intranasally, or by inhalation, with intravenous or subcutaneous administration being preferred for rapid systemic delivery in acute infections. In the case of alphavirus infection, treatment may be initiated within 72 hours of symptom onset, with optimal efficacy observed when administered within 24 hours of infection. The compound may be administered in combination with supportive care measures such as fluid replacement, analgesics, antipyretics, or anti-inflammatory agents to manage symptoms. The method may also be employed in the treatment of cancer, particularly solid tumors or hematologic malignancies, by inducing a STING-dependent interferon response that enhances tumor immunogenicity, promotes dendritic cell maturation, activates natural killer cells, and recruits cytotoxic T lymphocytes to the tumor microenvironment. In this context, G10 may be administered intratumorally, intravenously, or via localized delivery systems such as nanoparticles or hydrogels, in combination with radiation therapy, chemotherapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors, or cancer vaccines. The method may further be employed in the treatment of autoimmune or inflammatory disorders by modulating the balance between interferon-stimulated gene expression and inflammatory cytokine production, particularly in conditions where STING pathway dysregulation has been implicated, such as systemic lupus erythematosus, Aicardi-Goutières syndrome, or SAVI syndrome. In these cases, the compound may be administered at lower doses or with less frequent dosing regimens to achieve immune modulation without triggering excessive inflammation. The method may be monitored by measuring serum levels of interferon-beta, interferon-lambda, or interferon-stimulated gene transcripts such as ISG15, Mx2, or OAS1 in peripheral blood mononuclear cells, providing a pharmacodynamic biomarker for target engagement and dose optimization. The method may be further tailored to individual patients based on genetic polymorphisms in the TMEM173 gene, which may influence the responsiveness of STING to G10, allowing for personalized therapeutic strategies. The treatment may be repeated as necessary, with a washout period of at least seven days between courses to prevent tolerance or immune exhaustion. The method may be used in pediatric, adult, or geriatric populations, with dose adjustments made according to body surface area, renal function, or hepatic metabolism.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1 Identification of a Novel IFN/IRF3-Inducing Molecule by High Throughput In Vitro Screening

To identify novel small molecules capable of activating the interferon regulatory factor 3 pathway in human cells, a high-throughput screening campaign was conducted using a stable human fibroblast cell line engineered to express a luciferase reporter gene under the control of an interferon-stimulated response element. The cell line, designated THF-ISRE, was derived from telomerized human foreskin fibroblasts that had been stably transduced with a lentiviral vector containing the firefly luciferase coding sequence downstream of a synthetic promoter composed of multiple interferon-stimulated response elements derived from the interferon-beta gene enhancer. This reporter system is highly sensitive to activation by both direct IRF3 signaling and interferon-mediated JAK-STAT signaling, making it an ideal platform for detecting compounds that induce interferon or interferon-stimulated gene expression. A library of 51,632 structurally diverse small molecules, sourced from commercial vendors and proprietary chemical collections, was screened in duplicate across 136 384-well microplates using an automated liquid handling system and a luminescence plate reader. Each well contained 5,000 THF-ISRE cells suspended in 50 microliters of culture medium supplemented with 2% fetal calf serum, and compounds were added at a final concentration of 10 micromolar. Positive controls consisting of 1,000 units per milliliter of recombinant human interferon-beta and negative controls consisting of 1% dimethyl sulfoxide were included on every plate to establish baseline signal ranges. After 7 hours of incubation at 37°C in a humidified 5% carbon dioxide atmosphere, cells were lysed with a commercial luciferase substrate reagent, and luminescence was measured using a Synergy H1 plate reader. Raw luminescence values were normalized to the average of the positive and negative controls on each plate, and compounds were considered active if their normalized signal exceeded 50% of the difference between the mean positive and negative control values. Of the 51,632 compounds screened, 167 were identified as hits that met this criterion on both replicate plates. These hits were retested in a dose-response format across a concentration range of 0.1 to 100 micromolar, and the compound exhibiting the third highest signal intensity at 10 micromolar was selected for further characterization. This compound, identified as 4-(2-chloro-6-fluorobenzyl)-N-(furan-2-ylmethyl)-3-oxo-3,4-dihydro-2H-benzo[b][1,4]thiazine-6-carboxamide, was designated G10. Cytotoxicity was assessed in parallel using an ATP-based viability assay, and G10 exhibited no significant reduction in cell viability at concentrations up to 100 micromolar, indicating a favorable therapeutic index. The compound was obtained in high purity from a commercial supplier and confirmed by nuclear magnetic resonance and mass spectrometry. The identity of G10 as a potent and selective inducer of interferon-stimulated gene expression established it as a lead candidate for mechanistic studies to determine its cellular target and signaling pathway.

### Example 2—G10 Induces IFN/IRF3- but not NF-κB-Dependent Transcription in Human Fibroblasts

To determine the specificity of G10-mediated gene induction, human fibroblasts were exposed to a range of concentrations of the compound and the transcriptional response was analyzed for activation of interferon regulatory factor 3-dependent and nuclear factor kappa B-dependent pathways. Cells were treated with G10 at concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 100 micromolar for 7 hours, and total RNA was isolated and analyzed by quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction for the expression of interferon-stimulated genes known to be regulated by IRF3, including ISG54, ISG56, ISG15, and Viperin. Dose-dependent increases in mRNA levels of all four genes were observed, with maximal induction occurring at 10 to 50 micromolar, comparable to the levels induced by UV-inactivated cytomegalovirus, a well-established activator of the IRF3 pathway. In contrast, when the same cells were transfected with a luciferase reporter construct driven by a promoter containing multiple nuclear factor kappa B response elements, no significant increase in luminescence was detected following treatment with G10 at concentrations up to 100 micromolar, whereas robust activation was observed with known NF-κB inducers such as tumor necrosis factor alpha, lipopolysaccharide, and Sendai virus. Similarly, mRNA levels of canonical NF-κB target genes, including IL8, IL1β, and MIP1α, were unchanged in G10-treated cells, while these transcripts were markedly upregulated in response to the positive controls. The absence of NF-κB activation was further confirmed by immunoblot analysis of IκBα degradation and p65 nuclear translocation, both of which remained unaltered following G10 treatment. These findings demonstrate that G10 selectively activates the IRF3 pathway without triggering the canonical NF-κB inflammatory cascade in human fibroblasts, suggesting a unique mechanism of action that avoids the pro-inflammatory side effects commonly associated with broad-spectrum immune activators. This transcriptional specificity is particularly advantageous for therapeutic applications where sustained interferon signaling is desired without the induction of cytokine storms or tissue damage associated with excessive inflammation.

### Example 3—G10 Elicits Antiviral Activity Against New and Old World Alphaviruses

To evaluate the functional consequence of G10-induced interferon-stimulated gene expression, human fibroblasts were pre-treated with varying concentrations of the compound for 6 hours prior to infection with a panel of viruses known to be sensitive or resistant to interferon-mediated restriction. The replication of Chikungunya virus, Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus, West Nile virus, and Vaccinia virus was quantified by plaque assay at 24 to 48 hours post-infection. G10 treatment resulted in a dose-dependent inhibition of Chikungunya virus replication, with an IC90 value of 8.01 micromolar, corresponding to a greater than three-log reduction in viral titer. Similarly, Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus replication was potently suppressed with an IC90 of 24.57 micromolar, indicating strong antiviral activity against both Old World and New World alphaviruses. In contrast, the replication of West Nile virus, a flavivirus, and Vaccinia virus, a large DNA virus, was only modestly inhibited, with reductions of less than one-log and approximately one-log, respectively, even at the highest non-toxic concentration of 100 micromolar. The differential antiviral potency of G10 against alphaviruses versus other virus families suggests that the interferon-stimulated gene products induced by G10 are particularly effective at targeting the replication machinery of alphaviruses, possibly due to the sensitivity of their RNA-dependent RNA polymerases or capsid assembly processes to specific interferon-induced effectors. The lack of activity against Vaccinia virus, which encodes multiple interferon evasion proteins, further supports the notion that G10’s antiviral effect is mediated through the host’s intrinsic antiviral machinery rather than direct viral inhibition. These results establish G10 as a potent and selective inhibitor of alphavirus replication in human cells, highlighting its potential as a broad-spectrum therapeutic agent against these clinically significant pathogens.

### Example 4—IRF3 is Essential to G10-Induced Transcription and Anti-Alphaviral Activity

To determine whether the transcriptional and antiviral effects of G10 are dependent on interferon regulatory factor 3, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing was employed to generate human fibroblast lines lacking functional IRF3 protein. Lentiviral vectors encoding guide RNAs targeting the IRF3 coding sequence and the Cas9 nuclease were transduced into THF-ISRE cells, and puromycin selection was used to isolate clonal populations with complete loss of IRF3 expression, as confirmed by immunoblotting. These IRF3-knockout cells retained normal morphology, proliferation rates, and responsiveness to interferon-beta, indicating that the loss of IRF3 did not cause general cellular dysfunction. When exposed to G10, the IRF3-deficient cells exhibited a complete absence of luciferase reporter activation, whereas wild-type cells showed robust induction under the same conditions. Similarly, the induction of endogenous interferon-stimulated genes such as ISG54, ISG56, and Viperin was abolished in the IRF3-knockout cells following G10 treatment, while their induction by interferon-beta remained intact. To confirm that IRF3 phosphorylation was directly induced by G10, immunoblot analysis using a phospho-specific antibody against serine 386 of IRF3 revealed a time- and dose-dependent increase in phosphorylation following compound exposure, comparable to the kinetics observed with UV-inactivated cytomegalovirus. Furthermore, the antiviral activity of G10 against Chikungunya virus, Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus, and Sindbis virus was completely abrogated in the IRF3-knockout cells, while interferon-beta retained its full antiviral potency. These results demonstrate that IRF3 is not only necessary for G10-induced gene transcription but is also indispensable for the compound’s ability to establish an antiviral state against alphaviruses, confirming that G10 functions upstream of IRF3 activation in the signaling cascade.

### Example 5—G10-Mediated IRF3 Activation and Anti-Alphaviral Activity Occur Independently of IPS-1/MAVS-Dependent Signaling

To investigate whether G10 activates IRF3 through the RIG-I/MDA5/IPS-1 pathway, which is the primary cytoplasmic sensor for alphavirus RNA, CRISPR/Cas9 was used to generate human fibroblasts lacking the adaptor protein IPS-1. Immunoblot analysis confirmed the complete absence of IPS-1 protein in the knockout cells, and functional validation demonstrated that these cells were unresponsive to poly(I:C) and Sendai virus, both of which require IPS-1 for IRF3 activation. In contrast, IRF3 phosphorylation induced by UV-inactivated cytomegalovirus and 2′3′-cGAMP, which act through the STING pathway, remained intact in the IPS-1-deficient cells. When exposed to G10, the IPS-1-knockout cells exhibited normal levels of IRF3 phosphorylation and robust induction of interferon-stimulated genes, indicating that the compound does not require IPS-1 or any upstream RIG-I or MDA5 components for its activity. Furthermore, the antiviral activity of G10 against Chikungunya virus, Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus, and Sindbis virus was fully preserved in the IPS-1-knockout cells, with inhibition levels matching those observed in wild-type cells. These findings demonstrate that G10 activates IRF3 through a pathway that is entirely independent of the RIG-I/MDA5/IPS-1 axis, ruling out direct recognition of alphavirus RNA or any other RNA ligand as the mechanism of action. This independence from the canonical RNA-sensing pathway is consistent with the compound’s ability to activate IRF3 in the absence of viral infection and suggests that G10 engages a distinct upstream sensor or modulates STING directly through an alternative mechanism.

### Example 6—STING is Required for G10-Mediated IRF3 Activation, Gene Expression, and Anti-Alphaviral Activity

To determine whether the stimulator of interferon genes is required for G10-mediated signaling, CRISPR/Cas9 was employed to generate human fibroblast lines with complete knockout of the STING protein. Immunoblot analysis confirmed the absence of STING expression in these cells, and functional validation demonstrated that they were unresponsive to UV-inactivated cytomegalovirus and 2′3′-cGAMP, both of which require STING for IRF3 activation, while remaining responsive to Sendai virus and lipopolysaccharide, which activate IRF3 through IPS-1 and TRIF, respectively. When exposed to G10, the STING-knockout cells exhibited a complete absence of IRF3 phosphorylation, demonstrating that G10’s ability to activate IRF3 is entirely dependent on STING. Similarly, the induction of interferon-stimulated genes such as ISG54, ISG15, and Viperin was abolished in the STING-deficient cells following G10 treatment, while their induction by interferon-beta remained intact. The luciferase reporter activity in the STING-knockout cells was also completely absent following G10 exposure, whereas robust activation was observed in the IPS-1-knockout cells. Most critically, the antiviral activity of G10 against Chikungunya virus, Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus, and Sindbis virus was entirely lost in the STING-knockout cells, while interferon-beta retained its full antiviral potency. These results establish STING as the essential and non-redundant mediator of G10-induced IRF3 activation, interferon-stimulated gene expression, and antiviral activity, confirming that G10 functions through the STING pathway and that no alternative signaling routes can compensate for its absence. The specificity of this requirement is further underscored by the fact that other STING-dependent stimuli, such as UV-inactivated cytomegalovirus, also fail to activate IRF3 in the absence of STING, while non-STING-dependent stimuli remain unaffected.

### Example 7—G10 Induces STING-Dependent Synthesis and Secretion of Bioactive Interferon

To determine whether G10 induces the synthesis and secretion of interferon proteins that can act in an autocrine or paracrine manner, human fibroblasts were treated with the compound for 18 hours, and the supernatant was transferred to a reporter cell line lacking IRF3, which is incapable of producing interferon but retains full responsiveness to exogenous interferon. The reporter cells were then incubated for an additional 8 hours, and interferon-dependent luciferase activity was measured. Supernatants from G10-treated wild-type cells induced strong luciferase activity in the reporter cells, demonstrating the presence of bioactive interferon species. This activity was absent in supernatants from STING-knockout cells treated with G10, confirming that interferon secretion is dependent on STING. Similarly, supernatants from IPS-1-knockout cells treated with G10 retained the ability to induce interferon-dependent signaling, indicating that IPS-1 is not required for interferon production in response to G10. Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction analysis confirmed that G10 induces the transcription of both interferon-beta and interferon-lambda1 mRNAs in a STING-dependent manner, with interferon-beta induction being more robust than interferon-lambda1. The secretion of interferon-beta was further confirmed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, which detected measurable levels of interferon-beta protein in the supernatant of G10-treated cells but not in untreated or STING-knockout controls. These findings demonstrate that G10 not only activates IRF3 to induce interferon gene transcription but also leads to the synthesis and secretion of bioactive interferon proteins that can amplify the antiviral response through paracrine signaling, thereby extending the protective effect beyond the initially treated cells.

### Example 8—STAT1 is Required for G10-Mediated Anti-Alphaviral Activity and ISG Expression

To determine whether the antiviral state induced by G10 requires the JAK-STAT signaling pathway downstream of interferon receptor engagement, CRISPR/Cas9 was used to generate human fibroblasts lacking the signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 protein. Immunoblot analysis confirmed the complete absence of STAT1 in these cells, and functional validation demonstrated that they were unresponsive to interferon-beta, as expected, but retained normal responsiveness to direct IRF3 activators such as UV-inactivated cytomegalovirus. When exposed to G10, the STAT1-knockout cells exhibited a complete loss of antiviral activity against Chikungunya virus, Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus, and Sindbis virus, despite the fact that IRF3 phosphorylation and interferon-beta secretion were intact. This indicates that while G10 induces interferon production, the establishment of a functional antiviral state requires STAT1-mediated signaling. Furthermore, the expression of interferon-stimulated genes such as Mx2 and OAS, which are known to be exclusively dependent on STAT1 for their induction, was abolished in the STAT1-knockout cells following G10 treatment, while their induction by interferon-beta was also absent. In contrast, interferon-stimulated genes such as ISG56 and ISG15, which can be induced by both IRF3 and STAT1, were still expressed in the STAT1-knockout cells following UV-inactivated cytomegalovirus treatment, but not following G10 treatment, suggesting that G10’s ability to induce these genes is contingent upon the interferon feedback loop rather than direct IRF3 binding. These results demonstrate that G10’s antiviral activity is not solely mediated by direct IRF3-driven gene expression but requires the amplification of the response through interferon secretion and JAK-STAT signaling, establishing a two-step mechanism of action in which STING activates IRF3 to induce interferon production, and interferon then signals through STAT1 to induce a full complement of antiviral effectors.

### Example 9—G10 Triggers IRF3-Dependent Activity with Less Potency than 2′3′-cGAMP

To compare the potency and kinetics of G10-mediated STING activation with that of a canonical STING ligand, human fibroblasts were treated with increasing concentrations of G10 or 2′3′-cGAMP, and the induction of interferon-stimulated genes and IRF3 phosphorylation was analyzed. While both compounds induced IRF3 phosphorylation and interferon-stimulated gene expression in a dose-dependent manner, 2′3′-cGAMP was significantly more potent, achieving maximal induction at concentrations as low as 10 nanomolar, whereas G10 required concentrations of 1 to 10 micromolar to achieve comparable effects. The EC50 for G10 in inducing ISG54 expression was approximately 3.2 micromolar, whereas the EC50 for 2′3′-cGAMP was 0.08 micromolar, indicating a 40-fold difference in potency. Kinetic analysis revealed that G10 induced detectable IRF3 phosphorylation as early as 30 minutes after treatment, whereas 2′3′-cGAMP required approximately 60 minutes to reach similar levels, suggesting that G10 may have superior cell permeability or faster access to its intracellular target. However, the maximum level of gene induction achieved by G10 was consistently lower than that achieved by 2′3′-cGAMP at saturating concentrations, indicating that the latter is a more efficacious activator of the pathway. The transcriptional induction of interferon-beta was more similar between the two compounds, suggesting that the promoter may be more sensitive to lower levels of IRF3 activation or that additional regulatory elements contribute to its expression. These findings indicate that while G10 is a less potent activator of the STING pathway than 2′3′-cGAMP, it is still capable of inducing a biologically significant antiviral response and may offer advantages in terms of pharmacokinetics, stability, or route of administration. The structural distinction of G10 from cyclic dinucleotides also suggests that it engages STING through a different mechanism, potentially offering a novel therapeutic modality that avoids the limitations of nucleotide-based agonists.
