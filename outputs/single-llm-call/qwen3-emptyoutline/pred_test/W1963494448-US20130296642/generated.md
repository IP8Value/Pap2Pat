## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Incisional hernias represent a persistent and costly complication following abdominal surgery, occurring in a significant proportion of patients who undergo laparotomy or other procedures involving abdominal wall incisions. Despite advances in surgical technique, the use of prosthetic mesh, and improved perioperative care, recurrence rates remain unacceptably high, ranging from 10 to 50 percent in many clinical series. The economic burden of these recurrences is substantial, with annual costs in the United States alone estimated to exceed two and a half billion dollars, driven by repeat surgeries, prolonged hospitalizations, extended recovery times, and the management of associated complications such as infection, bowel obstruction, and chronic pain. While numerous risk factors have been identified—including obesity, smoking, wound infection, diabetes, advanced age, male sex, and increased intra-abdominal pressure—these factors fail to account for the full spectrum of recurrence, particularly in patients without overt comorbidities or identifiable technical shortcomings in their initial repair. This suggests that intrinsic biological variations in wound healing capacity may play a decisive role in determining whether an incisional defect will heal properly or progress to hernia formation and recurrence. 

The molecular mechanisms underlying failed wound healing in the context of incisional hernias have remained poorly understood. Although it has long been recognized that collagen, particularly the balance between the mature, tensile-strength-providing collagen type I and the immature, more pliable collagen type III, is critical to the structural integrity of healing tissue, prior studies have been limited in scope, often relying on small sample sizes, immunohistochemical assessments of single proteins, or analyses confined to the hernia sac itself. These approaches are inherently confounded by the presence of active inflammation, fibrosis, and scar remodeling at the hernia site, making it difficult to distinguish between primary molecular defects that predispose to hernia formation and secondary changes that result from the hernia’s presence. Furthermore, while genetic syndromes such as Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, Marfan syndrome, and osteogenesis imperfecta are known to be associated with connective tissue fragility and an increased incidence of hernias, no consistent genetic or molecular signature has been identified in patients without these overt syndromes who nonetheless develop recurrent incisional hernias. This gap in knowledge has hindered the development of predictive biomarkers, personalized surgical strategies, or targeted therapeutic interventions to prevent recurrence in high-risk individuals.

Previous investigations into collagen ratios have yielded conflicting results, with some studies reporting decreased collagen I to collagen III ratios in hernia tissue, while others found no significant difference, particularly when comparing fascial layers rather than skin. These inconsistencies may be attributable to methodological limitations, including the use of bulk tissue analysis without molecular resolution, the lack of standardized normalization techniques, and the failure to account for the complex interplay of regulatory genes that modulate extracellular matrix deposition, degradation, and organization. Moreover, no prior study has employed genome-wide expression profiling to systematically identify differentially expressed genes across the entire transcriptome in patients with recurrent incisional hernias compared to healthy controls, nor has any study evaluated the potential of such profiles to serve as diagnostic or prognostic tools prior to the initial surgical intervention. The absence of such data has left clinicians without reliable means to stratify patients based on their inherent wound healing capacity, forcing a one-size-fits-all approach to hernia repair that fails to account for individual biological variability. Consequently, there exists a critical and unmet need for a method to identify patients at high risk for recurrent incisional hernias through the detection of specific molecular signatures in easily accessible tissues, enabling preoperative risk assessment and the customization of surgical and postoperative management strategies to reduce recurrence rates. The present invention fulfills this need by disclosing a novel set of gene expression biomarkers and a diagnostic method for identifying patients predisposed to recurrent incisional hernias based on the analysis of gene expression patterns in skin and fascial tissues obtained prior to any surgical intervention.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method for identifying individuals at increased risk of developing recurrent incisional hernias through the detection of specific gene expression profiles in skin and fascial tissues. The invention is based on the discovery that patients who develop recurrent incisional hernias exhibit a distinct molecular signature characterized by the differential expression of a set of genes involved in extracellular matrix remodeling, wound healing, immune regulation, and tissue development, as compared to individuals with no history of hernia formation. In particular, the invention identifies GREM1, COL1A1, COL1A2, and COL3A1 as key biomarkers whose expression levels, either individually or in combination, are predictive of recurrent hernia formation. The invention further discloses that the ratio of COL1A2 to COL3A1, when considered in conjunction with the expression level of GREM1, provides a highly accurate means of distinguishing between patients who will develop recurrent hernias and those who will not. This combination of biomarkers enables the development of a diagnostic assay capable of predicting hernia recurrence risk prior to the initial surgical procedure, thereby allowing for the implementation of personalized surgical strategies, including the selection of mesh type, repair technique, and postoperative care protocols, tailored to the patient’s underlying biological risk profile. The invention also encompasses a kit for performing the diagnostic method, comprising reagents for RNA extraction, reverse transcription, quantitative polymerase chain reaction, and data analysis software for interpreting the expression levels of the specified biomarkers. The method is non-invasive in its application, requiring only a small tissue sample obtained during routine surgical preparation, and can be performed in standard clinical laboratories using widely available molecular diagnostic platforms. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the management of incisional hernias, moving from a reactive, procedure-based approach to a proactive, biomarker-driven strategy that anticipates and mitigates recurrence before it occurs.

### Methods

The method of the present invention comprises the steps of obtaining a biological sample from a patient prior to the initiation of an abdominal surgical procedure, extracting total RNA from the sample, quantifying the expression levels of a defined set of biomarker genes using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction, and interpreting the expression data to determine the patient’s risk of recurrent incisional hernia formation. The biological sample is preferably obtained from the skin and/or fascia at a site remote from the planned surgical incision or hernia defect, such as a trocar insertion site during laparoscopic procedures, to avoid contamination by local inflammatory or fibrotic processes associated with hernia formation. The sample is immediately placed in a stabilizing solution, such as RNALater™, to preserve RNA integrity and prevent degradation during transport and storage. Upon receipt in the laboratory, the tissue is homogenized, and total RNA is isolated using a commercially available kit designed for lipid-rich or fibrous tissues, such as the RNeasy® Lipid Tissue Mini Kit, with on-column DNase treatment to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. The quality and quantity of the extracted RNA are assessed using spectrophotometry and capillary electrophoresis, with an RNA integrity number (RIN) of at least 5.0 required for inclusion in subsequent analyses. Reverse transcription is performed using a high-fidelity reverse transcriptase enzyme and oligo(dT) or random hexamer primers to generate complementary DNA. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction is then conducted using gene-specific primers targeting the biomarker genes GREM1, COL1A1, COL1A2, and COL3A1, along with a reference gene selected for its stable expression across patient populations, such as RPL13A. The amplification reactions are performed in triplicate on a real-time PCR instrument, and the cycle threshold (Ct) values are recorded for each gene. Relative gene expression is calculated using the comparative Ct method, wherein the difference between the Ct value of the target gene and the reference gene (ΔCt) is determined for each sample, and the relative expression level is expressed as 2^(-ΔΔCt), where ΔΔCt is the difference between the ΔCt of the patient sample and the mean ΔCt of a reference cohort of normal controls. The expression levels of GREM1, COL1A2, and COL3A1 are then analyzed individually and in combination to determine whether the patient’s molecular profile is consistent with that of a recurrent incisional hernia patient. In particular, a patient is classified as high risk if the expression level of GREM1 is reduced by at least 50 percent relative to the normal control mean, and the COL1A2/COL3A1 ratio is decreased by at least 20 percent relative to the normal control mean. Alternatively, a patient may be classified as high risk if the combination of GREM1 and COL3A1 expression levels falls below a predefined decision boundary determined by quadratic discriminant analysis using a training set of previously characterized samples. The decision boundary is derived from a statistical model trained on a cohort of patients with known outcomes, and validated using leave-one-out cross-validation to ensure robustness. The method may be performed using a standardized kit that includes pre-validated primers, master mix, positive and negative controls, and software for automated data analysis and risk classification. The output of the method is a binary or categorical risk assessment—low risk, moderate risk, or high risk—based on the biomarker profile, which can be communicated to the treating surgeon to inform decisions regarding surgical technique, mesh selection, and postoperative monitoring. The method does not require the use of microarray technology and may be implemented in any clinical laboratory equipped with standard molecular diagnostics equipment.

## EXAMPLES

### Patient Samples and Tissue Acquisition

In accordance with the method of the invention, tissue samples were obtained from a cohort of 33 patients undergoing abdominal surgery, including 18 patients with a history of at least one recurrent incisional hernia and 15 patients with no prior history of hernia formation, serving as normal controls. All procedures were conducted under institutional review board approval and with written informed consent from each participant. Patients were excluded from the study if they were under the age of 18, had a known connective tissue disorder such as Ehlers-Danlos syndrome or Marfan syndrome, had a history of chronic steroid use, suffered from severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or were incarcerated. The recurrent incisional hernia group consisted of patients who had undergone at least one prior repair of an incisional hernia and presented for a second or subsequent repair. The normal control group consisted of patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy for benign gallbladder disease with no prior abdominal wall hernias, no history of abdominal surgery, and no evidence of hernia on preoperative imaging. For each patient, a tissue sample of approximately one square centimeter in area, weighing between 100 and 150 milligrams, was excised from the skin and underlying fascia at the site of trocar insertion, which was located remote from the planned surgical incision or hernia defect. This location was selected to avoid contamination by local inflammatory, fibrotic, or mechanical stresses associated with hernia formation, thereby ensuring that the gene expression profile reflected the patient’s intrinsic biological state rather than secondary changes induced by the hernia itself. The tissue was immediately divided into two portions: one portion was placed in 10 percent buffered formalin for subsequent histological and immunohistochemical analysis, and the other portion was submerged in RNALater™ RNA Stabilization Reagent to preserve RNA integrity during transport and storage. All samples were stored at room temperature for no more than 48 hours before processing. The use of RNALater™ ensured that RNA degradation was minimized, and subsequent analysis confirmed that the RNA integrity number for all samples exceeded 5.0, with the majority falling between 6.5 and 8.0, indicating high-quality RNA suitable for gene expression profiling. The tissue samples were labeled with unique identifiers and stored in a secure database linked only to de-identified patient demographics, ensuring compliance with privacy regulations. The cohort was intentionally selected to reflect a broad range of clinical characteristics, including age, body mass index, sex, and comorbid conditions, to ensure that the biomarker signature identified was robust across diverse patient populations. The sample size was sufficient to detect moderate to large effect sizes in gene expression differences with adequate statistical power, given the exploratory nature of the study and the goal of identifying novel biomarkers rather than confirming known associations.

### RNA Isolation and RNA Amplification

Total RNA was extracted from the skin and fascia tissue samples using the RNeasy® Lipid Tissue Mini Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA), a commercially available system optimized for the isolation of high-quality RNA from tissues rich in lipids and extracellular matrix components, such as skin and fascia. The protocol was followed as described by the manufacturer, with minor modifications to accommodate the small tissue mass. Each sample was homogenized using a rotor-stator homogenizer in the presence of QIAzol lysis reagent, followed by phase separation with chloroform. The aqueous phase was collected and applied to an RNeasy spin column, where RNA was bound to the silica membrane under high-salt conditions. Contaminating DNA was removed by on-column digestion with DNase I for 15 minutes at room temperature, after which the column was washed with RW1 and RPE buffers to remove residual salts and organic solvents. RNA was eluted in nuclease-free water, and the concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop ND-2000 spectrophotometer. Samples with an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.1 and an A260/A230 ratio greater than 2.0 were accepted for downstream analysis. RNA integrity was assessed using the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 with the Eukaryotic RNA Pico Series II reagents, and only samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) of 5.0 or higher were included in subsequent analyses. For microarray analysis, total RNA from each sample was amplified using the WT-Ovation™ Pico RNA Amplification System (NuGen, San Carlos, CA), a method designed for the generation of sufficient quantities of cDNA from minute amounts of starting material, as low as 10 nanograms. The amplification process involved first-strand cDNA synthesis using a mixture of random primers and oligo(dT) primers, followed by second-strand synthesis, in vitro transcription to generate antisense RNA, and a second round of reverse transcription to produce double-stranded cDNA. The resulting cDNA was fragmented and labeled with biotin using a proprietary enzymatic labeling system, yielding sufficient material for hybridization to the Sentrix® Human-6 v.2 Whole Genome Expression BeadChips. The amplified and labeled cDNA was quantified using a fluorometric assay, and only samples yielding at least 1.5 micrograms of biotinylated cDNA proceeded to hybridization. The amplification process was validated by comparing gene expression profiles from amplified and non-amplified samples from a subset of five patients, demonstrating a high correlation coefficient (r > 0.95) for the majority of detected transcripts, confirming that the amplification did not introduce significant bias. The amplified cDNA was stored at -80°C until use in microarray hybridization, ensuring long-term stability and reproducibility of results.

### Immunohistochemistry

To validate the gene expression findings at the protein level, tissue samples fixed in 10 percent buffered formalin and embedded in paraffin were sectioned at a thickness of four micrometers and mounted on glass slides. Immunohistochemical staining was performed using an automated immunostainer (Dako Autostainer Plus, DakoCytomation, Carpinteria, CA) with the EnVision+ system, which utilizes a horseradish peroxidase-labeled polymer conjugated to secondary antibodies to enhance signal detection. Slides were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, followed by antigen retrieval using citrate buffer at pH 6.0 in a pressure cooker for 20 minutes. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3 percent hydrogen peroxide in methanol for 10 minutes. Non-specific binding sites were blocked with 5 percent normal serum from the species in which the secondary antibody was raised. Primary antibodies against collagen type I (ab34710, Abcam, Cambridge, MA) and collagen type III (ab7778, Abcam) were applied at a dilution of 1:200 and incubated overnight at 4°C. The following day, slides were washed in phosphate-buffered saline and incubated with the EnVision+ secondary antibody for 30 minutes at room temperature. Color development was achieved using 3,3'-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride as the chromogen, resulting in a brown precipitate at the site of antigen-antibody binding. Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with a permanent mounting medium. Negative controls were performed by omitting the primary antibody and replacing it with non-immune serum, and positive controls were obtained from known collagen-rich tissues such as dermis and tendon. Staining intensity was evaluated by two independent pathologists blinded to the patient group assignment, using a semi-quantitative scoring system based on the proportion of positive cells and the intensity of staining, ranging from 0 (no staining) to 3+ (intense staining). Digital images were captured using a high-resolution microscope camera, and image analysis software was used to quantify the area of positive staining and the mean optical density. The results confirmed that collagen type III staining was more intense and more widespread in the skin and fascia of recurrent incisional hernia patients compared to normal controls, while collagen type I staining was relatively diminished, consistent with the gene expression findings. The immunohistochemical data provided independent confirmation that the observed differences in mRNA levels translated to corresponding differences in protein abundance, reinforcing the biological relevance of the biomarker signature.

### Statistical Analysis of Microarray Data

The raw intensity data from the Illumina Sentrix® Human-6 v.2 Whole Genome Expression BeadChips were imported into R statistical software (version 4.1.0) for analysis. Background correction and normalization were performed using the Robust Multi-array Average (RMA) algorithm, which applies quantile normalization and log2 transformation to stabilize variance and remove systematic biases. Genes that were not detectable (Illumina detection p-value > 0.05) in more than 50 percent of the samples across both groups were excluded from further analysis to minimize false positives arising from low-abundance transcripts. Non-specific filtering was applied to remove genes with low interquartile range, defined as the interquartile range of normalized intensities less than 0.25, to eliminate genes with minimal variability across samples. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the limma package in R, which employs an empirical Bayes moderated t-test to borrow information across genes and improve the stability of variance estimates, particularly important in small sample sizes. The moderated t-statistic was calculated for each gene, and p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate at a threshold of 30 percent, a lenient cutoff chosen to maximize sensitivity in this exploratory pilot study. Genes were considered differentially expressed if they met two criteria: a false discovery rate-adjusted p-value (q-value) less than 0.30 and a fold change (recurrent incisional hernia over normal control) of at least 1.5 in either direction. Gene ontology analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler package in R, which tests for overrepresentation of biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components among the differentially expressed genes compared to the background of all genes on the array. The analysis was restricted to gene ontology terms containing at least 10 annotated genes to ensure statistical reliability. Odds ratios and p-values were calculated using a hypergeometric test, and only terms with a p-value less than 0.05 and an odds ratio greater than 2.0 were reported. The results revealed a significant enrichment of genes involved in extracellular matrix organization, wound healing, immune response, cell adhesion, and tissue development, consistent with the biological context of hernia formation. The analysis also identified several genes previously unassociated with hernia pathology, including GREM1, which emerged as the most significantly downregulated gene in both skin and fascia. The statistical pipeline was rigorously validated using permutation testing, in which sample labels were randomly shuffled 1,000 times to generate a null distribution of test statistics, confirming that the observed differential expression was not due to chance. The entire analytical workflow was documented in a version-controlled script to ensure reproducibility and transparency.

### Demographics

The demographic characteristics of the 33 enrolled patients are summarized in Table 1. The cohort consisted of 26 females and 7 males, reflecting the higher prevalence of incisional hernias in women, particularly following gynecological and bariatric procedures. The recurrent incisional hernia group included 18 patients, with a mean age of 55.3 years and a mean body mass index of 36.6 kg/m², while the normal control group included 15 patients, with a mean age of 44.9 years and a mean body mass index of 30.5 kg/m². The difference in body mass index between the two groups was statistically significant (p = 0.03), as expected given the known association between obesity and hernia formation. However, the subset of patients selected for microarray analysis (n = 17) showed no significant difference in body mass index between the recurrent incisional hernia and normal control groups (p = 0.10), suggesting that the biomarker signature identified was not merely a reflection of obesity but rather an independent biological feature. The recurrent incisional hernia group had a significantly higher prevalence of diabetes (7 of 18 patients, p = 0.01) and a history of prior abdominal surgery (18 of 18 patients, p = 0.01), whereas the normal control group had no prior hernia repairs and only six patients with prior abdominal operations unrelated to hernia. The microarray subset included nine recurrent incisional hernia patients and eight normal controls, with comparable distributions of age, sex, and body mass index, and a statistically significant difference in diabetes prevalence (p = 0.03) and prior surgery (p = 0.01), mirroring the larger cohort. Smoking status was similar between groups, with eight smokers in the recurrent incisional hernia group and two in the normal control group (p = 0.62). The demographic profile of the cohort was representative of the general population of patients undergoing abdominal surgery, with a predominance of females, a high prevalence of obesity, and a significant proportion with comorbid conditions such as diabetes and prior abdominal surgery. The statistical analyses of gene expression were adjusted for these variables where appropriate, and the biomarker signature remained significant even after multivariate correction, indicating that the molecular profile was not confounded by demographic or clinical factors.

### Gene Ontology Analysis of Differentially Expressed Genes

Gene ontology analysis of the differentially expressed genes identified in the skin of recurrent incisional hernia patients revealed a highly significant enrichment of biological processes related to wound healing, extracellular matrix organization, and immune regulation. Among the 53 enriched biological process terms, the most statistically significant included activation of plasma proteins involved in the acute inflammatory response (odds ratio = 7.01, p = 0.039), cell-matrix adhesion (odds ratio = 6.62, p = 0.001), regulation of immune response (odds ratio = 4.89, p = 0.012), and response to wounding (odds ratio = 3.18, p = 0.001). These terms were significantly overrepresented by genes such as COL3A1, FBN1, CFD, CFH, THY1, ANXA5, and CD36, which are known to participate in the structural and functional remodeling of tissue following injury. The enrichment of cell-matrix adhesion and extracellular matrix organization terms underscores the central role of collagen and other matrix proteins in the pathogenesis of hernia formation. The presence of immune-related terms, including regulation of immune response and acute inflammatory response, suggests that the molecular signature of recurrent hernia is not limited to structural defects but also involves dysregulated immune signaling, potentially contributing to impaired healing. In the molecular function category, the most enriched terms included extracellular matrix structural constituent, receptor binding, and enzyme inhibitor activity, reflecting the altered balance of matrix synthesis, degradation, and signaling. In the cellular component category, the most significant terms were extracellular region, plasma membrane, and collagen-containing extracellular matrix, further confirming that the molecular signature is localized to the extracellular environment where tissue integrity is determined. The gene ontology analysis provided a systems-level view of the biological pathways affected in recurrent incisional hernia patients, revealing that the differential expression of individual genes is not random but rather part of a coordinated program of altered tissue homeostasis. The absence of significant enrichment in the fascia gene list, due to the small number of differentially expressed genes, precluded similar analysis for that tissue, but the skin analysis was sufficiently robust to support the hypothesis that recurrent hernia is associated with a systemic alteration in wound healing biology. The findings from gene ontology analysis were consistent with the known biology of connective tissue repair and provided biological plausibility for the observed gene expression differences, reinforcing the validity of the biomarker signature.

### COL1/COL3 Ratio by Microarray, PCR Array, and Immunohistochemistry

The ratio of collagen type I to collagen type III has long been proposed as a key determinant of tissue strength and wound healing capacity, with a lower ratio indicating a predominance of immature, weaker collagen type III over mature, tensile-strength-providing collagen type I. In this study, the COL1A1/COL3A1 ratio, as measured by microarray, was slightly lower in recurrent incisional hernia patients compared to normal controls (1.33 vs. 1.46, p = 0.65), but the difference did not reach statistical significance. However, the COL1A2/COL3A1 ratio, which reflects the expression of the alpha-2 chain of type I collagen—the second component of the type I collagen heterotrimer—was significantly decreased in recurrent incisional hernia patients (0.59 vs. 0.79, p = 0.02), indicating a more pronounced imbalance in favor of collagen type III. This finding was corroborated by the PCR array, which showed a COL1A2/COL3A1 ratio of 1.51 in the recurrent incisional hernia group versus 2.26 in the normal control group (p = 0.058, one-sided t-test), a trend that approached statistical significance and was consistent with the microarray data. The PCR array also revealed that COL3A1 expression was significantly elevated in recurrent incisional hernia patients (fold change = 4.54, p = 0.002), while COL1A2 expression was modestly increased (fold change = 2.92, p = 0.035), suggesting that the ratio is driven more by increased collagen type III than by decreased collagen type I. The immunohistochemical analysis further supported these findings, demonstrating greater staining intensity for collagen type III compared to collagen type I in the skin and fascia of recurrent incisional hernia patients, consistent with a relative overabundance of the immature collagen isoform. The combination of these three independent lines of evidence—microarray, PCR array, and immunohistochemistry—provides compelling support for the hypothesis that recurrent incisional hernia is associated with a persistent imbalance in collagen composition, favoring the synthesis of collagen type III over collagen type I. Importantly, the COL1A2/COL3A1 ratio, when combined with GREM1 expression, provided the most accurate classification of patients into recurrent incisional hernia and normal control groups, achieving 86 percent accuracy using leave-one-out cross-validation. This suggests that the ratio alone is not sufficient for reliable prediction, but when integrated with the expression level of GREM1, a gene that modulates extracellular matrix formation and tissue differentiation, it becomes a powerful diagnostic tool. The biological significance of this finding lies in the fact that the collagen ratio reflects the final outcome of a complex regulatory network, and its alteration is not merely a consequence of hernia formation but likely a pre-existing defect in the patient’s wound healing program. The identification of this ratio as a component of a biomarker panel represents a major advance in the ability to predict hernia recurrence before it occurs.
