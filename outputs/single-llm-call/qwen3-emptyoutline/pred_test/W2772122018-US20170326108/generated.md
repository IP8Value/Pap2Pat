# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Prion diseases represent a unique class of fatal neurodegenerative disorders characterized by the misfolding and pathological aggregation of the cellular prion protein, PrP^C, into an abnormal, protease-resistant isoform designated PrP^Sc. Unlike conventional infectious agents such as viruses or bacteria, prions propagate without the involvement of nucleic acid, relying instead on a self-templating mechanism in which PrP^Sc induces conformational changes in native PrP^C, leading to its conversion into the pathogenic form. This process results in the progressive accumulation of insoluble, aggregated PrP^Sc deposits within the central nervous system, triggering widespread neuronal loss, spongiform vacuolation, astrogliosis, and ultimately, rapid cognitive and motor decline. In humans, these diseases manifest as Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, Gerstmann-Sträussler-Scheinker syndrome, and fatal familial insomnia, while in animals, they include scrapie in sheep and goats, bovine spongiform encephalopathy in cattle, and chronic wasting disease in deer and elk. Despite decades of intensive research, no approved therapeutic intervention exists that can halt or reverse the progression of prion diseases in humans or animals. The fundamental challenge in developing effective treatments lies in the extraordinary stability of PrP^Sc aggregates, their resistance to conventional degradation pathways, and the difficulty of delivering therapeutic agents across the blood-brain barrier to reach sufficient concentrations within the brain parenchyma. Numerous compounds have been evaluated for their anti-prion activity in experimental models, including quinacrine, pentosan polysulfate, doxycycline, and various autophagy-inducing agents such as rapamycin and trehalose. While some of these compounds demonstrate modest reductions in PrP^Sc levels in cell culture systems, their efficacy in vivo has been inconsistent, and none have translated into clinically meaningful outcomes. Moreover, many of these agents exhibit significant off-target effects, poor bioavailability, or insufficient penetration into the central nervous system, limiting their therapeutic utility. The failure of previous efforts underscores the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies that not only reduce PrP^Sc burden but also achieve durable clearance of prion infectivity, ideally through mechanisms that leverage endogenous cellular quality control systems. Recent advances in understanding the role of autophagy— a conserved lysosomal degradation pathway responsible for the turnover of misfolded proteins and damaged organelles—have opened new avenues for intervention. Pharmacological induction of autophagy has been shown to enhance the clearance of PrP^Sc in neuronal and non-neuronal cell models, suggesting that augmenting the cell’s intrinsic degradative capacity may offer a more sustainable approach than direct inhibition of prion conversion. However, existing autophagy inducers often suffer from narrow therapeutic windows, delayed onset of action, or incomplete eradication of prion seeding activity, leaving a critical gap in the development of a clinically viable therapy. There remains a profound and unmet need for a compound that is orally bioavailable, capable of crossing the blood-brain barrier at therapeutically relevant concentrations, exhibits low systemic toxicity, and can induce a robust and sustained autophagic response sufficient to eliminate prion infectivity entirely, even after cessation of treatment. Such a compound would represent a transformative advance in the field of prion disease therapeutics, offering not only the potential to treat sporadic and inherited forms of human prion disease but also to serve as a prototype for the treatment of other protein misfolding disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and Huntington’s disease, which share common pathogenic features of protein aggregation and impaired proteostasis.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to the use of AR-12 and its structurally related analogues, particularly AR-14, as novel therapeutic agents for the treatment of prion diseases and other protein misfolding disorders characterized by the accumulation of pathogenic, self-propagating protein aggregates. Specifically, the invention discloses that AR-12, a celecoxib-derived small molecule previously characterized for its antitumor and antimicrobial properties, and its close structural analogue AR-14, are capable of inducing a potent and sustained activation of autophagy in mammalian cells, resulting in the efficient degradation of the pathological prion protein isoform PrP^Sc and the complete elimination of prion infectivity in multiple cell culture models. Unlike prior compounds that merely reduce PrP^Sc levels transiently or incompletely, AR-12 and AR-14, when administered over an extended period, achieve a state of sustained prion clearance that persists even after drug withdrawal, indicating true eradication of the infectious agent rather than temporary suppression. This effect is observed across diverse cell types, including neuronal and non-neuronal lines infected with multiple prion strains, and is demonstrably dependent on functional autophagy machinery, as evidenced by the attenuation of anti-prion activity in cells genetically deficient in the essential autophagy gene ATG5. The compounds exert their effect not through direct inhibition of PrP^C expression, interference with prion conversion kinetics, or disruption of membrane localization, but by stimulating the autophagic pathway, leading to increased formation of autophagosomes, enhanced fusion with lysosomes, and subsequent lysosomal degradation of PrP^Sc aggregates. This mechanism is supported by the time-dependent upregulation of the autophagy marker LC3-II, the synergistic accumulation of LC3-II in the presence of lysosomal inhibitors, and the preservation of autophagic flux under treatment conditions. Importantly, AR-12 and AR-14 are orally bioavailable, demonstrate favorable pharmacokinetic properties including effective penetration of the blood-brain barrier, and exhibit a wide therapeutic index with minimal cytotoxicity at concentrations required for anti-prion activity. In long-term treatment regimens spanning multiple cell passages, AR-12 completely abolishes prion seeding activity as measured by the highly sensitive real-time quaking-induced conversion assay, with no recurrence of infectivity observed even after 40 days of drug-free culture, a finding that has not been achieved with any previously reported compound. AR-14, while similarly effective in the short term and in non-neuronal cells, demonstrates a less durable effect in certain neuronal models, suggesting that structural differences between the two compounds may influence the stability of the cleared state. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising AR-12 or AR-14 in combination with pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, excipients, or other agents that enhance bioavailability, stability, or blood-brain barrier penetration. The invention also includes methods of treatment involving the administration of AR-12 or AR-14 to a mammal diagnosed with a prion disease, a method of reducing PrP^Sc burden in a subject, and a method of preventing the recurrence of prion infection following therapeutic intervention. The discovery that AR-12 and AR-14 can achieve complete and durable clearance of prion infectivity represents a paradigm shift in the therapeutic approach to prion diseases, moving beyond palliative suppression toward curative eradication, and establishes these compounds as first-in-class agents with transformative potential for the treatment of prion disorders and related neurodegenerative conditions.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive and novel therapeutic strategy for the treatment of prion diseases based on the pharmacological induction of autophagy through the administration of AR-12 and its structural analogues, particularly AR-14. Prion diseases, including Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease in humans, scrapie in sheep, bovine spongiform encephalopathy in cattle, and chronic wasting disease in cervids, are universally fatal neurodegenerative disorders characterized by the accumulation of a misfolded, protease-resistant isoform of the prion protein, PrP^Sc, which propagates by templating the conformational conversion of its normal cellular counterpart, PrP^C. The pathogenic cascade initiated by PrP^Sc leads to progressive synaptic dysfunction, neuronal death, gliosis, and the hallmark spongiform degeneration of the central nervous system, culminating in rapidly progressive dementia, ataxia, and death, typically within months of symptom onset. Despite extensive research efforts spanning over three decades, no approved therapy exists that can halt or reverse the progression of these diseases in humans or animals. The failure of prior therapeutic candidates—ranging from antimalarial drugs such as quinacrine and chlorpromazine to polymeric inhibitors like pentosan polysulfate and antibiotics such as doxycycline—can be attributed to their inability to achieve durable clearance of prion infectivity, their poor pharmacokinetic profiles, their limited penetration into the central nervous system, or their induction of significant off-target toxicities. While some compounds have demonstrated modest reductions in PrP^Sc levels in cell culture models, these effects are often transient, reversible upon drug withdrawal, and do not correlate with the elimination of prion seeding activity, as measured by ultrasensitive techniques such as real-time quaking-induced conversion (RT-QuIC). The present invention overcomes these longstanding limitations by identifying AR-12 and AR-14 as compounds capable of not only reducing PrP^Sc burden but achieving complete and sustained eradication of prion infectivity in multiple cell line models, a feat previously unattainable with any known therapeutic agent.

AR-12, also known as OSU-03012, is a derivative of the cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) inhibitor celecoxib, engineered to retain the antitumor properties of its parent compound while eliminating COX-2 inhibitory activity, thereby reducing gastrointestinal and cardiovascular side effects associated with traditional nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. AR-12 functions as a potent inhibitor of phosphoinositide-dependent kinase-1 (PDK1), a central node in the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway that regulates cell survival, proliferation, and metabolism. In cancer models, AR-12 has been shown to induce apoptosis, inhibit angiogenesis, and sensitize tumor cells to radiation and chemotherapy. Beyond its antineoplastic effects, AR-12 has demonstrated broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against bacterial, fungal, and viral pathogens, suggesting a more general mechanism of action related to the modulation of host cell proteostasis. In particular, AR-12 has been shown to downregulate the endoplasmic reticulum chaperone GRP78, leading to the activation of the unfolded protein response and the induction of autophagy via upregulation of key autophagy regulators such as Atg13 and PERK. These properties, combined with its oral bioavailability, established safety profile in human clinical trials for oncology indications, and documented ability to cross the blood-brain barrier, position AR-12 as a uniquely suited candidate for the treatment of neurodegenerative disorders involving protein misfolding. The present invention is the first to demonstrate that AR-12, and its close analogue AR-14, exert a potent and specific anti-prion effect in multiple prion-infected cell lines, including murine neuroblastoma (ScN2a), catecholaminergic neuronal (ScCAD5), and immortalized mouse embryonic fibroblast (ScMEF) cultures infected with distinct prion strains such as 22L, RML, and Me7. The anti-prion activity of AR-12 and AR-14 is not attributable to direct cytotoxicity, as extensive viability assays across a broad concentration range reveal a clear therapeutic window, with effective concentrations for PrP^Sc reduction well below those causing significant cell death. Furthermore, the compounds do not alter the expression levels or subcellular distribution of PrP^C in uninfected cells, indicating that their mechanism of action is selective for the pathological isoform and does not interfere with the physiological functions of the normal prion protein.

The anti-prion effect of AR-12 and AR-14 is mediated primarily through the induction of autophagy, a highly conserved intracellular degradation pathway responsible for the sequestration and lysosomal destruction of misfolded proteins, damaged organelles, and intracellular pathogens. Autophagy is initiated by the formation of a double-membrane phagophore, which expands to engulf cytoplasmic cargo and matures into an autophagosome. The autophagosome subsequently fuses with a lysosome to form an autolysosome, where the sequestered material is degraded by lysosomal hydrolases. A key molecular marker of autophagic flux is the lipidation of microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3-I) to its phosphatidylethanolamine-conjugated form, LC3-II, which becomes stably associated with the autophagosomal membrane. The present invention demonstrates that treatment with AR-12 or AR-14 results in a dose- and time-dependent increase in LC3-II levels in all tested prion-infected cell lines, consistent with the induction of autophagosome formation. Critically, the co-administration of bafilomycin A1, a specific inhibitor of vacuolar H+-ATPase that blocks autophagosome-lysosome fusion and thereby causes accumulation of LC3-II due to impaired degradation, results in a further enhancement of LC3-II levels in AR-12- and AR-14-treated cells. This observation confirms that the compounds stimulate autophagic flux rather than inhibit it, indicating that the observed increase in LC3-II reflects genuine enhancement of autophagic activity rather than a blockade of downstream steps. The functional importance of autophagy in mediating the anti-prion effect of AR-12 and AR-14 is further substantiated by experiments using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of the essential autophagy gene ATG5 in ScN2a cells. ATG5 is required for the elongation of the phagophore membrane and the formation of functional autophagosomes. In ATG5-deficient cells, the conversion of LC3-I to LC3-II is abolished, and autophagic flux is completely disrupted. In these autophagy-compromised cells, the anti-prion activity of AR-12 and AR-14 is significantly attenuated, although not entirely abolished, suggesting that while autophagy is the primary mechanism of PrP^Sc clearance, additional, autophagy-independent pathways may contribute to the overall effect. The partial retention of activity in ATG5-knockout cells may reflect the involvement of alternative degradation systems such as the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway or chaperone-mediated autophagy, or may indicate that AR-12 and AR-14 exert secondary effects on prion propagation that are independent of bulk autophagy. Nevertheless, the marked reduction in efficacy in the absence of functional autophagy provides compelling evidence that autophagy is the dominant and necessary mechanism for the full anti-prion activity of these compounds.

In short-term treatment regimens lasting 72 hours, AR-12 and AR-14 reduce PrP^Sc levels in a concentration-dependent manner across all tested cell lines. In ScN2a cells infected with the 22L prion strain, AR-12 achieves an EC50 of 1.5 µM, with maximal reduction observed at 3 µM, while AR-14, a structurally analogous compound, exhibits comparable potency with an EC50 of 1.5 µM and effective reduction at concentrations as low as 1.5 µM. In ScCAD5 cells, which are derived from a catecholaminergic neuronal lineage, AR-12 requires a higher concentration (EC50 = 4 µM) to achieve significant PrP^Sc reduction, suggesting that neuronal subtype-specific differences in autophagic capacity, prion strain characteristics, or drug metabolism may influence sensitivity. In non-neuronal ScMEF cells, both compounds demonstrate robust activity with EC50 values of 2 µM for AR-12 and 2 µM for AR-14, confirming that the anti-prion effect is not restricted to neuronal cells and may be broadly applicable across cell types. Importantly, cytotoxicity assays consistently demonstrate that the concentrations required for anti-prion activity are well below the median lethal dose (LD50) for each compound in each cell line, with AR-12 exhibiting an LD50 of 5 µM in ScN2a and ScMEF cells and 9 µM in ScCAD5 cells, and AR-14 showing an LD50 of 4 µM in ScN2a and 5 µM in ScMEF cells. This wide therapeutic index underscores the safety profile of these compounds and supports their potential for clinical translation. Immunofluorescence analysis using confocal microscopy further corroborates these findings, revealing a marked decrease in PrP^Sc immunoreactivity in AR-12- and AR-14-treated cells compared to vehicle controls, accompanied by a reorganization of lysosomal morphology, with increased intensity and condensation of LAMP1-positive vesicles, consistent with enhanced lysosomal activity and autophagic flux. The specificity of the effect is further confirmed by the absence of changes in PrP^C levels or distribution in uninfected cells, indicating that the compounds selectively target the pathological isoform without perturbing normal prion protein function.

The most significant and transformative finding of the present invention is the demonstration that prolonged treatment with AR-12 leads to the complete and durable eradication of prion infectivity in ScN2a cells, a result that has never been achieved previously with any other compound. In a long-term treatment paradigm spanning 40 days—comprising 20 days of continuous drug exposure followed by 20 days of drug-free culture—AR-12 treatment at 3 µM completely eliminates detectable PrP^Sc by immunoblot analysis and abolishes prion seeding activity in RT-QuIC assays at all dilutions tested. Crucially, this state of prion-free status is maintained for the entire duration of the post-treatment observation period, with no recurrence of seeding activity observed even after five additional cell passages in the absence of drug. This outcome stands in stark contrast to all previously reported studies, in which prion infectivity invariably re-emerges following drug withdrawal, indicating that prior therapies merely suppress rather than eliminate the infectious agent. The durability of this effect suggests that AR-12 does not merely degrade existing PrP^Sc aggregates but disrupts the self-propagating cycle of prion replication to such an extent that the cellular environment becomes inhospitable to prion reseeding. This may occur through the complete clearance of residual PrP^Sc seeds below the detection threshold of even the most sensitive assays, or through the induction of long-lasting epigenetic or proteostatic changes that prevent the reformation of infectious conformers. In contrast, AR-14, while equally effective in the short term and capable of achieving complete clearance in ScMEF cells infected with the Me7 prion strain, fails to maintain this state in ScN2a cells, with prion seeding activity reappearing after 40 days of drug-free culture. This differential effect between AR-12 and AR-14, despite their structural similarity, suggests that subtle differences in chemical structure, pharmacokinetics, or interaction with cellular targets may critically influence the stability of the cleared state. The fact that AR-14 achieves complete clearance in ScMEF cells but not in ScN2a cells further underscores the importance of cell type and prion strain context in determining therapeutic outcomes, highlighting the need for tailored approaches in future clinical applications.

The use of RT-QuIC as a readout in these studies represents a critical methodological advancement over traditional immunoblotting, which is inherently limited in sensitivity and prone to false negatives. In ScMEF cells treated with AR-12 or AR-14, immunoblot analysis showed no detectable PrP^Sc as early as the first passage, yet RT-QuIC analysis revealed residual seeding activity at low levels, indicating the presence of infectious prions below the detection limit of immunoblotting. This discrepancy was resolved only after extended treatment, when RT-QuIC also became negative, confirming that true eradication requires prolonged exposure and cannot be inferred from short-term immunoblot data. This finding has profound implications for the design of future preclinical and clinical trials, as it demonstrates that the standard practice of evaluating anti-prion efficacy based on immunoblotting alone is insufficient and potentially misleading. The present invention establishes RT-QuIC as the gold standard for assessing the completeness of prion clearance in vitro and provides a validated model for evaluating the curative potential of novel therapeutics. The ability of AR-12 to achieve complete and sustained clearance in multiple cell models, combined with its established safety profile, oral bioavailability, and blood-brain barrier penetration, positions it as the first compound capable of achieving a true cure in a prion disease model. The fact that this effect is observed in both neuronal and non-neuronal cells further supports the hypothesis that the mechanism of action is cell-autonomous and not dependent on specialized neuronal signaling pathways, suggesting broad applicability across tissues and disease contexts.

The pharmaceutical compositions of the present invention encompass AR-12 and AR-14 in pure form or in combination with pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, excipients, stabilizers, solubilizers, or delivery enhancers suitable for oral, intravenous, intranasal, or intrathecal administration. Given the demonstrated ability of AR-12 to cross the blood-brain barrier, oral administration is the preferred route, offering the advantages of patient compliance, ease of dosing, and suitability for long-term chronic treatment regimens. Formulations may include solid dosage forms such as tablets, capsules, or enteric-coated pellets, or liquid formulations such as suspensions or solutions, optionally containing permeation enhancers such as cyclodextrins, surfactants, or lipid-based carriers to further improve bioavailability. The compounds may also be encapsulated in nanoparticles, liposomes, or microparticles to prolong systemic circulation, enhance brain uptake, or reduce potential off-target effects. The invention further includes combination therapies in which AR-12 or AR-14 is administered in conjunction with other agents that modulate proteostasis, such as mTOR inhibitors, HSP90 inhibitors, or other autophagy enhancers, to achieve synergistic effects and reduce the likelihood of drug resistance. The invention also encompasses methods of treatment involving the administration of AR-12 or AR-14 to a mammal diagnosed with a prion disease, including humans, with the goal of reducing PrP^Sc burden, slowing disease progression, preventing neurodegeneration, and ultimately achieving sustained clearance of prion infectivity. The invention further includes methods of prophylaxis in individuals at high genetic risk for familial prion disease, such as carriers of pathogenic PRNP mutations, wherein administration of AR-12 or AR-14 prior to symptom onset may prevent or significantly delay the initiation of prion propagation. The invention also encompasses diagnostic methods in which the efficacy of AR-12 or AR-14 treatment is monitored by serial measurement of PrP^Sc levels in cerebrospinal fluid or blood-derived exosomes using RT-QuIC or other ultrasensitive detection platforms, enabling real-time assessment of therapeutic response and guiding treatment duration.

The mechanism by which AR-12 and AR-14 induce autophagy and eliminate prion infectivity is multifactorial and likely involves the coordinated modulation of several cellular pathways. AR-12 is known to inhibit PDK1, a kinase that activates AKT and downstream mTORC1, a central negative regulator of autophagy. By suppressing PDK1, AR-12 indirectly activates the autophagy-initiating complex ULK1/Atg13/FIP200, leading to the nucleation of autophagosomes. Additionally, AR-12 has been shown to downregulate GRP78, an endoplasmic reticulum chaperone that, when bound to misfolded proteins, inhibits the unfolded protein response. Reduction of GRP78 leads to the activation of PERK, which phosphorylates eIF2α, resulting in global translational attenuation and the selective translation of autophagy-related transcripts such as ATG12 and LC3. The resulting increase in autophagic flux enhances the delivery of PrP^Sc aggregates to lysosomes, where they are degraded by cathepsins and other hydrolases. The sustained nature of the effect observed with AR-12 may be due to its ability to induce long-term epigenetic reprogramming of autophagy-related genes, or to the elimination of a critical reservoir of prion seeds that act as nucleation points for further propagation. The failure of AR-14 to achieve the same durable effect may be attributed to differences in its chemical structure, which may affect its binding affinity to PDK1, its metabolic stability, or its ability to penetrate subcellular compartments where prion seeds are sequestered. The present invention does not require a complete understanding of all molecular mechanisms underlying this effect to be enabled, as the demonstrated efficacy in multiple models and the reproducible outcome of complete clearance are sufficient to establish utility and enable therapeutic application.

The therapeutic potential of AR-12 and AR-14 extends beyond prion diseases to other neurodegenerative conditions characterized by the accumulation of misfolded, self-propagating protein aggregates. Alzheimer’s disease, for example, involves the aggregation of amyloid-beta and tau proteins, Parkinson’s disease is defined by alpha-synuclein pathology, and Huntington’s disease is caused by polyglutamine-expanded huntingtin. All of these disorders share common features of proteostasis collapse and impaired autophagy, and numerous studies have demonstrated that enhancing autophagy can reduce the burden of these pathogenic proteins in cellular and animal models. The fact that AR-12 has already been shown to reduce amyloid-beta-induced neuritic degeneration through autophagy stimulation provides a direct precedent for its application in Alzheimer’s disease. Similarly, the ability of AR-12 to induce autophagy in non-neuronal cells suggests potential utility in systemic amyloidosis or other proteinopathies affecting peripheral tissues. The present invention thus provides a platform technology for the treatment of a broad spectrum of protein misfolding disorders, with AR-12 and AR-14 serving as lead compounds for the development of next-generation therapeutics. The established safety profile of AR-12 in human clinical trials for cancer, including its FDA IND approval and orphan drug designation in Europe, significantly de-risks its repurposing for neurodegenerative indications and accelerates the path to clinical translation. The present invention, therefore, represents not merely a new treatment for a currently untreatable disease, but a paradigm-shifting advance in the field of neurodegenerative therapeutics, offering the first realistic pathway toward a cure for prion diseases and a powerful new tool for the broader fight against protein misfolding disorders.
