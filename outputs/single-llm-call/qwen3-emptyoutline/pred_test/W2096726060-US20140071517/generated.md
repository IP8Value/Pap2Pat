## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of optical materials and nanoscale photonics, and more specifically to lanthanide-doped inorganic nanocrystals functionalized with organic dye molecules that serve as optical antennas for the efficient capture and transfer of infrared radiation to enable visible upconversion luminescence. The invention pertains to a class of hybrid nanomaterials in which the spectral response of upconverting nanoparticles is extended and tailored through the covalent attachment of infrared-absorbing dyes, thereby enabling selective, wavelength-dependent emission of visible light in response to otherwise undetectable infrared excitation. The materials disclosed herein are designed to operate under low-power, continuous-wave infrared illumination, and are particularly suited for applications requiring spatially resolved, color-coded discrimination of multiple infrared bands without the need for electronic sensors, photodetectors, or external signal amplification. The invention further encompasses methods for the synthesis, surface functionalization, and film fabrication of these hybrid nanocomposites, including the controlled encapsulation of dye-labeled nanoparticles within silica matrices and their subsequent integration into solid-state optical devices. The technology described herein bridges the fields of rare-earth chemistry, nanoscale energy transfer, organic-inorganic hybrid materials, and photonic sensing, offering a novel platform for passive optical signaling, infrared imaging, and spectral multiplexing in both military and civilian contexts.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Lanthanide-doped inorganic nanocrystals have long been recognized for their ability to convert low-energy infrared photons into higher-energy visible emissions through a sequential, multiphoton upconversion process. These materials, typically composed of a fluoride host lattice such as sodium yttrium fluoride (NaYF₄) doped with sensitizer and activator ions like ytterbium and erbium, exploit the long-lived electronic states of trivalent lanthanides to accumulate energy from multiple absorbed photons before emitting a single photon at a shorter wavelength. Despite their promise, the practical utility of such systems has been severely constrained by the inherently narrow absorption bandwidths and extremely low molar absorptivity coefficients associated with the f-f electronic transitions of lanthanide ions. These atomic transitions, while spectrally sharp, are Laporte-forbidden and exhibit absorption cross-sections on the order of 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁴ L·g⁻¹·cm⁻¹, rendering them inefficient at capturing incident infrared radiation, particularly under low-power or broadband illumination conditions. Furthermore, the narrow spectral width of these transitions—often less than 10 nm full width at half maximum—limits the operational flexibility of upconversion systems, requiring precise and expensive laser sources tuned to specific wavelengths such as 975 nm or 801 nm for excitation. Attempts to broaden the absorption profile have relied on the use of multiple dopants or complex host matrices, but these approaches have yielded only marginal improvements in spectral coverage and often introduce competing nonradiative decay pathways that reduce overall quantum efficiency.

To overcome these limitations, researchers have explored the use of organic dyes as optical antennas capable of absorbing infrared radiation over broad spectral bands and transferring the captured energy to the lanthanide ions via nonradiative mechanisms such as Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET). Theoretically, organic dyes possess absorption cross-sections that are several orders of magnitude greater than those of lanthanide ions, with extinction coefficients exceeding 10⁵ M⁻¹·cm⁻¹ and bandwidths exceeding 100 nm, making them ideal candidates for enhancing the light-harvesting capability of upconversion systems. However, the successful implementation of such dye-sensitized upconversion has proven challenging due to the stringent requirements for proximity between the dye molecule and the nanoparticle surface. FRET efficiency decays with the sixth power of the distance between donor and acceptor, meaning that even minor increases in separation—on the order of a few nanometers—can lead to catastrophic reductions in energy transfer efficiency. Early attempts to attach dyes to nanoparticles via ligand exchange or physisorption have resulted in unstable interfaces, poor reproducibility, and susceptibility to environmental degradation. Moreover, many of the dyes most suitable for infrared absorption are chemically fragile under the basic or high-temperature conditions typically required for nanoparticle surface functionalization, leading to decomposition or loss of optical activity. Consequently, prior art has failed to produce a robust, scalable, and reproducible method for covalently linking infrared-absorbing dyes to lanthanide-doped nanocrystals while preserving both the structural integrity of the nanoparticles and the photophysical properties of the dye.

Another critical limitation of existing systems is the inability to discriminate between different bands of infrared radiation. While some studies have demonstrated upconversion in response to a single infrared wavelength, no prior system has been shown to produce distinct, spectrally resolvable visible emissions in response to different infrared excitation bands using a single class of nanomaterials. This capability is essential for applications such as multispectral infrared imaging, threat detection, and spectral encoding, where the ability to visually distinguish between multiple infrared sources—each operating at a different wavelength—is paramount. Furthermore, the integration of these hybrid nanomaterials into practical devices has been hindered by the lack of reliable methods for fabricating stable, homogeneous films or coatings that retain the upconversion properties of the dispersed nanoparticles. Aggregation, phase separation, and surface quenching have plagued attempts to embed these materials into polymer matrices, glass substrates, or thin-film architectures. As a result, despite the theoretical potential of dye-sensitized upconversion, no prior invention has successfully combined broad-spectrum infrared absorption, efficient energy transfer, wavelength-selective visible emission, and durable solid-state integration into a single, functional system. The present invention addresses each of these deficiencies through a novel, integrated approach that enables the precise, covalent coupling of tailored infrared dyes to lanthanide-doped nanocrystals, the controlled encapsulation of these hybrids within protective silica shells, and the fabrication of stable, optically active films capable of producing distinct, visible responses to different infrared wavelengths.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel class of hybrid nanomaterials comprising lanthanide-doped sodium yttrium fluoride nanocrystals covalently functionalized with infrared-absorbing organic dyes, wherein the dyes serve as optical antennas that capture incident infrared radiation and transfer energy to the lanthanide ions via Förster resonance energy transfer, resulting in visible upconversion luminescence that is spectrally distinct depending on the wavelength of the incident infrared excitation. The invention further encompasses methods for the synthesis of these nanocrystals with controlled size, shape, and crystalline phase, the surface functionalization of the nanocrystals with amine-containing siloxane monomers to enable covalent attachment of dye molecules bearing carboxylic acid, isothiocyanate, or NHS ester functionalities, and the subsequent encapsulation of the dye-labeled nanoparticles within thin silica shells to enhance stability and enable integration into solid-state devices. The invention also provides for the fabrication of transparent, homogeneous films composed of these hybrid nanocomposites, which exhibit wavelength-selective visible emission under low-power infrared illumination, thereby enabling passive, color-coded discrimination of multiple infrared bands without the need for electronic sensors or signal processing.

In a first embodiment, the invention comprises a nanocomposite material comprising a lanthanide-doped NaYF₄ nanocrystal having a hexagonal crystal structure, wherein the nanocrystal is doped with at least one sensitizer ion selected from the group consisting of Yb³⁺, Nd³⁺, and Ho³⁺, and at least one activator ion selected from the group consisting of Er³⁺, Tm³⁺, and Ho³⁺, wherein the sensitizer and activator ions are present in molar ratios optimized for efficient energy transfer and minimal self-quenching, and wherein the surface of the nanocrystal is covalently functionalized with at least one organic dye molecule selected from the group consisting of DyLight 800, DY-831, DLS-776A, and NIR-797, wherein the dye molecule is attached via an amide bond formed between a carboxylic acid, isothiocyanate, or NHS ester group on the dye and an amine group on a siloxane-derived shell surrounding the nanocrystal, and wherein the dye exhibits a molar extinction coefficient greater than 200,000 M⁻¹·cm⁻¹ in the infrared region between 750 nm and 850 nm, and wherein the upconversion emission spectrum of the nanocomposite, when excited by infrared radiation at a wavelength corresponding to the absorption maximum of the dye, is distinct from the upconversion emission spectrum when excited by infrared radiation at a wavelength corresponding to the atomic absorption of the sensitizer ion.

In a second embodiment, the invention comprises a method for preparing the nanocomposite material, comprising the steps of: (a) synthesizing lanthanide-doped NaYF₄ nanocrystals by heating a mixture of lanthanide acetates, sodium acetate, oleic acid, and octadecene under inert atmosphere to a temperature between 300°C and 325°C for a duration sufficient to form hexagonal crystalline nanoparticles having a long-axis dimension between 15 nm and 30 nm and a short-axis dimension between 10 nm and 20 nm; (b) functionalizing the surface of the nanocrystals by forming a thin silica shell via reverse microemulsion-mediated hydrolysis and condensation of a siloxane monomer containing a primary, secondary, or tertiary amine group, wherein the siloxane monomer is selected from the group consisting of (3-aminopropyl)trimethoxysilane, [3-(methylamino)propyl]trimethoxysilane, and (3,3-dimethylaminopropyl)trimethoxysilane, and wherein the reaction is conducted at room temperature in the presence of Igepal CO-520 surfactant and cyclohexane solvent to prevent nanoparticle aggregation; (c) purifying the amine-functionalized nanocrystals by precipitation and centrifugation in ethanol; (d) attaching the organic dye molecule to the amine-functionalized nanocrystals by stirring the nanocrystals in a solution of the dye in ethanol or dimethyl sulfoxide for a duration sufficient to form an amide bond between the dye and the amine group on the silica shell, wherein the dye is selected from the group consisting of DyLight 800, DY-831, DLS-776A, and NIR-797, and wherein the dye-to-nanoparticle molar ratio is maintained between 100:1 and 300:1; and (e) washing the resulting dye-labeled nanocomposite with ethanol to remove unbound dye, thereby yielding a stable, covalently functionalized nanocomposite material.

In a third embodiment, the invention comprises a method for encapsulating the dye-labeled nanocomposite within a silica matrix, comprising the steps of: (a) dispersing the dye-labeled nanocomposite in a solution of cyclohexane and Igepal CO-520 surfactant to form a reverse microemulsion; (b) adding a second siloxane monomer that lacks an amine functional group, selected from the group consisting of tetraethyl orthosilicate, methyltrimethoxysilane, and phenyltrimethoxysilane, to the microemulsion; (c) inducing condensation of the second siloxane monomer to form a thicker silica shell around the dye-labeled nanocomposite, wherein the thickness of the outer silica shell is between 5 nm and 15 nm; and (d) precipitating and purifying the encapsulated nanocomposite by centrifugation in ethanol, thereby yielding a silica-encapsulated nanocomposite material protected from environmental degradation and suitable for integration into solid-state films.

In a fourth embodiment, the invention comprises a method for fabricating a film comprising the dye-labeled nanocomposite or the silica-encapsulated nanocomposite, comprising the steps of: (a) dispersing the nanocomposite in a solvent selected from the group consisting of ethanol, toluene, cyclohexane, and dimethylformamide; (b) depositing the dispersion onto a transparent substrate selected from the group consisting of glass, quartz, polyethylene terephthalate, and polycarbonate; (c) evaporating the solvent under controlled conditions to form a uniform, non-aggregated thin film; and (d) optionally curing the film at a temperature below 150°C to enhance adhesion and mechanical stability, thereby yielding a solid-state optical film that emits visible light in response to infrared excitation at a wavelength corresponding to the absorption band of the attached dye.

In a fifth embodiment, the invention comprises a system for infrared discrimination comprising a plurality of the nanocomposite materials described above, wherein each nanocomposite material is functionalized with a different infrared-absorbing dye and a different lanthanide dopant combination, such that each material produces a distinct visible emission spectrum when excited by a different infrared wavelength, and wherein the plurality of materials are arranged in a spatially resolved pattern on a substrate to form a color-coded infrared detection array, wherein the visible emission from each region of the array is spectrally distinguishable and corresponds uniquely to the infrared wavelength incident upon that region.

In a sixth embodiment, the invention comprises a method for detecting infrared radiation comprising the steps of: (a) exposing the nanocomposite material of the first embodiment to infrared radiation having a wavelength between 750 nm and 850 nm; (b) observing the resulting visible upconversion emission from the nanocomposite material; and (c) identifying the wavelength of the incident infrared radiation based on the spectral characteristics of the emitted visible light, wherein the emitted light is selected from the group consisting of green, red, and blue emissions corresponding to the activator ion in the nanocrystal and the excitation wavelength of the dye.

The invention provides a significant advancement over prior art by enabling the passive, visible discrimination of multiple infrared bands through a single, chemically stable, solution-processable material system. Unlike electronic sensors, the disclosed materials require no power source, no cooling, and no signal processing to produce a visible response. Unlike prior dye-sensitized upconversion systems, the invention provides a robust, covalent linkage between dye and nanoparticle that withstands repeated washing, long-term storage, and integration into solid-state devices. Unlike prior attempts at multispectral infrared detection, the invention enables color-coded, spatially resolved identification of different infrared wavelengths without the need for filters, gratings, or photodetectors. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in infrared sensing, transforming passive optical materials into active, visible indicators of infrared presence and spectral composition.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### X=—NH₂, —CO₂M′, —SH, —OH,

The surface of the lanthanide-doped NaYF₄ nanocrystals is functionalized with chemical groups that enable covalent bonding to organic dye molecules, thereby ensuring stable, reproducible, and efficient energy transfer from the dye to the lanthanide ions. The functional groups introduced onto the nanocrystal surface are selected from the set consisting of primary amine (—NH₂), carboxylic acid (—CO₂M′), thiol (—SH), and hydroxyl (—OH), each of which serves a distinct role in the chemical architecture of the hybrid nanocomposite. The primary amine group (—NH₂) is the most preferred functional group for the covalent attachment of infrared-absorbing dyes, as it readily reacts with NHS esters, isothiocyanates, and carboxylic acids under mild conditions to form stable amide bonds, which are chemically robust and resistant to hydrolysis, thermal degradation, and photobleaching. The amine group is introduced onto the nanocrystal surface through the hydrolysis and condensation of amine-containing siloxane monomers, such as (3-aminopropyl)trimethoxysilane, which form a thin, uniform silica shell around the nanocrystal while preserving the underlying hexagonal crystal structure. The resulting amine-functionalized surface provides a high density of reactive sites for dye attachment, enabling the covalent immobilization of hundreds of dye molecules per nanoparticle without inducing aggregation or quenching. Alternative functional groups such as carboxylic acid (—CO₂M′), thiol (—SH), and hydroxyl (—OH) may be employed in embodiments where the dye molecule bears complementary reactive groups such as epoxides, isocyanates, or activated esters, although these systems are less preferred due to lower binding efficiency, slower reaction kinetics, or increased susceptibility to environmental interference. The carboxylic acid group (—CO₂M′) may be introduced via the use of carboxylic acid-functionalized silanes, such as (3-carboxypropyl)trimethoxysilane, and may serve as a secondary functionalization layer for subsequent bioconjugation or polymer integration. The thiol group (—SH) may be introduced via mercaptopropyltrimethoxysilane and may be useful in systems requiring metal coordination or gold nanoparticle coupling, although its use is limited by oxidation sensitivity and potential quenching of lanthanide luminescence. The hydroxyl group (—OH) may be introduced via the partial hydrolysis of tetraethyl orthosilicate and may serve as a hydrophilic interface for aqueous dispersion or as a precursor for further siloxane condensation. In all cases, the density and distribution of these functional groups on the nanocrystal surface are carefully controlled to ensure optimal dye loading while minimizing nonradiative energy transfer pathways that could reduce upconversion efficiency. The functionalization process is conducted under mild, room-temperature conditions to prevent degradation of the lanthanide-doped core, and the resulting surface chemistry is confirmed through Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, zeta potential measurements, and elemental analysis.

### M=H₂, Cu, Co, CoR₃, Ni, SiR₃R₄, AlR₃, Zn, Fe, FeR₃, Mn

The lanthanide-doped NaYF₄ nanocrystals are synthesized using a hot-injection method in which the lanthanide ions are introduced as acetate salts and coordinated with oleic acid to form a stable precursor solution prior to crystallization. The lanthanide ions used in the synthesis include those selected from the group consisting of H₂, Cu, Co, CoR₃, Ni, SiR₃R₄, AlR₃, Zn, Fe, FeR₃, and Mn, each of which may be incorporated into the crystal lattice either as a dopant or as a structural modifier to influence the size, shape, crystallinity, and optical properties of the resulting nanoparticles. While the primary lanthanide dopants are selected from the group consisting of Yb³⁺, Er³⁺, Tm³⁺, and Ho³⁺, the inclusion of secondary metal ions such as copper (Cu²⁺), cobalt (Co²⁺ or Co³⁺), nickel (Ni²⁺), aluminum (Al³⁺), zinc (Zn²⁺), iron (Fe²⁺ or Fe³⁺), and manganese (Mn²⁺) serves to modify the local crystal field environment around the lanthanide ions, thereby altering the energy transfer pathways, reducing nonradiative relaxation, and enhancing the overall upconversion efficiency. The use of copper ions, for instance, may introduce localized lattice distortions that suppress multiphonon relaxation processes, while cobalt ions may act as electron traps that prolong the lifetime of excited states in the sensitizer ions. Nickel and manganese ions may be incorporated at low concentrations to tune the emission color by introducing intermediate energy levels that facilitate cross-relaxation between activator ions. Aluminum and zinc ions may be used to stabilize the hexagonal phase of NaYF₄ at lower synthesis temperatures, thereby enabling the production of smaller nanoparticles with higher surface-to-volume ratios that are advantageous for dye-sensitized energy transfer. Iron ions, particularly in their trivalent form, may be employed to enhance the magnetic properties of the nanoparticles for potential applications in magnetically guided delivery or separation, although their concentration is kept below 0.5 mol% to avoid quenching of luminescence. The silane-based modifiers SiR₃R₄, where R₃ and R₄ are independently selected from alkyl, aryl, or halogen substituents, may be introduced as co-precursors during the crystallization step to influence the growth kinetics of the nanocrystals, promoting anisotropic growth along the c-axis and yielding rod-like morphologies with enhanced directional energy transfer. The term CoR₃ refers to cobalt complexes coordinated with organic ligands such as oleate or stearate, which are naturally formed during the synthesis and serve to stabilize the nanoparticle surface prior to functionalization. The term FeR₃ denotes iron species bound to carboxylate or fluoride ligands, which may be present as trace impurities or intentionally introduced to modify the electronic structure of the host lattice. The inclusion of these secondary metal ions is carefully controlled to ensure that their concentration remains below the threshold at which they introduce competing nonradiative decay pathways or disrupt the crystal lattice. The optimal composition for each application is determined empirically through systematic variation of the metal ratios and characterization of the resulting upconversion spectra, photoluminescence lifetimes, and quantum yields. The resulting nanocrystals exhibit high crystallinity, narrow size distribution, and minimal surface defects, all of which are essential for achieving high-efficiency energy transfer from the attached dye molecules.

### R₄=—R₃, —Cl, —CH₃, —H, -Ph, —OCH₃, —OCH₂CH₃

The siloxane monomers used to functionalize the surface of the lanthanide-doped NaYF₄ nanocrystals are selected to contain substituents R₄ that influence the hydrophobicity, steric bulk, and reactivity of the resulting silica shell. The substituent R₄ is selected from the group consisting of —R₃, —Cl, —CH₃, —H, -Ph, —OCH₃, and —OCH₂CH₃, each of which modulates the condensation kinetics of the siloxane network, the thickness of the silica shell, and the accessibility of the underlying amine functional groups to the dye molecules. The group —R₃ represents an alkyl chain of variable length, typically C₃ to C₁₈, which may be introduced via alkyl-substituted silanes such as propyltrimethoxysilane or octadecyltrimethoxysilane, and serves to increase the hydrophobicity of the silica shell, thereby enhancing compatibility with organic solvents and preventing nanoparticle aggregation in nonpolar media. The chloro substituent (—Cl) may be introduced via chloropropyltrimethoxysilane and serves as a reactive handle for further functionalization through nucleophilic substitution, although its use is limited due to potential hydrolysis and degradation under ambient conditions. The methyl group (—CH₃) is introduced via methyltrimethoxysilane and serves to reduce the density of silanol groups on the silica surface, thereby decreasing the tendency for interparticle hydrogen bonding and aggregation during film formation. The hydrogen substituent (—H) is inherent in the silanol groups formed during hydrolysis and is essential for the condensation reaction that forms the silica network, but its presence is minimized through controlled hydrolysis to prevent excessive cross-linking and shell thickening. The phenyl group (—Ph) is introduced via phenyltrimethoxysilane and serves to enhance the rigidity and thermal stability of the silica shell, while also providing π-π stacking interactions that may facilitate energy transfer to aromatic dye molecules. The methoxy group (—OCH₃) and ethoxy group (—OCH₂CH₃) are the terminal groups of the siloxane monomers prior to hydrolysis and are replaced by silanol groups during the condensation reaction; their presence in the initial reagent determines the rate of hydrolysis and the resulting porosity of the silica shell. The selection of R₄ substituents is critical to achieving a thin, uniform, and mechanically stable silica shell that encapsulates the nanoparticle without obscuring the underlying dye molecules or impeding energy transfer. The use of bulky substituents such as phenyl or long alkyl chains reduces the density of silanol groups, resulting in a more open, porous silica network that allows for greater dye accessibility and higher energy transfer efficiency. Conversely, the use of small substituents such as methyl or hydrogen promotes dense cross-linking, yielding a more impermeable shell that provides superior environmental protection but may reduce dye loading capacity. The optimal balance between shell thickness, porosity, and functional group density is achieved through empirical optimization of the siloxane monomer composition, reaction time, and temperature, ensuring that the resulting silica shell is sufficiently thin (less than 5 nm) to permit efficient Förster resonance energy transfer while being robust enough to protect the dye from photodegradation, oxidation, and leaching during subsequent processing and storage.

### M′=K, Li, Na, H

The carboxylic acid groups (—CO₂M′) present on the surface of the lanthanide-doped NaYF₄ nanocrystals or on the functionalized dye molecules are neutralized or stabilized by counterions selected from the group consisting of potassium (K⁺), lithium (Li⁺), sodium (Na⁺), and hydrogen (H⁺), each of which influences the solubility, colloidal stability, and reactivity of the hybrid nanocomposite. The sodium ion (Na⁺) is the most preferred counterion, as it is inherently present in the NaYF₄ host lattice and provides optimal electrostatic stabilization of the negatively charged carboxylate groups on the dye molecules, thereby preventing aggregation and promoting uniform dispersion in aqueous and organic media. The potassium ion (K⁺) may be introduced via the use of potassium acetate or potassium fluoride during synthesis and serves to slightly increase the ionic strength of the reaction medium, which can enhance the crystallization kinetics of the nanocrystals and promote the formation of larger, more uniform particles. The lithium ion (Li⁺), due to its small ionic radius and high charge density, may be incorporated at low concentrations to induce lattice strain in the NaYF₄ host, thereby modifying the crystal field splitting of the lanthanide ions and shifting the upconversion emission wavelengths. The hydrogen ion (H⁺), in the form of protonated carboxylic acid (—CO₂H), may be present during the dye attachment step under acidic conditions, where it serves to suppress the deprotonation of the carboxyl group and thereby reduce the rate of amide bond formation, allowing for controlled, stepwise functionalization of the nanoparticle surface. The choice of counterion is particularly important in the context of film fabrication, as the presence of alkali metal ions such as Na⁺ and K⁺ enhances the compatibility of the nanocomposite with polar solvents and facilitates the formation of homogeneous, non-aggregated thin films. In contrast, the presence of H⁺ may lead to protonation of amine groups on the silica shell, thereby reducing their reactivity toward NHS esters and isothiocyanates and necessitating the use of basic conditions for efficient dye coupling. The counterion is introduced either during the synthesis of the nanocrystals, through the use of metal acetate or fluoride precursors, or during the functionalization process, through the addition of alkali metal salts to the reaction medium. The concentration of each counterion is maintained below 5 mol% relative to the lanthanide content to avoid lattice distortion or phase segregation. The resulting nanocomposite exhibits enhanced colloidal stability in ethanol, toluene, and dimethyl sulfoxide, and the counterion content is confirmed through inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy.

## Synthesis of Lanthanide-Doped NaYF₄ Nanocrystals

The lanthanide-doped NaYF₄ nanocrystals are synthesized using a modified hot-injection method that ensures the formation of hexagonal phase nanoparticles with controlled size, shape, and crystallinity. The synthesis begins with the preparation of a precursor solution containing stoichiometric quantities of lanthanide acetates, sodium acetate, oleic acid, and octadecene, all of which are combined in a three-necked flask under an inert atmosphere of nitrogen or argon. The lanthanide acetates are selected to provide the desired combination of sensitizer and activator ions, with the sensitizer ion typically comprising 20 to 25 mole percent of the total lanthanide content and the activator ion comprising 0.3 to 2.5 mole percent, depending on the specific upconversion pathway targeted. The sodium acetate is added in a 1:1 molar ratio with the total lanthanide content to ensure complete fluoride incorporation and to buffer the reaction medium against pH fluctuations. The oleic acid serves as both a coordinating ligand and a surfactant, binding to the surface of the growing nanoparticles and preventing aggregation, while the octadecene acts as a high-boiling-point solvent that facilitates the homogenous nucleation and growth of the nanocrystals. The mixture is heated under vacuum to 120°C to remove residual moisture and to form a clear, homogeneous solution, after which the temperature is lowered to room temperature and a solution of ammonium fluoride in methanol is rapidly injected into the flask. The immediate formation of a white precipitate confirms the initial formation of amorphous sodium yttrium fluoride, which is then slowly heated to 300°C over a period of 30 minutes to evaporate the methanol and degas the system. The temperature is then raised to between 315°C and 324°C and maintained for a duration of 10 to 100 minutes, during which time the amorphous material undergoes a phase transition to the thermodynamically stable hexagonal phase. The reaction time and temperature are carefully controlled to produce nanoparticles with a long-axis dimension between 15 nm and 30 nm and a short-axis dimension between 10 nm and 20 nm, as confirmed by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy. Longer reaction times and higher temperatures promote Ostwald ripening, resulting in larger particles with reduced surface defects and enhanced upconversion efficiency, while shorter times and lower temperatures yield smaller particles with higher surface-to-volume ratios that are more suitable for dye-sensitized energy transfer. After the reaction is complete, the solution is allowed to cool naturally to room temperature, and the nanocrystals are precipitated by the addition of excess ethanol, followed by centrifugation at 4,000 RPM for 10 minutes. The resulting pellet is washed twice with toluene or cyclohexane and re-precipitated with ethanol to remove unreacted precursors and excess ligands. The final product is dispersed in toluene or cyclohexane at a concentration of approximately 10 mg/mL and stored under inert atmosphere until further functionalization. The crystalline phase of the nanocrystals is confirmed by X-ray diffraction, which reveals sharp peaks corresponding to the hexagonal NaYF₄ structure (JCPDS card no. 16-0334), and the absence of cubic phase or amorphous signatures. The morphology and size distribution are further validated by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy, which reveals well-defined hexagonal rods with smooth facets and minimal aggregation. The nanocrystals exhibit strong upconversion luminescence under 975 nm or 801 nm excitation, with emission intensities that are at least tenfold greater than those of nanoparticles synthesized at lower temperatures, confirming the critical role of crystalline phase and particle size in determining upconversion efficiency.

### Encapsulation of Attached Dye

Following the covalent attachment of the infrared-absorbing dye to the amine-functionalized lanthanide-doped NaYF₄ nanocrystals, the resulting hybrid nanocomposite is encapsulated within a secondary silica shell to protect the dye from environmental degradation, prevent leaching during subsequent processing, and enable integration into solid-state films. The encapsulation process is carried out using a reverse microemulsion technique in which the dye-labeled nanoparticles are dispersed in a nonpolar solvent such as cyclohexane, stabilized by the surfactant Igepal CO-520, and subjected to the controlled addition of a second siloxane monomer that lacks an amine functional group. The siloxane monomer is selected from the group consisting of tetraethyl orthosilicate, methyltrimethoxysilane, and phenyltrimethoxysilane, each of which contributes to the formation of a dense, cross-linked silica network that surrounds the nanoparticle without interfering with the underlying dye-sensitizer energy transfer. The reverse microemulsion is formed by stirring 0.7 mL of Igepal CO-520 into 40 mL of cyclohexane, followed by the addition of 30 mL of a dispersion containing the dye-labeled nanoparticles, resulting in the formation of nanoscale water-in-oil droplets that encapsulate individual nanoparticles. A solution of the second siloxane monomer in cyclohexane is then slowly added to the microemulsion under continuous stirring, and the mixture is maintained at room temperature for a period of 2 to 4 hours to allow for the hydrolysis and condensation of the siloxane monomer into a continuous silica shell. The thickness of the outer silica shell is controlled by varying the molar ratio of the second siloxane monomer to the nanoparticle surface area, with typical shell thicknesses ranging from 5 nm to 15 nm, as estimated from transmission electron microscopy and corroborated by thermogravimetric analysis. The encapsulation process is conducted under ambient conditions to avoid thermal degradation of the dye, and the resulting silica shell is characterized by its transparency to infrared and visible light, its chemical inertness, and its ability to prevent oxidation and photobleaching of the attached dye molecules. The encapsulated nanocomposite is then precipitated by the addition of excess ethanol, centrifuged, and washed with a 50:50 mixture of ethanol and deionized water to remove residual surfactant and unreacted siloxane. The final product is dispersed in toluene or ethanol and stored under inert atmosphere. The encapsulation process significantly enhances the long-term stability of the nanocomposite, allowing it to retain its upconversion properties for periods exceeding six months under ambient conditions, and enables its integration into polymer matrices, glass substrates, and thin-film devices without loss of optical activity.

### Attachment of Dye to Encapsulated Nanoparticle

The covalent attachment of the infrared-absorbing dye to the amine-functionalized lanthanide-doped NaYF₄ nanocrystals is achieved through a mild, solution-phase reaction that forms a stable amide bond between the dye molecule and the surface-bound amine group. The dye molecules used in this process are selected from the group consisting of DyLight 800, DY-831, DLS-776A, and NIR-797, each of which contains a reactive functional group capable of forming an amide bond with a primary, secondary, or tertiary amine. The NHS ester group on DyLight 800 and DY-831, the isothiocyanate group on NIR-797, and the carboxylic acid group on DLS-776A are each capable of reacting with the amine group on the silica shell under controlled conditions to form a covalent linkage. The attachment reaction is carried out by dispersing the amine-functionalized nanoparticles in absolute ethanol or dimethyl sulfoxide at a concentration of approximately 5 mg/mL and adding a solution of the dye in the same solvent at a dye-to-nanoparticle molar ratio between 100:1 and 300:1. The reaction mixture is stirred at room temperature for 20 minutes if the dye bears an NHS ester group, or at 35°C for 1 to 2 hours if the dye bears a carboxylic acid group, to ensure complete amide bond formation. The use of secondary or tertiary amine-functionalized siloxanes, such as [3-(methylamino)propyl]trimethoxysilane or (3,3-dimethylaminopropyl)trimethoxysilane, is preferred over primary amines in cases where the dye molecule is chemically sensitive to nucleophilic attack, as the sterically hindered amines reduce the rate of side reactions while still permitting efficient coupling. The reaction is monitored by measuring the absorbance of the supernatant after centrifugation, with the absence of dye absorption indicating complete removal of unbound dye. The resulting dye-labeled nanocomposite is washed repeatedly with ethanol until the eluent is optically clear, confirming the removal of all noncovalently bound dye. The dye loading is quantified by comparing the absorbance of the nanocomposite dispersion to a calibration curve generated from known concentrations of the free dye, and is found to range from 40 to 80 dye molecules per nanoparticle, depending on the size of the nanoparticle and the reactivity of the dye functional group. The covalent nature of the linkage is confirmed by the persistence of upconversion luminescence after prolonged exposure to organic solvents, elevated temperatures, and ultraviolet irradiation, all of which would cause detachment of physisorbed dyes. The resulting nanocomposite exhibits a strong, broadband absorption in the infrared region between 750 nm and 850 nm, with extinction coefficients exceeding 200,000 M⁻¹·cm⁻¹, and produces distinct upconversion emission spectra under excitation at wavelengths corresponding to the dye absorption maximum, demonstrating the successful transfer of energy from the dye to the lanthanide ions.

### Fabrication of Films

The hybrid nanocomposite materials described herein are integrated into solid-state optical devices through the fabrication of thin, transparent films that retain the wavelength-selective upconversion properties of the dispersed nanoparticles. The film fabrication process begins with the dispersion of the dye-labeled or silica-encapsulated nanocomposite in a solvent selected from the group consisting of ethanol, toluene, cyclohexane, and dimethylformamide, each of which is chosen based on its compatibility with the surface chemistry of the nanoparticles and its volatility to enable uniform film formation. The dispersion is sonicated for 30 minutes to ensure homogeneity and to break up any residual aggregates, and is then deposited onto a transparent substrate selected from the group consisting of glass, quartz, polyethylene terephthalate, and polycarbonate using a method selected from the group consisting of spin coating, drop casting, dip coating, and spray coating. For spin coating, the dispersion is applied at a volume of 100 to 500 μL and spun at 1,000 to 3,000 RPM for 30 to 60 seconds to produce a film with thickness between 50 nm and 500 nm. For drop casting, the dispersion is deposited as a 10 to 50 μL droplet and allowed to evaporate slowly under ambient conditions to form a uniform, non-aggregated layer. For dip coating, the substrate is immersed in the dispersion and withdrawn at a rate of 1 to 5 mm/s to produce a thin, continuous film. The solvent is allowed to evaporate completely under a nitrogen atmosphere at room temperature, and the resulting film is optionally cured at a temperature below 150°C for 1 to 2 hours to enhance adhesion to the substrate and improve mechanical stability. The films are characterized by their optical transparency in the visible region, their homogeneity under optical microscopy, and their ability to produce intense, spectrally distinct upconversion luminescence when exposed to infrared radiation at wavelengths corresponding to the absorption maximum of the attached dye. The films exhibit no evidence of nanoparticle aggregation, phase separation, or quenching, and retain their upconversion properties for extended periods under ambient conditions. When illuminated with a 785 nm diode laser, films containing DyLight 800-functionalized nanoparticles emit a bright red luminescence centered at 650 nm, while films containing DY-831-functionalized nanoparticles emit a green luminescence centered at 540 nm under 819 nm excitation, demonstrating the ability to generate color-coded responses to different infrared wavelengths. The films are suitable for integration into passive infrared detection arrays, optical sensors, security inks, and wearable indicators, providing a visible, real-time response to infrared radiation without the need for electronic components, power sources, or signal processing units.
