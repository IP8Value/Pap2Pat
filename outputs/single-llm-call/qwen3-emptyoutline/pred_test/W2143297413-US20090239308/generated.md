# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The quantification of nucleic acid molecules at the single-molecule level has long been a challenge in molecular biology, particularly when precision, reproducibility, and statistical rigor are required. Traditional polymerase chain reaction (PCR) methods, including real-time quantitative PCR, rely on amplification kinetics and threshold cycle (Ct) values to infer the initial quantity of target sequences. However, these methods are inherently relative, susceptible to amplification efficiency variations, and limited in their ability to distinguish small differences in copy number, particularly when the fold-change is less than two-fold. These limitations become especially problematic in the context of copy number variation (CNV) analysis, where subtle gains or losses of genomic segments—ranging from hundreds of base pairs to several kilobases—can have profound implications for disease susceptibility, developmental disorders, cancer progression, and pharmacogenomic responses. While array-based technologies such as comparative genomic hybridization (array-CGH) and single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) microarrays offer genome-wide coverage, they lack the resolution and sensitivity necessary to detect small CNVs or to accurately quantify copy number in heterogeneous samples, such as those derived from tumor biopsies or mosaic tissues. Furthermore, these platforms are often unable to distinguish between diploid, triploid, or aneuploid states with high confidence when the copy number difference is marginal, and they require complex data normalization procedures that introduce additional sources of error.

Digital PCR (dPCR) emerged as a paradigm shift in nucleic acid quantification by transforming the amplification process from a bulk, population-based measurement into a series of binary, single-molecule reactions. In conventional dPCR, serial dilutions of a DNA sample are distributed across hundreds or thousands of individual reaction vessels, such as microfluidic droplets or micro-wells, such that each vessel contains either zero or one target molecule with high probability. After thermal cycling, each vessel is scored as positive or negative based on the presence or absence of fluorescence signal, and the initial concentration of the target is estimated using Poisson statistics. This approach eliminates the need for external standards, reduces the impact of inhibitors, and enables absolute quantification without reliance on amplification efficiency assumptions. However, prior implementations of dPCR were constrained by low throughput, manual handling, inconsistent partitioning, and limited multiplexing capability. The introduction of integrated nanofluidic biochips, such as the digital array, revolutionized this field by enabling the simultaneous, automated partitioning of a single sample into hundreds of identical nanoliter-volume chambers through precisely engineered microfluidic networks of channels and valves. Each panel of the digital array contains 765 reaction chambers, each with a volume of approximately six nanoliters, allowing for the parallel processing of up to twelve panels in a single run. This architecture not only increases throughput and reproducibility but also ensures uniform reaction conditions across all chambers, minimizing technical variability.

Despite these advances, a critical gap remained in the analytical framework for interpreting dPCR data in the context of copy number variation. While the estimation of absolute concentration for a single target gene from the number of positive chambers was well understood under Poisson assumptions, the accurate determination of the ratio between two target concentrations—such as a test gene and a reference gene—remained statistically underdeveloped. Existing methods often relied on simple division of the observed positive chamber counts, followed by propagation of error using Gaussian approximations. Such approaches are fundamentally flawed because they assume that the sampling distributions of the positive chamber counts are normal, which is not valid when the number of positive chambers is low or when the underlying concentration is near the detection limit. Moreover, these methods fail to account for the nonlinear relationship between the observed proportion of positive chambers and the true molecular concentration, which arises from the Poisson distribution governing molecule partitioning. As a result, confidence intervals derived from naive error propagation are systematically biased, often underestimating uncertainty and leading to false conclusions regarding the presence or absence of copy number alterations.

Previous attempts to address this problem employed Bayesian methodologies, which introduced prior probability distributions over the unknown concentrations to derive posterior credible intervals. While mathematically elegant, these approaches require assumptions about the prior distribution of molecular concentrations, which may not be biologically justified or empirically verifiable in clinical or diagnostic settings. Furthermore, Bayesian methods do not provide classical confidence intervals, which are the standard in regulatory and analytical contexts where the parameter of interest is considered a fixed, unknown quantity rather than a random variable. The need for a method that is both statistically rigorous, free of arbitrary assumptions, and directly interpretable in terms of classical confidence intervals became evident. There was a clear and unmet demand for a computational framework capable of taking the raw counts of positive chambers for two independently amplified genes, accounting for the inherent stochasticity of molecular partitioning, and producing a point estimate of their concentration ratio along with a statistically valid 95% confidence interval that reflects the true uncertainty in the measurement.

The invention disclosed herein fulfills this need by introducing a novel, non-parametric, and mathematically grounded method for determining the ratio of concentrations of two nucleic acid targets in a digital array platform. This method does not assume normality of the underlying sampling distributions, does not rely on Bayesian priors, and does not depend on approximations that break down at low counts or extreme ratios. Instead, it leverages the joint empirical distribution of the observed positive chamber counts for both targets and applies a numerical integration technique derived from the geometric interpretation of Fieller’s theorem to compute the exact sampling distribution of the ratio estimator. The method is generalizable to any number of panels, any target-to-reference gene ratio, and any level of molecular concentration, and it produces confidence intervals that are empirically validated through extensive simulation studies. Importantly, the method is implemented as a software algorithm that can be integrated directly into the data analysis pipeline of commercial digital array systems, enabling real-time, automated, and statistically robust CNV calling without requiring manual intervention or external calibration standards. This represents a fundamental advancement over prior art, which either lacked statistical rigor, relied on inappropriate assumptions, or failed to provide confidence intervals for the ratio of concentrations in a multiplexed digital PCR context. The invention thus enables the precise, reliable, and clinically actionable detection of copy number variations that were previously indistinguishable due to analytical noise or statistical uncertainty.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a method, system, and computer-readable medium for determining a ratio of concentrations of two nucleic acid targets in a digital array platform, and for computing a statistically valid confidence interval for that ratio, using a non-parametric numerical algorithm that does not rely on assumptions of normality, prior distributions, or asymptotic approximations. The invention is specifically designed for use in copy number variation (CNV) analysis, where the relative abundance of a test gene compared to a reference gene must be quantified with high precision and statistical certainty. The method operates on data obtained from a digital array biochip comprising a plurality of reaction chambers, each of a fixed and known volume, wherein a sample containing nucleic acid molecules of the two targets is partitioned into the chambers, amplified via polymerase chain reaction, and scored for fluorescence positivity. The method comprises the steps of: (a) counting the number of positive chambers for the first target gene and the number of positive chambers for the second target gene across one or more panels of the digital array; (b) estimating the concentration of each target gene from the respective positive chamber counts using a nonlinear transformation based on Poisson statistics; (c) constructing an empirical joint probability distribution of the estimated concentrations of the two targets based on the observed chamber counts; (d) numerically integrating over the joint distribution to compute the probability density function of the ratio of the two concentrations; and (e) determining a confidence interval for the ratio by identifying the range of ratio values that encompass a specified probability mass, typically 95%. The method is implemented as a software algorithm executable on a computer system coupled to the digital array platform, and it produces output that includes a point estimate of the concentration ratio and a confidence interval whose bounds are derived directly from the empirical data without reliance on parametric assumptions.

The invention further comprises a system for performing the method, comprising a digital array biochip with a plurality of reaction chambers, a thermal cycler configured to perform polymerase chain reaction on the partitioned samples, a fluorescence imaging system capable of detecting and recording the presence or absence of signal in each chamber, a data acquisition module for converting raw fluorescence data into binary positive/negative calls for each chamber, and a processor executing the algorithm to compute the concentration ratio and its confidence interval. The system may be integrated into a single instrument platform or operate as a standalone software module compatible with existing digital array hardware. The invention also encompasses a computer-readable storage medium encoded with instructions that, when executed by a processor, cause the processor to perform the steps of the method, including the numerical integration of the joint distribution of concentration estimates to determine the sampling distribution of the ratio.

The key innovation of the invention lies in its ability to compute a confidence interval for the ratio of two concentrations without assuming that the underlying sampling distributions are normal or symmetric. Traditional methods, which rely on error propagation formulas derived from Gaussian approximations, fail when the number of positive chambers is low, when the concentration ratio is extreme, or when the variance of the estimates is asymmetric. In such cases, these methods produce confidence intervals that are either too narrow, leading to false positives, or fail entirely when the confidence region intersects the axis of the reference gene concentration, resulting in infinite upper bounds. The present invention overcomes these limitations by treating the problem as a geometric one: the joint distribution of the two concentration estimates forms a two-dimensional probability surface, and the ratio of the concentrations corresponds to the slope of a line drawn from the origin to a point on that surface. By integrating the probability mass contained within wedges of increasing angular width centered on the estimated ratio, the algorithm constructs the exact sampling distribution of the ratio estimator. This approach is mathematically rigorous, computationally robust, and empirically validated across a wide range of concentrations and ratios, including those near the detection limit and those exceeding three-fold differences. The method has been shown through simulation and experimental validation to produce confidence intervals that contain the true ratio in 95% of cases, as predicted by classical statistical theory, even when the underlying distributions are highly skewed or multimodal.

The invention also provides a novel method for determining the optimal number of panels to use in a CNV experiment to achieve a desired level of precision. By simulating the effect of increasing the number of panels on the width of the confidence interval, the algorithm can predict the minimum number of panels required to distinguish between two hypothesized copy number states with a specified level of statistical power. This feature enables users to design experiments with confidence that the results will be interpretable and clinically meaningful, without the need for costly pilot studies or redundant measurements. The invention further includes a method for correcting for systematic biases that may arise from differential amplification efficiency between the two targets, by incorporating a calibration factor derived from control samples with known concentration ratios. This calibration factor is computed independently and applied as a multiplicative adjustment to the ratio estimate, ensuring that the final result reflects the true genomic copy number rather than technical artifacts of the amplification process.

The invention is applicable to any digital array platform that partitions a sample into a plurality of discrete reaction chambers and quantifies target molecules via endpoint fluorescence detection. It is particularly suited for applications in clinical diagnostics, oncology, prenatal testing, and genetic screening, where the detection of small copy number alterations—such as microdeletions, microduplications, or subchromosomal rearrangements—can have significant diagnostic, prognostic, or therapeutic implications. The method eliminates the need for reference standards, reduces inter-assay variability, and provides a level of statistical certainty that is unmatched by any prior art method for digital PCR-based CNV analysis. The invention thus represents a transformative advancement in the field of molecular diagnostics, enabling accurate, reliable, and legally defensible quantification of copy number variation in a manner that meets the highest standards of analytical rigor and regulatory compliance.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF SPECIFIC EMBODIMENTS

The invention is embodied in a method for determining the ratio of concentrations of two nucleic acid targets in a digital array platform, and for computing a statistically valid confidence interval for that ratio, using a non-parametric numerical algorithm that operates on the raw counts of positive reaction chambers. The method is implemented in a system comprising a digital array biochip, a thermal cycler, a fluorescence imaging system, a data acquisition unit, and a computing device executing a software algorithm. The digital array biochip is fabricated from a silicon or polymer substrate and contains a network of microfluidic channels and valves that, upon actuation, partition a liquid sample into a predetermined number of discrete reaction chambers. Each chamber has a fixed volume of approximately six nanoliters, and a single panel of the biochip contains exactly 765 such chambers. The sample to be analyzed is a nucleic acid mixture containing target sequences from two distinct genes: a test gene, whose copy number is to be determined, and a reference gene, whose copy number is assumed to be diploid and stable across the sample population. The sample is mixed with a PCR master mix containing primers and fluorescent probes specific to each gene, such that the test gene is labeled with one fluorophore (e.g., FAM) and the reference gene with another (e.g., VIC). The mixture is then loaded into the digital array, where the microfluidic system distributes the sample uniformly across all chambers of one or more panels. The array is sealed and subjected to thermal cycling, during which the target sequences are amplified exponentially if present in the chamber. At the end of the amplification cycles, the fluorescence intensity of each chamber is measured using an optical detection system capable of resolving the two distinct fluorophores independently. A binary classification algorithm then determines whether each chamber is positive or negative for each target based on a predefined fluorescence threshold, resulting in two sets of binary data: one indicating the number of positive chambers for the test gene, and the other indicating the number of positive chambers for the reference gene.

The first step of the method is to count the total number of positive chambers for each target across all panels used in the experiment. Let H₁ denote the total number of positive chambers for the test gene, and H₂ denote the total number of positive chambers for the reference gene. Let C denote the total number of chambers across all panels, which is equal to 765 multiplied by the number of panels used. For example, if five panels are analyzed, C equals 3,825. The observed proportion of positive chambers for the test gene is then calculated as p̂₁ = H₁ / C, and for the reference gene as p̂₂ = H₂ / C. These proportions are not direct estimates of the molecular concentrations, because the relationship between the probability of a chamber being positive and the true concentration of molecules per chamber is nonlinear. This relationship arises from the stochastic nature of molecular partitioning, which follows a Poisson distribution. The probability that a chamber contains zero molecules of a target is given by e^(-λ), where λ is the true average number of molecules per chamber. Therefore, the probability that a chamber contains one or more molecules—that is, the probability of a positive chamber—is p = 1 - e^(-λ). Solving for λ, the true concentration is expressed as λ = -ln(1 - p). This transformation is critical, because it accounts for the fact that multiple molecules may be present in a single chamber, leading to an undercount of the true number of molecules when only the number of positive chambers is observed. The method therefore computes an estimated concentration for the test gene as λ̂₁ = -ln(1 - p̂₁) and for the reference gene as λ̂₂ = -ln(1 - p̂₂). These estimates are unbiased in the limit of large C, and their sampling distributions are derived from the binomial distribution of the positive chamber counts, which, for sufficiently large C, can be approximated as normal. However, the transformation from p̂ to λ̂ introduces nonlinearity, and the resulting sampling distributions of λ̂₁ and λ̂₂ are not normal, nor are they symmetric, particularly when the concentrations are low or when the number of positive chambers is small.

The second step of the method is to construct an empirical joint probability distribution of the estimated concentrations λ̂₁ and λ̂₂. This is accomplished by simulating the partitioning process using a Monte Carlo algorithm. For each panel, the algorithm generates a large number of synthetic datasets by randomly sampling from the binomial distribution with parameters C and p̂₁ for the test gene, and C and p̂₂ for the reference gene. For each synthetic dataset, the algorithm computes the corresponding λ̂₁ and λ̂₂ using the nonlinear transformation. After generating tens of thousands of such synthetic pairs (λ̂₁, λ̂₂), the algorithm constructs a two-dimensional histogram representing the joint probability density function of the two concentration estimates. This histogram is discretized into fine bins, typically with a bin width of 0.01 in both dimensions, to ensure sufficient resolution for subsequent integration. The resulting distribution captures the full variability of the concentration estimates, including any asymmetry, skewness, or multimodality that may arise due to low counts or extreme ratios. This step is critical because it does not assume any parametric form for the distribution, such as bivariate normality, and instead relies entirely on the empirical data generated from the observed positive chamber counts.

The third step of the method is to compute the sampling distribution of the ratio estimator r̂ = λ̂₁ / λ̂₂. This is achieved by numerically integrating the joint probability density function over angular wedges in the two-dimensional plane defined by λ̂₁ and λ̂₂. For each possible value of the ratio r, the algorithm defines a wedge in the (λ̂₁, λ̂₂) plane bounded by two lines emanating from the origin with slopes r - Δr/2 and r + Δr/2, where Δr is a small increment in the ratio, typically 0.001. The algorithm then sums the probability mass contained within each wedge, which corresponds to the probability that the ratio r̂ falls within the interval [r - Δr/2, r + Δr/2]. This process is repeated for a range of r values from 0.01 to 10.0 in increments of Δr, resulting in a discrete probability density function q(r) for the ratio estimator. This function represents the complete sampling distribution of the ratio under the observed experimental conditions, and it is entirely data-driven, with no assumptions about the underlying distributions of λ̂₁ or λ̂₂. The algorithm ensures numerical stability by avoiding division by zero: if λ̂₂ is zero in any synthetic dataset, that dataset is discarded, and the algorithm continues until a sufficient number of valid pairs are obtained. The resulting q(r) is normalized so that the total area under the curve equals one.

The fourth step of the method is to determine a confidence interval for the ratio r = λ₁ / λ₂. This is done by identifying the range of ratio values that contain 95% of the probability mass in the distribution q(r). The algorithm begins by sorting the discrete values of r in ascending order and computing the cumulative probability distribution Q(r) = ∫₀ʳ q(x) dx. It then finds the lower bound r_low such that Q(r_low) = 0.025 and the upper bound r_high such that Q(r_high) = 0.975. These bounds define the 95% confidence interval [r_low, r_high] for the true ratio. The algorithm is designed to handle edge cases: if the cumulative probability never reaches 0.025, the lower bound is set to zero; if the cumulative probability never reaches 0.975, the upper bound is set to a maximum value determined by the resolution of the simulation, typically 100. This approach ensures that the confidence interval is always valid, even when the reference gene concentration is very low or when the ratio is extremely high. The confidence interval is not symmetric, and its width depends on the precision of the concentration estimates, which in turn depends on the number of positive chambers observed and the total number of panels used. The algorithm also computes the point estimate of the ratio as the mode of q(r), which corresponds to the most likely value of the true ratio given the data.

The fifth step of the method is to output the results in a format suitable for interpretation and decision-making. The output includes the point estimate of the ratio, the 95% confidence interval, the number of panels used, the total number of positive chambers for each target, and a quality metric indicating the reliability of the estimate. The quality metric is derived from the width of the confidence interval: if the interval width is less than 0.5, the result is classified as "high confidence"; if between 0.5 and 1.0, as "moderate confidence"; and if greater than 1.0, as "low confidence." The algorithm may also generate a graphical representation of the joint distribution, the wedge integration process, and the resulting ratio distribution, which can be displayed on a user interface for visual validation. The output is stored in a digital file format compatible with laboratory information management systems and may be transmitted electronically to a diagnostic reporting system.

The invention further includes a method for optimizing the number of panels required to achieve a desired level of precision. This is accomplished by simulating the effect of increasing the number of panels on the width of the confidence interval. For a given expected ratio and expected concentration of the reference gene, the algorithm generates synthetic datasets for increasing values of C, computes the resulting confidence interval width for each, and identifies the minimum number of panels required to achieve a pre-specified maximum interval width, such as 0.3. This feature allows users to design experiments with statistical power in mind, ensuring that the results will be interpretable and clinically actionable without unnecessary replication or resource expenditure.

The invention also includes a calibration procedure to correct for systematic biases arising from differences in amplification efficiency between the two targets. This is achieved by analyzing control samples with known concentration ratios, such as synthetic DNA constructs spiked into a reference genome at known multiples. For each known ratio, the algorithm computes the estimated ratio and records the bias as the difference between the estimated and known values. A calibration curve is then fitted to these bias values as a function of the true ratio, and the resulting function is used to adjust the estimated ratio from unknown samples. The calibration is applied as a multiplicative correction factor, ensuring that the final reported ratio reflects the true genomic copy number rather than technical artifacts of the amplification process. The calibration is performed once per assay design and is validated using independent control samples.

The invention has been validated through extensive simulation studies and experimental testing. In simulations, synthetic datasets were generated with known concentration ratios ranging from 0.5 to 4.0, and the algorithm was applied to compute the 95% confidence interval for each. In 94.8% of cases, the true ratio fell within the computed interval, confirming the statistical validity of the method. In experimental validation, synthetic RPP30 constructs were spiked into human genomic DNA at known ratios of 1:1, 1:1.5, 1:2, 1:2.5, 1:3, and 1:3.5, and the digital array was used to measure the ratio of RPP30 to RNase P. The algorithm correctly estimated each ratio, with the true value always falling within the 95% confidence interval. When only one panel was used, the confidence intervals overlapped for adjacent ratios, but with three or more panels, the intervals became sufficiently narrow to distinguish between all tested ratios. The method was also tested under conditions of low template concentration, high background noise, and partial amplification failure, and it continued to produce valid confidence intervals, demonstrating robustness to experimental variability.

The invention is implemented as a software module that can be integrated into existing digital array platforms, such as the Fluidigm BioMark system, without requiring hardware modifications. The software is written in a high-performance programming language and is optimized for parallel processing, allowing the numerical integration to be completed in under five minutes on a standard desktop computer. The software includes a user interface that guides the user through data import, panel selection, quality control, and result interpretation. The software also includes a database of precomputed calibration curves for common target-reference gene pairs, enabling plug-and-play operation for standardized assays. The invention may be used in clinical laboratories for the diagnosis of genetic disorders, in research settings for the study of copy number variation in cancer, and in regulatory testing for the validation of genomic assays. It provides a level of statistical rigor and analytical certainty that is unmatched by any prior method for digital PCR-based copy number analysis, and it enables the reliable detection of copy number alterations that were previously obscured by analytical noise or statistical uncertainty. The invention thus represents a fundamental advancement in molecular diagnostics, transforming digital PCR from a qualitative counting tool into a quantitative, statistically validated platform for genomic analysis.
