# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The early detection of cancer remains one of the most critical challenges in modern oncology, as the majority of cancer-related deaths occur not from the initial presence of malignant cells, but from the failure to identify disease at a stage when therapeutic intervention can still be curative. Despite decades of public health initiatives promoting population-wide screening for common malignancies such as colorectal, pancreatic, and lung cancer, adherence to existing screening protocols remains suboptimal, often due to the invasiveness, discomfort, cost, or logistical complexity of current methods. Colonoscopy, for instance, while highly effective, requires bowel preparation, sedation, and specialized personnel, leading to low compliance rates, particularly among asymptomatic individuals. Similarly, fecal immunochemical testing, though less invasive, suffers from limited sensitivity for precancerous lesions and early-stage tumors, and its performance is highly dependent on patient adherence to repeated testing schedules. These limitations underscore a pressing need for a non-invasive, scalable, and highly accurate screening modality capable of detecting cancer before the emergence of clinical symptoms or metastatic spread.

Recent advances in the analysis of circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) have generated significant interest as a potential platform for liquid biopsy-based cancer detection. cfDNA, which consists of small fragments of DNA released into the bloodstream through natural cellular turnover and pathological processes, has been shown to contain genetic and epigenetic signatures reflective of their tissue of origin. In patients with cancer, a subset of this cfDNA originates from tumor cells and is referred to as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA). The presence of tumor-specific mutations, structural rearrangements, and copy number alterations in ctDNA has been exploited in several diagnostic approaches, particularly in advanced-stage disease where tumor burden is high and ctDNA fraction is more readily detectable. However, in early-stage cancer, where tumor mass is minimal and shedding of DNA into circulation is sparse, the concentration of ctDNA may fall below the limit of detection of even the most sensitive sequencing technologies. In such cases, ctDNA may constitute less than 0.01% of the total cfDNA pool, rendering mutation-based detection strategies unreliable for population-level screening.

Moreover, the biological landscape of cfDNA is not limited to tumor-derived fragments. A growing body of evidence suggests that cancer induces systemic physiological changes that alter the behavior, gene expression, and epigenetic state of non-tumor cells, including immune cells, platelets, and stromal cells. These alterations, sometimes described as “tumor education,” manifest as changes in the fragmentation patterns, methylation profiles, and transcriptional signatures of cfDNA derived from these non-malignant cells. For example, platelets in cancer patients have been shown to carry distinct mRNA profiles that differ from those in healthy individuals, likely due to the uptake of tumor-derived RNA or the modulation of platelet RNA processing by tumor-secreted factors. Similarly, shifts in the relative abundance and apoptotic dynamics of circulating leukocyte populations have been correlated with cancer progression, suggesting that the entire hematopoietic system may be reprogrammed in response to the presence of a tumor. These systemic changes are reflected in the cfDNA landscape, as the fragmentation patterns of cfDNA are known to mirror the chromatin architecture and nucleosome positioning of their parent cells. Thus, the collective signal embedded in cfDNA—spanning both tumor-derived and non-tumor-derived fragments—may provide a more comprehensive and sensitive indicator of malignancy than ctDNA alone.

Despite the promise of this broader analytical approach, the interpretation of cfDNA signals at the genome-wide scale presents formidable technical and computational challenges. The human genome contains approximately three billion base pairs, and the cfDNA fragments derived from it are typically less than 200 base pairs in length. When sequenced at low to moderate depth—sufficient for cost-effective population screening but insufficient for reliable detection of single-nucleotide variants—the resulting data consist of millions of short reads distributed across the entire genome. Traditional methods of analysis, which focus on predefined genomic regions or known cancer-associated mutations, are ill-suited to extract meaningful patterns from such high-dimensional, noisy, and heterogeneous data. Furthermore, the presence of confounding variables—such as age, sex, batch-to-batch variation in laboratory processing, differences in sample collection protocols across institutions, and temporal drift in sequencing performance—can introduce spurious correlations that mimic disease-associated signals. Without rigorous control for these variables, machine learning models trained on such data risk learning artifacts of sample processing rather than true biological signatures of cancer, leading to models that perform well in discovery cohorts but fail to generalize to independent populations.

To address these challenges, a novel computational framework has been developed that enables the unbiased discovery of cancer-associated patterns in whole-genome cfDNA data while explicitly accounting for known biological and technical confounders. This framework leverages high-throughput, low-coverage whole-genome sequencing of plasma-derived cfDNA, coupled with a sophisticated machine learning architecture trained using cross-validation strategies specifically designed to isolate disease-relevant signals from confounding influences. Rather than relying on targeted sequencing or predefined biomarkers, the method treats the entire genome as a feature space, encoding the number of cfDNA fragments overlapping each annotated protein-coding gene as a numerical feature. These features are normalized to correct for technical biases such as GC content, read depth, and fragment length, ensuring that the resulting vector representation reflects true biological variation rather than sequencing artifacts. The model then employs a support vector machine classifier, trained using multiple cross-validation protocols that systematically hold out confounding variables—such as age, processing batch, institutional source, and date of sample processing—to evaluate whether the learned patterns generalize beyond these sources of variability.

The results demonstrate that this approach achieves high diagnostic accuracy for early-stage colorectal cancer, with sensitivity exceeding 85% at 85% specificity in standard cross-validation settings. Crucially, when the same model is evaluated using cross-validation procedures that control for confounding variables, performance remains robust, with sensitivity ranging from 71% to 85% depending on the specific confounder being held constant. This indicates that the model is not merely memorizing batch effects or demographic correlations but is instead identifying intrinsic biological signatures associated with the presence of cancer. Furthermore, when tumor fraction is estimated independently using copy number variation analysis, the model’s performance exceeds that of tumor fraction alone, suggesting that non-tumor-derived signals contribute meaningfully to diagnostic accuracy. These findings challenge the prevailing assumption that ctDNA must be the dominant driver of liquid biopsy performance and instead support a paradigm in which the collective state of the circulating genome—encompassing both malignant and non-malignant cellular contributions—provides a more sensitive and generalizable indicator of malignancy.

This invention represents a fundamental shift in the conceptual framework for blood-based cancer screening. Rather than seeking to isolate rare tumor-derived fragments from an overwhelming background of normal DNA, the method recognizes that cancer is a systemic disease that alters the biology of multiple cell types, and that these alterations are encoded in the fragmentation and abundance patterns of cfDNA across the entire genome. By combining low-cost, scalable sequencing with machine learning models trained under rigorous confounder-controlled validation, this approach enables the detection of early-stage cancer with high accuracy, without requiring prior knowledge of tumor-specific mutations or the use of ultra-high-depth sequencing. The method is inherently scalable, applicable to any cancer type for which sufficient cfDNA data can be collected, and adaptable to the inclusion of additional analytes such as proteins, RNA, or methylation markers to further enhance performance. Its implementation promises to transform cancer screening from a fragmented, low-adherence, and late-detection paradigm into a simple, blood-based, population-wide test capable of identifying malignancy at its most treatable stages.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The invention disclosed herein is a method for the detection of cancer in a subject using a machine learning-based analysis of circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) derived from a biological sample, wherein the method comprises the steps of obtaining a biological sample from the subject, extracting cfDNA from the sample, sequencing the cfDNA to generate a plurality of sequence reads, aligning the sequence reads to a reference genome, generating a feature vector that represents the number of cfDNA fragments overlapping each annotated protein-coding gene in the genome, normalizing the feature vector to correct for technical biases, and applying a trained machine learning classifier to the normalized feature vector to determine a cancer probability score. The machine learning classifier is trained on a dataset comprising cfDNA profiles from subjects with and without cancer, wherein the training process employs cross-validation procedures that explicitly control for confounding variables including age, processing batch, institutional source, and date of sample processing to ensure that the classifier learns disease-relevant biological signals rather than technical or demographic artifacts. The feature vector is generated by counting the number of cfDNA fragments that map to each protein-coding gene region, with each gene serving as a discrete genomic bin, and the normalization process includes trimming the top and bottom ten percent of feature counts to compute a trimmed mean, followed by Loess-based correction for GC bias. The machine learning classifier is implemented as a support vector machine with a radial basis function kernel, although other classification algorithms such as logistic regression may be employed. The classifier is trained using multiple cross-validation strategies, including k-fold, binned-age, k-batch, ordered k-batch, and balanced k-batch cross-validation, each designed to isolate the effect of a specific confounding variable. The output of the classifier is a continuous probability score indicating the likelihood that the subject has cancer, which may be thresholded to produce a binary classification result. The method is applicable to the detection of early-stage cancer, including stages I and II, and is particularly effective for cancers such as colorectal cancer, pancreatic cancer, and lung cancer, where traditional screening methods are inadequate. The biological sample is preferably plasma obtained from a peripheral blood draw, and the cfDNA is extracted using a magnetic bead-based isolation protocol. The sequencing is performed using paired-end, low-coverage whole-genome sequencing at approximately nine-fold coverage, and the reference genome used is the human reference genome assembly GRCh38. The method does not require prior knowledge of tumor-specific mutations, copy number alterations, or methylation patterns, and instead relies on the collective, genome-wide fragmentation profile of cfDNA to infer cancer status. The trained classifier is stored in a digital format and may be deployed in a clinical laboratory setting via a software interface that accepts raw sequencing data as input and returns a diagnostic probability score. The invention further encompasses a system for performing the method, comprising a sequencing instrument, a data processing unit configured to generate and normalize the feature vector, and a computing device executing the machine learning classifier. The invention also includes a computer-readable medium storing instructions that, when executed by a processor, cause the processor to perform the steps of the method. The method achieves diagnostic performance metrics including an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve greater than 0.80 and sensitivity greater than 70% at 85% specificity across multiple validation cohorts, even when confounding variables are rigorously controlled. The invention enables the detection of cancer in asymptomatic individuals without the need for invasive procedures, and is suitable for population-level screening due to its low cost, minimal sample volume requirements, and compatibility with existing laboratory infrastructure.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### I. CIRCULATING ANALYTES AND CELLULAR DECONSTRUCTION WITH BIOLOGICAL ASSAYS

The foundation of this invention lies in the recognition that circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) is not merely a passive byproduct of cellular turnover, but rather a dynamic and informative molecular archive that reflects the physiological and pathological state of the entire organism. While traditional liquid biopsy approaches have focused narrowly on the detection of tumor-specific mutations or copy number alterations within the minority fraction of cfDNA derived from malignant cells, this invention takes a fundamentally different approach by treating the entire cfDNA population as a composite signal that encodes systemic biological changes induced by cancer. The method does not assume that cancer can be detected solely through the presence of tumor-derived fragments; instead, it posits that the presence of a tumor alters the behavior of non-malignant cells throughout the body, and that these alterations are faithfully recorded in the fragmentation patterns, abundance profiles, and epigenetic signatures of cfDNA originating from those cells. This perspective is supported by emerging evidence that cancer induces a state of systemic immune modulation, platelet reprogramming, and stromal activation, all of which leave detectable traces in the bloodstream. For example, platelets in cancer patients have been shown to carry distinct mRNA profiles that differ significantly from those in healthy individuals, likely due to the uptake of tumor-derived RNA or the modulation of platelet RNA processing by tumor-secreted exosomes. Similarly, shifts in the relative proportions of circulating leukocyte subsets—such as a decrease in lymphocytes and an increase in monocytes—have been correlated with poor prognosis in multiple cancer types, suggesting that the hematopoietic system is actively reconfigured in response to malignancy. These changes are not isolated to a single cell type but represent a coordinated, organism-wide response that is reflected in the collective fragmentation landscape of cfDNA.

The cfDNA molecule itself is a product of chromatin structure and nucleosome positioning, and its fragmentation pattern is not random but rather follows the periodicity of nucleosome spacing and the accessibility of chromatin regions. In healthy individuals, the distribution of cfDNA fragment lengths and genomic origins is relatively stable and reproducible, shaped by the normal turnover of cells in tissues such as the liver, spleen, and bone marrow. In the presence of cancer, however, this baseline pattern is perturbed. Tumor cells, with their altered chromatin architecture and dysregulated apoptosis, release cfDNA fragments with distinct length profiles and genomic distributions. Simultaneously, non-tumor cells—particularly immune cells—undergo functional reprogramming in response to tumor-derived signals, leading to changes in their gene expression, epigenetic state, and cell death dynamics. These changes are reflected in the cfDNA fragments they release, which now carry a signature of their altered state. The result is a complex, high-dimensional signal that contains information not only about the tumor itself but also about the host’s systemic response to it. This signal is not easily interpretable through traditional hypothesis-driven approaches, which rely on predefined biomarkers or known cancer-associated mutations. Instead, it requires an unbiased, data-driven method capable of identifying subtle, distributed patterns across the entire genome. The invention leverages this insight by constructing a feature vector that captures the number of cfDNA fragments overlapping each annotated protein-coding gene, thereby encoding the relative abundance of fragments derived from each genomic region. This approach is fundamentally different from targeted sequencing methods, which focus on a few hundred to a few thousand base pairs of the genome, and from whole-genome bisulfite sequencing, which requires high coverage and complex bioinformatics to infer methylation status. Instead, the method relies on low-coverage sequencing to capture the broad, genome-wide distribution of fragment counts, treating each gene as a discrete bin that contributes to a high-dimensional vector representation of the sample.

The biological deconstruction of this signal is achieved through machine learning, which identifies patterns in the feature vector that are predictive of cancer status without requiring prior knowledge of the underlying biology. The model does not attempt to classify individual fragments as tumor-derived or non-tumor-derived; rather, it learns the collective statistical properties of the entire cfDNA profile that correlate with the presence of malignancy. This is a critical distinction: the method does not seek to isolate the tumor signal from the background noise, but instead recognizes that the background itself has been altered by the tumor. The resulting diagnostic signal is therefore not a single biomarker but a distributed, multi-feature signature that emerges from the interaction of multiple biological systems. This approach is analogous to the way the human immune system recognizes pathogens not by detecting a single molecular signature but by integrating signals from multiple receptors and cellular responses. Similarly, the machine learning model integrates information from thousands of genomic regions to form a coherent diagnostic decision. The power of this method lies in its ability to detect cancer even when the tumor-derived fraction is below the limit of detection of conventional methods, because the non-tumor-derived signal—altered by the tumor’s systemic influence—is sufficiently robust to serve as a proxy for malignancy. This is particularly important in early-stage cancer, where tumor burden is low and ctDNA levels are minimal, but where the host’s physiological response is already underway. The method thus transforms the problem of cancer detection from one of finding a needle in a haystack to one of recognizing that the entire haystack has changed shape. The invention further enables the discovery of novel biological insights by revealing which genomic regions contribute most to the diagnostic signal, thereby identifying genes and pathways that are most sensitive to the presence of cancer. These genes may not be directly mutated in the tumor, but they may be involved in immune regulation, apoptosis, or chromatin remodeling, providing new targets for mechanistic investigation and therapeutic intervention.

### II. SAMPLE PREPARATION

The sample preparation protocol is designed to ensure the integrity, consistency, and reproducibility of the cfDNA signal across diverse clinical and laboratory settings. The biological sample used in this invention is plasma, which is obtained from a peripheral blood draw using ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) as the anticoagulant. The use of EDTA is preferred because it effectively prevents coagulation while minimizing the release of genomic DNA from white blood cells, which could otherwise contaminate the cfDNA fraction. Following collection, the blood sample is processed within four hours to separate plasma from cellular components, using a two-step centrifugation protocol. The first centrifugation step is performed at 1,600 × g for ten minutes at room temperature to pellet the majority of blood cells, followed by a second centrifugation at 16,000 × g for ten minutes at 4°C to remove residual cellular debris and platelets. The resulting supernatant is carefully aspirated and aliquoted into cryogenic vials, which are immediately frozen at −80°C to preserve the cfDNA profile and prevent degradation. The use of a standardized, time-sensitive processing protocol is critical, as delays in processing or improper storage conditions can lead to the lysis of white blood cells and the introduction of genomic DNA contamination, which would obscure the true cfDNA signal. Each plasma sample is quantified for volume and stored under controlled conditions to ensure uniformity across the cohort.

The extraction of cfDNA from plasma is performed using a magnetic bead-based isolation kit, specifically the MagMAX cfDNA Isolation Kit (Applied Biosystems), which is optimized for the recovery of small DNA fragments in the range of 100 to 200 base pairs. The extraction protocol involves the binding of cfDNA to silica-coated magnetic beads under high-salt conditions, followed by washing steps to remove proteins, lipids, and other contaminants, and elution in a low-salt buffer to release the purified DNA. The entire process is carried out in a clean-room environment using aerosol-resistant pipette tips and dedicated equipment to prevent cross-contamination. The yield and fragment size distribution of the extracted cfDNA are assessed using a fluorometric assay and a bioanalyzer or equivalent instrument to confirm the presence of a dominant peak at approximately 167 base pairs, corresponding to the mononucleosomal length of cfDNA. Samples with excessive contamination from high-molecular-weight DNA or low yield are excluded from further analysis. The extracted cfDNA is then converted into sequencing libraries using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs), which employs a blunt-end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation workflow that is compatible with low-input DNA and minimizes amplification bias. The library preparation is performed in a controlled, automated fashion using a liquid-handling robot to ensure consistency across samples. Each library is barcoded with a unique dual-index combination to enable multiplexing of multiple samples in a single sequencing run. The libraries are then quantified using qPCR with primers specific to the adapter sequences, and normalized to a target concentration prior to sequencing.

Sequencing is performed on an Illumina platform using paired-end reads of 150 base pairs in length, with a target sequencing depth of approximately nine-fold coverage of the human genome. This depth is deliberately chosen to balance cost, throughput, and analytical sensitivity; it is sufficient to capture genome-wide fragment abundance patterns but insufficient to reliably detect single-nucleotide variants or small indels, thereby avoiding the confounding effects of sequencing errors and somatic mutations that are irrelevant to the method’s purpose. The sequencing run is performed using a high-throughput instrument such as the NovaSeq 6000, with multiple samples pooled per lane to maximize efficiency. Quality control metrics are collected at multiple stages, including the percentage of reads mapping to the reference genome, the distribution of insert sizes, the GC content profile, and the level of duplication. Samples that fail to meet predefined thresholds—for example, less than 70% mapping rate, excessive GC bias, or high duplication rates—are excluded from further analysis. The raw sequencing data are stored in FASTQ format and transferred to a high-performance computing cluster for downstream processing. The entire sample preparation pipeline is designed to minimize batch effects and to ensure that the resulting cfDNA profiles reflect true biological variation rather than technical artifacts. All reagents are sourced from the same lot where possible, and all processing steps are performed by trained personnel following standardized operating procedures. The use of process control samples—plasma from healthy donors processed alongside experimental samples—is incorporated into each batch to monitor for drift in extraction efficiency, library preparation yield, and sequencing performance. This rigorous and standardized approach to sample preparation is essential to the success of the invention, as it ensures that the machine learning model is trained on high-quality, reproducible data that accurately represent the underlying biology of cancer.

### III. EXAMPLE SYSTEMS

The invention is implemented through a series of integrated systems that enable the automated, scalable, and reproducible analysis of cfDNA for cancer detection. These systems are modular in design, allowing for flexibility in deployment across clinical laboratories, research institutions, and point-of-care settings. The first component of the system is the sample acquisition and preparation module, which includes the collection of peripheral blood in EDTA tubes, the centrifugation and plasma separation apparatus, and the automated liquid-handling platform for cfDNA extraction and library preparation. This module is designed to be compatible with standard clinical workflows and can be integrated into existing laboratory information systems. The second component is the sequencing module, which consists of an Illumina sequencing instrument, such as the NovaSeq 6000 or NextSeq 2000, configured for paired-end sequencing at low coverage. This module is connected to a high-throughput data pipeline that automatically transfers raw FASTQ files to a secure data storage server upon completion of each sequencing run. The third component is the data processing and feature extraction module, which is implemented as a software pipeline running on a high-performance computing cluster. This pipeline includes a series of bioinformatics tools for read alignment, feature counting, and normalization. The alignment step is performed using BWA-MEM version 0.7.15, which maps sequencing reads to the human reference genome GRCh38 with high sensitivity and accuracy. Following alignment, the number of reads overlapping each annotated protein-coding gene is counted using a custom script that leverages the GENCODE or RefSeq gene annotation database. Each gene is treated as a discrete genomic bin, and the resulting counts form the raw feature vector for each sample. To correct for technical biases, the feature vector is normalized using a two-step process: first, the top and bottom ten percent of gene counts are excluded to compute a trimmed mean, and each gene count is divided by this value to achieve relative scaling; second, a Loess regression model is applied to correct for GC content bias, which is known to influence sequencing efficiency and fragment recovery. The normalized feature vector is then stored in a structured data format, such as a tab-separated values file or a compressed binary format, for downstream analysis.

The fourth component of the system is the machine learning classifier module, which is implemented as a software application running on a dedicated computing server or cloud-based infrastructure. This module loads the trained classifier model, which is stored as a serialized file in a standard format such as Pickle or Joblib, and applies it to the normalized feature vector of each sample. The classifier is a support vector machine with a radial basis function kernel, trained using the Scikit-learn library, and optimized for high-dimensional data. The input to the classifier is the 20,000-dimensional feature vector representing the normalized fragment counts across protein-coding genes, and the output is a continuous probability score between 0 and 1, indicating the likelihood that the sample is from a subject with cancer. The classifier is accompanied by a user interface that allows laboratory personnel to upload raw sequencing data, initiate analysis, and view results in real time. The interface displays the diagnostic probability score, a confidence interval derived from bootstrapping, and a binary classification result based on a predefined threshold. The system also includes a quality control dashboard that flags samples with low sequencing depth, high duplication rates, or abnormal feature distributions, and provides recommendations for retesting or exclusion. The fifth component is the data management and reporting module, which integrates with hospital information systems to transmit diagnostic results to electronic health records, generate patient reports, and maintain audit trails for regulatory compliance. The system is designed to be HIPAA-compliant, with encrypted data transmission, role-based access control, and secure backup protocols. The entire system is validated according to CLIA and CAP guidelines, and the software is version-controlled using Git, with each model update accompanied by a full audit trail of training data, hyperparameters, and performance metrics. The system can be deployed as an on-premise solution or as a cloud-based service, enabling remote analysis for laboratories without in-house computational resources. The modularity of the system allows for future expansion, such as the integration of additional analytes like proteins or RNA, or the incorporation of new machine learning architectures, without requiring a complete overhaul of the infrastructure. This comprehensive system ensures that the invention can be translated from a research discovery into a clinically validated diagnostic tool with high reproducibility, scalability, and reliability.

### IV. MACHINE LEARNING TOOLS

The machine learning tools employed in this invention are selected and configured to handle the unique challenges of high-dimensional, low-coverage whole-genome cfDNA data. The primary algorithm used is the support vector machine (SVM) with a radial basis function (RBF) kernel, which is particularly well-suited for classification tasks involving non-linear decision boundaries and high-dimensional feature spaces. The RBF kernel maps the input feature vector into an infinite-dimensional space, enabling the SVM to find an optimal separating hyperplane even when the relationship between the features and the class label is complex and non-linear. This is essential in the context of cfDNA analysis, where the diagnostic signal is distributed across thousands of genomic regions and cannot be captured by linear models that assume additive, independent contributions. The SVM is trained using a supervised learning approach, where the input data consist of normalized feature vectors from subjects with known cancer status, and the output is a binary classification label. The training process involves the optimization of two key hyperparameters: the regularization parameter C, which controls the trade-off between maximizing the margin and minimizing classification error, and the kernel coefficient γ, which determines the influence of a single training example. These hyperparameters are tuned using a random search strategy within predefined bounds, with performance evaluated using cross-validation to avoid overfitting. The SVM is implemented using the Scikit-learn library, which provides efficient, well-documented, and reproducible implementations of machine learning algorithms.

In addition to the SVM, alternative machine learning models are evaluated during the development phase to ensure robustness and to identify the most effective algorithm for the task. These include logistic regression, which is a linear model that estimates the probability of cancer based on a weighted sum of the input features, and random forest, which is an ensemble method that combines multiple decision trees to improve generalization and reduce variance. Logistic regression is included as a baseline model because of its interpretability and computational efficiency, while random forest is included because of its ability to capture interactions between features and its robustness to noise. However, in comparative evaluations, the SVM consistently outperforms these alternatives in terms of area under the curve and sensitivity at fixed specificity, particularly when evaluated under confounder-controlled cross-validation protocols. This superior performance is attributed to the SVM’s ability to effectively model the non-linear, distributed nature of the cfDNA signal, where the diagnostic information is not concentrated in a few highly predictive genes but is instead spread across a large number of genes with small, additive effects. The SVM also demonstrates greater stability across different validation strategies, maintaining high performance even when the training and test sets are stratified by age, batch, or institution, indicating that it is less susceptible to learning spurious correlations introduced by confounding variables.

The machine learning pipeline is further enhanced by the use of dimensionality reduction techniques, such as principal component analysis (PCA) and truncated singular value decomposition (SVD), which are applied optionally to reduce the number of features and mitigate the curse of dimensionality. However, in this invention, the application of dimensionality reduction is found to provide no significant improvement in performance and is therefore omitted from the final model. This is because the original feature space—comprising the fragment counts across protein-coding genes—already contains the most relevant biological information, and reducing its dimensionality risks discarding subtle but important signals. The decision to retain the full feature space is supported by the observation that the diagnostic signal is highly distributed, with no single gene or small set of genes dominating the classification. Instead, the model relies on the collective contribution of thousands of genes, each with a small but consistent effect. The training process is conducted using five-fold cross-validation, with stratification by cancer status to ensure balanced representation of positive and negative samples in each fold. The performance of the model is evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and sensitivity at 85% specificity, which are selected as the primary metrics because they reflect the clinical utility of the test in a screening context, where high specificity is required to minimize false positives and where sensitivity must be maximized to ensure that early-stage cancers are not missed. The model is trained on a dataset of 817 samples, with 546 cases of colorectal cancer and 271 non-cancer controls, and is validated using multiple cross-validation strategies to ensure generalizability. The use of a single, well-characterized algorithm—SVM with RBF kernel—ensures reproducibility and facilitates regulatory approval, as the model’s behavior is deterministic and can be fully characterized and documented. The machine learning tools are not proprietary or novel in isolation, but their application to cfDNA fragment counting at low coverage, combined with confounder-controlled validation, constitutes a novel and non-obvious invention.

### V. SELECTION OF INPUT FEATURES

The selection of input features is a critical component of this invention, as it determines the biological information that the machine learning model will use to make its diagnostic decisions. Rather than relying on pre-selected biomarkers, known cancer mutations, or methylation sites, the invention employs a genome-wide, unbiased approach in which every annotated protein-coding gene serves as a potential feature. This approach is grounded in the hypothesis that cancer induces systemic changes that are reflected in the fragmentation patterns of cfDNA across the entire genome, and that these changes are too complex and distributed to be captured by a limited set of predefined markers. The feature set is constructed by counting the number of cfDNA fragments that align to each protein-coding gene region, as defined by the GENCODE v44 annotation database, which includes approximately 20,000 genes. Each gene is treated as a discrete genomic bin, and the count of fragments overlapping that bin is recorded as a numerical value for each sample. This results in a feature vector of dimensionality 20,000, where each element corresponds to the normalized abundance of cfDNA fragments derived from a specific gene. The choice of protein-coding genes as the unit of measurement is deliberate: these regions are well-annotated, conserved across individuals, and represent a substantial portion of the genome that is actively transcribed and regulated, making them sensitive to changes in chromatin structure and cellular state. Moreover, protein-coding genes are less prone to repetitive elements and mapping ambiguities than intergenic regions, which enhances the reliability of fragment counting.

The raw fragment counts are then subjected to a rigorous normalization process to correct for technical biases that could otherwise confound the analysis. The first step is trimmed mean normalization, in which the top and bottom ten percent of gene counts are excluded from the calculation of the mean, and each gene count is divided by this trimmed mean. This step ensures that extreme outliers—such as genes with exceptionally high or low coverage due to sequencing artifacts or local genomic features—do not disproportionately influence the scaling of the feature vector. The second step is Loess-based GC bias correction, which models the relationship between the GC content of each gene and its observed fragment count, and applies a non-parametric regression to adjust for systematic deviations. This correction is essential because GC-rich regions tend to be over-represented in sequencing data due to higher amplification efficiency, while AT-rich regions are under-represented, leading to artificial differences in fragment abundance that are unrelated to biology. The Loess correction is applied independently to each sample, using a sliding window approach to smooth the relationship between GC content and fragment count across the entire genome. The resulting normalized feature vector is free from technical artifacts and reflects true biological variation in cfDNA fragmentation patterns. The use of gene-level bins, rather than smaller windows such as 50-kilobase bins or CpG islands, is preferred because it provides a biologically meaningful unit of measurement that corresponds to functional genomic elements. Smaller bins would increase noise and reduce statistical power, while larger bins would obscure fine-grained patterns of fragmentation that may be diagnostic. The feature selection process is entirely data-driven and does not involve prior filtering based on differential expression, methylation, or mutation status, ensuring that the model remains unbiased and capable of discovering novel signals. The resulting feature set is high-dimensional, sparse, and highly informative, and its composition is what enables the machine learning model to detect cancer with high accuracy even in the absence of tumor-specific mutations. The selection of this feature set represents a fundamental departure from existing liquid biopsy methods, which rely on targeted sequencing of known cancer genes, and instead embraces a holistic, systems-level view of cancer biology.

### VI. USE OF MACHINE LEARNING MODEL FOR MULTI-ANALYTE ASSAYS

Although the primary embodiment of this invention relies solely on cfDNA fragment counts as input to the machine learning model, the framework is explicitly designed to be extensible to the integration of additional biological analytes, thereby enabling the development of multi-analyte assays with enhanced diagnostic performance. The machine learning architecture is agnostic to the type of input data and can accommodate any numerical feature vector, whether derived from sequencing, mass spectrometry, immunoassays, or other modalities. This flexibility allows for the seamless incorporation of orthogonal signals such as circulating proteins, cell-free RNA, microRNAs, metabolites, or methylation profiles, each of which may provide complementary information about the presence of cancer. For example, proteins such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), or tissue polypeptide-specific antigen (TPS) are already used in clinical practice as biomarkers for certain cancers, but their individual sensitivity and specificity are limited. When combined with the cfDNA-based diagnostic signal, these proteins may enhance the model’s ability to distinguish between cancer and benign conditions, particularly in cases where the cfDNA signal is ambiguous. Similarly, cell-free RNA fragments, which reflect gene expression changes in circulating cells, may provide additional insight into the tumor microenvironment and immune response, thereby improving the model’s biological interpretability. Methylation markers, which are highly stable and tissue-specific, could be incorporated as a separate feature set, with each CpG site serving as a binary or continuous variable representing its methylation status.

The integration of multiple analytes is achieved by concatenating the feature vectors from each modality into a single, unified input vector for the machine learning classifier. For instance, if the cfDNA feature vector contains 20,000 elements (gene counts), and a protein panel includes 50 analytes measured by immunoassay, the combined feature vector would have 20,050 dimensions. The machine learning model is then retrained on this expanded dataset, with the same cross-validation procedures applied to ensure that the added features contribute meaningfully to diagnostic performance and do not introduce new confounding effects. The advantage of this approach is that it allows for the incremental improvement of the diagnostic model without requiring a complete redesign of the underlying algorithm. Each new analyte is evaluated independently for its contribution to the area under the curve and sensitivity at fixed specificity, and only those that demonstrate statistically significant improvement are retained. This modular architecture also enables the development of personalized screening strategies, in which the combination of analytes is tailored to an individual’s risk profile, age, sex, or family history. For example, in a patient with a strong family history of colorectal cancer, the model might place greater weight on cfDNA fragmentation patterns associated with early-stage adenomas, while in an older patient with comorbidities, the model might incorporate inflammatory markers to distinguish cancer from chronic inflammation. The system is designed to be adaptive, with the ability to update the model as new analytes are validated and new data become available. The machine learning framework also supports the use of feature selection algorithms, such as L1 regularization or recursive feature elimination, to identify the most informative combination of analytes and to reduce the dimensionality of the input space without sacrificing performance. This capability transforms the invention from a single-modality test into a platform for comprehensive, multi-analyte cancer screening, capable of detecting a wide range of malignancies with high accuracy and broad applicability.

### VII. CLASSIFIER GENERATION

The generation of the classifier is a systematic, multi-stage process that ensures the model is trained on high-quality, representative data and validated under rigorous conditions to guarantee clinical reliability. The process begins with the assembly of a training dataset comprising cfDNA profiles from 546 subjects diagnosed with colorectal cancer and 271 subjects without cancer, with demographic and clinical metadata including age, sex, cancer stage, institutional source, and date of sample processing. The dataset is preprocessed to remove samples with low sequencing quality, high contamination, or missing metadata, resulting in a final cohort of 817 samples. The cfDNA fragments from each sample are aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38 using BWA-MEM, and the number of fragments overlapping each protein-coding gene is counted to generate a raw feature vector. These counts are then normalized using trimmed mean scaling and Loess-based GC correction to eliminate technical biases. The normalized feature vectors are standardized by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation across the training set, ensuring that all features contribute equally to the model regardless of their absolute scale. The classifier is then trained using a support vector machine with a radial basis function kernel, implemented in the Scikit-learn library. The hyperparameters of the SVM—namely, the regularization parameter C and the kernel coefficient γ—are optimized using a random search strategy over a predefined grid of values, with performance evaluated using five-fold cross-validation stratified by cancer status. The best-performing combination of hyperparameters is selected based on the highest mean area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) across the validation folds.

To ensure that the classifier generalizes beyond the training data and is not learning spurious correlations introduced by confounding variables, multiple cross-validation strategies are applied in sequence. The first strategy is standard k-fold cross-validation, in which samples are randomly partitioned into five folds, and the model is trained and tested five times, each time using a different fold as the test set. This provides an initial estimate of performance under ideal conditions. Subsequently, more stringent validation strategies are applied: binned-age cross-validation, in which samples are grouped into age categories and folds are constructed to ensure that each age bin is represented in both training and test sets; k-batch cross-validation, in which samples are grouped by laboratory processing batch and all samples from a given batch are assigned to the same fold to evaluate batch-to-batch consistency; ordered k-batch cross-validation, in which samples are ordered by date of processing and partitioned sequentially to assess temporal drift; and balanced k-batch cross-validation, in which samples are grouped by both batch and institutional source, and downsampling is applied to ensure equal representation of each institution in each fold. Each of these strategies isolates the effect of a specific confounding variable and evaluates whether the classifier’s performance remains robust when that variable is held constant. The classifier that achieves the highest performance across all validation strategies is selected as the final model. This model is then retrained on the entire dataset using the optimized hyperparameters and serialized into a file format that can be deployed in a clinical setting. The generation process is fully documented, with every step—from raw data alignment to final model serialization—tracked in a version-controlled repository. The model is validated using bootstrapping to estimate confidence intervals for AUC and sensitivity, and its performance is compared against alternative classifiers, including logistic regression and random forest, to confirm its superiority. The resulting classifier is not a black box but a transparent, reproducible, and clinically validated tool that can be audited, updated, and regulated according to established medical device standards.

### VIII. CANCER DIAGNOSIS AND DETECTION

The application of the machine learning classifier to cancer diagnosis and detection represents a paradigm shift in the field of liquid biopsy, enabling the identification of malignancy at its earliest and most treatable stages with unprecedented accuracy and scalability. The method is specifically designed for the detection of early-stage cancers, including colorectal, pancreatic, lung, and other solid tumors, where traditional screening methods are inadequate due to low sensitivity, invasiveness, or poor adherence. When applied to a plasma sample from an asymptomatic individual, the classifier generates a continuous probability score that reflects the likelihood of cancer, with values ranging from 0 to 1. A threshold is established based on clinical requirements—for example, a threshold of 0.5 may be used to maximize sensitivity, while a higher threshold of 0.8 may be used to maximize specificity. The output is interpreted as a binary classification: a score above the threshold indicates a positive result, suggesting the presence of cancer, while a score below the threshold indicates a negative result, suggesting the absence of cancer. The method achieves a sensitivity of 85% at 85% specificity in standard cross-validation, and maintains sensitivity above 70% even under the most stringent confounder-controlled validations, demonstrating its robustness in real-world conditions. This level of performance is substantially superior to existing blood-based tests for colorectal cancer, such as fecal immunochemical testing, which has a sensitivity of approximately 70% for advanced adenomas and 50% for early-stage cancer, and to ctDNA-based assays, which often fail to detect tumors with low tumor fraction.

The diagnostic utility of the method is further enhanced by its ability to operate without prior knowledge of tumor-specific mutations or genomic alterations. Unlike targeted sequencing approaches that require the identification of known driver mutations, this invention relies on the collective, genome-wide fragmentation pattern of cfDNA, which is altered by the systemic effects of cancer regardless of the underlying genetic drivers. This makes the method applicable to a wide range of cancer types, including those with heterogeneous mutational landscapes or low mutation burden, such as pancreatic or ovarian cancer. The classifier does not require the detection of copy number variations, methylation changes, or structural rearrangements, and instead learns the statistical signature of cancer from the abundance of cfDNA fragments across thousands of genes. This approach is particularly powerful in early-stage disease, where tumor-derived DNA is scarce but the host’s physiological response is already underway. The method is also capable of detecting pre-malignant lesions, such as advanced adenomas, which are known to shed cfDNA but are often missed by conventional screening. The diagnostic result is delivered as a quantitative score, allowing clinicians to assess the degree of suspicion and to guide further diagnostic workup. A low probability score may be used to reassure patients and avoid unnecessary invasive procedures, while a high probability score triggers referral for confirmatory imaging or endoscopic evaluation. The method is designed for population-level screening, requiring only a single blood draw and minimal sample processing, making it feasible for implementation in primary care settings, community health centers, and national screening programs. The low cost of sequencing at nine-fold coverage, combined with the scalability of the machine learning pipeline, enables the test to be offered at a price point comparable to existing screening modalities, while providing significantly higher accuracy. The invention thus transforms cancer screening from a fragmented, low-adherence, and late-detection paradigm into a simple, blood-based, population-wide test capable of identifying malignancy at its most treatable stages.

### IX. INDICATIONS

The invention is indicated for the non-invasive detection of cancer in asymptomatic individuals for the purpose of population-level screening, with particular utility in the early detection of colorectal cancer, pancreatic cancer, lung cancer, and other solid malignancies for which current screening methods are inadequate. The method is suitable for use in individuals aged 50 to 84 years, as this age range corresponds to the population with the highest incidence of colorectal cancer and the greatest benefit from early detection. The test is intended for use in individuals without a known diagnosis of cancer, prior history of malignancy, or active inflammatory or autoimmune conditions that may confound the cfDNA signal. It is not intended for use in monitoring disease progression, assessing treatment response, or detecting recurrence in patients with known cancer, as the model was trained exclusively on newly diagnosed, treatment-naïve cases. The method is also not indicated for use in pediatric populations, pregnant individuals, or individuals with hematologic malignancies, as the biological basis of cfDNA fragmentation in these populations differs significantly from that of solid tumors. The test may be used as a first-line screening tool in average-risk populations, replacing or supplementing existing modalities such as colonoscopy, fecal immunochemical testing, or low-dose computed tomography. In high-risk populations, such as those with a family history of cancer or inherited syndromes, the test may be used as an adjunct to existing surveillance protocols to enhance sensitivity and reduce the burden of invasive procedures. The diagnostic result should always be interpreted in conjunction with clinical findings, patient history, and other diagnostic tests, and a positive result should be confirmed by histopathological examination. The method is designed for use in clinical laboratories that are certified under CLIA and CAP standards, and the software platform must be validated for diagnostic use in accordance with regulatory guidelines. The invention is not intended for use in self-administered or at-home testing without clinical oversight, as the interpretation of results requires medical expertise. The method may be extended to other indications, including the detection of other cancers, the assessment of cancer risk in individuals with premalignant conditions, and the differentiation of cancer from benign inflammatory or infectious diseases, provided that additional training data are collected and the model is retrained accordingly. The indications for use are based on the performance characteristics demonstrated in the validation cohort, and future studies will be conducted to expand the indications to additional populations and cancer types.
