# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The prediction of three-dimensional protein structures from amino acid sequences remains one of the most enduring and computationally demanding challenges in molecular biology. For decades, researchers have pursued a variety of methodologies to infer the native conformation of proteins based solely on their primary sequence, with fragment assembly emerging as one of the most empirically successful ab initio approaches. This strategy relies on the observation that short segments of polypeptide chains, typically ranging from three to nine residues in length, frequently recur across structurally unrelated proteins. By assembling these fragments—selected from databases of experimentally determined structures—into plausible full-length models, computational systems such as Rosetta and Quark have achieved notable success in predicting protein folds without reliance on homologous templates. The fidelity of these predictions is critically dependent on the quality and diversity of the fragment libraries employed, which serve as the primary source of local structural constraints during the assembly process.

Fragment libraries, as traditionally constructed, contain hundreds to thousands of candidate fragments for each residue position along the target sequence. Each fragment represents a local three-dimensional conformation derived from known protein structures in the Protein Data Bank, selected based on sequence similarity and structural compatibility. While these libraries have been instrumental in enabling fragment-based folding algorithms, their internal structural content has largely remained underutilized beyond the narrow scope of assembly. The rich array of geometric and conformational data embedded within each fragment—including backbone torsion angles, inter-residue distances, secondary structure propensities, and spatial orientations of side chains—has not been systematically extracted, quantified, or repurposed for other computational tasks in structural biology. As a result, the potential of fragment libraries as sources of high-fidelity structural priors for broader prediction pipelines has remained largely untapped.

Recent advances in protein structure prediction have shifted toward end-to-end deep learning frameworks that directly map sequence information to structural outputs. Systems such as AlphaFold2 and trRosetta have demonstrated unprecedented accuracy by leveraging co-evolutionary signals from multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) and deep neural networks to predict inter-residue distances, torsion angles, and other structural properties. These methods typically operate in two stages: first, they predict a set of structural constraints—such as Cβ–Cβ distances, φ and ψ torsion angles, or solvent accessibility—from sequence features alone; second, they employ gradient descent optimization to generate a three-dimensional structure that satisfies these constraints. The quality of the final predicted structure is therefore heavily contingent upon the accuracy of the predicted constraints. While these approaches have achieved remarkable success, they remain fundamentally limited by their reliance on sequence-derived features. The absence of direct structural priors from known conformations introduces a critical gap: the model must infer local geometry purely from evolutionary patterns, which may be sparse, noisy, or absent for proteins with few homologs.

This limitation is particularly acute for targets with low sequence depth, where the statistical signals underlying co-evolutionary analysis are weak or non-existent. In such cases, even the most sophisticated neural architectures struggle to recover accurate local conformations, leading to significant degradation in prediction quality. Meanwhile, fragment libraries—constructed from experimentally determined structures—offer a complementary source of information. Unlike sequence-based features, they encode actual physical geometries observed in nature, capturing not only statistical tendencies but also the precise spatial relationships between atoms in folded contexts. The structural information contained within these libraries is inherently local, highly resolved, and empirically grounded, making it an ideal complement to the global, co-evolutionary signals used by modern predictors.

Despite their potential, fragment libraries have not been integrated into these newer prediction paradigms in a systematic or differentiable manner. Existing methods either treat fragments as discrete templates for assembly or discard them entirely in favor of purely data-driven approaches. There has been no framework that explicitly encodes the structural content of fragment libraries as continuous, differentiable potentials for gradient-based folding, nor has there been a neural architecture capable of learning high-level representations of fragment libraries as input features for property prediction. Consequently, the structural richness of fragment libraries remains siloed within the domain of traditional assembly methods, while modern deep learning systems operate in a structural vacuum, relying solely on evolutionary statistics.

This invention bridges this critical gap by introducing a novel computational framework that systematically extracts, models, and leverages the structural information embedded within fragment libraries for two distinct but synergistic purposes: first, as explicit, protein-specific potentials for gradient-descent-based protein folding; and second, as high-dimensional input features for deep neural networks that predict protein structural properties. The method transforms fragment libraries from static, discrete templates into dynamic, continuous, and differentiable sources of structural guidance. By modeling the distribution of structural properties across fragments using weighted Gaussian mixture models, we generate smooth, differentiable energy functions that can be integrated into existing folding pipelines. Simultaneously, by encoding fragment libraries through a hierarchical deep neural network architecture, we enable the direct incorporation of local structural priors into property prediction models, significantly enhancing their accuracy even in the absence of strong evolutionary signals.

The innovation lies not merely in the extraction of structural features from fragments, but in the formalization of these features as mathematical constructs that are compatible with modern optimization and learning frameworks. This represents a paradigm shift: fragment libraries are no longer viewed as collections of discrete templates to be sampled during assembly, but as continuous probability distributions that inform every step of structure prediction. The resulting system is capable of improving both the accuracy of predicted structures and the reliability of intermediate property predictions, particularly for challenging targets where sequence information is insufficient. This invention thus redefines the role of fragment libraries in computational structural biology, transforming them from auxiliary components into central, indispensable elements of next-generation protein structure prediction systems.

## SUMMARY

This invention discloses a novel computational framework for protein structure prediction that leverages the previously underutilized structural information contained within fragment libraries to significantly enhance the accuracy and robustness of both protein property prediction and gradient-descent-based protein folding. The invention comprises two interrelated, synergistic components: a method for converting fragment libraries into differentiable, protein-specific potential functions for structural optimization, and a deep neural network architecture that uses fragment libraries as input features to predict key structural properties with superior accuracy compared to existing methods.

The first component of the invention involves the transformation of fragment libraries into weighted Gaussian mixture models (wGMMs) that capture the probability distributions of local structural properties—such as backbone torsion angles (φ, ψ), backbone angles (θ, τ), and pairwise distances between Cα–Cα and Cβ–Cβ atoms—across all residue positions of a target protein. Each fragment in the library is assigned a confidence weight based on its predicted root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) from the native structure, enabling the construction of weighted distributions that prioritize high-quality fragments. These wGMMs are then converted into negative log-likelihood potentials, which serve as continuous, differentiable energy terms that can be integrated into existing gradient-based folding algorithms. When incorporated into a folding pipeline such as SAMF, these fragment-derived potentials act as complementary constraints to those derived from predicted inter-residue distances, significantly improving the convergence toward native-like conformations. The resulting models exhibit higher average TM-scores for top-ranked decoys, increased rates of correct topology prediction, and improved discrimination between high- and low-quality structural models, thereby enhancing both the accuracy and reliability of the final output.

The second component of the invention is a deep neural network architecture termed FA-DNN (Fragment-Assisted Deep Neural Network), which integrates fragment libraries as explicit input features for the prediction of both one-dimensional (1D) and two-dimensional (2D) protein structural properties. FA-DNN consists of a fragment library encoder module and a structural property predictor module. The encoder processes the fragment library as a high-dimensional tensor, applying hierarchical convolutional operations to extract spatial and sequential relationships among residues within fragments and across fragment positions. The resulting embeddings are combined with traditional sequence-derived features—including position-specific scoring matrices (PSSMs), one-hot encodings of the target sequence, and direct coupling analysis (DCA) statistics—to form a comprehensive input representation for the predictor. The predictor then outputs continuous predictions for backbone torsion angles (φ, ψ, θ, τ) and Cβ–Cβ pairwise distances. Unlike prior approaches that rely exclusively on evolutionary information, FA-DNN leverages the empirical structural priors encoded in fragment libraries to compensate for weak or absent co-evolutionary signals, resulting in substantially improved prediction accuracy across a diverse set of protein targets.

The invention has been validated across multiple independent benchmark datasets, including CASP13 free modeling (FM), CASP13 template-based modeling (TBM), CAMEO “hard” targets, and CASP14 FM targets. In all cases, the incorporation of fragment-derived potentials in gradient-based folding led to statistically significant improvements in TM-score, with the top-ranked models showing an average gain of 8.84% over baseline systems. Similarly, FA-DNN outperformed state-of-the-art predictors such as SPOT-1D, Spider3, RaptorX, and trRosetta in the prediction of torsion angles and Cβ–Cβ distances, achieving the lowest mean absolute error across all evaluated metrics. Notably, FA-DNN demonstrated exceptional performance on targets with limited multiple sequence alignment depth, where traditional methods fail due to insufficient evolutionary information, confirming the critical role of fragment-derived structural priors in overcoming this limitation.

The invention further introduces novel fragment-level evaluation metrics that quantify the accuracy of structural properties within fragment libraries, enabling systematic comparison of library construction algorithms and providing a foundation for quality control in fragment selection. These metrics, defined as expectations of errors across fragments and positions, offer a more nuanced and informative assessment than traditional precision and coverage measures, which fail to capture the fidelity of internal geometric relationships.

This invention fundamentally redefines the role of fragment libraries in computational structural biology. Rather than serving as discrete, static templates for assembly, fragment libraries are now treated as continuous, probabilistic sources of structural knowledge that can be seamlessly integrated into modern, differentiable prediction pipelines. The resulting system achieves superior performance in both structure prediction and property estimation, offering a generalizable framework applicable to any protein target, regardless of evolutionary complexity. The invention thus provides a powerful, scalable, and broadly applicable solution to one of the most persistent challenges in structural bioinformatics: the accurate prediction of protein conformation from sequence alone.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Example Environment

The invention operates within a computational environment designed for high-throughput protein structure prediction, integrating a suite of specialized software modules, high-performance computing resources, and structured data repositories to enable the systematic extraction, modeling, and application of fragment library information. The system is implemented as a modular pipeline that can be deployed on distributed computing clusters, cloud-based infrastructure, or high-end workstations equipped with multi-GPU configurations. The environment is structured to support the sequential execution of three core phases: fragment library generation, structural property modeling, and structure or property prediction.

At the foundational level, the system accesses a curated database of experimentally determined protein structures, such as the Protein Data Bank (PDB), from which fragment libraries are constructed. To ensure temporal consistency and prevent data leakage, all structural data used in fragment library construction is restricted to entries archived prior to the target prediction window—for example, structures released before May 1, 2018, for predictions on CASP13 and CASP14 targets. The fragment library construction module employs state-of-the-art algorithms such as DeepFragLib, NNMake, or Flib-Coevo to generate sets of candidate fragments for each residue position along the target protein sequence. Each fragment is a contiguous segment of three to fifteen residues, extracted from known structures and aligned to the target sequence via sequence similarity and structural compatibility metrics. The resulting fragment library is stored as a structured dataset containing the atomic coordinates of each fragment, its predicted RMSD relative to the native structure, and associated metadata such as secondary structure assignments and sequence identity.

The system then transitions to the structural property extraction and modeling phase. Here, the fragment library is processed to extract six key structural properties: the backbone torsion angles φ and ψ, the backbone angles θ and τ, and the pairwise distances between Cα–Cα and Cβ–Cβ atoms. These properties are computed for every residue within every fragment, and their distributions are modeled using weighted Gaussian mixture models (wGMMs). The weighting scheme assigns higher probabilities to fragments with lower predicted RMSD values, using a softmax transformation with a temperature parameter of 0.1 to sharply emphasize high-confidence fragments. Each wGMM is fitted independently for each residue position and each structural property, with four mixture components selected based on Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) optimization to balance model complexity and fit quality. The resulting wGMM parameters—means, variances, and component weights—are stored in a structured format that enables rapid access during the folding or prediction phase.

The modeled wGMMs are then converted into differentiable potential functions using a negative log-likelihood formulation, which transforms the probability distributions into energy penalties that can be minimized during structural optimization. These potentials are integrated into a gradient-based folding framework such as SAMF, which already employs inter-residue distance constraints derived from deep learning models like trRosetta. The system computes the combined energy landscape by summing the trRosetta-derived distance potentials with the fragment-derived potentials for torsion angles and backbone geometry. The resulting energy function is minimized using a self-adaptive gradient descent algorithm that adjusts step sizes and learning rates dynamically based on convergence behavior. During optimization, the system generates multiple decoy structures, ranks them by total energy, and selects the top-ranked model for final evaluation.

Simultaneously, the fragment library is processed by the FA-DNN module, which encodes the library as a four-dimensional tensor of dimensions L × 50 × 15 × D, where L is the length of the target protein, 50 is the number of top fragments retained per position, 15 is the maximum fragment length, and D is the feature dimensionality (including one-hot secondary structure and sine-cosine encodings of torsion angles). This tensor is fed into a hierarchical convolutional encoder composed of eight residual blocks, each containing two one-dimensional convolutional layers with 64 filters and a kernel size of three. The encoder extracts high-level representations of local fragment geometry, with residual connections preserving gradient flow and enabling deep training. The hidden states of the first residue in each fragment are extracted and averaged across the 50 fragments at each position to produce a single embedding vector per residue. These embeddings are concatenated with traditional sequence-derived features—including the target sequence’s one-hot encoding, PSSM profiles generated by DeepMSA, and DCA-derived pairwise statistics—to form a unified input representation for the predictor module.

The predictor module is a two-dimensional residual neural network with thirty residual blocks, each containing two convolutional layers with 64 filters and a 3×3 kernel. Batch normalization and dropout layers are applied to mitigate overfitting, and the network outputs two distinct predictions: a one-dimensional vector of torsion angles for each residue and a two-dimensional matrix of Cβ–Cβ distances between residue pairs. The distance predictions are mapped using a hyperbolic tangent function to emphasize short-range interactions, and the final outputs are converted back to real distances using the inverse transformation. The entire system is trained end-to-end on a curated dataset of 5,916 high-resolution protein chains from the PDB, with loss computed as the weighted mean absolute error across all predicted properties.

The environment is supported by a high-performance computing infrastructure capable of parallelizing the processing of multiple protein targets. Each target is processed independently, allowing for scalable deployment across hundreds of compute nodes. The system outputs a ranked list of predicted structures and corresponding property predictions, accompanied by confidence scores derived from the wGMM entropy and FA-DNN prediction variance. All intermediate data—including fragment libraries, wGMM parameters, and neural network embeddings—are stored in standardized formats for reproducibility, auditing, and future refinement. The system is designed to be modular, allowing for the substitution of alternative fragment library construction algorithms, different neural architectures, or alternative potential functions without requiring a complete overhaul of the pipeline.

### Structural Properties of Proteins and Fragments

The structural properties of proteins are defined by the precise spatial arrangements of atoms along the polypeptide chain, which collectively determine the three-dimensional fold and biological function of the molecule. These properties can be categorized into one-dimensional (1D) and two-dimensional (2D) descriptors, each capturing distinct aspects of local and global geometry. The 1D properties include backbone torsion angles φ and ψ, which define the rotational freedom around the N–Cα and Cα–C bonds, respectively, and backbone angles θ and τ, which describe the planar and dihedral geometry of successive Cα atoms. These angles govern the local conformation of the polypeptide backbone and are strongly correlated with secondary structure elements such as α-helices, β-strands, and turns. The 2D properties, in contrast, describe spatial relationships between pairs of atoms, most notably the distance between Cα–Cα and Cβ–Cβ atoms, which reflect the compactness and packing of the protein core and are critical for determining tertiary structure.

Fragment libraries, as constructed from known protein structures, encapsulate a rich ensemble of these properties for each residue position along the target sequence. Each fragment represents a local conformation sampled from the structural universe of known proteins, and collectively, the fragments at a given position form a probabilistic distribution over possible local geometries. For instance, at a position predicted to be part of an α-helix, the fragment library will contain a high proportion of fragments with φ and ψ angles clustered near the canonical helical values of approximately −60° and −45°, respectively. Similarly, at a β-strand position, the distribution will be centered around extended conformations near φ = −120° and ψ = 120°. These distributions are not uniform; they are shaped by the underlying physical and evolutionary constraints that govern protein folding, making them highly informative priors for structure prediction.

Importantly, the structural information contained within fragments extends beyond the simple assignment of torsion angles. Each fragment is a complete three-dimensional structure, meaning that it encodes not only the backbone geometry but also the relative positions of side-chain atoms, the distances between non-adjacent residues, and the orientation of hydrogen bonds and van der Waals contacts. The Cα–Cα and Cβ–Cβ distances within a fragment capture the spatial compactness of local regions, which is particularly valuable for predicting the geometry of loops, turns, and irregular secondary structure elements that are poorly constrained by sequence-based methods. These distances are not simply pairwise measurements; they reflect the cumulative effect of steric packing, hydrogen bonding networks, and hydrophobic collapse, all of which are implicitly encoded in the fragment’s native context.

The utility of these properties is further amplified when fragments are weighted according to their predicted structural fidelity. Fragments with low predicted RMSD values are more likely to resemble the native conformation of the target protein, and thus their structural properties carry greater predictive power. By assigning higher weights to these high-confidence fragments, the system effectively filters out noise and outliers, focusing the prediction on the most biologically plausible conformations. This weighting scheme transforms the fragment library from a static collection of templates into a dynamic, probabilistic representation of structural likelihoods.

The fragment-level nature of these properties is a key innovation. Traditional methods for evaluating protein structure prediction often assess properties at the residue level, computing the accuracy of φ or ψ angles for each individual residue against the native structure. This approach, while useful, fails to capture the contextual dependencies inherent in local structure. A single residue’s torsion angle is not determined in isolation; it is constrained by the geometry of its immediate neighbors. Fragment libraries, by definition, preserve these local correlations. A fragment containing residues i–i+6 encodes not only the torsion angles of each residue but also the relative distances between residues i and i+3, i and i+4, and so on. These intra-fragment relationships are preserved in the wGMM models, allowing the system to learn not just the marginal distributions of individual properties but their joint distributions.

This distinction is critical. A model that predicts φ and ψ angles independently may generate conformations that are individually plausible but geometrically inconsistent. In contrast, the wGMMs derived from fragments capture the co-occurrence of specific angle combinations with specific distance patterns, ensuring that the predicted structure satisfies both local and global geometric constraints simultaneously. This is particularly important for the prediction of β-sheet topology, where the alignment of adjacent strands depends on the precise inter-residue distances and torsion angles of multiple residues. The fragment-based approach ensures that these constraints are modeled together, rather than as independent variables.

Moreover, the fragment library encodes structural information that is often invisible to sequence-based methods. For example, a residue with low sequence conservation may still adopt a highly conserved local geometry if it resides in a structurally critical region, such as the active site of an enzyme or the interface of a protein complex. Sequence-based predictors, which rely on co-evolutionary signals, may fail to detect such constraints due to the absence of homologous sequences. Fragment libraries, however, contain examples of such geometries from structurally similar proteins, even if they are not evolutionarily related. This allows the system to recognize and reproduce conserved local folds that are not evident from sequence alone.

The structural properties extracted from fragments are also more robust to errors in sequence alignment. In proteins with shallow multiple sequence alignments, the PSSM profiles used by most deep learning models become noisy and unreliable. But fragment libraries do not depend on homology; they depend on structural similarity. A fragment with a particular combination of φ, ψ, θ, τ, and Cβ–Cβ distances can be identified and reused regardless of the sequence context, as long as the geometry matches. This makes the fragment-based approach uniquely suited for predicting the structures of proteins with few or no homologs, a class of targets that has historically been intractable for sequence-based methods.

Finally, the fragment library captures the inherent flexibility and conformational diversity of protein regions. Not every residue has a single, well-defined conformation; many positions, particularly in loops and termini, sample multiple stable states. The fragment library reflects this diversity by retaining multiple fragments with different geometries for the same position. The wGMM models capture this multimodal distribution, allowing the folding algorithm to explore multiple plausible conformations during optimization. This is in stark contrast to traditional predictors that output a single, deterministic value for each property, which can lead to overconfidence and structural misfolding when the true conformation lies outside the predicted mode.

In summary, the structural properties embedded in fragment libraries are not merely a collection of geometric measurements; they are a dense, context-sensitive, and empirically grounded representation of the local folding landscape. By modeling these properties as continuous, weighted distributions rather than discrete templates, the invention unlocks their full potential as a source of structural guidance that is both rich and reliable.

### Evaluation of Fragment Library

The evaluation of fragment libraries is a critical step in ensuring the reliability and predictive power of any structure prediction system that seeks to leverage their structural content. Traditional metrics such as precision and coverage, while widely used, provide only a coarse-grained assessment of library quality. Precision measures the proportion of fragments within a library that are structurally similar to the native structure, typically defined as having a root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) below a fixed threshold, such as 2.0 Å. Coverage, on the other hand, quantifies the fraction of residue positions in the target protein that are spanned by at least one such “good” fragment. While these metrics are useful for determining whether a library contains sufficient structural diversity to support assembly, they fail to capture the fidelity of the internal geometric relationships within the fragments themselves.

This invention introduces a suite of novel fragment-level metrics that evaluate the accuracy of structural properties at a much finer resolution, enabling a comprehensive and quantitative assessment of fragment library quality. These metrics are defined as expectations over all fragments and all residue positions, ensuring that the evaluation reflects not just the presence of good fragments, but the precision with which they encode the local geometry of the target protein. The seven metrics encompass both one-dimensional and two-dimensional structural properties: the accuracy of secondary structure assignment, the mean absolute error of torsion angles φ and ψ, the mean absolute error of backbone angles θ and τ, and the mean absolute error of Cα–Cα and Cβ–Cβ pairwise distances.

The secondary structure accuracy metric is computed by assigning each fragment a class—helix, strand, coil, or other—based on the majority secondary structure of its constituent residues, and then comparing this assignment to the native structure. The accuracy is defined as the proportion of fragments at each position that match the native secondary structure, averaged across all positions. This metric is particularly sensitive to the ability of the library to capture the correct local fold, as even a single misplaced helix or strand can lead to catastrophic misfolding during assembly.

The torsion angle and backbone angle error metrics are computed by measuring the angular deviation between each fragment’s φ, ψ, θ, and τ values and those of the corresponding residues in the native structure. Because torsion angles are periodic, the error is calculated as the minimum angular distance on the unit circle, ensuring that deviations of 350° are treated as equivalent to 10°. The error for each fragment is averaged across its residues, and then the expectation over all fragments at each position is computed, followed by an overall average across all positions. This yields a single, interpretable value for each angle type, reflecting the average deviation of the fragment library from the native conformation.

Similarly, the pairwise distance error metrics measure the mean absolute difference between the Cα–Cα and Cβ–Cβ distances within each fragment and those in the native structure. These distances are computed for all residue pairs within the fragment, and the average error is taken across all fragments and positions. Because these distances reflect the spatial packing of the protein core, their accuracy is a direct indicator of the library’s ability to reproduce the correct tertiary fold.

These fragment-level metrics are fundamentally different from residue-level evaluations used in prior work. In traditional assessments, the accuracy of a torsion angle prediction is computed by comparing the predicted value for a single residue to its native value. This approach ignores the fact that the torsion angle at position i is not independent of the angles at positions i−1 and i+1. A fragment library, by preserving the local context, allows the evaluation to account for these correlations. A library may have a high residue-level accuracy for φ angles but still contain fragments that are geometrically inconsistent—such as fragments where φ is correct but ψ is incompatible with the observed Cα–Cα distance. The fragment-level metrics detect such inconsistencies, revealing the true structural fidelity of the library.

The evaluation of fragment libraries constructed by DeepFragLib, NNMake, and Flib-Coevo using these metrics demonstrates a clear hierarchy of performance. DeepFragLib consistently outperforms the other algorithms across all seven metrics, with particularly pronounced advantages in torsion angle accuracy and Cβ–Cβ distance precision. The error values for φ and ψ angles in DeepFragLib are consistently lower than 10°, and the Cβ–Cβ distance errors are below 0.8 Å, indicating that the fragments are not merely similar to the native structure but are geometrically precise. The secondary structure accuracy exceeds 90% for most targets, confirming that the library not only captures the correct fold but does so with high fidelity.

Furthermore, the use of weighted fragment libraries—where fragments are assigned confidence scores based on their predicted RMSD—further improves the accuracy of all seven metrics. The softmax weighting scheme, with a temperature parameter of 0.1, effectively suppresses low-confidence fragments while amplifying the contribution of high-quality ones. This results in a more concentrated and accurate distribution of structural properties, reducing the noise that would otherwise arise from including structurally divergent fragments. The weighted libraries exhibit a marked reduction in angular and distance errors, demonstrating that the predicted RMSD values serve as reliable indicators of structural quality.

The fragment-level metrics also reveal limitations in other algorithms. NNMake, while widely used in Rosetta, exhibits higher angular errors and lower secondary structure accuracy, particularly in β-sheet regions. Flib-Coevo, despite its use of co-evolutionary information, shows inconsistent performance across targets, with some libraries containing fragments that are structurally plausible but geometrically misaligned. These findings underscore the superiority of DeepFragLib’s fragment selection strategy, which balances sequence similarity with structural compatibility in a way that maximizes geometric fidelity.

Importantly, these metrics are not merely descriptive; they are predictive. A strong correlation exists between the fragment-level accuracy of torsion angles and the final prediction performance of downstream models. Targets with fragment libraries exhibiting low angular error consistently yield higher TM-scores in folding experiments and lower mean absolute error in FA-DNN predictions. This establishes a direct link between the quality of the fragment library and the success of the prediction pipeline, validating the use of these metrics as a guide for library construction and selection.

The evaluation framework introduced here transforms fragment library assessment from a binary, threshold-based process into a continuous, multidimensional analysis. It enables researchers to not only determine whether a library is “good enough” but to understand precisely how and why it is good, identifying which structural properties are best captured and which require improvement. This level of granularity is essential for the iterative refinement of fragment library construction algorithms and for the development of adaptive prediction systems that can adjust their reliance on fragment information based on its measured quality.

### Prediction of Protein Structure

The prediction of protein three-dimensional structure from amino acid sequence is a problem of immense biological and computational significance. Traditional methods have relied on either template-based modeling, which exploits homologous structures, or ab initio folding, which attempts to simulate the physical forces governing protein folding. The latter, particularly fragment assembly, has proven to be one of the most effective approaches for targets lacking homologs, but its success has been constrained by the discrete and static nature of fragment libraries. This invention introduces a novel paradigm in which fragment libraries are transformed into continuous, differentiable potential functions that guide the folding process with unprecedented precision and reliability.

The core innovation lies in the conversion of fragment libraries into weighted Gaussian mixture models (wGMMs) that represent the probability distributions of key structural properties across all residue positions. These wGMMs are not merely statistical summaries; they are mathematical constructs that encode the likelihood of observing a particular conformation at each position, given the ensemble of fragments. Each wGMM is fitted to the torsion angles φ and ψ, the backbone angles θ and τ, and the pairwise distances Cα–Cα and Cβ–Cβ, with each component of the mixture representing a distinct conformational state. The weights assigned to each fragment, derived from its predicted RMSD, ensure that high-confidence fragments dominate the distribution, while low-confidence fragments contribute minimally. This results in a smooth, continuous probability landscape that reflects the true structural uncertainty of the target protein.

These wGMMs are then converted into negative log-likelihood potentials, which serve as energy penalties in a gradient-based folding framework. For each residue position and each structural property, the potential function computes the likelihood that a given conformation in the predicted structure matches the distribution encoded by the wGMM. If the predicted torsion angle φ at position i is close to the dominant mode of the wGMM, the potential is low, indicating high compatibility. If the angle lies in a low-probability region of the distribution, the potential rises sharply, penalizing the structure and driving it toward more probable conformations. This mechanism ensures that the folding algorithm is not merely sampling from the fragment library but is actively optimizing toward the most statistically likely conformations.

The integration of these potentials into a folding pipeline such as SAMF represents a fundamental advancement. SAMF, which already employs inter-residue distance constraints derived from deep learning models like trRosetta, is extended to include six additional potential functions—one for each structural property. The total energy of a predicted structure is now the sum of the trRosetta distance potential and the six fragment-derived potentials, each weighted by a manually tuned coefficient optimized on the CASP12FM dataset. During optimization, the gradient of this combined energy function is computed and used to update the atomic coordinates of the protein structure. The fragment-derived potentials act as strong local constraints, guiding the backbone torsions and side-chain packing toward conformations that are not only geometrically plausible but empirically supported by known protein structures.

This approach yields several critical advantages over traditional fragment assembly. First, it eliminates the need for discrete sampling. In fragment assembly, the folding process proceeds by selecting one fragment at each position and stitching them together, a process that is inherently combinatorial and prone to local minima. In contrast, the wGMM-based approach treats the structure as a continuous variable, allowing for smooth, gradient-driven optimization that can navigate complex energy landscapes with greater efficiency. Second, the potentials are differentiable, meaning that the entire system can be trained end-to-end, enabling the automatic adjustment of weights and parameters to maximize prediction accuracy. Third, the potentials are protein-specific, meaning that they are tailored to the unique structural context of each target, rather than relying on generic fragment templates.

The results of this approach are striking. When evaluated on the CASP13 FM, CASP13 TBM, and CAMEO datasets, the inclusion of fragment-derived potentials leads to a statistically significant improvement in the average TM-score of the top-ranked decoys, with an increase of 8.84% over the baseline system that relies solely on trRosetta distance constraints. The number of targets with TM-scores above the 0.5 threshold—a widely accepted criterion for correct topology prediction—also increases substantially, indicating that the system is not merely improving the average quality of predictions but is reliably producing correct folds for a greater number of challenging targets.

Moreover, the fragment-derived potentials improve the discrimination between high- and low-quality models. The gap between the best decoy (highest TM-score) and the top-ranked decoy (lowest energy) narrows from 0.1189 to 0.0827, demonstrating that the energy function is more accurately correlated with structural fidelity. This is a crucial advancement, as the ability to rank models by energy is essential for practical applications in drug design and protein engineering, where selecting the best model from a set of candidates is often the limiting factor.

The system also demonstrates robustness across diverse protein topologies. Targets with complex β-sheets, long loops, and non-canonical folds—all of which are notoriously difficult for sequence-based methods—show the greatest improvement, confirming that the fragment-derived potentials provide critical structural guidance where evolutionary signals are weak or absent. The potentials are particularly effective in regions with low sequence conservation, where traditional methods fail, because they rely not on homology but on structural recurrence.

The method is also scalable and generalizable. The wGMM framework can be applied to any fragment library, regardless of its construction algorithm, and can be extended to include additional structural properties such as side-chain rotamer distributions, hydrogen bond probabilities, or solvent accessibility. The differentiable nature of the potentials ensures compatibility with future advances in deep learning and optimization, making this a foundational innovation in the field of protein structure prediction.

### Prediction of Protein Structural Properties

The accurate prediction of protein structural properties—such as backbone torsion angles, backbone angles, and inter-residue distances—is a foundational task in computational biology, serving as the critical intermediate step between sequence and structure. Traditional methods for this task rely almost exclusively on sequence-derived features, including position-specific scoring matrices (PSSMs), evolutionary couplings, and amino acid composition. While these features have enabled significant progress, they are fundamentally limited by their dependence on the availability and quality of multiple sequence alignments (MSAs). For proteins with few homologs, these features become sparse, noisy, or entirely absent, leading to a dramatic degradation in prediction accuracy. This invention overcomes this limitation by introducing a deep neural network architecture, FA-DNN, that incorporates fragment libraries as explicit, high-dimensional input features, thereby enabling accurate prediction of structural properties even in the absence of strong evolutionary signals.

FA-DNN is a hierarchical neural network composed of two interconnected modules: a fragment library encoder and a structural property predictor. The encoder takes as input a fragment library represented as a four-dimensional tensor of dimensions L × 50 × 15 × D, where L is the length of the target protein, 50 is the number of top fragments retained per position, 15 is the maximum fragment length, and D is the feature dimensionality. Each fragment is encoded using a combination of one-hot representations of secondary structure and sine-cosine transformations of torsion angles φ, ψ, θ, and τ, ensuring that the geometric information is preserved in a differentiable, continuous form. The tensor is processed through eight residual convolutional blocks, each containing two one-dimensional convolutional layers with 64 filters and a kernel size of three. The use of residual connections enables the network to learn deep representations without suffering from vanishing gradients, while the convolutional operations capture the spatial relationships between residues within each fragment.

The key innovation of the encoder lies in its ability to extract high-level, context-sensitive representations of fragment geometry. Rather than treating each fragment as an independent entity, the network learns to identify recurring patterns across fragments—such as the co-occurrence of specific torsion angle combinations with particular Cβ–Cβ distances—and encodes these patterns into a compact embedding. The hidden states of the first residue in each fragment are extracted, effectively aligning the fragment-level features with the target protein’s residue positions. These states are then averaged across the 50 fragments at each position to produce a single, dense embedding vector per residue. This vector encapsulates not just the average geometry of fragments at that position, but the diversity and confidence of the structural ensemble, effectively transforming the fragment library into a continuous, probabilistic representation of local structure.

The structural property predictor then takes these embeddings as input, along with traditional sequence-derived features: the one-hot encoding of the target sequence, the PSSM profile generated by DeepMSA, and the pairwise statistics derived from direct coupling analysis (DCA). These features are tiled both horizontally and vertically to form a two-dimensional input matrix, which is concatenated with the DCA-derived pairwise features. The predictor is a 2D residual neural network with thirty residual blocks, each composed of two convolutional layers with 64 filters and a 3×3 kernel. Batch normalization and dropout are applied to enhance generalization, and the network outputs two distinct predictions: a one-dimensional vector of torsion angles for each residue and a two-dimensional matrix of Cβ–Cβ distances between residue pairs.

The use of fragment library embeddings as input fundamentally alters the nature of the prediction task. Instead of inferring local geometry from evolutionary patterns alone, the network now has access to direct structural priors derived from known protein conformations. This is particularly powerful for targets with shallow MSAs, where the PSSM and DCA features provide little information. In such cases, the fragment embeddings serve as a structural anchor, guiding the network toward conformations that are physically plausible and empirically supported. The result is a dramatic improvement in prediction accuracy, with FA-DNN outperforming state-of-the-art methods such as SPOT-1D, Spider3, RaptorX, and trRosetta across all evaluated metrics.

For torsion angles, FA-DNN achieves the lowest mean absolute error (MAE) for φ, ψ, θ, and τ on all test sets, with improvements of up to 15% over SPOT-1D and 20% over Spider3. The gains are most pronounced for θ and τ, which are poorly predicted by sequence-based methods due to their sensitivity to local packing and backbone geometry. The fragment library provides the necessary context to resolve these ambiguities, allowing the network to distinguish between similar torsion angle combinations that correspond to different structural contexts.

For Cβ–Cβ distances, FA-DNN reduces the MAE by over 6% compared to trRosetta and nearly 10% compared to RaptorX. This is particularly significant because distance prediction is a key bottleneck in end-to-end folding systems, and even small improvements in distance accuracy translate into large gains in final structure quality. The fragment library’s ability to encode precise spatial relationships between residues—even those separated by multiple positions—enables the network to learn long-range geometric constraints that are invisible to co-evolutionary analysis.

The performance of FA-DNN is further validated on the CASP14 FM dataset, where it demonstrates consistent superiority over existing methods, particularly on targets with minimal sequence depth. For example, on targets T1037 and T1042, which have fewer than five detectable homologs, trRosetta’s predictions are essentially random, while FA-DNN maintains high accuracy due to its reliance on fragment-derived structural priors. This confirms that the fragment library is not merely a supplementary feature but a critical enabler of accurate prediction in the most challenging cases.

The system is also robust to variations in fragment library quality. When the fragment library is constructed using lower-performing algorithms such as NNMake or Flib-Coevo, the performance of FA-DNN degrades proportionally, demonstrating that the network is not learning generic patterns but is specifically leveraging the structural fidelity of the fragments. This sensitivity to library quality further validates the biological relevance of the embeddings and confirms that the system is not overfitting to noise.

In summary, FA-DNN represents a paradigm shift in protein property prediction. By integrating fragment libraries as input features, it transforms the task from one of statistical inference to one of structural retrieval, enabling accurate, reliable, and generalizable predictions even when evolutionary signals are absent. This innovation not only improves the state of the art but opens the door to a new class of structure prediction systems that combine the power of deep learning with the empirical grounding of known protein structures.

### Example Method and Example Implementations

The invention is implemented as a fully automated, end-to-end computational pipeline that transforms a protein sequence into a high-accuracy three-dimensional structure through the synergistic integration of fragment library-derived potentials and deep neural network predictions. The method begins with the input of a target protein sequence, which is processed by a homology search engine to generate a multiple sequence alignment (MSA) using DeepMSA. Simultaneously, the sequence is submitted to a fragment library construction module that retrieves candidate fragments from a curated structural database, restricted to entries archived prior to the prediction window. The fragment library is constructed using DeepFragLib, which selects the top 50 fragments per position based on sequence similarity and structural compatibility, and assigns each fragment a predicted RMSD value derived from a learned model.

The fragment library is then processed by two parallel modules. The first module, the wGMM potential generator, extracts the six structural properties—φ, ψ, θ, τ, Cα–Cα, and Cβ–Cβ distances—from each fragment and computes their weighted distributions using a softmax transformation with a temperature parameter of 0.1. Each property at each residue position is modeled as a Gaussian mixture with four components, fitted using maximum likelihood estimation. The resulting parameters—means, variances, and component weights—are stored in a structured format and converted into negative log-likelihood potential functions, which are then integrated into a gradient-based folding framework such as SAMF. The folding process begins with a randomly initialized structure and proceeds through iterative optimization, where the total energy is computed as the weighted sum of the trRosetta distance potential and the six fragment-derived potentials. The gradient of this energy function is computed using automatic differentiation, and the atomic coordinates are updated using a self-adaptive learning rate scheduler. After 500 optimization steps, 50 decoy structures are generated, ranked by total energy, and the top-ranked model is selected as the final prediction.

The second module, FA-DNN, receives the same fragment library and MSA as input. The fragment library is encoded as a tensor of dimensions L × 50 × 15 × D, where D = 10, comprising one-hot secondary structure encodings and sine-cosine transformations of the four torsion angles. This tensor is processed by the fragment library encoder, which applies eight residual convolutional blocks to extract high-level embeddings. These embeddings are concatenated with the one-hot sequence encoding, the PSSM profile, and the DCA-derived pairwise statistics to form a unified input representation. The predictor module, a 30-block 2D residual network, then outputs the predicted torsion angles and Cβ–Cβ distances. The distance predictions are mapped using a hyperbolic tangent function to emphasize short-range interactions, and the final outputs are converted back to real distances using the inverse transformation.

The outputs of both modules are combined to generate a final structure prediction. The FA-DNN predictions are used to initialize the distance constraints in the folding pipeline, replacing or augmenting the trRosetta predictions. The fragment-derived potentials are then applied during optimization, ensuring that the final structure satisfies both the learned geometric constraints and the empirically derived structural priors. The resulting model is evaluated using standard metrics, including TM-score, RMSD, and the GDT-TS score, and is ranked against other predictions in benchmark competitions such as CASP and CAMEO.

The system is implemented in Python using PyTorch and TensorFlow, with custom CUDA kernels for efficient tensor operations. The wGMM fitting and potential computation are parallelized across multiple CPU cores, while the neural network training and inference are accelerated using eight NVIDIA V100 GPUs. The entire pipeline is containerized using Docker and deployed on a high-performance computing cluster, enabling the simultaneous processing of hundreds of protein targets. All intermediate data—including fragment libraries, wGMM parameters, neural network embeddings, and predicted structures—are stored in standardized formats (HDF5 and PDB) for reproducibility and archival.

In practical applications, the system is deployed in pharmaceutical and biotechnology settings for protein design, drug discovery, and enzyme engineering. For example, in the design of a novel enzyme with a unique active site, the system can be used to predict the structure of a scaffold protein with no known homologs, enabling the precise placement of catalytic residues based on accurate torsion angle and distance predictions. In structural genomics, the system can be used to predict the folds of uncharacterized proteins from metagenomic sequences, accelerating functional annotation. In structural biology, the system can generate high-confidence models for cryo-EM density maps, guiding model building and refinement.

The invention is not limited to the specific implementations described herein. Alternative fragment library construction algorithms, such as Flib-Coevo or NNMake, can be substituted without altering the core methodology. The wGMM model can be extended to include additional structural properties, such as side-chain rotamers or hydrogen bond probabilities. The neural network architecture can be replaced with transformer-based encoders or graph neural networks to capture longer-range dependencies. The folding framework can be adapted to use other optimization algorithms, such as simulated annealing or Monte Carlo sampling. The system’s modularity ensures that it can evolve in tandem with advances in computational biology, maintaining its position as the gold standard for protein structure prediction.
