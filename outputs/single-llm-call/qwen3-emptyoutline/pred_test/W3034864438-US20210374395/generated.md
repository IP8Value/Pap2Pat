# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to systems and methods for extracting structured information from form-like documents using learned representations of extraction candidates, particularly in domains where documents exhibit diverse layouts and lack standardized markup. The invention is applicable to business-critical document processing workflows such as invoice processing, purchase order automation, tax form retrieval, and insurance quote extraction, where large volumes of heterogeneous documents must be parsed with high accuracy and minimal manual intervention. The core innovation lies in a neural architecture that learns dense, field-agnostic embeddings for candidate values based on their spatial and textual neighborhood context, while simultaneously learning field-specific embeddings that encode the typical linguistic and positional patterns associated with each target field. This dual-embedding framework enables the system to generalize effectively to previously unseen document templates without requiring template-specific training or rule-based heuristics. The invention operates on raw scanned or digitally rendered documents, leveraging optical character recognition (OCR) outputs without reliance on underlying HTML, XML, or other structured markup. It is particularly suited for enterprise-scale document processing environments where the cost of manual data entry is prohibitive and existing rule-based or template-matching systems fail due to the sheer diversity of vendor formats. The invention is implemented as a software-based extraction engine that can be integrated into document management systems, robotic process automation pipelines, or enterprise resource planning platforms, providing a scalable, trainable, and interpretable solution to the long-standing challenge of unstructured document understanding.

## BACKGROUND

The automated extraction of structured data from form-like documents has long been a critical yet unsolved problem in business process automation. Enterprises routinely receive thousands of invoices, purchase orders, receipts, and tax forms each week, each generated by different vendors with unique layouts, fonts, spacing, and formatting conventions. Traditional approaches to this problem have relied heavily on manual data entry, which is labor-intensive, error-prone, and economically unsustainable at scale. Alternative solutions have attempted to automate this process using rule-based systems that rely on handcrafted heuristics—such as keyword matching, fixed-position extraction, or template matching—each of which suffers from severe limitations. Rule-based systems require extensive manual curation for each new document template, making them brittle and non-scalable. Template-matching approaches, while more robust than simple heuristics, still require a pre-existing library of templates and fail catastrophically when presented with a document from an unseen template, which is the norm in real-world environments where vendors frequently update their forms. Moreover, these systems are incapable of learning from data and cannot adapt to subtle variations in layout, font size, or word order that do not conform to predefined patterns.

Recent advances in natural language processing and computer vision have inspired new approaches to document understanding, particularly those that attempt to combine textual content with spatial layout information. However, most of these methods are either too reliant on document markup (e.g., HTML parsing for web pages), require pixel-level image analysis (e.g., convolutional neural networks over scanned document images), or assume a fixed grid structure (e.g., treating documents as 2D arrays of text tokens). These assumptions are incompatible with the reality of form-like documents, which are often scanned, poorly aligned, contain noise, and lack any consistent structural encoding. Furthermore, many existing models treat the extraction task as a sequence labeling problem, akin to named entity recognition, which is ill-suited for form-like documents because they contain minimal prose and are dominated by tabular or grid-like structures where meaning is derived from spatial relationships rather than syntactic context. Other approaches attempt to model documents as graphs, connecting text elements based on proximity or visual alignment, but these methods are computationally expensive, require complex preprocessing, and often fail to generalize beyond the templates seen during training.

Another persistent challenge is the imbalance between the number of candidate values and the number of true values. For example, a single invoice may contain dozens of date-like strings, currency amounts, or alphanumeric identifiers, but only one of each corresponds to the target field (e.g., invoice date, total amount). Traditional machine learning models trained on this data suffer from extreme class imbalance, where negative examples vastly outnumber positive ones, leading to biased predictions. While techniques such as down-sampling or cost-sensitive learning have been applied, they do not address the fundamental issue: the lack of a learned, generalizable representation that can distinguish true values from false ones based on contextual patterns rather than superficial features like text content or absolute position. Furthermore, many prior systems incorporate the candidate text itself as a direct input feature, which introduces a dangerous form of overfitting: if the training data contains only dates from a specific year or currency formats from a specific region, the model may learn to associate those superficial characteristics with correctness, rather than the underlying structural and linguistic cues that define the field’s true value. This leads to poor generalization when the model encounters documents from new vendors or regions.

There is also a critical lack of interpretability in existing models. When an extraction fails, it is often impossible to determine why—was the candidate too far from the key phrase? Was the OCR engine unable to detect a critical word? Was the field embedding misaligned? Without insight into the internal reasoning of the model, debugging and improving performance becomes a black-box trial-and-error process. Existing systems provide no mechanism for analyzing learned representations, making it difficult to validate model behavior, identify systematic errors, or transfer knowledge across domains. This lack of transparency is a major barrier to adoption in regulated industries such as finance, healthcare, and legal services, where explainability and auditability are mandatory.

The present invention addresses all of these shortcomings by introducing a novel neural architecture that decouples the representation of extraction candidates from the representation of target fields, enabling the model to learn generalizable, context-sensitive embeddings that encode the spatial and linguistic patterns associated with each field. By excluding the candidate’s own text from the input features, the model is forced to rely solely on its surrounding context—textual neighbors and their relative positions—to determine whether a candidate is likely to be the correct value. This design prevents overfitting to superficial characteristics and ensures that the model learns to recognize the structural signatures of fields rather than memorizing specific values. The use of self-attention mechanisms to model interactions among neighboring text tokens allows the system to weigh the relevance of each context element dynamically, capturing complex spatial dependencies that simple positional heuristics cannot. Additionally, the field embeddings are learned in the same latent space as the candidate embeddings, enabling direct similarity-based scoring that naturally clusters true candidates near their corresponding field representations while pushing false candidates away. This geometric organization of embeddings not only improves accuracy but also provides an interpretable visualization of model behavior, allowing engineers and domain experts to analyze failure cases, correct annotation errors, and refine the system iteratively. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in document information extraction: from brittle, template-dependent rule systems to a flexible, data-driven, and interpretable representation learning framework that generalizes to unseen templates with minimal supervision.

## SUMMARY

The invention comprises a system and method for extracting structured information from form-like documents by learning dense, context-aware representations of extraction candidates and target fields, enabling accurate and generalizable extraction without reliance on predefined templates or document markup. The system operates in three primary stages: ingestion, candidate generation, and neural scoring with assignment. During ingestion, documents are rendered as images and processed through an optical character recognition service to produce a hierarchical structure of text elements, each associated with a bounding box in a two-dimensional coordinate system. From this OCR output, candidate values are generated for each target field in a predefined schema by applying type-specific detectors—such as date recognizers, currency extractors, integer finders, and address parsers—to identify all potential matches for each field. These candidates are then scored independently by a neural network model that learns a field-agnostic embedding for each candidate based on its textual and spatial neighborhood, and a field-specific embedding for each target field, where the similarity between these two embeddings determines the likelihood that the candidate is the correct value for the field. Crucially, the candidate embedding is computed solely from the text of neighboring tokens and their relative positions with respect to the candidate, excluding the candidate’s own text to prevent overfitting to superficial features such as specific dates or currency formats. The neighborhood of each candidate is defined as a region extending to the left of the candidate and upward for approximately ten percent of the page height, encompassing all text tokens whose bounding boxes overlap by more than half with this region. Each neighboring text token is embedded using a learned word embedding table, while the relative position of each neighbor—computed as the normalized difference between its centroid and the candidate’s centroid—is embedded using a nonlinear positional embedding composed of two ReLU-activated layers with dropout to capture fine-grained spatial relationships. The candidate’s own absolute position is embedded using a linear projection. These embeddings are then aggregated using a self-attention mechanism that allows each neighbor to influence the representation of every other neighbor, enabling the model to downweight irrelevant or obstructing context elements. The resulting neighbor embeddings are max-pooled to produce a single, order-invariant neighborhood encoding, which is concatenated with the candidate position embedding and projected into a final candidate representation of fixed dimensionality. Separately, each target field is assigned a learned embedding that encodes the typical key phrase patterns associated with that field. The final score for a candidate-field pair is computed as the cosine similarity between the candidate embedding and the field embedding, linearly rescaled to lie within the range [0,1]. This score is then used by an assignment module to select the highest-scoring candidate for each field, ensuring that at most one value is assigned per field. The entire system is trained end-to-end using binary cross-entropy loss, where the ground truth label for each candidate is determined by whether it matches the manually annotated correct value for its field and document. The learned embeddings exhibit clear geometric clustering in latent space, with true candidates for a given field forming tight, coherent clusters near their field embedding, while false candidates are dispersed and distant. This structure not only enables high extraction accuracy but also provides interpretable insights into model behavior, allowing users to visualize and diagnose errors by examining the spatial relationships between embeddings. The invention achieves state-of-the-art performance on multiple benchmark datasets, significantly outperforming baseline models that use bag-of-words features or multilayer perceptrons, and demonstrates robust generalization to entirely unseen document templates, making it uniquely suited for real-world deployment in dynamic, high-volume document processing environments.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention is a comprehensive system and method for extracting structured information from form-like documents using learned representations of extraction candidates and target fields. The system is designed to operate in environments where documents vary widely in layout, formatting, and structure, and where no single template dominates the input corpus. The core innovation lies in the neural scoring architecture, which learns to represent both extraction candidates and target fields in a shared latent space, enabling similarity-based scoring that generalizes across templates and domains. The system is composed of four primary components: document ingestion, candidate generation, neural scoring, and assignment. Each component is described in detail below, with emphasis on the novel architecture and its operational principles.

The document ingestion stage begins with the input of a form-like document, which may be in native digital format (such as PDF or DOCX) or as a scanned image. The document is first rendered into a high-resolution image to preserve all visual elements, including text placement, font size, and spacing. This rendered image is then processed by a cloud-based optical character recognition (OCR) service, which outputs a hierarchical structure of text elements. This hierarchy consists of characters at the lowest level, grouped into words, which are in turn grouped into paragraphs and blocks. Each node in this hierarchy is associated with a bounding box defined in a two-dimensional Cartesian coordinate system, where the origin is located at the top-left corner of the document page. The coordinates of each bounding box are normalized by dividing the x and y positions by the width and height of the page, respectively, ensuring that the system is invariant to the physical dimensions or resolution of the input document. The text within each word is case-normalized, and special tokens are introduced for numbers, out-of-vocabulary terms, and padding. This normalized, structured representation of the document serves as the foundational input for all subsequent stages of the system.

The candidate generation stage leverages the observation that each target field in a schema corresponds to a well-defined data type, such as date, currency amount, integer, email address, or postal code. For each field in the target schema, a set of candidate generators is applied to the OCR output to identify all possible instances of the corresponding type. For example, if the schema includes fields such as invoice_date, due_date, and delivery_date, then a date detector is applied to the entire document text to extract every span that matches a valid date format, regardless of its semantic meaning. Similarly, currency detectors identify all monetary amounts, integer detectors identify all whole numbers, and so on. These detectors are implemented using a combination of rule-based pattern matching and machine learning models trained on large corpora of web and document text, ensuring high recall. The precision of these generators is intentionally sacrificed in favor of recall, as the subsequent neural scoring stage is responsible for distinguishing true values from false ones. This design choice ensures that the system does not miss any potential candidates due to overly restrictive detection rules. The result of this stage is a set of candidate-value pairs for each field, where each candidate is associated with its normalized bounding box coordinates and the text of its neighboring tokens. The number of candidates per field may vary significantly—for common fields such as invoice_date, there may be five to ten candidates, while for rare or low-precision fields such as total_tax_amount, there may be dozens or even hundreds. This high recall with low precision is intentional and is addressed by the scoring mechanism.

The neural scoring stage is the heart of the invention and represents a radical departure from prior approaches. Rather than treating the extraction task as a classification problem over raw text or as a sequence labeling problem over document layout, the invention learns two separate embeddings: one for the extraction candidate and one for the target field. These embeddings are computed in a shared latent space, and the similarity between them determines the likelihood that the candidate is the correct value for the field. The candidate embedding is computed exclusively from the candidate’s neighborhood—its surrounding text and spatial context—excluding the candidate’s own text. This exclusion is a critical design decision that prevents the model from overfitting to superficial characteristics such as specific dates, currency symbols, or numeric patterns that may be common in the training data but not generalizable to new documents. For example, if all training invoices were dated before 2019, a model that incorporates the candidate text might learn to associate “2018” with correctness, whereas the proposed system learns to associate the spatial and linguistic context of the date—such as the presence of the word “Date” above it and its alignment with other fields—with correctness, regardless of the actual value.

The neighborhood of a candidate is defined as a region extending to the left of the candidate and upward for approximately ten percent of the page height. This region is chosen based on empirical observations that key phrases—words that indicate the semantic meaning of a field—are typically located to the left or above the value they describe. Any text token whose bounding box overlaps by more than fifty percent with this region is considered a neighbor. The number of neighbors per candidate varies, but all candidates are padded to a fixed maximum number of neighbors to enable batched processing. Each neighbor is represented by two features: its textual content and its relative position with respect to the candidate. The textual content of each neighbor is embedded using a learned word embedding table, which maps each word in a vocabulary of the 512 most frequent tokens (case-normalized) to a dense vector of dimension d. Words not in the vocabulary are mapped to a special out-of-vocabulary token. The relative position of each neighbor is computed as the difference between its normalized centroid coordinates and those of the candidate, resulting in a two-dimensional vector. This relative position vector is then embedded using a nonlinear positional embedding consisting of two fully connected layers with ReLU activation and dropout, which allows the model to learn complex, non-linear mappings between spatial distances and their semantic relevance. For example, the model learns that a neighbor directly above the candidate is more likely to be a key phrase than one directly to the right, even if the Euclidean distance is similar. The candidate’s own absolute position—its normalized centroid coordinates—is embedded using a single linear projection layer, producing a d-dimensional vector that encodes its location on the page.

The neighbor embeddings are then combined using a self-attention mechanism to capture interactions among neighbors. Each neighbor’s word embedding and positional embedding are concatenated to form a 2d-dimensional vector. These vectors are packed into a matrix H of size N × 2d, where N is the number of neighbors. Three linear projection matrices—W_q, W_k, and W_v—are applied to H to generate query, key, and value embeddings, each of dimension 2d. For each neighbor i, the attention weight α_i is computed as the softmax of the dot product between the query of neighbor i and the keys of all neighbors, scaled by the square root of 2d to stabilize gradients. The self-attended neighbor encoding h_i is then computed as the weighted sum of the value embeddings using these attention weights. This mechanism allows the model to dynamically determine which neighbors are most relevant to the candidate. For instance, if a neighbor is partially obscured by another text element between it and the candidate, the attention mechanism can assign it a low weight, effectively ignoring it. This is a significant improvement over prior approaches that treat all neighbors equally or use fixed-distance windows. The self-attended neighbor encodings are then projected into a 4d-dimensional space using a linear transformation with ReLU activation, and then projected back down to 2d dimensions to maintain consistency with the embedding size.

The final candidate encoding is formed by concatenating the max-pooled neighbor encoding (a 2d-dimensional vector) with the candidate position embedding (a d-dimensional vector), resulting in a 3d-dimensional vector. This concatenated vector is then passed through a linear layer with ReLU activation and projected down to a final candidate embedding of dimension d. The use of max-pooling ensures that the candidate encoding is invariant to the arbitrary ordering of neighbors in the input, which is essential since the OCR output does not guarantee a consistent left-to-right or top-to-bottom ordering of text elements. The resulting candidate embedding encodes all relevant information about the candidate’s context—its textual neighbors, their spatial relationships, and its own position on the page—without incorporating the candidate’s own text.

Separately, each target field in the schema is assigned a learned field embedding of dimension d. This embedding is initialized randomly and updated during training to encode the typical key phrase patterns associated with that field. For example, the field embedding for invoice_date will learn to be similar to the embeddings of candidates that are surrounded by words such as “Date,” “Invoice Date,” or “Dated,” while being dissimilar to candidates surrounded by “Due Date” or “Delivery Date.” The field embedding is not derived from any specific candidate or document; it is a global representation learned across the entire training corpus. During inference, for each candidate-field pair, the cosine similarity between the candidate embedding and the field embedding is computed. This similarity score reflects how well the candidate’s context matches the learned pattern of the target field. The score is then linearly rescaled using a constant offset and scaling factor to ensure that it lies within the range [0,1], making it interpretable as a probability. The model is trained using binary cross-entropy loss, where the ground truth label for each candidate is 1 if it matches the manually annotated correct value for its field and document, and 0 otherwise. The optimization objective is to minimize the distance between positive candidates and their corresponding field embeddings while maximizing the distance between negative candidates and those same embeddings, as well as between field embeddings of different types. This results in a geometric structure in the latent space where true candidates for a field cluster tightly around its field embedding, while false candidates are scattered and distant.

The assignment stage takes the scores produced by the neural scoring model and assigns exactly one candidate to each field in the schema. This is done by selecting the candidate with the highest score for each field, provided that the score exceeds a configurable threshold. If no candidate exceeds the threshold, the field is left unassigned. In cases where multiple candidates for the same field have scores above the threshold, the highest-scoring one is selected. This simple assignment rule is sufficient because the neural scoring model has already learned to rank candidates with high fidelity. The threshold can be tuned per field based on the desired balance between precision and recall, and can be adjusted dynamically based on performance metrics in production. The system does not require any post-processing rules, such as disambiguating between similar fields (e.g., invoice date vs. due date), because the field embeddings are learned to be distinct from one another. For example, even if a candidate has the word “Date” nearby, the system will assign it to invoice_date only if its context matches the learned pattern of invoice_date, not due_date. This eliminates the need for handcrafted disambiguation logic, which is a major source of fragility in traditional systems.

The training process for the system is fully supervised and requires only a small number of manually annotated documents. For each document in the training set, human annotators specify the correct value for each field in the schema. These annotations are then used to label the candidates generated by the candidate generators: a candidate is labeled positive if it matches the ground truth value for its field, and negative otherwise. Due to the high number of negative candidates, the training set is downsampled to maintain at most forty negative candidates for each positive candidate per field, to prevent the model from being overwhelmed by the class imbalance. The validation set is not downsampled to provide an accurate estimate of model performance. The model is trained using the Rectified Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001 for fifty epochs, with early stopping based on validation loss. The model architecture is designed to be lightweight and efficient, with no convolutional layers or recurrent units, making it suitable for deployment in cloud-based or edge environments with limited computational resources.

The learned representations of the system exhibit remarkable interpretability. When visualized using t-SNE, the candidate embeddings for a given field form tight, coherent clusters near the corresponding field embedding, while candidates for other fields are clearly separated. This geometric structure allows engineers to diagnose failure cases by examining the spatial position of misclassified candidates. For example, if a candidate that should be labeled as invoice_date appears near the due_date cluster, it may indicate that the key phrase in the document contains ambiguous wording such as “Date of Invoice,” which is used for both fields. In such cases, the annotators can be prompted to clarify the labeling, or the model can be retrained with additional examples. Similarly, if a candidate is far from all field clusters, it may indicate that the OCR engine failed to detect a crucial key phrase, such as “Date,” due to scanning noise. This level of insight is impossible with black-box models and enables continuous improvement of the system through human-in-the-loop feedback.

The system has been evaluated on two distinct datasets: a proprietary corpus of 14,237 invoices from 14,237 different vendors and a publicly available corpus of 626 scanned receipts from the ICDAR 2019 Robust Reading Challenge. On the invoice dataset, the system achieved a 3.6-point improvement in end-to-end F1 score over a baseline model using bag-of-words features and a 1.7-point improvement over a multilayer perceptron baseline using the same input features. On the receipt dataset, the improvement was even more pronounced, with a 20-point gain in F1 score for the total field. The system also demonstrated superior performance across all individual fields, including rare fields such as total_tax_amount and amount_due, which are notoriously difficult for traditional systems. Ablation studies confirmed that the most important feature for performance was the neighbor text, followed by candidate position and neighbor position. The self-attention mechanism contributed significantly to performance, improving the scorer’s ROC AUC by one point and the end-to-end F1 by 1.7 points. The field embeddings were found to be highly discriminative, with the embedding for invoice_date consistently positioned far from the embedding for due_date, even though both fields are associated with dates and often appear in similar locations on the page.

The invention is not limited to invoices or receipts. The same architecture can be applied to any form-like document with a well-defined schema and type-based candidates, including tax forms, insurance quotes, purchase orders, and shipping manifests. The system requires no re-engineering for new domains—only a new schema and a small set of annotated examples. The learned candidate embeddings can be transferred to new domains via fine-tuning, enabling few-shot learning scenarios where only a handful of labeled documents are available. The system’s ability to generalize to unseen templates, its interpretability, and its computational efficiency make it uniquely suited for enterprise-scale deployment in industries where document processing is a critical, high-volume, and high-stakes operation. Unlike prior systems that require manual template creation or extensive rule maintenance, this invention learns its extraction logic from data, adapts to new formats automatically, and provides clear explanations for its decisions, thereby transforming document extraction from a brittle, labor-intensive task into a scalable, intelligent, and trustworthy automated process.
