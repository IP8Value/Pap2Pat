## BACKGROUND

The extraction of structured knowledge from unstructured textual data has become a foundational component in the development of large-scale knowledge systems capable of supporting intelligent reasoning, question answering, and semantic search. Open Information Extraction (OpenIE) systems have emerged as a dominant paradigm for this task, enabling the automatic derivation of subject-predicate-object triples from natural language text without reliance on predefined ontologies or schema constraints. These systems operate by identifying syntactic patterns and semantic relationships within sentences, producing triples in the form of (noun phrase, relation phrase, noun phrase), thereby constructing vast Open Knowledge Graphs (OpenKGs) that capture a broad spectrum of factual assertions derived from diverse textual corpora. While the scalability and adaptability of OpenIE methods make them uniquely suited for domain-agnostic knowledge acquisition, a critical limitation inherent in these systems is the absence of canonicalization—meaning that multiple surface forms referring to the same underlying entity or relation are treated as distinct and unrelated entries within the knowledge graph.

For instance, consider the triples (NBC-TV, has headquarters in, NYC), (NBC Television, is in, New York City), and (NBC-TV, has main office in, NYC). Although each triple conveys essentially the same factual content—namely, that the broadcasting entity NBC has its principal location in the city commonly known as New York City—the OpenKG constructed from these extractions would contain three separate, non-merged assertions. This fragmentation arises because OpenIE systems operate at the lexical level, treating surface forms as atomic units without access to semantic equivalence knowledge. Consequently, entities such as “NYC,” “New York City,” and “the City of New York” are not recognized as referring to the same real-world location, and relations such as “has headquarters in,” “is in,” and “has main office in” are not understood as semantically aligned predicates. This lack of canonicalization leads to a proliferation of redundant facts, which significantly degrades the utility of OpenKGs in downstream applications.

The consequences of this fragmentation are both computational and functional. From a computational perspective, the storage and indexing of duplicate or near-duplicate triples consume excessive memory and processing resources, increasing the cost of graph traversal, query execution, and knowledge inference. From a functional perspective, the inability to recognize equivalence between surface forms results in incomplete or inconsistent query results. For example, a user querying for all facts associated with “New York City” would fail to retrieve information linked to “NYC,” even though both refer to the same entity. This undermines the reliability of knowledge graphs in applications such as semantic search engines, recommendation systems, and automated reasoning tools, where completeness and precision are paramount. Furthermore, machine learning models that rely on OpenKGs for training—such as link prediction systems, entity typing frameworks, or relation extraction pipelines—are hindered by the noise introduced by non-canonicalized mentions, leading to suboptimal performance and reduced generalization.

Previous approaches to addressing this issue have largely relied on either external knowledge bases or rule-based clustering techniques. Entity Linking (EL) methods attempt to map noun phrases to canonical entries in established knowledge bases such as Wikipedia or Wikidata. However, these approaches are fundamentally limited by their dependence on pre-existing knowledge bases, which are often incomplete, domain-specific, or outdated. Many entity mentions, particularly those referring to niche entities, emerging concepts, or domain-specific jargon, do not appear in such databases, rendering EL systems ineffective for a substantial portion of OpenKG content. Alternative methods have employed string similarity metrics, such as edit distance or token overlap, to cluster phrases based on lexical resemblance. While these techniques are computationally efficient, they are highly sensitive to surface form variation and fail to capture semantic equivalence when lexical overlap is minimal, as in the case of “Kodagu” and “Coorg,” which refer to the same geographic region in India but share no common tokens.

More sophisticated approaches have attempted to leverage distributional semantics by embedding noun and relation phrases into continuous vector spaces using pretrained word representations such as GloVe or FastText, followed by clustering via Hierarchical Agglomerative Clustering (HAC). These methods improve upon lexical approaches by capturing semantic similarity through contextual co-occurrence patterns. However, they remain fundamentally disconnected from the structural context of the knowledge graph itself. That is, while two noun phrases may have similar embeddings based on their textual context, their placement within the triple structure—i.e., the relations they participate in and the other entities they connect to—is ignored. This leads to incorrect clustering decisions, such as grouping together entities that share similar linguistic features but refer to distinct concepts, or failing to group entities that are semantically identical but appear in structurally dissimilar contexts.

The state-of-the-art method known as CESI addresses this limitation by integrating knowledge graph embeddings with side information derived from external lexical resources. CESI employs the HolE (Holistic Embedding) model to learn joint representations of entities and relations, and then applies HAC to cluster the resulting embeddings. It further incorporates side information from sources such as paraphrase databases, entity linking outputs, and morphological normalization rules to guide clustering decisions. While CESI represents a significant advancement, it remains a two-stage pipeline: first, embeddings are learned independently of clustering; second, clustering is performed as a post-processing step using a separate algorithm. This architectural separation prevents the model from jointly optimizing the representation space and the cluster assignments, resulting in suboptimal solutions where the learned embeddings are not tailored to the clustering objective, and the clustering algorithm is unable to refine the embeddings based on cluster structure.

Moreover, existing methods do not adequately account for the probabilistic nature of equivalence. In real-world knowledge extraction, the assertion that two mentions refer to the same entity is rarely binary; rather, it is a matter of degree, influenced by context, frequency of co-occurrence, and semantic plausibility. Hard clustering methods, which assign each mention to exactly one cluster, are ill-suited to this reality, as they force ambiguous or polysemous mentions into a single category, thereby introducing errors. Soft clustering, which allows a mention to belong to multiple clusters with varying degrees of membership, offers a more nuanced and accurate representation of uncertainty, but has not been effectively integrated with joint representation learning in prior work.

There is therefore a critical and unmet need for a unified, end-to-end neural architecture capable of simultaneously learning high-dimensional representations of noun and relation phrases while dynamically inferring their cluster memberships in a probabilistic, context-aware, and structure-sensitive manner. Such an architecture must be capable of leveraging both the structural topology of the knowledge graph and the contextual information derived from the source text, without relying on external knowledge bases or rigid clustering heuristics. It must also be able to handle ambiguity, polysemy, and partial equivalence through soft clustering, and must optimize representation learning and cluster assignment in a single, coherent framework. The invention described herein fulfills this need by introducing a novel variational autoencoder-based framework that jointly models entity and relation canonicalization through a generative probabilistic architecture, enabling the first truly integrated solution to the OpenKG canonicalization problem.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a novel neural architecture for the canonicalization of noun phrases and relation phrases within Open Knowledge Graphs, termed Canonicalizing Using Variational Autoencoders (CUVA). The invention provides a unified, end-to-end framework that simultaneously learns distributed representations of entity and relation mentions while inferring their cluster assignments in a probabilistic, context-aware, and structure-sensitive manner. Unlike prior methods that rely on sequential pipelines or external knowledge bases, CUVA integrates three core components into a single trainable model: a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) for each of entities and relations, a Knowledge Graph Embedding (KGE) module that encodes structural relationships, and a side information constraint mechanism that incorporates external equivalence signals. The VAE components model each latent entity and relation as a Gaussian distribution in a low-dimensional latent space, enabling soft clustering where each mention may belong to multiple clusters with varying probabilities. The KGE module ensures that the learned representations are informed by the topological structure of the knowledge graph, preserving the relational context in which each mention appears. The side information component allows the model to incorporate external signals of equivalence—such as those derived from paraphrase databases, entity linking systems, or morphological normalization rules—as soft constraints during training, thereby guiding the clustering process without requiring hard mappings.

The input to the CUVA system comprises a set of triples extracted from an Open Information Extraction system, each consisting of a head noun phrase, a relation phrase, and a tail noun phrase, along with a collection of side information pairs indicating potential equivalence between mentions. The output is a set of probabilistic cluster assignments for all noun phrases and relation phrases, where each assignment reflects the likelihood that a given mention corresponds to a particular latent entity or relation. The model is trained using a two-stage optimization procedure that first initializes the Gaussian mixture components using hierarchical clustering of pretrained embeddings, and then alternates between training the encoder to refine cluster assignments and training the decoder to reconstruct the input representations under the learned latent structure. During training, the model minimizes a composite loss function that includes the evidence lower bound (ELBO) of the VAE, a knowledge graph embedding loss, a side information loss computed as a weighted mean squared error over equivalence constraints, and L1 regularization to prevent overfitting.

The CUVA architecture fundamentally departs from prior approaches by eliminating the separation between representation learning and clustering. Instead of first learning embeddings and then applying a separate clustering algorithm, CUVA treats cluster assignment as a latent variable that is jointly optimized with the embedding space through variational inference. This enables gradients to flow from the clustering objective back into the embedding parameters, allowing the model to iteratively refine both the representations and their groupings in a mutually reinforcing manner. As a result, mentions that are structurally similar—i.e., participate in similar relations and connect to similar entities—are pulled closer in the embedding space, while also being assigned to the same latent cluster with high probability. The use of Gaussian mixture models in the latent space further allows the model to capture polysemy and ambiguity, as a single mention can be associated with multiple Gaussians, each representing a different sense or interpretation.

The invention demonstrates superior performance across four benchmark datasets for entity canonicalization, including two established datasets (Base and Ambiguous), a large-scale dataset (ReVerb45K), and a newly introduced dataset (CANONICNELL) derived from the NELL knowledge base. On all benchmarks, CUVA achieves state-of-the-art results, outperforming prior methods such as CESI, GloVe+HAC, and HolE-based approaches, with statistically significant improvements in macro, micro, and pair F1 scores. Notably, CUVA achieves these gains without requiring any manual annotation or ground-truth cluster labels, operating entirely in an unsupervised setting. The model’s ability to generalize to new domains is further evidenced by its performance on CANONICNELL, a dataset whose provenance is independent of the ReVerb extraction system, demonstrating that CUVA is not merely overfitting to specific extraction patterns but is learning a generalizable canonicalization strategy.

The invention also provides a novel training strategy that prevents the decoder from bypassing the latent variables and learning directly from the input, a common failure mode in variational autoencoders. This is achieved by first training the encoder using weak supervision derived from hierarchical clustering, and then freezing the encoder while training the decoder to reconstruct the input solely from the latent representations. This two-step procedure ensures that the latent space is meaningfully structured and that the decoder learns to generate plausible mentions from cluster centroids rather than memorizing input patterns. The integration of a differentiable soft argmax function enables the model to compute cluster assignments in a differentiable manner, allowing end-to-end gradient propagation through the entire pipeline, including the knowledge graph embedding module.

The CUVA architecture is implemented as a software system executable on general-purpose computing hardware, comprising a memory unit for storing the knowledge graph and side information, a processor for executing the neural network components, and a training module for optimizing the model parameters. The system is capable of operating in batch mode on large-scale knowledge graphs and can be deployed in real-time environments for incremental canonicalization as new triples are extracted. The invention further includes a method for evaluating the quality of canonicalization using standard clustering metrics, including macro F1, micro F1, and pair F1, as well as a mechanism for interpreting the learned clusters through qualitative analysis of representative mentions.

The invention provides a technical solution to the long-standing problem of redundancy and inconsistency in Open Knowledge Graphs by enabling the first fully integrated, probabilistic, and structure-aware canonicalization system. It eliminates the need for external knowledge bases, avoids the limitations of hard clustering, and overcomes the architectural separation inherent in prior pipeline approaches. The result is a knowledge graph that is compact, semantically coherent, and optimized for downstream reasoning tasks, thereby unlocking new capabilities in semantic search, automated reasoning, and knowledge discovery.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention described herein is a neural architecture for the canonicalization of noun phrases and relation phrases within Open Knowledge Graphs, implemented as a system and method for simultaneously learning distributed representations and probabilistic cluster assignments through a variational autoencoder framework. The architecture, referred to as Canonicalizing Using Variational Autoencoders (CUVA), is designed to operate on a set of triples extracted from an Open Information Extraction system, where each triple consists of a head noun phrase, a relation phrase, and a tail noun phrase. The system is trained without supervision and does not require any predefined ontology, canonical entity list, or manually annotated cluster labels. Instead, it leverages the structural relationships within the knowledge graph, the contextual information derived from the source text, and optional side information signals to infer latent entities and relations that underlie the surface forms of the mentions.

The core of the CUVA architecture consists of two distinct but interdependent variational autoencoder modules: one for entities and one for relations. Each module is structured as a mixture of Gaussians in the latent space, where each Gaussian component corresponds to a latent entity or relation. The model assumes that each observed noun phrase or relation phrase is generated by sampling from one of these latent Gaussians, with the probability of assignment determined by a soft clustering mechanism. The latent space is low-dimensional, typically ranging from 50 to 200 dimensions, and is designed to capture the semantic essence of each entity or relation while abstracting away surface-level variations in phrasing. The encoder component of each VAE maps an input mention—represented as a high-dimensional embedding—to a mean and variance vector that parameterize a Gaussian distribution in the latent space. The reparameterization trick is employed to sample a latent variable from this distribution, enabling gradient-based optimization through stochastic sampling. The decoder then reconstructs the original mention embedding from the sampled latent variable, ensuring that the latent space is structured such that meaningful clusters emerge naturally from the reconstruction objective.

To ensure that the learned representations are informed by the structural context of the knowledge graph, the CUVA architecture incorporates a Knowledge Graph Embedding (KGE) module that operates on the cluster assignments inferred by the VAEs. Specifically, for each triple (h, r, t), the model first computes the probability that the head noun phrase h, the relation phrase r, and the tail noun phrase t belong to each of the K latent entities or relations. These probabilities are then transformed into soft cluster assignments using a differentiable soft argmax function, which approximates the non-differentiable argmax operation while preserving gradient flow. The resulting soft assignments are used to index into lookup tables containing the mean vectors of the Gaussians for entities and relations, yielding continuous representations for each mention in terms of its most probable latent entity or relation. These representations are then fed into a HolE (Holistic Embedding) model, which computes a scoring function over the triple to determine the plausibility of the assertion. The difference between the predicted score and the ground truth (implicitly assumed to be positive for all observed triples) is used to compute a KGE loss, which is backpropagated through the entire architecture to refine both the latent representations and the cluster assignments.

In addition to the structural signal provided by the knowledge graph, the CUVA architecture incorporates side information in the form of equivalence constraints between mentions. These constraints are derived from external sources such as entity linking systems, paraphrase databases, morphological normalization rules, and term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) overlap metrics. Each constraint is represented as a pair of mentions with an associated plausibility score indicating the confidence that the two mentions refer to the same entity or relation. The model computes a side information loss by calculating the mean squared error between the embeddings of the two mentions in the learned representation space, weighted by the plausibility score. This loss is added to the overall objective function, encouraging the model to pull together mentions that are known to be equivalent while allowing the system to ignore low-confidence or noisy constraints.

The training of the CUVA model is conducted in two distinct stages to prevent the decoder from bypassing the latent variables and learning directly from the input. In the first stage, the encoder is trained while the decoder is held fixed, using weak supervision derived from hierarchical agglomerative clustering (HAC) applied to pretrained GloVe embeddings. The HAC algorithm is applied separately to the noun phrases and relation phrases, using a threshold to determine the number of clusters. The within-cluster means and variances of the GloVe embeddings are then used to initialize the parameters of the Gaussian mixture components in the VAEs. This initialization provides a reasonable starting point for the latent space, ensuring that the model begins with a structured representation rather than a random one. The encoder is then trained to predict the cluster assignments generated by HAC, minimizing a negative log-likelihood loss between the predicted assignment probabilities and the HAC labels. During this phase, the side information loss is also included to correct for errors introduced by the HAC clustering.

In the second stage, the encoder is frozen, and the decoder is trained to reconstruct the input embeddings from the latent variables sampled from the Gaussian mixture. The decoder is optimized to minimize the evidence lower bound (ELBO), which consists of a reconstruction term that encourages the decoder to accurately reproduce the input and a Kullback-Leibler divergence term that regularizes the latent distribution to match a prior (typically a standard normal distribution). The KGE loss and side information loss are also included in this stage, ensuring that the decoder learns to generate plausible mentions from the latent centroids while respecting the structural and equivalence constraints. The two-stage training procedure ensures that the latent space is meaningfully structured and that the decoder does not simply memorize the input, thereby preserving the generative nature of the VAE.

The final model produces, for each noun phrase and relation phrase, a vector of cluster assignment probabilities across all K latent entities or relations. During inference, each mention is assigned to the cluster with the highest probability using a winner-takes-all strategy, although the full probability distribution is retained for downstream applications that benefit from soft clustering. The model is trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1e-3 for the first stage and 1e-4 for the second stage, with L1 regularization applied to both encoder and decoder parameters to prevent overfitting. The batch size is set to 50 during training and 5 during evaluation, and negative sampling is employed during the KGE loss computation to improve convergence.

The CUVA architecture is implemented as a software system comprising a memory unit for storing the knowledge graph and side information, a processor for executing the neural network components, and a training module for optimizing the model parameters. The system is capable of operating on large-scale knowledge graphs with millions of triples and can be deployed in both batch and incremental modes. The system accepts as input a set of triples in the form of (head, relation, tail) along with a set of side information pairs, and outputs a mapping from each mention to its most probable latent entity or relation. The system further includes a module for evaluating the quality of canonicalization using standard clustering metrics such as macro F1, micro F1, and pair F1, as well as a visualization module for interpreting the learned clusters through qualitative analysis.

The invention provides several technical advantages over prior approaches. First, by integrating representation learning and clustering into a single framework, CUVA eliminates the suboptimality inherent in pipeline methods that treat these tasks as separate. Second, by using a variational autoencoder with a Gaussian mixture prior, CUVA enables soft clustering, allowing for the representation of ambiguity and polysemy in a principled manner. Third, by incorporating a differentiable KGE module, CUVA ensures that the learned representations are grounded in the structural context of the knowledge graph, rather than being derived solely from lexical co-occurrence. Fourth, by incorporating side information as soft constraints, CUVA leverages external knowledge without requiring hard mappings or external databases, making it more robust and generalizable. Fifth, by employing a two-stage training procedure, CUVA prevents the decoder from bypassing the latent variables, ensuring that the latent space is meaningfully structured and that the model learns a true generative process.

The CUVA architecture has been evaluated on four benchmark datasets for entity canonicalization, including Base, Ambiguous, ReVerb45K, and CANONICNELL. On all datasets, CUVA achieves state-of-the-art performance, outperforming prior methods such as CESI, GloVe+HAC, and HolE-based approaches. On the ReVerb45K dataset, CUVA achieves a pair F1 score of 0.385, compared to 0.379 for CESI, representing a statistically significant improvement. On the CANONICNELL dataset, which is derived from the NELL knowledge base and has no relation to the ReVerb extraction system, CUVA achieves a pair F1 score of 0.392, demonstrating its ability to generalize to new domains. Qualitative analysis of the learned clusters reveals that CUVA successfully groups mentions such as “Kodagu” and “Coorg,” which refer to the same geographic region despite having no lexical overlap, and correctly identifies equivalence between “has headquarters in,” “is in,” and “has main office in,” even when these relations appear in different syntactic contexts. The model also demonstrates the ability to handle polysemy, as mentions such as “Bill” are assigned to multiple clusters corresponding to different individuals, with the highest probability assigned to the most frequent referent.

The invention further includes a method for extending the CUVA architecture to support dynamic knowledge graphs, where new triples are continuously added over time. In this mode, the model can be incrementally updated by retraining the encoder and decoder on new data while preserving the learned latent structure, allowing the system to adapt to evolving knowledge without requiring full retraining. The system can also be extended to support multi-modal inputs, such as images or structured tables, by augmenting the input embeddings with features derived from these modalities.

The CUVA architecture is implemented in the PyTorch deep learning framework and is executable on standard computing hardware, including systems with Intel x86 processors and NVIDIA V100 GPUs. The system requires approximately 16 gigabytes of RAM for training on large datasets and can be deployed on cloud-based infrastructure for scalable operation. The invention further includes a method for exporting the learned latent representations and cluster assignments in standard formats such as JSON or RDF, enabling integration with existing knowledge graph platforms and semantic web tools.

The invention provides a novel and non-obvious solution to the problem of canonicalization in Open Knowledge Graphs, overcoming the limitations of prior methods by integrating representation learning, structural modeling, and soft clustering into a single, end-to-end trainable framework. The resulting system is more accurate, more generalizable, and more scalable than any previous approach, enabling the construction of compact, semantically coherent knowledge graphs that are optimized for downstream reasoning and inference tasks.

### Further Comments and/or Embodiments

The CUVA architecture can be extended in several ways to enhance its performance, adaptability, and scalability. One such extension involves the integration of type information for noun phrases, such as entity types derived from a predefined ontology or inferred from the context in which the mention appears. For example, if a noun phrase is known to be of type “Person,” “Location,” or “Organization,” this information can be encoded as an additional feature vector and concatenated with the mention embedding before being passed to the encoder. This would enable the model to disambiguate mentions that are otherwise identical in form but refer to different entities—for instance, distinguishing between “Bill Cosby” and “Bill Nye” by recognizing that both are persons but belong to different semantic categories. The inclusion of type information would also help mitigate errors such as the clustering of unrelated individuals who share a common name, as observed in the qualitative analysis of the ReVerb45K dataset.

Another extension involves the incorporation of temporal information into the model. In many knowledge graphs, the validity of a triple may depend on the time at which it was extracted or the time period to which it refers. For example, the triple (Apple, has headquarters in, Cupertino) may have been true in 2010 but may no longer be relevant if Apple were to relocate. By augmenting the input representation with a temporal embedding that encodes the year or date associated with the triple, the model could learn to distinguish between canonical entities that change over time, such as political leaders or corporate executives, and those that remain stable. This would enable the system to support temporal canonicalization, where the same surface form may refer to different latent entities depending on the time context.

A further embodiment involves the use of multi-modal inputs, where the noun phrases and relation phrases are accompanied by images, audio, or structured data. For instance, if a noun phrase such as “Eiffel Tower” is associated with an image of the landmark, the visual features extracted from the image can be encoded using a convolutional neural network and fused with the textual embedding before being passed to the CUVA encoder. This would allow the model to leverage visual evidence to resolve ambiguity—for example, distinguishing between “Eiffel Tower” in Paris and “Eiffel Tower” in Las Vegas based on visual context. Similarly, if a relation phrase such as “is married to” is accompanied by a textual quote from a news article, the model could use the surrounding discourse to determine the strength and validity of the relation, thereby improving the quality of the canonicalization.

The CUVA architecture can also be adapted for use in low-resource settings, where side information is scarce or unavailable. In such cases, the model can be trained using only the knowledge graph structure and the VAE components, without the side information loss. The two-stage training procedure ensures that the model still learns meaningful representations and cluster assignments, albeit with reduced accuracy. This makes the system suitable for deployment in domains where external resources such as paraphrase databases or entity linkers are not available, such as in proprietary or domain-specific knowledge bases.

The system can be further enhanced by incorporating active learning mechanisms, wherein the model identifies ambiguous or uncertain mentions—those with low entropy in their cluster assignment probabilities—and requests human feedback to resolve the ambiguity. This feedback can then be incorporated as new side information, allowing the system to iteratively improve its performance over time. This embodiment is particularly useful in applications such as enterprise knowledge management, where domain experts can provide feedback on canonicalization decisions, enabling the system to adapt to organizational terminology and evolving business semantics.

The CUVA architecture can also be deployed in a distributed setting, where the knowledge graph is partitioned across multiple nodes, and each node runs a local instance of the model. The latent representations and cluster assignments from each node are periodically synchronized through a consensus mechanism, such as federated averaging, to ensure global consistency. This enables the system to scale to knowledge graphs with billions of triples, as would be required for web-scale applications.

The invention further includes a method for evaluating the quality of the learned clusters using external validation metrics such as normalized mutual information (NMI) and adjusted rand index (ARI), in addition to the standard F1 scores. These metrics provide complementary insights into the clustering performance, with NMI measuring the mutual information between the predicted and gold-standard clusters and ARI measuring the agreement between clusterings while accounting for chance. The inclusion of these metrics allows for a more comprehensive evaluation of the system’s performance across different dimensions of clustering quality.

The CUVA architecture can also be extended to support cross-lingual canonicalization, where noun phrases and relation phrases are expressed in multiple languages. In this embodiment, the input embeddings are generated using a multilingual language model such as mBERT or XLM-R, and the latent space is shared across languages, enabling the model to identify equivalent entities and relations across linguistic boundaries. For example, the model could recognize that “New York City” in English and “Nueva York” in Spanish refer to the same entity, even in the absence of direct translation pairs. This would enable the construction of multilingual knowledge graphs that are canonicalized across languages, facilitating cross-lingual knowledge transfer and semantic search.

Finally, the invention includes a method for generating explanations for the canonicalization decisions made by the model. For each cluster, the system identifies the most representative mentions based on their assignment probabilities and generates a natural language description of the latent entity or relation. For example, the cluster containing “NYC,” “New York City,” and “the Big Apple” could be labeled as “a major metropolitan area in the northeastern United States.” This explanation is generated using a template-based or neural generation module that maps the cluster centroid to a descriptive phrase. This embodiment enhances the interpretability of the system, making it suitable for applications where human users need to understand and validate the canonicalization decisions, such as in legal, medical, or scientific knowledge bases.

### 1. INTRODUCTION

The canonicalization of noun phrases and relation phrases within Open Knowledge Graphs represents a fundamental challenge in the field of knowledge extraction and semantic reasoning. Open Information Extraction systems have enabled the automatic construction of vast knowledge graphs from unstructured text, capturing millions of factual assertions without requiring predefined ontologies or schema constraints. However, the absence of canonicalization in these systems leads to a proliferation of redundant and inconsistent triples, where multiple surface forms refer to the same underlying entity or relation. This fragmentation undermines the utility of Open Knowledge Graphs in downstream applications, including semantic search, question answering, and machine learning inference, by introducing noise, increasing computational overhead, and reducing query completeness. Despite significant advances in representation learning and clustering techniques, existing methods remain fundamentally limited by their reliance on sequential pipelines, external knowledge bases, or rigid clustering heuristics that fail to capture the probabilistic and structural nature of equivalence.

The present invention introduces Canonicalizing Using Variational Autoencoders (CUVA), a novel neural architecture that addresses these limitations by unifying representation learning and cluster assignment into a single, end-to-end trainable framework. Unlike prior approaches that first learn embeddings and then apply clustering as a post-processing step, CUVA models the canonicalization task as a joint inference problem, where the latent representations of entities and relations and their cluster assignments are learned simultaneously through variational inference. The architecture employs two variational autoencoders—one for entities and one for relations—each structured as a mixture of Gaussians in the latent space, enabling soft clustering where each mention may belong to multiple clusters with varying degrees of membership. This probabilistic formulation allows the model to handle ambiguity and polysemy, accommodating cases where a single mention may refer to multiple entities depending on context.

The CUVA architecture further incorporates a Knowledge Graph Embedding (KGE) module that encodes the structural relationships between entities and relations, ensuring that the learned representations are grounded in the topological context of the knowledge graph. By integrating the KGE module with the VAE components, the model ensures that mentions that participate in similar relations and connect to similar entities are pulled closer in the embedding space, while also being assigned to the same latent cluster. This structural awareness distinguishes CUVA from prior methods that rely solely on lexical or distributional similarity, which often fail to distinguish between semantically distinct entities that share similar surface forms.

In addition to structural information, CUVA leverages side information derived from external sources such as paraphrase databases, entity linking systems, and morphological normalization rules. These signals are incorporated as soft constraints in the form of equivalence pairs with associated plausibility scores, allowing the model to refine its cluster assignments without requiring hard mappings or external databases. The side information loss is computed as a weighted mean squared error between the embeddings of equivalent mentions, encouraging the model to align representations that are known to refer to the same entity or relation.

The training of CUVA follows a two-stage procedure designed to prevent the decoder from bypassing the latent variables and learning directly from the input. In the first stage, the encoder is trained using weak supervision derived from hierarchical clustering of pretrained embeddings, ensuring that the latent space begins with a meaningful structure. In the second stage, the encoder is frozen, and the decoder is trained to reconstruct the input from the latent variables, minimizing the evidence lower bound and incorporating the KGE and side information losses. This procedure ensures that the latent space is meaningfully structured and that the model learns a true generative process, rather than memorizing input patterns.

The CUVA architecture has been evaluated on four benchmark datasets for entity canonicalization, including Base, Ambiguous, ReVerb45K, and CANONICNELL. On all datasets, CUVA achieves state-of-the-art performance, outperforming prior methods such as CESI, GloVe+HAC, and HolE-based approaches. The model demonstrates superior generalization on CANONICNELL, a dataset derived from the NELL knowledge base and independent of the ReVerb extraction system, confirming that CUVA learns a generalizable canonicalization strategy rather than overfitting to specific extraction patterns. Qualitative analysis reveals that CUVA successfully groups mentions such as “Kodagu” and “Coorg,” which refer to the same geographic region despite having no lexical overlap, and correctly identifies equivalence between relations such as “has headquarters in,” “is in,” and “has main office in,” even when these relations appear in different syntactic contexts.

The invention provides a technical solution to the long-standing problem of redundancy and inconsistency in Open Knowledge Graphs by enabling the first fully integrated, probabilistic, and structure-aware canonicalization system. The resulting knowledge graph is compact, semantically coherent, and optimized for downstream reasoning tasks, thereby unlocking new capabilities in semantic search, automated reasoning, and knowledge discovery. The CUVA architecture is implemented as a software system executable on general-purpose computing hardware and can be deployed in both batch and incremental modes, making it suitable for large-scale and real-time applications. The invention further includes methods for extending the architecture to support type information, temporal context, multi-modal inputs, cross-lingual canonicalization, and explainable decision-making, ensuring its adaptability to a wide range of knowledge extraction scenarios.

### 2. RELATED WORK

The canonicalization of noun phrases and relation phrases within Open Knowledge Graphs has been the subject of extensive research, with approaches ranging from rule-based clustering to distributional embedding methods. Early work in this domain focused on string similarity metrics, such as edit distance and token overlap, to group mentions that share lexical features. The approach introduced by Galárraga et al. (2014a) utilized inverse document frequency (IDF) weighted token overlap as a feature for canonicalization, reasoning that infrequent terms are more likely to be discriminative and indicative of equivalence. While this method was effective for simple cases, such as “William Shakespeare” and “Shakespeare,” it failed to capture semantic equivalence in cases where mentions shared no common tokens, such as “Kodagu” and “Coorg.” Subsequent efforts by Yates and Etzioni (2009) extended this approach by incorporating additional string-based features, including prefix and suffix matching, but these methods remained fundamentally limited by their reliance on surface form similarity and their inability to generalize to novel or ambiguous mentions.

A more sophisticated class of approaches leveraged distributional semantics by embedding mentions into continuous vector spaces using pretrained word representations such as GloVe or FastText. These embeddings capture semantic similarity through co-occurrence patterns in large corpora, allowing mentions with similar meanings to be positioned close together in the embedding space. The GloVe+HAC method, which applies hierarchical agglomerative clustering to GloVe embeddings, demonstrated improved performance over string-based methods by capturing semantic equivalence beyond lexical overlap. However, this approach remained disconnected from the structural context of the knowledge graph, leading to incorrect clusterings when mentions were semantically similar but structurally dissimilar. For example, the mentions “is born in” and “has died in” may have similar embeddings due to their shared argument types (person and location), but they refer to fundamentally different relations. The failure to incorporate structural information resulted in a high rate of false positives and a lack of discriminative power.

The CESI architecture, proposed by Vashishth et al. (2018a), represented a significant advancement by integrating knowledge graph embeddings with side information. CESI employed the HolE (Holistic Embedding) model to learn joint representations of entities and relations, capturing the structural topology of the knowledge graph. These embeddings were then clustered using hierarchical agglomerative clustering, with side information from paraphrase databases, entity linking systems, and morphological normalization rules used to guide the clustering process. While CESI outperformed prior methods, it remained a two-stage pipeline, with representation learning and clustering treated as separate tasks. This architectural separation prevented the model from jointly optimizing the embedding space and the cluster assignments, resulting in suboptimal solutions where the learned embeddings were not tailored to the clustering objective, and the clustering algorithm was unable to refine the embeddings based on cluster structure.

Other approaches attempted to address the canonicalization problem through entity linking, mapping noun phrases to canonical entries in external knowledge bases such as Wikipedia or Wikidata. While these methods were effective for well-known entities, they were fundamentally limited by their dependence on pre-existing knowledge bases, which are often incomplete, domain-specific, or outdated. Many entity mentions, particularly those referring to niche entities, emerging concepts, or domain-specific jargon, do not appear in such databases, rendering entity linking systems ineffective for a substantial portion of OpenKG content. Furthermore, entity linking systems are prone to errors due to incorrect disambiguation, leading to the propagation of noise into the canonicalization process.

Concept Resolver, introduced by Krishnamurthy and Mitchell (2011), attempted to address this issue by making the “one sense per category” assumption, where each mention is assumed to refer to a single concept within a predefined schema. However, this assumption is often violated in practice, as many mentions are polysemous or refer to multiple concepts depending on context. KB-Unify, proposed by Delli Bovi et al. (2015), aimed to unify multiple canonical and open knowledge graphs into a single graph, but required a sense inventory that was not always available, limiting its applicability to domains with rich ontological resources.

The CESI architecture, while state-of-the-art, still suffered from several critical limitations. First, its pipeline structure prevented the model from jointly optimizing representation learning and clustering, resulting in a suboptimal solution. Second, its reliance on hard clustering meant that ambiguous or polysemous mentions were forced into a single cluster, introducing errors. Third, its use of external knowledge bases introduced dependency on external resources that may not be available in all domains. Finally, the model did not account for the probabilistic nature of equivalence, treating all side information as binary constraints rather than soft signals with varying degrees of confidence.

The present invention overcomes these limitations by introducing a unified, end-to-end framework that simultaneously learns distributed representations and probabilistic cluster assignments through a variational autoencoder architecture. Unlike prior methods, CUVA does not separate representation learning from clustering but instead treats cluster assignment as a latent variable that is jointly optimized with the embedding space through variational inference. The use of a Gaussian mixture model in the latent space enables soft clustering, allowing for the representation of ambiguity and polysemy. The integration of a differentiable KGE module ensures that the learned representations are grounded in the structural context of the knowledge graph, while the incorporation of side information as soft constraints allows the model to leverage external knowledge without requiring hard mappings. The two-stage training procedure ensures that the latent space is meaningfully structured and that the model learns a true generative process, rather than memorizing input patterns. Together, these innovations enable CUVA to achieve state-of-the-art performance on multiple benchmarks, demonstrating its superiority over all prior approaches in terms of accuracy, generalization, and robustness.

### 3. OPEN KGS CANONICALIZATION USING VAE

The Canonicalizing Using Variational Autoencoders (CUVA) architecture is designed to address the canonicalization of noun phrases and relation phrases within Open Knowledge Graphs through a unified, probabilistic, and structure-aware framework. The core assumption underlying CUVA is that each observed noun phrase or relation phrase is generated by sampling from a latent Gaussian distribution, where each Gaussian corresponds to a canonical entity or relation. This generative modeling approach enables the system to capture the inherent uncertainty in canonicalization, allowing a single mention to belong to multiple latent entities with varying degrees of probability. The architecture consists of two distinct variational autoencoders: one for entities and one for relations, each structured as a mixture of Gaussians in the latent space. The encoder component of each VAE maps an input mention—represented as a high-dimensional embedding—to a mean and variance vector that parameterize a Gaussian distribution. The reparameterization trick is employed to sample a latent variable from this distribution, enabling gradient-based optimization through stochastic sampling. The decoder then reconstructs the original mention embedding from the sampled latent variable, ensuring that the latent space is structured such that meaningful clusters emerge naturally from the reconstruction objective.

The latent space is low-dimensional, typically ranging from 50 to 200 dimensions, and is designed to capture the semantic essence of each entity or relation while abstracting away surface-level variations in phrasing. For example, the mentions “NYC,” “New York City,” and “the City of New York” are mapped to similar latent representations, as they refer to the same entity, while “NYC” and “Nairobi” are mapped to distinct representations due to their semantic difference. The use of a mixture of Gaussians allows the model to capture polysemy, as a single mention may be associated with multiple Gaussians, each representing a different sense or interpretation. This soft clustering capability distinguishes CUVA from hard clustering methods, which assign each mention to exactly one cluster and are therefore unable to handle ambiguity.

To ensure that the learned representations are informed by the structural context of the knowledge graph, the CUVA architecture incorporates a Knowledge Graph Embedding (KGE) module that operates on the cluster assignments inferred by the VAEs. For each triple (h, r, t), the model first computes the probability that the head noun phrase h, the relation phrase r, and the tail noun phrase t belong to each of the K latent entities or relations. These probabilities are then transformed into soft cluster assignments using a differentiable soft argmax function, which approximates the non-differentiable argmax operation while preserving gradient flow. The resulting soft assignments are used to index into lookup tables containing the mean vectors of the Gaussians for entities and relations, yielding continuous representations for each mention in terms of its most probable latent entity or relation. These representations are then fed into a HolE (Holistic Embedding) model, which computes a scoring function over the triple to determine the plausibility of the assertion. The difference between the predicted score and the ground truth (implicitly assumed to be positive for all observed triples) is used to compute a KGE loss, which is backpropagated through the entire architecture to refine both the latent representations and the cluster assignments.

The integration of the KGE module with the VAE components ensures that mentions that participate in similar relations and connect to similar entities are pulled closer in the embedding space, while also being assigned to the same latent cluster. This structural awareness distinguishes CUVA from prior methods that rely solely on lexical or distributional similarity, which often fail to distinguish between semantically distinct entities that share similar surface forms. For example, the mentions “is born in” and “has died in” may have similar embeddings due to their shared argument types (person and location), but the KGE module ensures that they are assigned to different latent relations because they connect to different entities in the knowledge graph.

In addition to structural information, CUVA leverages side information derived from external sources such as paraphrase databases, entity linking systems, and morphological normalization rules. These signals are incorporated as soft constraints in the form of equivalence pairs with associated plausibility scores, allowing the model to refine its cluster assignments without requiring hard mappings or external databases. The side information loss is computed as a weighted mean squared error between the embeddings of equivalent mentions, encouraging the model to align representations that are known to refer to the same entity or relation. This approach allows CUVA to leverage external knowledge while remaining robust to noise and errors in the side information sources.

The training of CUVA follows a two-stage procedure designed to prevent the decoder from bypassing the latent variables and learning directly from the input. In the first stage, the encoder is trained using weak supervision derived from hierarchical clustering of pretrained embeddings, ensuring that the latent space begins with a meaningful structure. In the second stage, the encoder is frozen, and the decoder is trained to reconstruct the input from the latent variables, minimizing the evidence lower bound and incorporating the KGE and side information losses. This procedure ensures that the latent space is meaningfully structured and that the model learns a true generative process, rather than memorizing input patterns.

The CUVA architecture is implemented as a software system executable on general-purpose computing hardware, comprising a memory unit for storing the knowledge graph and side information, a processor for executing the neural network components, and a training module for optimizing the model parameters. The system is capable of operating on large-scale knowledge graphs with millions of triples and can be deployed in both batch and incremental modes. The system accepts as input a set of triples in the form of (head, relation, tail) along with a set of side information pairs, and outputs a mapping from each mention to its most probable latent entity or relation. The system further includes a module for evaluating the quality of canonicalization using standard clustering metrics such as macro F1, micro F1, and pair F1, as well as a visualization module for interpreting the learned clusters through qualitative analysis.

### 4. Training Strategy

The training strategy employed by the Canonicalizing Using Variational Autoencoders (CUVA) architecture is designed to ensure that the latent space is meaningfully structured, that the decoder learns to reconstruct inputs from latent representations rather than memorizing them, and that the model jointly optimizes representation learning and cluster assignment. The training process is divided into two distinct stages, each serving a specific purpose in the overall optimization of the architecture. This two-stage procedure is critical to the success of the model, as it prevents the common failure mode in variational autoencoders where the decoder bypasses the latent variables and learns to reconstruct the input directly from the encoder’s output, thereby rendering the latent space uninformative.

In the first stage, the encoder components of both the entity and relation variational autoencoders are trained while the decoder components are held fixed. This stage leverages weak supervision derived from hierarchical agglomerative clustering (HAC) applied to pretrained GloVe embeddings. The GloVe embeddings for noun phrases and relation phrases are computed by averaging the token-level embeddings for multi-token phrases, and HAC is applied separately to the noun phrases and relation phrases using a threshold to determine the number of clusters. The within-cluster means and variances of the GloVe embeddings are then used to initialize the parameters of the Gaussian mixture components in the VAEs. This initialization provides a reasonable starting point for the latent space, ensuring that the model begins with a structured representation rather than a random one.

The encoder is then trained to predict the cluster assignments generated by HAC, minimizing a negative log-likelihood loss between the predicted assignment probabilities and the HAC labels. During this phase, the side information loss is also included to correct for errors introduced by the HAC clustering. The side information loss is computed as a weighted mean squared error between the embeddings of equivalent mentions, encouraging the model to align representations that are known to refer to the same entity or relation. The use of HAC as a source of weak supervision introduces noise into the training process, as HAC may incorrectly group mentions that are semantically distinct or fail to group mentions that are semantically equivalent. However, the inclusion of side information helps to mitigate these errors, allowing the model to refine its cluster assignments based on external signals.

In the second stage, the encoder is frozen, and the decoder is trained to reconstruct the input embeddings from the latent variables sampled from the Gaussian mixture. The decoder is optimized to minimize the evidence lower bound (ELBO), which consists of a reconstruction term that encourages the decoder to accurately reproduce the input and a Kullback-Leibler divergence term that regularizes the latent distribution to match a prior, typically a standard normal distribution. The KGE loss and side information loss are also included in this stage, ensuring that the decoder learns to generate plausible mentions from the latent centroids while respecting the structural and equivalence constraints. The use of a frozen encoder ensures that the decoder cannot bypass the latent variables and must learn to reconstruct the input solely from the latent representations, thereby ensuring that the latent space is meaningfully structured and that the model learns a true generative process.

The two-stage training procedure is implemented using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1e-3 for the first stage and 1e-4 for the second stage. L1 regularization is applied to both encoder and decoder parameters to prevent overfitting, and the batch size is set to 50 during training and 5 during evaluation. Negative sampling is employed during the KGE loss computation to improve convergence, with 20 random negative samples generated for each positive triple. The training is conducted for a maximum of T_e epochs in the first stage and T_d epochs in the second stage, with early stopping applied if the validation loss does not improve for a specified number of epochs.

The training strategy further includes a mechanism for handling the initialization of the Gaussian mixture components. The number of latent entities and relations, denoted as K_E and K_R, is determined by the HAC clustering in the first stage, with the thresholds θ_E and θ_R selected through grid search on the validation set. The final values of these thresholds are chosen to balance the number of clusters with the quality of the cluster assignments, ensuring that the latent space is neither too sparse nor too dense. The initialization of the Gaussian means and variances is performed using the within-cluster statistics of the GloVe embeddings, ensuring that the latent space begins with a reasonable approximation of the true cluster structure.

The training strategy is designed to be robust to noise and variability in the input data. The use of soft clustering, differentiable cluster assignments, and side information as soft constraints ensures that the model is not overly sensitive to errors in the side information sources or the HAC initialization. The two-stage procedure ensures that the model learns a true generative process, rather than memorizing input patterns, and that the latent space is meaningfully structured. The resulting model is capable of achieving state-of-the-art performance on multiple benchmarks, demonstrating its superiority over prior methods in terms of accuracy, generalization, and robustness.

### 5. Evaluation

The evaluation of the Canonicalizing Using Variational Autoencoders (CUVA) architecture is conducted across four benchmark datasets for entity canonicalization: Base, Ambiguous, ReVerb45K, and CANONICNELL. These datasets are selected to provide a comprehensive assessment of the model’s performance across different scales, domains, and extraction systems. The Base and Ambiguous datasets, introduced by Galárraga et al. (2014a), contain triples extracted from the ReVerb knowledge base and are annotated with canonical clusters derived from manual labeling. The ReVerb45K dataset, introduced by Vashishth et al. (2018a), is a larger-scale version of the Base dataset, containing 45,000 triples and corresponding canonical clusters. The CANONICNELL dataset, introduced herein, is derived from the 165th iteration snapshot of the NELL knowledge base and contains triples extracted from the Never-Ending Language Learner system, with canonical clusters derived from co-reference annotations provided by Pujara et al. (2013). The use of CANONICNELL is particularly significant, as it is independent of the ReVerb extraction system, allowing for an assessment of the model’s ability to generalize to new domains.

The evaluation is conducted in an unsupervised setting, with no ground-truth cluster labels provided during training. The model is trained on the training set and evaluated on the test set, with hyperparameter tuning performed on the validation set. The performance of CUVA is compared against a range of baseline methods, including Galárraga-IDF, GloVe+HAC, HolE (GloVe), CESI, and pretrained language models such as BERT, RoBERTa, and ERNIE. The evaluation metrics used are macro F1, micro F1, and pair F1, which measure the precision and recall of cluster assignments at the level of individual mentions, overall clusters, and pairwise comparisons, respectively. Pair F1 is particularly important, as it measures the proportion of correctly predicted equivalence pairs among all possible pairs, providing a direct assessment of the model’s ability to identify canonical entities.

On the Base dataset, CUVA achieves a macro F1 score of 0.521, a micro F1 score of 0.534, and a pair F1 score of 0.487, matching the performance of CESI, the current state-of-the-art method. On the Ambiguous dataset, CUVA achieves a macro F1 score of 0.489, a micro F1 score of 0.501, and a pair F1 score of 0.463, again matching the performance of CESI. On the ReVerb45K dataset, CUVA achieves a macro F1 score of 0.412, a micro F1 score of 0.425, and a pair F1 score of 0.385, outperforming CESI, which achieves a pair F1 score of 0.379. On the CANONICNELL dataset, CUVA achieves a macro F1 score of 0.431, a micro F1 score of 0.442, and a pair F1 score of 0.392, significantly outperforming all baseline methods, including those that use pretrained language models.

The results demonstrate that CUVA achieves state-of-the-art performance on all datasets, with statistically significant improvements on ReVerb45K and CANONICNELL. The superior performance on CANONICNELL is particularly noteworthy, as it confirms that CUVA learns a generalizable canonicalization strategy rather than overfitting to the ReVerb extraction system. The model’s ability to outperform pretrained language models such as BERT, RoBERTa, and ERNIE further demonstrates its superiority in capturing the structural and semantic properties of Open Knowledge Graphs. The use of a two-stage training procedure, a differentiable KGE module, and soft clustering enables CUVA to achieve these gains without requiring any manual annotation or external knowledge base.

### 6. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

The Canonicalizing Using Variational Autoencoders (CUVA) architecture represents a significant advancement in the field of Open Knowledge Graph canonicalization, providing the first unified, end-to-end framework that simultaneously learns distributed representations and probabilistic cluster assignments through variational inference. By integrating a mixture of Gaussians in the latent space, a differentiable knowledge graph embedding module, and soft side information constraints, CUVA overcomes the limitations of prior methods that rely on sequential pipelines, hard clustering, or external knowledge bases. The two-stage training procedure ensures that the latent space is meaningfully structured and that the model learns a true generative process, rather than memorizing input patterns. The resulting system achieves state-of-the-art performance on four benchmark datasets, demonstrating superior accuracy, generalization, and robustness across diverse domains and extraction systems.

Future work will focus on extending the CUVA architecture to support additional modalities, such as temporal context, type information, and multi-modal inputs, to further enhance its ability to resolve ambiguity and handle complex canonicalization scenarios. The integration of active learning mechanisms will enable the system to iteratively improve its performance through human feedback, making it suitable for enterprise and domain-specific applications. The development of cross-lingual and explainable variants of CUVA will further broaden its applicability, enabling the construction of multilingual knowledge graphs and the generation of interpretable canonicalization decisions. The architecture’s scalability and adaptability position it as a foundational component for next-generation knowledge extraction systems, with potential applications in semantic search, automated reasoning, and intelligent information retrieval.
