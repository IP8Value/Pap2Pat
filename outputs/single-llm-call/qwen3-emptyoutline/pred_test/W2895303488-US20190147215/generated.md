# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of automated image analysis in biological microscopy, specifically to a system and method for the segmentation of whole cells in two-dimensional fluorescent microscopy images using a single-channel input. The invention enables the precise delineation of individual cell boundaries, including both cytoplasmic and nuclear regions, without reliance on nuclear staining or multi-channel imaging. This capability is particularly valuable in high-throughput cellular assays where the use of additional fluorescent markers is constrained by instrumental limitations, biological toxicity concerns, or experimental design requirements. The method integrates deep learning-based pixel-wise classification with classical image processing techniques to achieve robust, scalable, and reproducible segmentation of cells across diverse experimental conditions, including variations in cell type, staining protocol, magnification, and pharmacological treatment. The invention finds application in drug discovery, toxicology screening, cellular dynamics studies, and automated phenotypic profiling, where accurate quantification of cell number, morphology, and spatial distribution is critical for data integrity and biological interpretation.

## BACKGROUND

The fundamental unit of life, the cell, serves as the primary subject of inquiry in numerous biological and biomedical disciplines. Accurate visualization and quantitative analysis of cellular structures are essential for understanding physiological processes, disease mechanisms, and therapeutic responses. Fluorescent microscopy has become a cornerstone technique for observing subcellular components with high spatial resolution, enabling researchers to probe the localization and dynamics of proteins, organelles, and other molecular entities. However, the manual interpretation of such images is inherently subjective, labor-intensive, and prone to inter-observer variability, rendering it unsuitable for large-scale studies requiring high-throughput analysis.

Historically, automated cell segmentation has relied heavily on multi-channel imaging strategies, particularly those employing a nuclear counterstain such as DAPI or Hoechst in conjunction with a cytoplasmic or membrane marker. These dual-channel approaches leverage the distinct spatial relationship between the nucleus and the surrounding cytoplasm: the nucleus is segmented first using its high-contrast, blob-like morphology, and then serves as a seed region to guide the expansion of the cell boundary into the adjacent cytoplasmic region. While effective under controlled conditions, this paradigm is fundamentally limited by its dependence on nuclear labeling, which introduces several practical constraints. Nuclear stains may be cytotoxic in live-cell imaging applications, interfere with endogenous cellular processes, or be incompatible with certain fluorescent protein tags due to spectral overlap. Moreover, the use of multiple channels reduces the number of available channels for multiplexed biomarker detection, thereby limiting the depth of biological insight obtainable from a single experiment.

Alternative approaches have been developed for single-channel segmentation, particularly in brightfield or phase-contrast microscopy, where nuclear contrast is absent and cell boundaries are inferred from intensity gradients and texture patterns. These methods often employ thresholding, edge detection, or morphology-based techniques such as watershed segmentation, active contours, or graph cuts. However, these traditional image processing methods are highly sensitive to variations in illumination, noise, cell density, and staining intensity. They typically assume uniform cell shape and size, making them ill-suited for complex biological systems where cells exhibit extreme morphological heterogeneity—such as elongated fibroblasts, clustered epithelial cells, or drug-induced cytoskeletal rearrangements. Furthermore, these algorithms frequently fail to distinguish between overlapping or touching cells, leading to under-segmentation artifacts that compromise downstream quantification.

Recent advances in deep learning, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have demonstrated remarkable success in pixel-level classification tasks across diverse imaging domains. Architectures such as U-Net have become standard for biomedical image segmentation due to their ability to learn hierarchical feature representations and preserve spatial context through skip connections. In the context of cell segmentation, several deep learning models have been proposed that utilize two-channel inputs—combining phase contrast with nuclear fluorescence, or cytoplasmic and nuclear stains—to predict cell boundaries. However, these methods still require the presence of a nuclear marker, thereby inheriting the same limitations as conventional multi-channel approaches. A few studies have attempted single-channel segmentation using CNNs on brightfield images, but these have been largely confined to simple cell types with low density and minimal morphological variation. No prior method has demonstrated robust, generalizable performance across a broad spectrum of fluorescently labeled cytoplasmic markers, varying cell lines, multiple magnifications, and complex drug-induced phenotypes—all while operating on a single input channel.

A critical gap remains in the field: the absence of a fully automated, single-channel segmentation system capable of accurately resolving individual cell boundaries in images where the nucleus is hypointense relative to the hyperintense cytoplasm, and where cell shapes vary dramatically due to biological or experimental factors. Such a system would eliminate the need for nuclear staining, maximize the utility of available fluorescence channels, and enable consistent analysis across diverse experimental paradigms. Existing methods either lack the generalizability to handle such variability or require manual intervention to correct segmentation errors, which negates the benefits of automation. Furthermore, the evaluation metrics commonly used in the literature—such as Dice coefficient or Jaccard index—are inadequate for datasets containing hundreds of overlapping cells, as they treat the entire image as a single binary mask and fail to account for the one-to-many or many-to-one relationships between segmented objects and ground truth entities. There is therefore a compelling need for a novel, end-to-end methodology that combines the representational power of deep learning with the precision of classical image analysis to achieve accurate, scalable, and biologically meaningful single-channel whole-cell segmentation.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel system and method for the automated segmentation of whole cells in two-dimensional fluorescent microscopy images using only a single input channel. The system operates without requiring any nuclear or membrane-specific staining, instead leveraging the intrinsic contrast between hyperintense cytoplasmic regions and hypointense nuclei and background to achieve accurate delineation of individual cell boundaries. The method comprises three integrated stages: (1) the training of a deep convolutional neural network to generate pixel-wise probability maps for nuclei, cytoplasm, and background; (2) the detection and segmentation of individual nuclear seeds using a multi-scale blob detection algorithm combined with shape-constrained watershed segmentation; and (3) the application of a seeded watershed transform to segment entire cells using the segmented nuclei as seeds and the cytoplasmic probability map as a guidance field. The invention further includes a novel segmentation similarity metric designed to quantitatively evaluate the accuracy of cell segmentation results against ground truth in datasets containing hundreds of overlapping cells, accounting for both partial overlaps and one-to-many mappings.

The deep learning model is trained on image patches extracted from single-channel fluorescent microscopy images and is configured to predict three distinct classes: nuclei, cytoplasm, and background. The architecture is a modified U-Net with an asymmetric design, incorporating five contracting layers with increasing filter depths (32, 64, 128, 128, 256) and a corresponding expanding path with deconvolution and concatenation operations. The model is optimized using a weighted root mean square deviation loss function that enforces both pixel-wise accuracy and a global constraint that the sum of the predicted probabilities for nuclei, cytoplasm, and background at each pixel must equal one. The training process employs data augmentation via 90-degree rotations and utilizes overlapping patches to ensure comprehensive coverage of the input image space.

Following inference on a new image, the resulting probability maps are processed to extract reliable nuclear seeds. A multi-level Laplacian of Gaussian blob detector is applied to identify potential nuclear regions across multiple scales, followed by automated multi-level Otsu thresholding to generate a binary nuclear mask. To resolve touching nuclei that form large connected components, an inverse distance transform is computed and subjected to an extended h-minima transform, which suppresses shallow regional minima and isolates true nuclear centers. A seeded watershed transform is then applied to this transformed space, using the detected minima as seeds to partition the nuclear mask into individual nuclei.

The final cell segmentation stage employs the segmented nuclei as seeds within a seeded watershed algorithm applied to a transformed version of the original input image. The transformation involves Gaussian smoothing, intensity scaling, and logarithmic conversion to enhance contrast and reduce noise. A three-level Otsu thresholding procedure is used to define the background region based on the expected total cell area, which is estimated from the number of detected nuclei and prior knowledge of average cell size. The resulting background mask, in conjunction with the segmented nuclei, serves as the marker image for the seeded watershed transform, which partitions the cytoplasmic region into individual cell boundaries. Each nucleus is associated with exactly one cell, ensuring a one-to-one correspondence between nuclear seeds and segmented cells.

The invention further introduces a novel segmentation similarity metric that evaluates the quality of segmentation by computing both local overlap and global matching between reference and target segmentations. For each object in the reference segmentation, the metric identifies the target object with maximum overlap and computes the Dice coefficient. Additionally, it identifies the set of one-to-one correspondences between reference and target objects and computes the ratio of correctly matched pairs. The final similarity score is a weighted combination of these two components, allowing for nuanced evaluation in complex datasets where partial overlaps and segmentation errors are common.

The system is capable of operating on images acquired at multiple magnifications and with diverse cytoplasmic markers, including but not limited to Texas Red, Cy5, dsRed, MitoTracker, and proprietary dyes. It demonstrates robust performance across different cell lines—including HeLa, U2OS, HEPG2, and fibroblasts—and under varying pharmacological conditions that induce dramatic morphological changes. The method requires no manual parameter tuning for each new dataset and achieves segmentation accuracy comparable to or exceeding that of two-channel methods, while eliminating the need for nuclear staining. The entire pipeline, from image input to final segmentation, is fully automated and computationally efficient, with inference times of four to six seconds per image on standard hardware.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### System Overview

The system of the present invention is a fully automated, end-to-end pipeline for the segmentation of whole cells in two-dimensional fluorescent microscopy images using only a single input channel. The architecture is modular, comprising three distinct but interdependent components: a deep learning inference engine, a nuclear seed detection module, and a seeded watershed segmentation engine. These components operate sequentially on an input image, transforming it from raw pixel intensities into a labeled segmentation mask where each connected region corresponds to a single cell. The system is designed to be agnostic to the specific fluorescent marker used, provided that the marker localizes to the cytoplasm and produces a hyperintense signal relative to the nucleus and background. This design philosophy enables broad applicability across diverse biological contexts, including live-cell imaging, high-throughput screening, and phenotypic drug discovery.

The input to the system is a single-channel grayscale image captured using a fluorescence microscope, with dimensions typically ranging from 1024×1024 to 4096×4096 pixels. Prior to processing, the image undergoes a preprocessing step to correct for non-uniform illumination, which is common in widefield microscopy due to optical aberrations and uneven light source distribution. This is accomplished via top-hat filtering with a structuring element of 200×200 pixels, which effectively removes low-frequency background gradients while preserving fine structural details. The image is then resampled to a standardized pixel size of approximately 0.65 micrometers per pixel, corresponding to a nominal 10× magnification, to ensure consistency in feature scale across images acquired at different objective settings. This normalization step is critical for the stability of the deep learning model, which is trained on patches of fixed spatial dimensions.

The core of the system is a deep convolutional neural network that has been pre-trained on a diverse dataset of manually annotated or semi-automatically segmented images. The model is implemented using the MXNet framework and is based on a modified U-Net architecture, with key structural adaptations to enhance performance on the specific task of single-channel cytoplasmic segmentation. The network accepts 160×160 pixel image patches as input and outputs three probability maps, each of the same spatial dimensions, corresponding to the likelihood that each pixel belongs to the nucleus, cytoplasm, or background class. The architecture is asymmetric, with a contracting path consisting of five convolutional blocks, each followed by max-pooling, and an expanding path that reconstructs the spatial resolution through transposed convolutions and concatenations with corresponding feature maps from the contracting path. The number of filters increases progressively in the contracting path (32, 64, 128, 128, 256) and decreases in the expanding path, with intermediate layers incorporating dropout regularization to mitigate overfitting during training. The output layer employs a softmax activation to ensure that the three probability values at each pixel sum to one, enforcing a hard constraint on class mutual exclusivity.

During inference, the input image is divided into overlapping 176×176 pixel patches, with a 16-pixel overlap on all sides, to ensure complete coverage and avoid boundary artifacts. Each patch is processed independently through the trained network, producing a 160×160 pixel prediction for each of the three classes. The predictions are then stitched together using a weighted averaging scheme over the overlapping regions, resulting in a full-resolution probability map for nuclei, cytoplasm, and background. These probability maps are not binary masks but continuous representations of confidence, which are subsequently used as inputs to the downstream segmentation modules.

The nuclear seed detection module receives the nuclei probability map and transforms it into a set of discrete, individual nuclear centroids. This is necessary because the probability map, while accurate, often contains large, fused regions corresponding to touching nuclei. A multi-scale Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) blob detector is applied to identify regions of blob-like intensity that match the expected morphology of a nucleus. The LoG filter is convolved with the probability map at multiple sigma values, corresponding to nuclear diameters ranging from 3 to 15 pixels, to detect nuclei of varying sizes. The resulting response map is thresholded using an automated multi-level Otsu algorithm, which determines optimal thresholds based on the intensity distribution of the probability map. The selected threshold is chosen to isolate the hypointense nuclear regions while suppressing false positives from low-confidence cytoplasmic regions. The resulting binary mask is then refined using an inverse distance transform, which computes the Euclidean distance of each pixel to the nearest background pixel. This transformation converts the binary mask into a topographic surface where nuclear centers correspond to deep valleys. An extended h-minima transform is applied to this surface to suppress shallow local minima that arise from noise or partial overlaps, retaining only the most prominent minima as candidate nuclear seeds. A final seeded watershed transform is applied to the inverse distance transform, using the retained minima as markers, to partition the binary nuclear mask into distinct, non-overlapping nuclear regions. The output of this stage is a labeled image where each nucleus is assigned a unique integer identifier.

The seeded watershed segmentation module receives the labeled nuclear seed image and the cytoplasm probability map as inputs, along with the original preprocessed image. The original image is transformed to enhance contrast and reduce noise: a Gaussian filter with a standard deviation of 1.5 pixels is applied, followed by intensity scaling to normalize the dynamic range, and finally a logarithmic transformation to compress high-intensity values and enhance low-intensity boundaries. A three-level Otsu thresholding procedure is applied to this transformed image to determine the background region. The threshold selection is constrained by an estimate of the total expected cell area, which is derived from the number of detected nuclei and a prior assumption of average cell size based on the cell line and magnification. This ensures that the background mask is not overly inclusive or exclusive, preventing the misclassification of cytoplasmic regions as background or vice versa. The final marker image for the watershed transform consists of the labeled nuclear seeds and the identified background region. The seeded watershed algorithm is then applied to the transformed intensity image, using these markers to guide the segmentation. The algorithm treats the transformed intensity values as a topographic surface, where high intensity corresponds to high elevation, and the seeds act as catchment basins. The algorithm floods the surface from the seed points, with boundaries forming at ridges between adjacent basins. The result is a segmentation of the entire image into distinct cell regions, each associated with exactly one nucleus. The output of the system is a labeled segmentation mask, where each connected component corresponds to a single cell, and each cell is uniquely labeled with an identifier that matches its corresponding nuclear seed.

The system is implemented as a software suite written in Python and C++, with the deep learning inference engine leveraging the MXNet library, and the image processing components built upon the Insight Toolkit (ITK) and OpenCV. The entire pipeline is designed for scalability and can be deployed on cloud computing platforms, desktop workstations, or high-performance computing clusters. Training is performed offline on labeled datasets, and once the model is trained, inference on new images requires no further human intervention. The system is capable of processing images acquired at different magnifications, with different fluorescent markers, and under diverse biological conditions, making it a general-purpose tool for automated cell analysis.

### Development of a Training Model

The development of the deep learning model underlying the present invention is a rigorous, iterative process grounded in the principles of supervised learning, architectural optimization, and statistical validation. The model is trained to predict three distinct pixel-wise classes—nucleus, cytoplasm, and background—from single-channel fluorescent microscopy images, with the goal of achieving high accuracy, robustness to variability, and generalizability across unseen datasets. The training data consists of a curated collection of 108 high-resolution images (2048×2048 pixels) acquired from five distinct experimental conditions, encompassing multiple cell lines, fluorescent markers, and drug treatments. Each image is accompanied by a corresponding ground truth segmentation mask, generated using a two-channel automated segmentation method that employs both a cytoplasmic marker and a nuclear counterstain. These ground truth masks serve as the supervisory signal for training, providing pixel-level labels that define the spatial extent of nuclei, cytoplasm, and background.

To prepare the data for training, each full-resolution image is subdivided into overlapping 176×176 pixel patches, with a 16-pixel overlap on all sides. This overlapping strategy ensures that every pixel in the original image is represented in at least one patch, while the central 160×160 region of each patch is used as the input to the neural network, thereby avoiding edge artifacts introduced by the padding. The ground truth labels for each patch are extracted correspondingly, resulting in a training dataset of thousands of labeled image patches. To augment the dataset and improve model generalization, each patch is subjected to rotational augmentation, with 90-degree, 180-degree, and 270-degree rotations applied, effectively multiplying the training set size by four. No other augmentations, such as scaling, flipping, or brightness perturbations, are applied, as these were found to introduce artifacts that degraded model performance in preliminary experiments.

The neural network architecture is a modified U-Net, designed to balance computational efficiency with representational capacity. The contracting path consists of five sequential blocks, each comprising a convolutional layer with a 3×3 kernel, followed by a max-pooling operation with a 2×2 stride. The number of filters in each block increases progressively: 32, 64, 128, 128, and 256. This design allows the network to extract increasingly abstract and context-rich features as the spatial resolution decreases. The final layer of the contracting path produces a 5×5 feature map with 256 channels, capturing high-level semantic information about the image content. To prevent overfitting, dropout layers with a rate of 50% are inserted after the third, sixth, and ninth convolutional layers. The expanding path mirrors the contracting path in reverse, but with transposed convolutions (also known as deconvolutions) replacing the pooling operations to upsample the feature maps. The number of filters in the expanding path is carefully chosen to match the number of channels from the corresponding contracting layer during concatenation, ensuring that spatial context is preserved. Three concatenation operations are performed, linking the feature maps from the fourth, third, and second contracting layers to their corresponding expanding layers. This skip-connection strategy enables the network to recover fine-grained spatial details that are lost during downsampling, a critical feature for accurate pixel-level segmentation.

The architecture is intentionally asymmetric, with the expanding path containing fewer convolutional layers and a different filter configuration than the contracting path. This asymmetry is not arbitrary but is the result of extensive empirical testing, which revealed that a symmetric U-Net with identical filter counts in both paths led to over-smoothing of boundaries and reduced segmentation accuracy. The asymmetric design allows the network to prioritize the accurate reconstruction of cytoplasmic boundaries over fine nuclear details, which is more aligned with the ultimate goal of whole-cell segmentation. The final layer of the network is a 1×1 convolution that maps the 128-channel feature map to three output channels, each representing the probability of a pixel belonging to one of the three classes. A softmax activation function is applied to these outputs to ensure that the sum of the probabilities across the three classes equals one at every pixel, enforcing a hard constraint that each pixel must be assigned to exactly one class.

The training objective is defined by a weighted root mean square deviation (RMSD) loss function, which simultaneously optimizes for pixel-wise classification accuracy and global class consistency. The loss function is formulated as the sum of three RMSD terms—one for each class—weighted by coefficients wn, wc, and wb, respectively, plus a regularization term that penalizes deviations from the constraint that the sum of the three predicted probabilities equals one. The weights for the nucleus, cytoplasm, and background classes are all set to one, as preliminary experiments demonstrated that equal weighting yielded the most balanced performance across all classes. The regularization term is also weighted equally, ensuring that the model does not produce probability maps that violate the fundamental assumption of mutual exclusivity. This loss function is minimized using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001, a batch size of 32, and a total of 30 training epochs. The choice of 30 epochs was determined through empirical validation, where training beyond this point resulted in diminishing returns in validation performance and increased risk of overfitting. Training is performed on a cloud-based infrastructure equipped with an NVIDIA Tesla K80 GPU, with each epoch requiring approximately 11 to 13 minutes to complete. The total training time for one fold of the cross-validation procedure is approximately six hours.

To evaluate the training process, a 10-fold cross-validation scheme is employed, with the 108 images partitioned into training (80%), validation (10%), and test (10%) sets in each fold. The model is trained independently for each fold, and performance is assessed using both pixel-level metrics (accuracy, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve) and segmentation-level metrics (the novel similarity measure described later). The model demonstrates consistent convergence across folds, with the validation loss plateauing after 25–30 epochs. The final model is selected based on the lowest validation loss across all folds, and its weights are retained for inference. The training procedure is reproducible, with the same hyperparameters and data preprocessing steps applied uniformly across all experiments. The resulting model is not specialized to any particular cell line, marker, or experimental condition but instead learns a generalized representation of cytoplasmic and nuclear morphology that is robust to the wide variability observed in real-world biological datasets.

### Single Channel Whole Cell Segmentation Workflow

The single-channel whole cell segmentation workflow represents the operational core of the invention, transforming an input fluorescence image into a precisely labeled segmentation mask of individual cells without the need for nuclear staining. This workflow is executed in a deterministic, sequential manner, with each stage building upon the output of the previous one to achieve increasingly refined and biologically meaningful results. The process begins with the acquisition of a single-channel fluorescent image, where the fluorophore is localized to the cytoplasm and produces a hyperintense signal relative to the nucleus and background. The image is first preprocessed to correct for illumination non-uniformity using top-hat filtering, followed by resampling to a standardized pixel size of 0.65 micrometers per pixel, ensuring scale-invariance across images acquired at different magnifications.

The next stage involves the application of the pre-trained deep learning model to generate pixel-wise probability maps for the nucleus, cytoplasm, and background. The input image is divided into overlapping 176×176 pixel patches, each of which is processed independently by the neural network. The network outputs three probability maps, each of size 160×160 pixels, representing the likelihood that each pixel belongs to one of the three classes. These probability maps are then stitched together using a weighted averaging scheme over the overlapping regions, resulting in full-resolution probability maps that retain the spatial integrity of the original image. The nucleus probability map exhibits high values in regions corresponding to hypointense nuclear areas, while the cytoplasm probability map highlights the hyperintense cytoplasmic regions, and the background map identifies regions of low signal intensity. These maps are not binary but represent continuous confidence scores, which are essential for the subsequent segmentation stages.

The third stage of the workflow is the detection and segmentation of individual nuclear seeds. The nucleus probability map is processed using a multi-scale Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) blob detector, which convolves the map with LoG kernels of varying standard deviations (ranging from 1.5 to 7.5 pixels) to detect blob-like structures across multiple spatial scales. This step is critical because nuclei vary in size depending on the cell type, cell cycle phase, and imaging magnification. The resulting response map is then subjected to automated multi-level Otsu thresholding, which identifies three intensity thresholds that optimally separate the image into background, hypointense nuclei, and hyperintense nuclei. The third threshold is selected as the final threshold to define the nuclear mask, ensuring that only the most probable nuclear regions are retained. The resulting binary mask, however, often contains large connected components due to touching nuclei. To resolve these, an inverse distance transform is computed, converting the binary mask into a topographic surface where the distance to the nearest background pixel is encoded as intensity. An extended h-minima transform is then applied to this surface with a default h-value of 3 micrometers, which suppresses shallow local minima that arise from noise or partial overlaps, leaving only the most prominent minima as candidate nuclear centers. A seeded watershed transform is then applied to the inverse distance transform, using the retained minima as markers, to partition the binary nuclear mask into distinct, non-overlapping nuclear regions. The output is a labeled image where each nucleus is assigned a unique integer identifier.

The final stage of the workflow is the segmentation of entire cells using the segmented nuclei as seeds. The original preprocessed image is transformed to enhance the contrast between cytoplasm and background. This transformation involves three sequential operations: first, a Gaussian filter with a standard deviation of 1.5 pixels is applied to reduce high-frequency noise; second, the intensity values are scaled linearly to span the full dynamic range of the data type; and third, a logarithmic transformation is applied to compress the high-intensity cytoplasmic regions and enhance the visibility of low-intensity boundaries between cells. A three-level Otsu thresholding procedure is then applied to this transformed image to determine the background region. The threshold selection is constrained by an estimate of the total expected cell area, which is calculated by multiplying the number of detected nuclei by the average expected cell area for the given cell line and magnification. This constraint ensures that the background mask is neither too large (which would misclassify cytoplasm as background) nor too small (which would fail to identify true background regions). The final marker image for the seeded watershed transform is constructed by combining the labeled nuclear seeds and the identified background region. The seeded watershed algorithm is then applied to the transformed intensity image, using these markers to guide the segmentation. The algorithm treats the transformed intensity values as a topographic surface, with high intensities representing high elevations. The algorithm floods the surface from the seed points, with boundaries forming at ridges between adjacent catchment basins. The result is a segmentation of the entire image into distinct cell regions, each associated with exactly one nucleus. The output is a labeled segmentation mask, where each connected component corresponds to a single cell, and each cell is uniquely labeled with an identifier that matches its corresponding nuclear seed. The final segmentation is free of overlapping cells, accurately delineates cell boundaries even in densely packed regions, and is robust to variations in staining intensity, cell morphology, and imaging conditions.

### Assessment of Segmentation Results

The assessment of segmentation results in the context of this invention is a sophisticated, multi-faceted process designed to evaluate both the pixel-level accuracy of the deep learning model and the biological fidelity of the final cell segmentation. Traditional metrics such as Dice coefficient or Jaccard index, which treat the entire image as a single binary mask, are inadequate for datasets containing hundreds of overlapping cells, as they fail to capture the one-to-many or many-to-one relationships between segmented objects and ground truth entities. To address this limitation, the invention introduces a novel segmentation similarity metric that quantifies the agreement between the automated segmentation and a reference segmentation at both the local and global levels.

The segmentation similarity metric is defined as a weighted combination of two components: a local overlap score and a global matching score. Let the reference segmentation be denoted as IR with a set of labeled regions R = {r1, r2, ..., rN}, and the target segmentation as IT with a set of labeled regions T = {t1, t2, ..., tM}. For each reference region ri, the metric identifies the target region tj that exhibits the maximum overlap with ri, and computes the Dice coefficient between them. The local overlap score is the average of these maximum Dice coefficients across all reference regions. This component captures the degree to which each true cell is accurately represented in the segmentation, even if partially or incompletely. The global matching score is derived from the set of one-to-one correspondences between reference and target regions, defined as the set PT^R of pairs (ri, tj) such that each region in R is matched to exactly one region in T and vice versa. The global matching score is the ratio of the cardinality of PT^R to the sum of the number of regions in R and T. This component captures the proportion of correctly matched cell pairs, penalizing both over-segmentation and under-segmentation. The final segmentation similarity score is computed as a weighted sum of these two components, with a weighting factor k set empirically to 0.6, giving greater emphasis to local overlap, which is more directly relevant to biological interpretation.

The metric is applied to evaluate the performance of the system across multiple experimental conditions. In Experiment 1, the segmentation results are compared against a set of 10 semi-automated ground truth segmentations generated by an expert biologist using CellProfiler, with manual corrections applied to split merged cells and remove false positives. The average segmentation similarity score across 1,666 segmented cells is 0.87, indicating a high degree of agreement between the automated and manual segmentations. When compared against the automated two-channel segmentation used for training, the average score is 0.86, demonstrating that the single-channel method achieves performance comparable to a method that requires an additional nuclear stain. In Experiment 2, a 10-fold cross-validation is performed on 108 images, and the segmentation similarity scores are computed for each fold. The average score across all datasets is 0.84, with standard deviations ranging from 0.09 to 0.17, indicating robust performance across diverse cell types and staining conditions. Notably, one dataset with elongated actin fibers exhibited a lower score (0.62), highlighting a known limitation of the method in cases where cytoplasmic and nuclear intensities are nearly indistinguishable. In Experiment 3, the model trained on datasets 1–5 is tested on a completely independent dataset of 15 images acquired with a different marker and cell line. The average segmentation similarity score is 0.84, demonstrating strong generalizability to unseen experimental conditions.

The pixel-level classification performance of the deep learning model is evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC) metrics. For each fold of the cross-validation, the nucleus and cytoplasm probability maps are thresholded at varying values between 0 and 1 to compute sensitivity and specificity. The AUC for nucleus classification exceeds 0.95, and for cytoplasm classification exceeds 0.93, indicating excellent discriminative power. The pixel-level accuracy at a threshold of 0.5 is 0.915 for nuclei and 0.878 for cytoplasm. These results confirm that the model is capable of accurately distinguishing between the three classes at the pixel level, even in regions of low contrast or high noise.

The system’s performance is further validated by visual inspection of segmentation results against ground truth. In all cases, the automated segmentation accurately delineates cell boundaries, resolves touching cells, and preserves the morphological integrity of individual cells, even under conditions of high density or drug-induced shape changes. The method consistently outperforms traditional thresholding and watershed methods, which frequently fail to separate overlapping cells or produce fragmented segmentations. The novel similarity metric provides a statistically rigorous and biologically meaningful basis for evaluating segmentation quality, enabling objective comparison across different algorithms and datasets. The results demonstrate that the invention achieves segmentation accuracy that is not only comparable to, but in many cases superior to, methods that rely on multi-channel imaging, thereby validating its utility as a standalone, single-channel solution.

### Examples

The practical utility and robustness of the invention are demonstrated through a series of representative examples drawn from diverse experimental conditions, each highlighting a different aspect of the system’s capabilities. The first example involves an image of HeLa cells stained with a dsRed-labeled tandem FYVE domain construct, which localizes to endosomal membranes and produces a punctate cytoplasmic signal. Under control conditions, the cells exhibit a characteristic distribution of bright puncta throughout the cytoplasm. Following treatment with a compound that depletes PI(3)P, the signal becomes diffuse and uniform, resulting in a homogeneous hyperintense cytoplasm with no discernible subcellular features. Despite this dramatic change in staining pattern, the system successfully segments the individual cell boundaries, accurately separating touching cells and preserving the overall morphology. The segmentation results show a high degree of correspondence with the semi-automated ground truth, with a segmentation similarity score of 0.89, demonstrating the system’s insensitivity to variations in staining texture and intensity distribution.

The second example involves U2OS cells stained with MitoTracker Red, a dye that accumulates in mitochondria in a membrane potential-dependent manner. In untreated cells, the mitochondria form a filamentous network that extends throughout the cytoplasm, creating a complex, interconnected pattern of high-intensity regions. In drug-treated cells, the mitochondrial network collapses into fragmented, perinuclear aggregates. The system is able to segment the cells accurately in both conditions, correctly identifying the cell boundaries even in regions where the cytoplasmic signal is highly heterogeneous. The segmentation avoids over-segmentation of mitochondrial fragments into false cells and does not merge adjacent cells despite the presence of dense, high-intensity regions. The segmentation similarity score for this dataset is 0.91, indicating exceptional performance under challenging morphological conditions.

The third example illustrates the system’s ability to operate across different magnifications. An image acquired at 20× magnification (0.325 μm/pixel) is processed using the same model and parameters as images acquired at 10×. Despite the doubling of spatial resolution, the system correctly identifies cell boundaries and separates touching cells with a segmentation similarity score of 0.85. This demonstrates that the system is not dependent on a fixed pixel size, as the training process and preprocessing steps effectively normalize the input scale, allowing the model to generalize across magnifications.

The fourth example involves a dataset of fibroblasts stained with a proprietary dye from the GE Cytiva Cell Health kit, which localizes to mitochondria and produces a diffuse, low-contrast cytoplasmic signal. In this case, the contrast between the nucleus and cytoplasm is minimal, and the background exhibits significant noise. Traditional segmentation methods fail to produce coherent results, often producing fragmented or merged cells. The present system, however, leverages the deep learning model’s ability to learn high-level features that distinguish cytoplasm from background, even in the absence of strong intensity gradients. The segmentation results show a high degree of accuracy, with a similarity score of 0.83, and correctly identify cell shapes that are elongated and irregular.

The fifth example presents a case of severe cell overcrowding, with over 200 cells per image, many of which are in direct contact. In such scenarios, conventional watershed algorithms fail to separate cells due to weak boundary gradients. The present system, however, uses the segmented nuclei as seeds to guide the watershed transform, ensuring that each cell is associated with exactly one nucleus. The result is a segmentation that accurately resolves hundreds of touching cells, with a similarity score of 0.87. This example underscores the critical advantage of the seeded watershed approach over unconstrained watershed methods.

In all examples, the segmentation results are visually indistinguishable from expert manual annotations, with only minor discrepancies observed in regions of extreme cell density or where the cytoplasmic signal is nearly uniform. The system consistently outperforms two-channel automated segmentation methods, not because it is more accurate, but because it avoids the errors inherent in the nuclear staining step—such as incomplete labeling, photobleaching, or spectral crosstalk—that are propagated into the final segmentation. The examples collectively demonstrate that the invention is not merely a technical novelty but a practical, deployable solution that enables high-throughput, single-channel cell segmentation in real-world biological research settings.
