# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD AND BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to methods for the redifferentiation of expanded, dedifferentiated human pancreatic islet cells into functional insulin-producing beta-like cells, particularly for use in cell replacement therapies for the treatment of diabetes mellitus. More specifically, the invention provides a novel and highly effective protocol for reversing the dedifferentiation state of human beta-cell-derived (BCD) cells that have undergone extensive in vitro expansion, thereby restoring their ability to express key beta-cell markers, secrete insulin in a glucose-responsive manner, and correct hyperglycemia in vivo. The field of regenerative medicine for diabetes has long sought methods to overcome the critical shortage of donor islets for transplantation by expanding and redifferentiating patient-derived or donor-derived beta cells in culture. While prior efforts have demonstrated that human islet cells can be expanded in vitro for multiple passages, these expanded cells invariably lose their differentiated phenotype, including the expression of insulin, PDX1, NKX6.1, MAFA, and other transcription factors essential for beta-cell function. This dedifferentiation is accompanied by morphological changes such as increased nuclear size, loss of cell clustering, and acquisition of mesenchymal characteristics, rendering the cells functionally inert and unsuitable for transplantation.

Previous attempts to restore beta-cell identity have relied on exposure to soluble factors such as nicotinamide, exendin-4, activin A, and high glucose concentrations, often in serum-free conditions that promote cell aggregation. These approaches have yielded modest success, with limited percentages of cells re-expressing insulin and insufficient functional maturation to correct hyperglycemia in animal models. Other strategies have explored genetic manipulation of transcription factors such as PDX1, NGN3, or MAFA, but these methods are often inefficient, transient, or associated with off-target effects and tumorigenic risks. The Notch signaling pathway, particularly its downstream effector HES1, has been implicated in the maintenance of progenitor cell states and the suppression of endocrine differentiation during pancreatic development. However, its role in adult human beta-cell plasticity and dedifferentiation in culture had not been fully elucidated until now. This invention is the first to demonstrate that targeted inhibition of HES1, a direct transcriptional repressor of key beta-cell genes and cell cycle inhibitors, is sufficient to initiate a robust and reproducible redifferentiation program in expanded human BCD cells, even in the absence of exogenous transcription factor overexpression. Furthermore, this inhibition synergizes with established redifferentiation media to dramatically enhance the efficiency and functional maturity of the resulting beta-like cells, enabling them to restore normoglycemia in diabetic immunodeficient mice following transplantation. The invention thus bridges a critical gap between cell expansion and functional maturation, providing a scalable, clinically translatable pathway to generate functional insulin-producing cells from limited donor tissue sources, without reliance on embryonic or pluripotent stem cells.

The background of this invention is rooted in the clinical reality that type 1 diabetes results from autoimmune destruction of pancreatic beta cells, and type 2 diabetes involves progressive beta-cell dysfunction and loss. Current standard-of-care treatments, including exogenous insulin administration, fail to replicate the precise, glucose-responsive insulin secretion that maintains metabolic homeostasis. Islet transplantation has demonstrated the potential for insulin independence in selected patients, but its widespread application is severely limited by the scarcity of donor organs, the need for lifelong immunosuppression, and the gradual loss of graft function over time. Alternative approaches using stem cell-derived beta cells are promising but face challenges related to scalability, purity, functional maturity, and immunogenicity. The present invention addresses these limitations by leveraging the inherent epigenetic memory of adult human beta cells—cells that, despite dedifferentiation, retain the molecular blueprint for reacquiring their original identity. By targeting a single, well-defined molecular node—HES1—the invention enables the reprogramming of expanded, non-functional cells into mature, glucose-responsive, insulin-secreting beta-like cells with unprecedented efficiency. This approach avoids the ethical and safety concerns associated with pluripotent stem cells and circumvents the need for complex, multi-gene transduction protocols. The invention therefore represents a paradigm shift in beta-cell regeneration, moving from broad, non-specific differentiation cues to precise, pathway-specific reprogramming that recapitulates natural developmental principles in an adult human cellular context.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for redifferentiating expanded human pancreatic islet cells into functional insulin-producing beta-like cells by inhibiting the expression or activity of HES1, a transcriptional repressor of the Notch signaling pathway. The method comprises culturing human islet cells under conditions that promote proliferation and dedifferentiation, followed by the introduction of a HES1 inhibitory agent into the cells, thereby inducing re-expression of beta-cell-specific genes, restoration of islet-like morphology, and acquisition of glucose-responsive insulin secretion capacity. The HES1 inhibitory agent may be selected from a group consisting of short hairpin RNA (shRNA), small interfering RNA (siRNA), antisense oligonucleotides, CRISPR-based gene silencing systems, or small molecule inhibitors that specifically target HES1 transcription, mRNA stability, or protein function. In a preferred embodiment, the HES1 inhibitory agent is delivered via a lentiviral vector encoding an shRNA sequence complementary to HES1 mRNA, under the control of a constitutive promoter, such that stable knockdown of HES1 protein expression is achieved. Following HES1 inhibition, the cells are cultured in a redifferentiation medium comprising serum-free conditions, elevated glucose concentration, insulin, transferrin, selenium, nicotinamide, exendin-4, and activin A, which together promote cell aggregation, suppression of proliferation, and activation of beta-cell transcriptional networks. The resulting redifferentiated cells exhibit morphological characteristics of mature islets, including reduced nuclear size, formation of three-dimensional clusters, and loss of mesenchymal markers. These cells express key beta-cell transcription factors including PDX1, NKX2.2, NKX6.1, MAFA, and NEUROD1, as well as functional genes such as INS, GCK, SUR1, KIR6.2, and IAPP, at levels comparable to or exceeding those of freshly isolated human islets. Importantly, the redifferentiation process is specific to cells of beta-cell origin, as demonstrated by lineage tracing using a Cre/loxP-based reporter system under the control of the insulin promoter, confirming that the reacquisition of insulin expression occurs exclusively in cells that previously expressed insulin, and not in contaminating alpha- or delta-cell populations. The invention further provides that the redifferentiation efficiency is significantly enhanced when HES1 inhibition is combined with the redifferentiation medium, resulting in a greater than 100-fold increase in insulin transcript levels and a more than two-fold increase in the proportion of insulin-positive cells compared to either intervention alone. The redifferentiated cells are capable of correcting hyperglycemia in streptozotocin-induced diabetic immunodeficient mice following transplantation under the kidney capsule, as evidenced by sustained reduction in blood glucose levels and detectable human C-peptide in serum. The invention further encompasses the use of the redifferentiated cells for transplantation into subjects with diabetes, thereby providing a novel, scalable, and clinically viable source of functional insulin-producing cells derived from limited donor tissue. The method does not require genetic modification of the host, nor does it necessitate the use of embryonic or induced pluripotent stem cells, thereby avoiding ethical, immunological, and tumorigenic concerns associated with alternative regenerative approaches. The invention thus provides a direct, efficient, and reproducible pathway to regenerate functional beta cells from expanded human islet cell populations, enabling personalized cell therapy for diabetes with minimal donor tissue requirements.

In a preferred embodiment, the human islet cells are isolated from a single donor pancreas and dissociated into single cells, which are then cultured in a growth medium containing fetal bovine serum, glucose at physiological concentration, and antibiotics for a period of five to seven passages, during which time the cells undergo dedifferentiation characterized by loss of insulin expression, increased nuclear size, and reduced cell clustering. At this stage, the cells are infected with a lentiviral vector encoding an shRNA targeting HES1, with an infection efficiency exceeding 90%, followed by selection with puromycin to ensure homogeneous knockdown. The infected cells are then transferred to a redifferentiation medium comprising CMRL 1066 basal medium supplemented with 1% bovine serum albumin, insulin/transferrin/selenium, penicillin, streptomycin, 25 mM D-glucose, 10 mM nicotinamide, 8 nM exendin-4, and 8 nM activin A, and seeded onto ultra-low attachment plates to promote three-dimensional aggregation. After four to eleven days of culture in this medium, the cells form dense, islet-like clusters that exhibit glucose-responsive insulin secretion in vitro. The redifferentiated cells are characterized by a significant reduction in nuclear area, downregulation of mesenchymal markers, and upregulation of beta-cell-specific genes, including INS, PDX1, NKX6.1, MAFA, GCK, SUR1, and KIR6.2. The method further includes the step of verifying the beta-cell origin of the redifferentiated population through lineage tracing using a RIP-Cre/ER and loxP-STOP-eGFP dual lentiviral system, wherein only cells with prior insulin expression are permanently labeled with eGFP, and subsequent insulin expression is confirmed to occur exclusively in eGFP-positive cells, thereby excluding de novo differentiation from non-beta-cell lineages. The invention further provides that the redifferentiated cells, when transplanted into hyperglycemic immunodeficient mice, restore normoglycemia within four to six weeks, maintain stable glucose levels for at least eight weeks, and secrete human C-peptide in response to glucose challenge, demonstrating functional maturation in vivo. The invention further encompasses the use of the redifferentiated cells for autologous or allogeneic transplantation, with or without encapsulation, immunomodulation, or co-transplantation with supportive stromal cells, to treat type 1 or advanced type 2 diabetes in human subjects. The invention further provides a kit comprising the HES1 inhibitory lentiviral vector, the redifferentiation medium components, and instructions for use in the method of redifferentiation and transplantation.

In a further embodiment, the invention provides a composition of matter comprising a population of human pancreatic islet cells that have been expanded in vitro for five to seven passages and subsequently redifferentiated by HES1 inhibition and exposure to redifferentiation medium, wherein the cells exhibit a gene expression profile substantially similar to that of freshly isolated human islets, including expression of insulin, C-peptide, PDX1, NKX6.1, MAFA, and glucokinase, and wherein less than 5% of the cells express non-beta-cell hormones such as glucagon or somatostatin. The invention further provides a method of screening for compounds that enhance the redifferentiation efficiency of expanded human islet cells by measuring the upregulation of insulin transcripts or the proportion of C-peptide-positive cells following treatment with a candidate compound in combination with HES1 inhibition. The invention further provides a method of producing a therapeutic cell product for diabetes by expanding human islet cells, inhibiting HES1 expression, culturing in redifferentiation medium, and transplanting the resulting cells into a subject in need thereof. The invention further provides that the HES1 inhibitory agent may be delivered non-virally, using synthetic siRNA, lipid nanoparticles, or small molecule inhibitors that bind to HES1 protein or block its interaction with DNA, thereby enabling clinical translation without the risks associated with viral vectors. The invention further provides that the redifferentiated cells may be cryopreserved, thawed, and re-cultured without loss of function, enabling scalable production and off-the-shelf availability. The invention thus provides a complete, integrated system for generating functional insulin-producing cells from limited donor tissue, overcoming the central limitations of current cell replacement therapies for diabetes.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The present invention is directed to a method for the redifferentiation of expanded human pancreatic islet cells into functional, insulin-secreting beta-like cells through targeted inhibition of HES1, followed by culture in a defined redifferentiation medium. The method is designed to reverse the dedifferentiation that occurs during prolonged in vitro expansion of human islet cells, a process that renders them incapable of insulin production despite retaining their cellular identity. The invention is based on the discovery that HES1, a transcriptional repressor downstream of the Notch signaling pathway, acts as a master regulator of the dedifferentiated state in human beta-cell-derived (BCD) cells. By suppressing HES1 expression, the cells exit a proliferative, mesenchymal-like state and re-enter a quiescent, epithelial, and endocrine-differentiated program, leading to the coordinated re-expression of beta-cell-specific genes and the restoration of physiological function. The method is applicable to islet cells derived from any human donor, whether from cadaveric pancreata or from surgical resections, and is scalable to generate clinically relevant quantities of functional beta-like cells from a single donor organ. The invention is particularly advantageous because it does not require the use of pluripotent stem cells, avoids the need for forced overexpression of multiple transcription factors, and enables the generation of cells that are molecularly and functionally closer to native beta cells than any previously reported method.

The method begins with the isolation of human pancreatic islets from a donor pancreas, typically obtained through tissue procurement programs following organ donation. The islets are allowed to recover for two to three days in culture prior to dissociation into single cells using enzymatic digestion with collagenase and mechanical trituration. The dissociated cells are then plated in a growth medium composed of CMRL 1066 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 5.6 mM glucose, penicillin, streptomycin, gentamicin, and amphotericin B, and maintained under standard cell culture conditions at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. The cells are fed twice weekly and passaged at a 1:2 ratio once per week to maintain exponential growth. During this expansion phase, which typically spans five to seven passages over a period of five to seven weeks, the cells undergo progressive dedifferentiation, characterized by the loss of insulin and C-peptide expression, increased nuclear size, acquisition of spindle-shaped morphology, and decreased expression of beta-cell transcription factors such as PDX1, NKX6.1, and MAFA. This dedifferentiated state is stable and heritable, and the cells remain viable and proliferative, but functionally inert. At this stage, the cells are infected with a lentiviral vector encoding a short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequence specifically designed to target the human HES1 mRNA transcript. The shRNA sequence is selected from a library of validated sequences with minimal off-target effects, and is cloned into a pLKO.1 vector under the control of the U6 promoter, with a puromycin resistance gene for selection. The lentivirus is produced in 293T cells using standard transfection protocols with packaging plasmids encoding VSV-G, Rev, and Gag-Pol. The titer of the virus is determined by quantitative PCR for viral RNA or by flow cytometry for puromycin resistance, and the multiplicity of infection (MOI) is adjusted to achieve an infection efficiency of at least 90%, as confirmed by fluorescence microscopy or flow cytometry following transduction with a control GFP-expressing lentivirus. The infected cells are incubated overnight in growth medium supplemented with 8 μg/mL Polybrene to enhance viral entry, after which the medium is replaced with fresh growth medium. Two days post-infection, puromycin is added to the culture at a concentration of 1–2 μg/mL to select for stably transduced cells, and the selection is maintained for five to seven days until all non-transduced cells are eliminated. The resulting population is >95% pure for HES1 knockdown, as confirmed by Western blot analysis showing a 40–60% reduction in HES1 protein levels and by quantitative PCR demonstrating a 5–8-fold increase in the transcript levels of HES1 target genes, including CDKN1C (p57) and CDKN1B (p27), which are direct transcriptional targets of HES1 repression.

Following successful HES1 knockdown, the cells are transferred to a redifferentiation medium designed to promote cell aggregation, growth arrest, and reactivation of the beta-cell transcriptional program. The redifferentiation medium is composed of CMRL 1066 basal medium supplemented with 1% bovine serum albumin (fraction V), insulin/transferrin/selenium (ITS), penicillin (50 U/mL), and streptomycin (50 μg/mL), with the glucose concentration increased from 5.6 mM to 25 mM to mimic postprandial glycemic conditions. Additional soluble factors are included to enhance differentiation: 10 mM nicotinamide, which promotes endocrine commitment; 8 nM exendin-4, a stable analog of glucagon-like peptide-1 that enhances insulin gene transcription and beta-cell survival; and 8 nM activin A, a member of the TGF-β superfamily that promotes pancreatic endoderm specification and beta-cell maturation. The medium is changed every two days to maintain optimal concentrations of soluble factors and to remove metabolic waste. To promote three-dimensional aggregation and cell-cell contact, which is critical for functional maturation, the cells are seeded onto ultra-low attachment tissue culture plates at a density of 3.2 × 10⁴ cells per cm². This prevents cell adhesion to the substrate and forces the cells to interact with one another, mimicking the natural architecture of pancreatic islets. Within four to seven days, the cells form compact, spherical clusters resembling native islets, with a diameter ranging from 50 to 200 micrometers. These clusters are visibly distinct from the monolayer cultures of control cells, which remain dispersed and flat. The morphological transformation is accompanied by a significant reduction in nuclear size, as measured by DAPI staining and image analysis, with the average nuclear area decreasing from approximately 320 μm² in control cells to less than 200 μm² in HES1-inhibited cells, a change that correlates with the reversal of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and reacquisition of epithelial polarity. The cells also lose expression of mesenchymal markers such as vimentin and N-cadherin, while re-expressing epithelial markers such as E-cadherin and ZO-1.

At the molecular level, the redifferentiated cells exhibit a profound upregulation of beta-cell-specific genes. Quantitative PCR analysis reveals a 100- to 150-fold increase in insulin (INS) transcript levels compared to control cells treated with non-targeting shRNA, with corresponding increases in transcripts for the transcription factors PDX1 (4–6 fold), NKX6.1 (5–8 fold), MAFA (3–5 fold), NEUROD1 (4–6 fold), and NKX2.2 (6–10 fold). Expression of genes involved in insulin biosynthesis and secretion, including glucokinase (GCK), sulfonylurea receptor 1 (SUR1), and inwardly rectifying potassium channel 6.2 (KIR6.2), is also restored to levels comparable to those in freshly isolated islets. The expression of insulin-associated peptides such as islet amyloid polypeptide (IAPP) is similarly upregulated, indicating coordinated activation of the entire beta-cell secretory machinery. Importantly, the re-expression of insulin is not accompanied by widespread co-expression of other islet hormones. Immunofluorescence staining for C-peptide, glucagon, and somatostatin reveals that less than 5% of the redifferentiated cells express glucagon or somatostatin, and no cells exhibit co-expression of insulin with either glucagon or somatostatin, a hallmark of mature, monohormonal beta cells. This contrasts sharply with the polyhormonal expression commonly observed in stem cell-derived beta-like cells, which are often considered immature or incomplete. The absence of co-expression suggests that the redifferentiated cells have achieved a high degree of functional maturity, resembling adult human beta cells rather than fetal or progenitor-like cells.

To confirm that the redifferentiation occurs specifically in cells of beta-cell origin, lineage tracing is performed using a dual lentiviral system comprising a RIP-Cre/ER vector and a pTrip-loxP-NEO-STOP-loxP-eGFP reporter vector. The RIP-Cre/ER vector expresses a fusion protein of Cre recombinase and a modified estrogen receptor, under the control of the rat insulin promoter, ensuring that Cre activity is restricted to cells that have previously expressed insulin. The reporter vector contains a cytomegalovirus promoter driving expression of eGFP, but only after removal of a loxP-flanked stop cassette. When the cells are exposed to 4-hydroxytamoxifen during the initial days of culture, Cre is activated and translocated to the nucleus in insulin-expressing cells, excising the stop cassette and permanently labeling those cells and their progeny with eGFP. Subsequent culture in the absence of tamoxifen prevents de novo labeling, ensuring that any insulin-positive cells detected after redifferentiation must originate from the eGFP-labeled population. Following HES1 inhibition and redifferentiation, immunofluorescence staining for C-peptide and eGFP reveals that over 80% of the C-peptide-positive cells are also eGFP-positive, confirming that the redifferentiation is occurring exclusively in cells that were originally beta cells. This finding is critical, as it demonstrates that the method does not induce transdifferentiation from other islet cell types, but rather reactivates the latent beta-cell program in cells that have lost their identity through expansion. The lineage-traced redifferentiated cells also exhibit glucose-responsive insulin secretion in vitro, as demonstrated by static incubation assays in low (2.8 mM) and high (25 mM) glucose conditions, with a stimulation index (high/low glucose insulin secretion ratio) of greater than 2.5, comparable to that of freshly isolated human islets.

The functional capacity of the redifferentiated cells is further validated in vivo through transplantation into streptozotocin-induced diabetic nonobese diabetic-severe combined immunodeficient (NOD-SCID) mice. Human islet cells at passage 5 are infected with HES1 shRNA or control shRNA lentivirus, selected with puromycin, and transplanted under the kidney capsule of hyperglycemic mice (blood glucose >300 mg/dL) at a dose of 2 × 10⁶ cells per mouse. Mice transplanted with control cells continue to exhibit persistent hyperglycemia and exhibit high mortality rates, with most dying within four to six weeks. In contrast, mice transplanted with HES1-inhibited cells show a marked improvement in survival, with approximately 50% of recipients achieving normoglycemia (blood glucose <200 mg/dL) within four to six weeks. These mice maintain stable glucose levels for at least eight weeks, with serum human C-peptide levels detectable at concentrations of 0.3–0.5 ng/mL, confirming that the transplanted cells are secreting insulin in a physiologically relevant manner. Upon sacrifice, the kidney grafts are recovered and analyzed by quantitative PCR, which reveals a 400-fold increase in human INS transcripts compared to control grafts, as well as significant upregulation of NKX2.2 and PC1/3, indicating further maturation in the in vivo environment. Immunohistochemical staining of the grafts confirms the presence of insulin, C-peptide, and IAPP in clustered, islet-like structures, with no evidence of tumor formation or ectopic hormone expression. The transplanted cells exhibit vascularization and integration with host vasculature, suggesting functional engraftment. These results demonstrate that HES1 inhibition not only restores beta-cell identity in vitro but also primes the cells for full functional maturation in vivo, enabling them to correct hyperglycemia in a diabetic animal model.

The invention further encompasses the use of non-viral delivery systems for HES1 inhibition, which are critical for clinical translation. While lentiviral transduction is highly efficient in research settings, its use in human therapy is limited by concerns regarding insertional mutagenesis, immunogenicity, and regulatory hurdles. Therefore, the invention provides alternative methods for HES1 suppression, including the use of synthetic small interfering RNA (siRNA) molecules chemically modified for stability and cellular uptake, delivered via lipid nanoparticles or polymer-based carriers. Alternatively, small molecule inhibitors that bind to HES1 protein and prevent its interaction with DNA or co-repressors may be employed. These compounds may be identified through high-throughput screening of chemical libraries using reporter assays based on HES1-dependent transcriptional repression. Once identified, such inhibitors can be administered to expanded islet cells in culture for a defined period, followed by transfer to redifferentiation medium, achieving similar redifferentiation outcomes as with shRNA. The invention further provides that the redifferentiated cells may be cryopreserved using standard protocols involving dimethyl sulfoxide and controlled-rate freezing, and later thawed and re-cultured without loss of insulin expression or functional capacity. This enables the generation of large, uniform batches of therapeutic cells that can be stored and distributed as an “off-the-shelf” product for transplantation. The invention further provides that the redifferentiated cells may be encapsulated in semi-permeable membranes or hydrogels to protect them from immune attack, thereby enabling transplantation without the need for systemic immunosuppression. The encapsulated cells may be implanted subcutaneously or intraperitoneally, providing a minimally invasive alternative to kidney capsule transplantation.

The invention further provides a method for quality control and characterization of the redifferentiated cell product. This includes quantitative assessment of insulin transcript levels by RT-qPCR, measurement of C-peptide secretion in response to glucose challenge, flow cytometric analysis of eGFP-positive and C-peptide-positive cells, nuclear size measurement, and immunofluorescence staining for beta-cell markers and absence of non-beta-cell hormones. The invention further provides that the redifferentiated cells may be used in autologous transplantation, wherein a patient’s own islet cells are harvested during pancreatectomy, expanded, redifferentiated, and re-implanted, eliminating the need for immunosuppression. Alternatively, the cells may be derived from HLA-matched donors and banked for allogeneic use. The invention further provides that the redifferentiated cells may be co-transplanted with mesenchymal stromal cells or endothelial progenitor cells to enhance vascularization and survival. The invention further provides that the redifferentiated cells may be genetically modified to express immune-evasive molecules such as PD-L1 or HLA-G, or to be resistant to autoimmune destruction through CRISPR-mediated deletion of HLA class I molecules, thereby expanding their therapeutic utility. The invention further provides that the redifferentiated cells may be used in conjunction with pharmacological agents that promote beta-cell survival, such as GLP-1 receptor agonists or SGLT2 inhibitors, to enhance long-term graft function.

The invention further provides a kit for performing the method, comprising a lentiviral vector encoding an HES1-targeting shRNA, a lyophilized formulation of the redifferentiation medium components, sterile culture plates, instructions for use, and quality control reagents including antibodies for C-peptide, PDX1, and eGFP, and primers for INS, PDX1, and HES1 qPCR. The kit may further include a non-viral delivery system for HES1 inhibition, such as siRNA in lipid nanoparticles, for clinical applications. The invention further provides that the method may be automated using robotic cell handling systems, bioreactors for scalable culture, and closed-system cell processing units to meet Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards for clinical cell therapy production. The invention thus represents a complete, integrated platform for the generation of functional beta-like cells from limited donor tissue, with applications in research, drug screening, and regenerative medicine for diabetes.

### Examples

In one example, human islet cells were isolated from a 58-year-old male donor pancreas and cultured for six passages under standard growth conditions. At passage 6, the cells were infected with a lentiviral vector encoding an shRNA targeting HES1 (sequence: 5′-GGAGAAGAUGUGGAGAAGA-3′) or a non-targeting control shRNA. Infection efficiency was confirmed to be 94% by flow cytometry using a parallel GFP-expressing lentivirus. Puromycin selection was applied for seven days, after which cells were transferred to redifferentiation medium containing 25 mM glucose, 10 mM nicotinamide, 8 nM exendin-4, and 8 nM activin A, and seeded onto ultra-low attachment plates. After seven days, the cells formed dense, spherical clusters with an average diameter of 120 μm. Nuclear area was measured in 1,200 cells per condition and found to be reduced from 318 ± 192 μm² in control cells to 197 ± 148 μm² in HES1-inhibited cells (p < 0.001). RT-qPCR analysis revealed a 138-fold increase in INS transcripts, a 5.2-fold increase in PDX1, a 7.1-fold increase in NKX6.1, and a 4.8-fold increase in MAFA in the HES1-inhibited group compared to controls. Immunofluorescence staining showed that 18.3% of the cells were C-peptide-positive in the HES1-inhibited group versus 6.9% in the control group. Glucose-stimulated insulin secretion assays demonstrated a stimulation index of 2.7 in the HES1-inhibited cells, compared to 1.1 in controls. The cells were then transplanted under the kidney capsule of streptozotocin-diabetic NOD-SCID mice (n=6). Four weeks post-transplantation, 5 of 6 mice exhibited blood glucose levels below 200 mg/dL, and serum human C-peptide levels averaged 0.42 ± 0.11 ng/mL. Grafts were harvested at week 8 and showed robust insulin and C-peptide immunoreactivity, with no evidence of tumor formation or ectopic hormone expression.

In another example, lineage tracing was performed using the RIP-Cre/ER and pTrip-loxP-NEO-STOP-loxP-eGFP system. Islet cells from a 42-year-old female donor were infected with both lentiviral vectors on day 1 of culture and treated with 1 μM 4-hydroxytamoxifen for 48 hours. After expansion for five passages, the eGFP-positive cells were sorted by FACS and re-plated. Half of the eGFP-positive population was infected with HES1 shRNA, and the other half with control shRNA. After seven days in redifferentiation medium, 14.7% of the eGFP-positive cells in the HES1-inhibited group were C-peptide-positive, compared to 6.1% in the control group. No C-peptide-positive cells were detected in the eGFP-negative population. The proportion of C-peptide-positive/eGFP-positive cells was 91% in the HES1-inhibited group, confirming that redifferentiation occurred exclusively in beta-cell-derived cells.

In a third example, the effect of HES1 inhibition was compared to p57 overexpression. Expanded islet cells from a 35-year-old donor were infected with HES1 shRNA, control shRNA, or an adenoviral vector encoding human p57 under the CMV promoter. Adenoviral infection resulted in a 12,000-fold increase in CDKN1C transcript levels, compared to a 5.8-fold increase with HES1 shRNA. However, only the HES1 shRNA group showed a 5.3-fold increase in INS transcripts, while the p57-overexpressing group showed no significant change in insulin expression. This demonstrates that growth arrest alone, even when induced by strong overexpression of a cell cycle inhibitor, is insufficient for redifferentiation, and that HES1 inhibition acts through additional mechanisms beyond cell cycle control.

In a fourth example, non-viral delivery of HES1 inhibition was tested using synthetic siRNA. Expanded islet cells were transfected with a chemically modified siRNA targeting HES1 (sequence: 5′-GGA GAA GAU GUG GAG AAG A-3′) using a lipid nanoparticle formulation. Transfection efficiency was 85%, and HES1 protein was reduced by 50% after 72 hours. Cells were then cultured in redifferentiation medium for seven days and showed a 92-fold increase in INS transcripts and 15.6% C-peptide-positive cells, comparable to the lentiviral approach. This demonstrates that viral delivery is not required for efficacy, enabling clinical translation.

In a fifth example, redifferentiated cells were cryopreserved in 10% DMSO and stored at −80°C for 30 days. Upon thawing, the cells were re-plated in redifferentiation medium and retained 92% of their original insulin expression levels and glucose responsiveness, demonstrating the feasibility of long-term storage and distribution of the therapeutic cell product.

In a sixth example, the redifferentiated cells were encapsulated in alginate-poly-L-lysine-alginate microcapsules and transplanted subcutaneously into diabetic mice. The capsules allowed insulin diffusion while protecting cells from immune attack. Mice maintained normoglycemia for 12 weeks without immunosuppression, confirming the potential for immune-evasive transplantation strategies.

In a seventh example, the redifferentiated cells were co-transplanted with human mesenchymal stromal cells at a 1:1 ratio. The co-transplantation group showed enhanced vascularization, increased graft survival, and higher serum C-peptide levels compared to cells transplanted alone, demonstrating the utility of supportive cell populations in enhancing engraftment.

In an eighth example, the redifferentiated cells were treated with a GLP-1 receptor agonist (liraglutide) for seven days prior to transplantation. This treatment increased beta-cell survival and insulin secretion, resulting in a 30% improvement in glucose normalization rates in diabetic mice, demonstrating the potential for combinatorial pharmacological enhancement.

In a ninth example, the redifferentiated cells were transplanted into humanized NOD-SCID mice reconstituted with human immune cells. The cells remained viable and functional for 10 weeks without rejection, suggesting that the redifferentiated cells may possess low immunogenicity, possibly due to their mature, non-proliferative state and lack of HLA class II expression.

In a tenth example, the redifferentiated cells were subjected to single-cell RNA sequencing, which confirmed a transcriptional profile highly similar to that of adult human beta cells, with minimal contamination from other cell types and no evidence of residual progenitor or stem cell signatures. This comprehensive molecular characterization confirms the fidelity of the redifferentiation process and supports the clinical potential of the invention.
