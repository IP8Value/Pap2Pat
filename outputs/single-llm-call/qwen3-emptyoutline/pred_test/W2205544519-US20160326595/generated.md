# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Prostate cancer remains one of the most prevalent malignancies affecting men worldwide, with significant disparities in incidence, aggressiveness, and mortality rates observed across ancestral populations. In the United States, men of African ancestry consistently exhibit higher rates of diagnosis at advanced stages, more aggressive histopathological features, and significantly elevated mortality compared to their Caucasian counterparts. These disparities persist even after accounting for socioeconomic factors, access to care, and screening practices, suggesting an underlying biological basis for differential disease behavior. While extensive genomic characterization of prostate cancer has been conducted over the past decade, the majority of these studies have focused predominantly on cohorts of European descent, leaving a critical gap in the understanding of the molecular architecture of prostate cancer in men of African ancestry. This imbalance has led to the widespread application of biomarkers, prognostic models, and therapeutic strategies derived from populations that do not fully represent the genetic diversity of the global prostate cancer patient population.

The molecular landscape of prostate cancer has been increasingly defined through large-scale genomic studies, which have identified recurrent somatic alterations such as the TMPRSS2-ERG gene fusion, deletions of the PTEN tumor suppressor gene, mutations in SPOP, and structural rearrangements involving CHD1. These alterations are frequently observed in early-stage, clinically localized disease and are thought to play pivotal roles in tumor initiation and progression. However, the prevalence and functional significance of these alterations vary substantially across ancestral groups. For instance, the TMPRSS2-ERG fusion, once considered a hallmark genomic event in prostate cancer, is markedly less frequent in tumors from men of African ancestry compared to those of European descent. Similarly, deletions of PTEN, a well-established driver of castration resistance and poor prognosis, are reported to occur at significantly lower frequencies in African American men, even in the context of high-grade disease. These observations challenge the assumption of a uniform molecular pathogenesis across populations and raise fundamental questions about whether alternative, population-specific genomic drivers may be responsible for the more aggressive clinical course observed in African American patients.

Despite these emerging disparities, the search for alternative genomic alterations in African American prostate cancer has been hindered by limited sample sizes, insufficient representation in public genomic databases, and a historical underinvestment in research focused on underrepresented populations. Consequently, the genomic features that distinguish aggressive prostate cancer in African American men have remained largely elusive. While some studies have suggested that differences in androgen receptor signaling, inflammatory pathways, or epigenetic regulation may contribute to the observed disparities, none have identified a recurrent, clonally selected, structurally defined genomic alteration that is both highly prevalent in African American tumors and strongly associated with adverse clinical outcomes. The absence of such a marker has impeded the development of ancestry-informed diagnostic tools, risk stratification algorithms, and targeted therapeutic approaches tailored to this high-risk population.

Furthermore, the clinical implications of these genomic differences extend beyond mere observation. The reliance on biomarkers derived from predominantly Caucasian cohorts—such as PTEN loss or ERG expression—for prognostication and treatment selection may lead to misclassification of African American patients, resulting in either under-treatment of aggressive disease or overtreatment of indolent tumors. This misalignment between molecular biology and clinical practice contributes to persistent health inequities and undermines efforts to achieve precision oncology for all. There is therefore an urgent and unmet need to identify novel, population-specific genomic alterations that are not only statistically associated with African American ancestry but also mechanistically linked to tumor progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. Such discoveries would not only enhance our understanding of the fundamental biology of prostate cancer but also provide actionable targets for the development of diagnostic assays, prognostic classifiers, and potentially novel therapeutic strategies designed specifically for men of African ancestry.

This invention addresses this critical gap by identifying a previously unrecognized, recurrent, and clinically significant genomic alteration centered on the LSAMP locus at chromosome 3q13.31, which is highly prevalent in prostate cancers from African American men and strongly associated with disease recurrence and advanced pathological stage. Unlike previously described alterations such as PTEN deletion or TMPRSS2-ERG fusion, this alteration is virtually absent in Caucasian American prostate cancers and is not merely a correlative observation but is demonstrably linked to aggressive clinical behavior. The discovery of this alteration provides the first molecular marker that is both ancestrally specific and prognostically robust in early-stage prostate cancer, offering a foundation for the development of ancestry-informed clinical tools that have thus far been lacking. The invention further encompasses the methods for detecting this alteration through a variety of molecular diagnostic platforms, including fluorescence in situ hybridization, next-generation sequencing, and digital PCR, enabling its integration into routine clinical pathology workflows. By establishing LSAMP locus deletion as a defining genomic feature of aggressive prostate cancer in African American men, this invention transforms the paradigm of prostate cancer genomics from a one-size-fits-all model to a precision medicine framework that acknowledges and responds to the biological diversity of human populations.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a novel genomic alteration characterized by recurrent deletion or structural rearrangement of the LSAMP locus at chromosomal region 3q13.31 in prostate cancer tissues derived from men of African ancestry, and to the use of this alteration as a diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic biomarker for prostate cancer. The invention provides a method for identifying prostate cancer in a subject of African ancestry that exhibits a high likelihood of aggressive clinical behavior, including biochemical recurrence, metastasis, or disease-specific mortality, by detecting the presence of a deletion or structural rearrangement involving the LSAMP gene or its flanking regulatory regions. This deletion is distinguished by its high frequency in African American prostate cancers, its near absence in Caucasian American prostate cancers, and its strong statistical association with adverse clinical outcomes independent of traditional pathological parameters such as Gleason score or clinical stage.

The invention further encompasses a diagnostic assay for detecting the LSAMP locus deletion in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples, fresh frozen tissue, or circulating tumor DNA, utilizing probes or primers designed to specifically hybridize to or amplify genomic sequences within the 3q13.31 region encompassing the LSAMP gene, ZBTB20, GAP43, and GSK3B. The assay may be implemented using fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), next-generation sequencing, quantitative PCR, digital PCR, or microarray-based copy number analysis, each of which is configured to detect homozygous or heterozygous loss of the LSAMP locus with a sensitivity and specificity sufficient for clinical application. The invention further includes a kit comprising one or more probes, primers, or sequencing libraries optimized for the detection of LSAMP locus deletions, along with instructions for use in determining the risk of disease progression in a patient diagnosed with prostate cancer.

The invention also provides a method for stratifying patients with prostate cancer into distinct risk categories based on the presence or absence of the LSAMP locus deletion. Patients exhibiting this deletion are classified as having high-risk disease, warranting more intensive surveillance, earlier adjuvant therapy, or enrollment in clinical trials targeting the downstream consequences of LSAMP loss. Conversely, patients lacking this deletion, even if otherwise classified as high-grade by conventional criteria, may be candidates for active surveillance or less aggressive treatment regimens, thereby reducing overtreatment and its associated morbidities.

The invention further discloses the biological mechanism underlying the clinical significance of LSAMP deletion, wherein loss of LSAMP expression leads to dysregulation of cell adhesion, migration, and signaling pathways critical for maintaining epithelial integrity and suppressing metastatic dissemination. The LSAMP gene encodes a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily of cell adhesion molecules, and its deletion results in the loss of a tumor-suppressive function that normally constrains invasive behavior in prostate epithelial cells. The deletion is not merely a passenger event but is selected for during tumor evolution, as evidenced by its recurrence across multiple independent tumors and its association with clonal expansion and disease recurrence. The invention further identifies that the LSAMP deletion frequently co-occurs with interchromosomal rearrangements and is mutually exclusive with PTEN deletion, suggesting a distinct molecular subclass of prostate cancer that is prevalent in African American men and biologically distinct from the PTEN-driven subclass common in Caucasian men.

The invention further provides a method for identifying therapeutic vulnerabilities in LSAMP-deleted prostate cancers by targeting pathways that become essential for tumor cell survival in the absence of LSAMP-mediated adhesion and signaling. These include inhibitors of integrin signaling, Rho GTPase pathways, or components of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR cascade, which are hyperactivated as compensatory mechanisms in LSAMP-deficient cells. The invention further encompasses the use of LSAMP deletion as a predictive biomarker for response to these targeted therapies, enabling the selection of patients most likely to benefit from such interventions.

In addition, the invention includes a method for monitoring disease progression or treatment response in patients with LSAMP-deleted prostate cancer by serially assessing the presence of LSAMP locus deletion in circulating tumor DNA obtained from blood samples. This non-invasive approach allows for real-time tracking of clonal dynamics, detection of emerging resistance mechanisms, and early identification of metastatic transformation, thereby facilitating timely therapeutic adjustments.

The invention further provides a computer-implemented system for integrating LSAMP deletion status with clinical, pathological, and genomic data to generate a personalized risk score for prostate cancer progression. This system utilizes machine learning algorithms trained on multi-institutional cohorts to predict the probability of biochemical recurrence, metastasis, or cancer-specific death based on the presence of LSAMP deletion in conjunction with other variables such as Gleason score, PSA level, age, and tumor volume.

The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the molecular classification of prostate cancer by identifying a population-specific genomic alteration that is not only highly prevalent in African American men but also mechanistically linked to aggressive disease biology. It provides the first clinically actionable biomarker that directly addresses the disparity in prostate cancer outcomes between African American and Caucasian American men, enabling the development of ancestry-informed diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic strategies that are both scientifically rigorous and ethically imperative.

### DEFINITIONS

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms shall have the meanings ascribed below, unless the context clearly indicates otherwise. The terms used herein are intended to be interpreted in accordance with their ordinary and customary meaning in the field of oncology, molecular diagnostics, and genomic medicine, as understood by a person having ordinary skill in the art at the time of filing.

The term “African ancestry” refers to individuals whose genetic heritage is primarily derived from populations indigenous to the African continent, including but not limited to individuals of African American, Afro-Caribbean, Sub-Saharan African, or African diaspora descent. This term is not limited to self-reported ethnicity but encompasses individuals whose genomic ancestry, as determined by principal component analysis or other population genetic methods, clusters with reference populations such as Yoruba (YRI), African Caribbean (ACB), or African American (ASW) from the 1000 Genomes Project or HapMap Consortium. The term “Caucasian ancestry” refers to individuals whose genetic heritage is primarily derived from populations indigenous to Europe, including individuals of European American, White, or non-Hispanic White descent, as confirmed by genomic clustering with reference populations such as Utah residents with Northern and Western European ancestry (CEU).

The term “LSAMP locus” refers to a genomic region on chromosome 3q13.31 encompassing the LSAMP gene (Limbic System-Associated Membrane Protein), along with its proximal regulatory elements, flanking genes including ZBTB20, GAP43, and GSK3B, and the intervening intergenic sequences spanning approximately 1 megabase to 25 megabases. The LSAMP locus is defined as the genomic interval bounded by the most distal recurrent deletion breakpoints observed in prostate cancer samples from African American men, as determined by whole genome sequencing and fluorescence in situ hybridization. The locus includes the entire coding sequence of LSAMP, its promoter region, introns, untranslated regions, and any enhancer or silencer elements located within the defined boundaries that are consistently deleted in association with prostate cancer progression.

The term “deletion” refers to the loss of one or both alleles of the LSAMP locus, resulting in a reduction in copy number relative to the diploid state. A homozygous deletion refers to the complete absence of both copies of the LSAMP locus in tumor cells, whereas a heterozygous deletion refers to the loss of one copy, resulting in a copy number of one. Deletions may be focal, affecting only a portion of the locus, or broad, encompassing the entire region from ZBTB20 to GSK3B. Deletions may also be accompanied by structural rearrangements such as inversions, translocations, or duplications that disrupt the integrity of the LSAMP gene or its regulatory elements, even in the absence of net copy number loss.

The term “structural rearrangement” refers to any genomic alteration involving the reorganization of DNA segments within or adjacent to the LSAMP locus, including but not limited to inversions, translocations, duplications, or complex rearrangements that result in the disruption of the LSAMP gene’s coding sequence, splicing pattern, or regulatory landscape. Such rearrangements may generate novel fusion transcripts, alter enhancer-promoter interactions, or lead to the loss of critical exons or non-coding regulatory elements, thereby impairing the normal function of LSAMP.

The term “recurrent” refers to the observation of the same genomic alteration—in this case, deletion or structural rearrangement of the LSAMP locus—in multiple independent prostate cancer samples from different individuals, with a frequency significantly higher than expected by chance. Recurrence is established when the alteration is detected in at least 15% of tumors from a defined population, with statistical significance (p < 0.05) when compared to a control population.

The term “prostate cancer” refers to any malignant neoplasm arising from the epithelial cells of the prostate gland, including adenocarcinoma, ductal carcinoma, acinar carcinoma, and variants thereof. The term encompasses both localized and advanced disease, including clinically localized tumors, locally invasive tumors, and metastatic disease, regardless of Gleason score, TNM stage, or PSA level.

The term “biochemical recurrence” refers to the rise in serum prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels following definitive local therapy, such as radical prostatectomy or radiation therapy, in the absence of radiographic evidence of metastasis, as defined by the Phoenix Consensus Conference criteria (PSA nadir + 2 ng/mL).

The term “aggressive disease” refers to prostate cancer characterized by one or more of the following features: biochemical recurrence within three years of definitive therapy, development of metastatic disease, progression to castration-resistant prostate cancer, or disease-specific mortality. Aggressive disease is distinguished from indolent disease, which remains localized and asymptomatic for prolonged periods without requiring intervention.

The term “tumor suppressor gene” refers to a gene whose normal function is to inhibit cell proliferation, promote apoptosis, maintain genomic stability, or suppress invasive and metastatic behavior. Loss of function of a tumor suppressor gene, through deletion, mutation, or epigenetic silencing, contributes to tumorigenesis. LSAMP is identified herein as a tumor suppressor gene in prostate cancer, as its deletion is associated with increased tumor cell invasion, metastasis, and poor clinical outcomes.

The term “fluorescence in situ hybridization” or “FISH” refers to a cytogenetic technique that uses fluorescently labeled DNA probes to detect and localize the presence or absence of specific DNA sequences on chromosomes or within interphase nuclei. FISH is used herein to detect deletions of the LSAMP locus in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections or tissue microarrays, using probes designed to hybridize to the LSAMP gene and a control probe targeting a reference locus on chromosome 3.

The term “next-generation sequencing” or “NGS” refers to high-throughput DNA sequencing technologies that enable the parallel sequencing of millions of DNA fragments, including whole genome sequencing, whole exome sequencing, targeted panel sequencing, and RNA sequencing. NGS is used herein to identify somatic deletions, structural variants, and fusion transcripts involving the LSAMP locus with single-base resolution.

The term “digital PCR” refers to a highly sensitive and quantitative form of polymerase chain reaction that partitions a sample into thousands of individual reactions, allowing for absolute quantification of nucleic acid targets. Digital PCR is used herein to detect low-frequency LSAMP deletions in circulating tumor DNA or in samples with low tumor purity.

The term “copy number variation” or “CNV” refers to a type of structural variation in the genome involving gains or losses of DNA segments greater than one kilobase in size. CNVs are detected herein using algorithms such as ASCAT, Control-FREEC, or cn.MOPS, which analyze sequencing depth or SNP array intensity data to infer copy number states across the genome.

The term “tumor purity” refers to the proportion of tumor cells within a tissue sample relative to non-neoplastic cells such as stromal cells, immune cells, or benign epithelial cells. Tumor purity is estimated herein using histopathological review, computational deconvolution of sequencing data, or flow cytometry.

The term “clinical grade” refers to the classification of prostate cancer based on standardized pathological criteria, including Gleason score, TNM stage, surgical margin status, lymph node involvement, and extraprostatic extension. Clinical grade is used herein as a covariate in multivariate analyses to determine whether LSAMP deletion provides independent prognostic information beyond established pathological parameters.

The term “ancestry-informed” refers to diagnostic, prognostic, or therapeutic approaches that are specifically calibrated or optimized for a defined ancestral population based on the distinct genomic, biological, or clinical characteristics observed in that population. The methods and compositions described herein are ancestry-informed because they are designed to detect and interpret LSAMP deletion specifically in the context of African ancestry, where the alteration is prevalent and clinically significant.

The term “therapeutic vulnerability” refers to a biological dependency or pathway that becomes essential for the survival or proliferation of tumor cells following the loss of a tumor suppressor gene. In the context of this invention, therapeutic vulnerabilities are identified in LSAMP-deleted prostate cancers through functional genomic screens, transcriptomic profiling, or pharmacological profiling, and include targets such as integrin β1, FAK, RhoA, or PI3K.

The term “circulating tumor DNA” or “ctDNA” refers to cell-free DNA fragments released into the bloodstream by tumor cells through apoptosis, necrosis, or active secretion. ctDNA is used herein as a non-invasive source of tumor genomic material for the detection of LSAMP deletion in serial monitoring of disease progression or treatment response.

The term “machine learning algorithm” refers to a computational method that learns patterns from data without being explicitly programmed.
