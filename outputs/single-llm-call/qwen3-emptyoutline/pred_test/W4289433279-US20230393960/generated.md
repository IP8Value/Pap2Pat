# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

In the domain of algorithmic decision-making systems, particularly those deployed in high-stakes environments such as financial fraud detection, credit scoring, criminal risk assessment, and employment screening, the ethical and legal imperative to prevent discriminatory outcomes has become increasingly urgent. Regulatory frameworks in jurisdictions such as the United States have long recognized two principal forms of unlawful discrimination: disparate treatment and disparate impact. Disparate treatment refers to intentional discrimination against individuals based on protected attributes such as race, gender, national origin, or religion. In contrast, disparate impact arises when a seemingly neutral policy or algorithm produces disproportionately adverse outcomes for members of a protected group, even in the absence of explicit intent to discriminate. While disparate treatment has traditionally been addressed by excluding protected attributes from model inputs—thereby ensuring that decisions are not directly informed by race, gender, or country of origin—the challenge of disparate impact remains substantially more complex. This is because bias can emerge indirectly through correlations between non-sensitive features and protected attributes, or through the inherent structure of training data that reflects historical inequities.

Existing machine learning fairness techniques have primarily focused on post-hoc adjustments to model outputs or modifications to training procedures in an effort to enforce statistical parity across protected groups. Prominent among these are demographic parity, equalized odds, and equal opportunity. Demographic parity mandates that the probability of a positive outcome be identical across all subgroups defined by a protected attribute. While conceptually straightforward, this criterion often leads to unacceptable trade-offs in domains where the base rates of the target event vary meaningfully across groups. For instance, in fraud detection, the underlying prevalence of fraudulent transactions differs significantly between countries due to variations in legal enforcement, economic conditions, and technological infrastructure. Enforcing demographic parity in such a context would necessitate either tolerating excessive fraud in low-fraud regions or unjustly rejecting legitimate transactions in high-fraud regions, thereby undermining the core objective of the system.

Equalized odds, a more nuanced fairness criterion, requires that both the true positive rate and the false positive rate be equal across protected subgroups. This formulation acknowledges that individuals who are equally qualified—those who would, in truth, exhibit the target behavior—should be given equal opportunity for favorable outcomes, while also ensuring that no subgroup is systematically misclassified as positive when they are not. However, equalized odds imposes a rigid constraint that both error rates must be perfectly aligned across all subgroups, a condition that is often mathematically infeasible without introducing non-deterministic decision thresholds or sacrificing predictive performance. Moreover, the practical implementation of equalized odds typically requires multiple recalibrations of the model—one for each distinct value of the protected attribute—rendering it computationally prohibitive in settings involving high-arity categorical variables such as country of residence, currency type, or postal code, where the number of categories may number in the hundreds or even thousands.

Further compounding these challenges is the fact that most fairness interventions are designed under the assumption of binary protected attributes, such as male/female or black/white. This limitation renders them ill-suited for real-world applications where protected attributes are inherently multi-valued and often interdependent. For example, a fraud detection system may need to account for bias across country, currency, device type, and IP region simultaneously. Current approaches either treat each attribute independently, thereby failing to capture joint biases, or attempt to enumerate all possible combinations of attribute values, which leads to an exponential explosion in computational complexity. Additionally, many fairness techniques require access to the internal parameters of the machine learning model, including feature weights, gradients, or training data, which is often infeasible in industrial settings due to proprietary constraints, data privacy regulations, or legacy system architectures. Even when such access is available, the need to retrain or fine-tune models for each fairness constraint introduces significant operational overhead, latency, and risk of model degradation.

Moreover, the majority of existing fairness heuristics are applied during the training phase, requiring iterative retraining cycles that are incompatible with real-time inference systems. In production environments, where transaction volumes exceed tens of thousands per second and decisions must be rendered in under 100 milliseconds, model retraining is neither scalable nor economically viable. Post-hoc calibration techniques, which adjust decision thresholds after model inference, offer a more practical alternative, yet they have been largely confined to binary protected attributes and have not been generalized to handle multiple, high-arity, potentially correlated attributes in a single, unified framework. Furthermore, there is a critical absence of methods that allow end users to dynamically prioritize which type of error—false positives or false negatives—is more costly in their specific operational context. In fraud detection, for example, a false positive (rejecting a legitimate transaction) may result in customer dissatisfaction and lost revenue, while a false negative (approving a fraudulent transaction) may lead to financial loss, chargebacks, and reputational damage. The relative cost of these errors is not static; it varies across business units, product lines, and geographic markets. Yet no existing fairness methodology provides a mechanism for users to encode these business-specific priorities into the fairness calibration process.

The current state of the art also fails to address the issue of attribute dependence. In many datasets, protected attributes are not statistically independent. For instance, a user’s country of residence is often strongly correlated with the currency they use, the payment method they prefer, or the device they access the platform from. Applying fairness constraints independently to each of these attributes results in redundant recalibrations, unnecessary computational burden, and potentially conflicting threshold adjustments. There is no established method to identify and prune such redundant or highly correlated attributes prior to calibration, leading to inefficient and sometimes contradictory fairness interventions.

Finally, there is a lack of transparency and interpretability in how fairness is achieved. Most techniques treat fairness as a black-box optimization problem, providing no insight into which thresholds were selected, why certain subgroups were adjusted more than others, or how the final decision boundary was derived. This opacity undermines regulatory compliance, auditability, and stakeholder trust. In regulated industries, it is not sufficient to merely achieve fairness; one must also be able to demonstrate, explain, and justify how fairness was attained in a manner that is verifiable by auditors, regulators, and affected individuals.

The present invention addresses these critical shortcomings by introducing a novel fairness measure—Relaxed Equalized Odds—and a corresponding one-shot, model-agnostic, post-hoc calibration heuristic that enables the practical, scalable, and customizable enforcement of fairness across multiple high-arity protected attributes in real-time decision systems. Unlike prior approaches, this invention does not require access to model internals, does not necessitate retraining, and does not impose rigid equality constraints that are incompatible with real-world base rate variations. Instead, it introduces a statistically grounded relaxation of the equalized odds criterion, permitting deviations within a defined tolerance window around the mean performance metrics across subgroups. This relaxation ensures that fairness is achieved without sacrificing predictive utility or operational feasibility. Furthermore, the invention provides end users with the ability to select whether to prioritize equal false positive rates, equal true positive rates, or both, based on their specific cost structures. The heuristic operates on aggregate statistics derived from model outputs, making it compatible with any classification model regardless of architecture, and can be implemented as a lightweight post-processing layer that requires no modification to existing infrastructure. It further incorporates an attribute pruning mechanism that identifies and eliminates redundant protected attributes based on statistical dependence, thereby reducing computational complexity. For scenarios involving multiple attributes, the invention offers two distinct calibration regimes: a strong regime that enforces fairness across all joint subpopulations, and a weak regime that enforces fairness per attribute independently, allowing users to choose the appropriate level of granularity based on the number of attributes and the desired balance between fairness and efficiency. The entire process is deterministic, convergent, and executable in a single pass, making it uniquely suited for deployment in high-throughput, low-latency environments such as online payment systems, e-commerce fraud platforms, and automated lending engines. This invention thus represents a paradigm shift in algorithmic fairness, moving from theoretical idealism to practical, business-aware, and legally defensible implementation.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a novel method for achieving algorithmic fairness in machine learning-based decision systems through a post-hoc, model-agnostic calibration technique that enforces a relaxed version of the equalized odds fairness criterion across one or more high-arity protected attributes without requiring retraining, access to model internals, or deterministic equality constraints. The invention comprises a formalized fairness measure known as Relaxed Equalized Odds, which permits the false positive rates and true positive rates of a classification model to vary across subgroups defined by protected attributes, provided that such variations remain within a statistically defined tolerance interval centered on the mean performance across all subgroups. This relaxation enables the system to accommodate natural differences in base rates and operational realities while still ensuring that no subgroup is systematically disadvantaged or overburdened by misclassification errors. The invention further introduces a one-shot fairness heuristic that operates exclusively on the output scores of an existing classification model and a set of protected attribute values, without requiring any knowledge of the underlying features, model architecture, or training data. The heuristic employs a grid-based search over a range of decision thresholds, computes aggregate performance metrics—such as false positive rate and true positive rate—for each subgroup at each threshold value, and iteratively prunes and selects optimal thresholds that satisfy the Relaxed Equalized Odds criterion. Crucially, the heuristic allows the end user to specify a selection metric that weights the importance of false positives versus false negatives, enabling customization based on business cost structures; for instance, a user may prioritize minimizing false positives in a customer service context where rejecting legitimate transactions is more damaging than accepting fraudulent ones, or vice versa in a high-risk fraud scenario where financial loss must be minimized at all costs. The invention further extends this capability to multiple protected attributes through two distinct calibration regimes: a strong regime that ensures fairness across all joint subpopulations formed by combinations of attribute values, and a weak regime that enforces fairness independently per attribute, thereby offering a scalable solution for systems with numerous protected attributes. To enhance computational efficiency, the invention incorporates an attribute pruning mechanism that identifies and removes statistically dependent protected attributes using a chi-square test of independence, thereby eliminating redundancy and reducing the dimensionality of the calibration space. The entire process is deterministic, convergent, and executable in a single pass, making it suitable for real-time deployment in high-throughput environments such as online payment processing, credit scoring, and automated risk assessment systems. The invention is entirely model-agnostic, meaning it can be applied to any classification model—whether logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting, or neural network—without modification to the model itself. It requires only the model’s predicted probabilities and the corresponding protected attribute values for each decision instance, making it compatible with legacy systems, third-party models, and black-box APIs. Furthermore, because the heuristic operates on aggregate statistics, it can be implemented in a privacy-preserving manner by adding controlled noise to the computed metrics, thereby satisfying differential privacy requirements without compromising fairness outcomes. The invention thus provides a practical, flexible, legally defensible, and operationally feasible solution to the longstanding challenge of algorithmic bias in real-world decision systems, enabling organizations to comply with anti-discrimination regulations while maintaining predictive accuracy, operational efficiency, and business integrity.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention disclosed herein is a comprehensive system and method for achieving algorithmic fairness in classification models used for high-stakes decision-making applications, particularly in contexts where protected attributes such as country, currency, gender, race, or age are present, and where the consequences of misclassification are significant and asymmetric. The core innovation lies in the introduction of a novel fairness criterion—Relaxed Equalized Odds—and a corresponding one-shot, model-agnostic calibration heuristic that enables the practical, scalable, and customizable enforcement of this criterion across multiple protected attributes without requiring model retraining, access to internal parameters, or deterministic equality constraints. This system operates as a post-processing layer that takes as input the output scores of an existing classification model and the values of one or more protected attributes associated with each decision instance, and outputs calibrated decision thresholds that ensure fairness according to the specified criteria. The entire process is deterministic, convergent, and computationally efficient, making it suitable for deployment in real-time, high-throughput environments such as online fraud detection, automated lending, credit scoring, and employment screening systems.

The first component of the invention is the formal definition of Relaxed Equalized Odds, which serves as the mathematical foundation for the fairness objective. Unlike the classical equalized odds criterion, which demands that the false positive rate and true positive rate be exactly equal across all subgroups defined by a protected attribute, the Relaxed Equalized Odds criterion permits these rates to vary within a statistically defined tolerance window around the mean value across all subgroups. Specifically, for a protected attribute D with K distinct values, the criterion requires that the false positive rate (FPR) and true positive rate (TPR) for each subgroup d_i ∈ D satisfy the condition that their respective values lie within μ ± σ, where μ is the mean FPR or TPR across all K subgroups, and σ is the standard deviation of these rates. This relaxation is not a weakening of fairness in principle, but rather a pragmatic adaptation to the realities of real-world data distributions. In many domains, such as fraud detection, the underlying base rates of the target event—whether a transaction is fraudulent, a loan applicant is creditworthy, or a recidivist will reoffend—differ systematically across subgroups due to structural, economic, or behavioral factors. Enforcing exact equality in such contexts would necessitate either tolerating excessive false negatives in low-risk groups or imposing excessive false positives in high-risk groups, thereby undermining the utility and legitimacy of the system. The Relaxed Equalized Odds criterion, by contrast, ensures that no subgroup is systematically subjected to rates that are outliers relative to the overall distribution, thereby preserving the spirit of equal opportunity and equal error burden while respecting the heterogeneity of real-world populations. This criterion is mathematically grounded in statistical robustness and is designed to be interpretable, auditable, and defensible under regulatory scrutiny. It does not require perfect parity, but rather a bounded, statistically significant similarity in error profiles across subgroups, which aligns more closely with the legal concept of disparate impact as interpreted in modern civil rights jurisprudence.

The second component of the invention is the fairness heuristic, a computationally efficient, iterative procedure that calibrates the decision thresholds of a classification model to satisfy the Relaxed Equalized Odds criterion. This heuristic operates in four distinct phases: choice of constraints, threshold grid initialization, performance computation, and iterative pruning and selection. The first phase, choice of constraints, empowers the end user to determine the nature of the fairness objective based on their operational priorities. The user may select to enforce similarity in false positive rates only, similarity in true positive rates only, or similarity in both. This selection is encoded as a selection metric, which is a weighted combination of precision and recall derived from the F-measure family. Specifically, if the user wishes to prioritize equal false positive rates, the selection metric is set to F_0.5, which places twice the weight on precision (the inverse of false positive rate) compared to recall (true positive rate). If the user wishes to prioritize equal true positive rates, the selection metric is set to F_2, which places twice the weight on recall compared to precision. If both are equally important, the selection metric defaults to F_1, which treats precision and recall symmetrically. This flexibility is a critical innovation, as it allows the system to be tailored to the specific cost structure of the application domain. In fraud detection, for example, a financial institution may be more concerned with minimizing false positives—rejections of legitimate transactions that lead to customer churn—than with maximizing true positives, and thus would select F_0.5. Conversely, a law enforcement agency using a risk assessment tool may prioritize minimizing false negatives—failing to flag high-risk individuals—over false positives, and would therefore select F_2. This user-driven customization is absent in all prior fairness techniques, which impose a fixed, one-size-fits-all fairness objective.

The second phase, threshold grid initialization, involves the construction of a discrete, linearly spaced grid of potential decision thresholds over the unit interval. This grid, denoted as G_threshold, typically consists of values ranging from 0.6 to 0.9 in increments of 0.01, though the range and granularity may be adjusted based on the distribution of the model’s output scores and the desired precision of calibration. The grid serves as the search space over which the heuristic explores possible threshold configurations. Each threshold value in the grid represents a potential cutoff point: any instance with a predicted probability above the threshold is classified as positive, and any instance below is classified as negative. By evaluating the model’s performance at each threshold, the heuristic can determine how changes in the decision boundary affect the false positive and true positive rates across subgroups.

The third phase, performance computation, involves iterating over each threshold value in the grid and, for each subgroup defined by the protected attribute(s), computing the corresponding false positive rate and true positive rate. This is done using only the model’s predicted probabilities and the ground truth labels, along with the protected attribute values for each instance. Importantly, this phase requires no access to the model’s internal parameters, training data, or feature space. The computation is entirely based on aggregate statistics derived from the model’s outputs, making the heuristic model-agnostic and compatible with any classification algorithm. The result of this phase is a performance table that maps each threshold value to the FPR and TPR for every subgroup. This table forms the basis for the subsequent pruning and selection steps.

The fourth and final phase, iterative pruning and selection, is where the heuristic enforces the Relaxed Equalized Odds criterion. Beginning with the full set of threshold-subgroup performance pairs, the heuristic identifies and removes—i.e., prunes—any threshold-subgroup combination where the FPR or TPR for that subgroup falls outside the μ ± σ tolerance window defined by the Relaxed Equalized Odds criterion. This pruning step is applied independently to each performance metric (FPR and TPR) based on the user’s selection metric. For example, if the user has selected F_0.5, then only the FPR values are subject to the pruning constraint; TPR values are ignored. The result is a pruned set of threshold-subgroup pairs that satisfy the fairness criterion. From this pruned set, the heuristic then selects, for each subgroup, the threshold that maximizes the user’s chosen selection metric. This ensures that, among all thresholds that satisfy the fairness constraint, the one that best balances the user’s operational priorities is chosen. The selected thresholds are then compiled into a mapping from each subgroup to its optimal decision threshold, forming the calibrated model F_select. The heuristic then evaluates whether this mapping satisfies the Relaxed Equalized Odds criterion across all subgroups. If it does, the process terminates. If it does not, the heuristic iteratively repeats the pruning and selection steps using the updated pruned set until convergence is achieved. This iterative refinement is guaranteed to converge because the set of possible thresholds is finite and discrete, and each iteration strictly reduces the set of candidate solutions while moving closer to the optimal configuration. The final output is a set of subgroup-specific thresholds that collectively ensure fairness according to the Relaxed Equalized Odds criterion while maximizing the user’s chosen performance metric.

The invention further extends this core heuristic to handle multiple protected attributes through two distinct calibration regimes: a strong regime and a weak regime. The strong regime enforces the Relaxed Equalized Odds criterion across all joint subpopulations formed by the Cartesian product of the attribute values. For example, if the protected attributes are country and currency, with 50 countries and 10 currencies, the strong regime considers all 500 possible combinations (e.g., US-USD, IN-INR, CA-CAD) and ensures that the FPR and TPR for each of these joint subpopulations lie within the μ ± σ tolerance window. This regime provides the highest level of fairness assurance but is computationally intensive and is recommended only when the number of protected attributes is small (e.g., fewer than 10) and the total number of joint subpopulations remains manageable. The weak regime, by contrast, enforces the Relaxed Equalized Odds criterion independently for each protected attribute, treating them as univariate. In the same example, the weak regime would ensure fairness across countries as a single attribute and across currencies as another, without considering their combinations. This reduces the computational complexity from exponential to linear in the number of attributes and is recommended for systems with many protected attributes or where joint dependencies are not of primary concern. The user may choose between these regimes based on their desired balance between fairness granularity and computational efficiency.

To further enhance scalability and eliminate redundancy, the invention incorporates an attribute pruning mechanism that identifies and removes statistically dependent protected attributes prior to calibration. This mechanism computes the chi-square statistic between every pair of protected attributes to determine whether their values are statistically independent. Attributes with a p-value below a predefined threshold (e.g., 0.01) are considered dependent and are pruned from the set of attributes to be calibrated. For example, if country and currency are highly correlated—such that users from the United States almost exclusively use USD, and users from India almost exclusively use INR—then calibrating thresholds for both attributes would be redundant, as the fairness constraint on one would implicitly constrain the other. Removing such dependencies reduces the dimensionality of the calibration space, minimizes computational overhead, and prevents conflicting or redundant threshold adjustments. The pruning is performed using a scalable implementation of the frequent pattern tree data structure, which efficiently identifies and aggregates subpopulations with sufficient sample size to yield reliable performance estimates. This is critical because, as a rule of thumb, reliable estimates of FPR and TPR require at least 100 instances per subgroup. The heuristic therefore prunes away any joint subpopulation (in the strong regime) or univariate subgroup (in the weak regime) that contains fewer than 100 instances, ensuring that all calibrated thresholds are based on statistically significant data.

The invention is entirely model-agnostic, meaning it can be applied to any classification model regardless of its architecture, training procedure, or internal representation. It requires only the predicted probability scores and the protected attribute values for each decision instance. This makes it compatible with legacy systems, third-party models, and black-box APIs that do not expose their internal parameters. The calibration layer can be implemented as a lightweight software module that sits between the model’s output and the final decision engine, requiring no changes to the model itself. This is a critical advantage in industrial settings where model retraining is prohibitively expensive, time-consuming, or prohibited by contractual or regulatory constraints.

Moreover, because the heuristic operates solely on aggregate statistics—FPR, TPR, and selection metrics—it can be implemented in a privacy-preserving manner by adding controlled noise to these statistics before performing the calibration. This enables compliance with differential privacy standards, ensuring that the fairness calibration process does not inadvertently leak information about individual instances or subgroups. The noise can be calibrated to preserve the statistical properties of the performance metrics while preventing inference attacks, thereby making the system suitable for deployment in highly regulated environments such as healthcare, finance, and public sector applications.

The invention has been validated across multiple real-world datasets, including proprietary commercial transaction data from a global payment processor, the UCI Income Prediction dataset, the ProPublica Recidivism dataset, and the Health Prediction dataset. In each case, the heuristic successfully achieved fairness according to the Relaxed Equalized Odds criterion while maintaining or improving predictive performance relative to baseline methods such as Equalized Odds and Calibrated Equalized Odds. In the fraud detection case study, the system reduced bias across 47 countries by ensuring that FPRs and TPRs for each country fell within two standard deviations of the global mean, without increasing overall fraud rates or decreasing legitimate transaction approval rates. In the income prediction scenario, the system ensured that false negative rates for male and female applicants were statistically similar, thereby preventing gender-based discrimination in salary estimation, while achieving better false positive rates than competing methods. In the criminal recidivism context, the system successfully equalized false positive rates between African American and Caucasian defendants, addressing a well-documented source of algorithmic bias, while maintaining comparable true negative rates. These results demonstrate that the invention is not only theoretically sound but also empirically effective across diverse domains and data distributions.

The invention further provides a mechanism for auditability and explainability. At the conclusion of the calibration process, the system generates a detailed report that includes the final thresholds selected for each subgroup, the mean and standard deviation of the performance metrics, the pruned attributes, the selection metric used, and the number of iterations required for convergence. This report can be presented to regulators, auditors, or affected individuals to demonstrate compliance with anti-discrimination laws and to justify the fairness outcomes. The deterministic nature of the heuristic ensures that the same inputs will always produce the same outputs, enabling reproducibility and verification.

In summary, the invention represents a transformative advancement in the field of algorithmic fairness. It moves beyond the limitations of existing techniques by introducing a statistically grounded, user-customizable, model-agnostic, and computationally efficient method for achieving fairness in real-world decision systems. It enables organizations to comply with legal and ethical obligations without sacrificing performance, scalability, or operational integrity. By relaxing the rigid constraints of traditional fairness criteria and providing a flexible, practical framework for calibration, the invention bridges the gap between theoretical fairness and real-world implementation, offering a robust, defensible, and deployable solution to one of the most pressing challenges in artificial intelligence today.
