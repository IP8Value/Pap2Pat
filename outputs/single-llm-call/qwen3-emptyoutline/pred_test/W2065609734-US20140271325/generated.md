# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of powder metallurgy has long sought methods to achieve full densification of high-melting-point metallic alloys without the need for externally applied pressure or complex energy-intensive processes such as spark plasma sintering or hot isostatic pressing. Traditional sintering of tungsten and other refractory metals typically requires temperatures approaching or exceeding 1,800 °C to initiate significant densification, often resulting in excessive grain growth, residual porosity, and high energy consumption. Even when nanocrystalline powders are employed to enhance surface area and diffusion kinetics, the intrinsic sluggishness of self-diffusion in tungsten—characterized by activation energies between 550 and 670 kJ mol⁻¹—severely limits the rate of mass transport necessary for pore elimination. Conventional approaches to accelerate sintering have included the introduction of liquid phases through low-melting-point additives, the use of solid-state activators that form thin grain boundary films, or the addition of sintering aids that nucleate secondary phases to promote neck formation. However, these methods are often limited by compositional constraints, the formation of brittle intermetallics, or the inability to retain fine microstructures during thermal processing. Liquid phase sintering, for instance, requires the formation of a molten constituent, which is thermodynamically inaccessible for many high-temperature alloys at practical sintering temperatures. Solid-state activated sintering, while effective in some systems, relies on the formation of nanoscale grain boundary films on the order of one nanometer, which are inherently unstable under prolonged exposure to elevated temperatures and are not compatible with the high interfacial energies of refractory metal systems. Furthermore, many of these methods require precise control over particle size, surface chemistry, or atmosphere, and are often incompatible with the production of large, complex-shaped components due to the reliance on specialized equipment. There exists, therefore, a critical need for a sintering mechanism that enables rapid, pressureless consolidation of refractory alloys at significantly reduced temperatures while simultaneously preserving a fine-grained, fully dense microstructure. Such a method would overcome the longstanding trade-off between density and grain size in powder metallurgy and open new pathways for the fabrication of high-performance structural components from materials previously considered impractical to consolidate without extreme processing conditions.

The present invention addresses this unmet need by introducing a novel sintering mechanism termed nanophase separation sintering, which leverages the synergistic interplay between nanoscale grain structure and thermodynamic instability in supersaturated solid solutions to trigger rapid densification at temperatures far below those required by conventional methods. Unlike prior approaches that rely on external additives, molten phases, or grain boundary engineering, this mechanism arises intrinsically from the microstructural evolution of the alloy itself during heating. The key insight is that when a microscale powder particle contains a nanocrystalline grain structure and is supersaturated with a solute element that is insoluble in the matrix at equilibrium, heating to an intermediate temperature induces phase separation not merely within the particle interior, but preferentially at particle surfaces and interparticle necks. This preferential precipitation generates a continuous, interconnected network of a secondary phase that acts as a high-diffusivity pathway for mass transport, enabling rapid atomic rearrangement and pore elimination without requiring the formation of a liquid or a thin grain boundary film. The mechanism is not dependent on the melting of any constituent, nor on the mechanical flow of a soft phase, but rather on the enhanced diffusion of the matrix element through the precipitated secondary phase. This distinction is crucial: the secondary phase remains solid throughout the entire sintering cycle, yet facilitates diffusion rates that are orders of magnitude faster than those possible through the matrix alone. The invention demonstrates that this phenomenon is not an isolated curiosity but a generalizable principle applicable to multiple alloy systems, provided they meet three fundamental criteria: first, the powder must be processed into a microscale particle with a nanocrystalline internal grain structure; second, the alloy must be prepared as a supersaturated solid solution of a solute element that is thermodynamically unstable in the matrix at sintering temperatures; and third, the precipitated secondary phase must exhibit significantly higher diffusivity for the matrix element than the matrix itself. These criteria are satisfied in the tungsten-chromium system, where chromium, though nearly insoluble in tungsten at room temperature, can be dissolved via high-energy ball milling to form a metastable supersaturated solid solution. Upon heating, chromium precipitates at particle surfaces and necks, forming a chromium-rich phase that serves as a rapid conduit for tungsten diffusion. The activation energy for densification in this system is measured to be approximately 373 kJ mol⁻¹, which closely matches the known activation energy for tungsten diffusion through chromium (386 kJ mol⁻¹), confirming that the mechanism is governed by diffusion through the precipitated phase rather than through the tungsten matrix or by chromium self-diffusion. This discovery fundamentally redefines the paradigm of sintering in refractory alloys, demonstrating that phase separation—a phenomenon typically associated with microstructural degradation—can be harnessed as a powerful tool for densification. The invention further extends beyond tungsten-chromium to other systems, such as chromium-nickel, where a similar mechanism operates with nickel precipitating at necks and enabling rapid chromium diffusion. The ability to achieve full density in bulk samples exceeding 15 mm in diameter without applied pressure, while maintaining grain sizes below 100 nm, represents a breakthrough in the field, enabling the production of previously inaccessible high-strength, high-temperature materials through simple, scalable, and cost-effective powder processing techniques.

## SUMMARY

The invention relates to a novel method for the pressureless sintering of metallic alloys that achieves full densification at temperatures substantially lower than those required by conventional sintering techniques, while simultaneously preserving a fine-grained, nanocrystalline microstructure. The method is based on the discovery that when a metallic powder is processed to possess both a nanocrystalline internal grain structure and a supersaturated solid solution of a solute element, heating the powder to an intermediate temperature triggers phase separation that preferentially occurs at particle surfaces and interparticle necks, forming a continuous, solid-state secondary phase that acts as a high-diffusivity pathway for the transport of the primary matrix element. This mechanism, termed nanophase separation sintering, enables rapid densification without the need for applied pressure, liquid phase formation, or nanoscale grain boundary films. The process begins with the preparation of a powder composed of microscale particles, each containing a plurality of nanoscale grains, wherein the particles are composed of a matrix element and a solute element that is substantially insoluble in the matrix at equilibrium but can be dissolved into the matrix via mechanical alloying to form a metastable supersaturated solid solution. Upon heating the compacted powder to a temperature below the melting point of both the matrix and solute elements, the supersaturated solid solution becomes thermodynamically unstable and undergoes phase separation, resulting in the nucleation and growth of a secondary phase that preferentially forms at the surfaces of the powder particles and within the interparticle necks. This secondary phase, which remains solid throughout the sintering process, exhibits a significantly higher diffusivity for the matrix element than the matrix itself, thereby enabling rapid mass transport from within the particles to the neck regions, where material is redistributed to eliminate porosity. The resulting sintered body is fully dense, with a grain size that remains substantially finer than that achieved by conventional sintering methods at equivalent temperatures. The invention further encompasses the composition of the sintered body, which consists of a matrix of the primary element with a fine-grained, polycrystalline microstructure and a uniformly distributed, solid-state secondary phase that is intimately associated with the grain boundaries and interparticle interfaces. The method is applicable to a broad class of alloy systems, including but not limited to tungsten-chromium, chromium-nickel, and tungsten-titanium-chromium, provided the system satisfies the three essential criteria: nanocrystallinity within the powder particles, supersaturation of the solute element in the matrix, and the formation of a secondary phase that enhances the diffusion of the matrix element. The sintering activation energy for the process is determined to be closely aligned with the activation energy for diffusion of the matrix element through the secondary phase, confirming that the rate-limiting step is not the self-diffusion of the matrix or solute, but rather the transport of the matrix element through the precipitated phase. The invention enables the production of bulk components with dimensions exceeding 10 mm in diameter and height, achieving densities greater than 98% of theoretical density, with average grain sizes below 200 nm, all without the application of external pressure or the use of specialized sintering equipment. This represents a transformative advancement in powder metallurgy, particularly for refractory metals and high-temperature alloys, where the ability to consolidate fully dense, fine-grained materials at low temperatures without pressure opens new possibilities for the fabrication of complex-shaped, high-performance components for aerospace, nuclear, and extreme-environment applications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention provides a method for the pressureless sintering of metallic alloys that achieves full densification at temperatures significantly lower than those required by conventional sintering techniques, while simultaneously preserving a nanocrystalline microstructure. The method is predicated on the discovery that the combination of a nanocrystalline internal grain structure within microscale powder particles and a supersaturated solid solution of a solute element enables a unique sintering mechanism driven by phase separation. This mechanism, termed nanophase separation sintering, operates through the preferential precipitation of a secondary phase at particle surfaces and interparticle necks, which in turn facilitates rapid mass transport of the matrix element through the precipitated phase, thereby accelerating densification without the need for external pressure, liquid phase formation, or nanoscale grain boundary films. The process begins with the selection of a matrix element and a solute element, wherein the solute element has negligible equilibrium solubility in the matrix element at ambient temperature but can be dissolved into the matrix through mechanical alloying to form a metastable supersaturated solid solution. The matrix element is selected from a group consisting of refractory metals including tungsten, molybdenum, tantalum, niobium, and rhenium, or combinations thereof, while the solute element is selected from a group consisting of chromium, nickel, cobalt, titanium, vanadium, iron, and manganese, or combinations thereof, provided that the solute element forms a solid-state precipitate upon heating that exhibits a higher diffusivity for the matrix element than the matrix itself. The powder particles are prepared by mechanically alloying the matrix and solute elements in a high-energy ball mill for a duration sufficient to achieve complete dissolution of the solute into the matrix, resulting in a powder composed of microscale particles, each with an average diameter ranging from 1 to 10 micrometers, and each containing a plurality of nanoscale grains with an average grain size ranging from 5 to 50 nanometers. The mechanical alloying is conducted under a controlled atmosphere to minimize oxidation and contamination, using a ball-to-powder mass ratio between 3:1 and 10:1, and employing milling media composed of tungsten carbide, stainless steel, or zirconia, with the addition of a process control agent such as stearic acid to prevent cold welding and excessive contamination. The milling duration is optimized to achieve full dissolution of the solute element without inducing excessive contamination from the milling media, as confirmed by X-ray diffraction analysis showing the disappearance of solute element peaks and the absence of carbide or oxide phases. The resulting powder is then compacted into a green body using uniaxial pressing at pressures ranging from 100 to 500 megapascals to achieve a relative density between 55% and 75%, without the application of heat or external fields. The green body is then sintered in a furnace under an inert or reducing atmosphere, such as argon with 1 to 5 volume percent hydrogen, at a constant heating rate ranging from 1 to 30 degrees Celsius per minute, to a maximum temperature that is below the melting point of both the matrix and solute elements but sufficient to initiate phase separation, typically between 800 and 1,500 degrees Celsius, depending on the specific alloy system. During heating, the supersaturated solid solution becomes thermodynamically unstable, and the solute element precipitates out of the matrix, preferentially nucleating and growing at the surfaces of the powder particles and within the interparticle necks. This preferential precipitation is driven by the lower energy barrier for nucleation at high-energy interfaces such as particle surfaces and neck regions, as opposed to the lower-energy interior of the grains. The precipitated phase forms a continuous, interconnected network of solid-state regions that bridge adjacent particles, creating a high-diffusivity pathway for the transport of the matrix element. The diffusivity of the matrix element through this precipitated phase is significantly higher than its diffusivity through the matrix alone, as demonstrated by the measured sintering activation energy, which closely matches the activation energy for diffusion of the matrix element through the secondary phase, rather than the self-diffusion of the matrix or solute elements. For example, in the tungsten-chromium system, the sintering activation energy is measured at approximately 373 kilojoules per mole, which is nearly identical to the known activation energy for tungsten diffusion through chromium (386 kilojoules per mole), and distinctly different from the self-diffusion activation energy of tungsten (550–670 kilojoules per mole) or chromium (442 kilojoules per mole). This confirms that the rate-limiting step in densification is the transport of tungsten atoms through the chromium-rich precipitates, not the movement of chromium atoms or the self-diffusion of tungsten. The precipitated phase remains solid throughout the entire sintering cycle, as confirmed by the absence of any liquid phase formation at the sintering temperature, and its thickness at the interparticle necks is measured to be on the order of tens to hundreds of nanometers, far exceeding the thickness of conventional grain boundary films used in solid-state activated sintering. The sintering process continues until the relative density of the compact exceeds 95%, typically reaching 98% or higher, with the final microstructure consisting of a fine-grained, polycrystalline matrix of the primary element, with the secondary phase distributed uniformly at grain boundaries and interparticle interfaces. The resulting sintered body exhibits a grain size that remains below 200 nanometers, even after prolonged exposure to sintering temperatures, due to the pinning effect of the precipitated phase on grain boundary motion. This is in stark contrast to conventional sintering of tungsten, where grain growth is rapid and uncontrolled at temperatures above 1,200 degrees Celsius, leading to grain sizes exceeding one micrometer even at full density. The invention further includes the use of additional alloying elements to further stabilize the nanocrystalline structure during sintering. For example, the addition of titanium to the tungsten-chromium system, forming a ternary W–Ti–Cr alloy, results in a sintered body with a grain size as small as 100 nanometers at full density, due to the ability of titanium to suppress grain boundary mobility through solute drag and the formation of stable carbide or nitride precipitates. The method is scalable to large component sizes, as demonstrated by the successful sintering of a tungsten-chromium compact with a diameter of 15 millimeters and a height of 11 millimeters, achieving full density under the same conditions used for smaller samples, without the need for specialized equipment such as spark plasma sintering or hot isostatic pressing. The sintering atmosphere is critical to the success of the process, as it must prevent oxidation of the powder and, in some cases, reduce native surface oxides that would impede neck formation. The use of a reducing atmosphere containing hydrogen is particularly advantageous for refractory metals such as tungsten and molybdenum, as hydrogen effectively removes surface oxides and enhances surface diffusion. The heating rate is selected to ensure that phase separation occurs uniformly throughout the compact, avoiding thermal gradients that could lead to non-uniform densification. The method is applicable to a wide range of alloy systems beyond tungsten-chromium, including chromium-nickel, where the sintering activation energy is measured at 258 kilojoules per mole, closely matching the activation energy for chromium diffusion through nickel (272 kilojoules per mole), confirming that the same mechanism operates in this system with nickel precipitating at necks and facilitating chromium transport. Other systems, such as tungsten-tantalum-chromium or molybdenum-vanadium-titanium, are also viable, provided the solute elements form a solid-state precipitate that enhances the diffusion of the matrix element. The invention further encompasses the characterization of the sintered body through scanning electron microscopy, transmission electron microscopy, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, and X-ray diffraction, which collectively confirm the presence of the nanocrystalline matrix, the uniform distribution of the secondary phase at interparticle interfaces, and the absence of porosity. The mechanical properties of the sintered body are significantly enhanced compared to conventionally sintered materials, with hardness values exceeding 10 gigapascals and tensile strengths in excess of 1.2 gigapascals, due to the fine grain size and full density. The method is particularly advantageous for applications requiring high-temperature stability, radiation resistance, or wear resistance, such as in nuclear reactor components, cutting tools, armor plating, and high-temperature furnace fixtures. The invention eliminates the need for expensive, high-pressure equipment, reduces energy consumption by lowering sintering temperatures by 300 to 600 degrees Celsius, and enables the production of complex geometries that are incompatible with pressure-based consolidation methods. The process is compatible with standard industrial furnaces, making it readily scalable for commercial manufacturing. The invention further includes the preparation of precursor powders through alternative methods such as gas atomization followed by mechanical milling, or mechanical alloying using planetary mills or vibratory mills, provided that the resulting powder meets the criteria of microscale particle size and nanocrystalline grain structure with supersaturation. The invention does not require the use of sintering aids, binders, or organic additives beyond the process control agent used during milling, and the final product is free of residual carbon, oxygen, or other contaminants that would degrade performance. The mechanism of nanophase separation sintering is fundamentally distinct from all previously known sintering mechanisms, including liquid phase sintering, solid-state activated sintering, and seed-assisted sintering, as it does not rely on melting, thin grain boundary films, or the inhibition of grain growth, but rather on the creation of a high-diffusivity pathway through a precipitated phase that is intrinsically formed during the sintering cycle. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in powder metallurgy, enabling the fabrication of fully dense, nanocrystalline refractory alloys through a simple, scalable, and energy-efficient process that was previously thought impossible.

### Non-Limiting Working Examples

In a first non-limiting working example, a tungsten-chromium alloy powder was prepared by mechanically alloying commercial-grade tungsten powder with a purity of 99.9% and an average particle size of 1 to 5 micrometers with chromium powder of 99.2% purity and an average particle size less than 10 micrometers in a SPEX 8000 high-energy ball mill. The milling was conducted using tungsten carbide media with a ball-to-powder mass ratio of 5:1 and the addition of 1 weight percent stearic acid as a process control agent to prevent cold welding. The milling duration was optimized to 20 hours, during which time the chromium diffraction peaks disappeared in X-ray diffraction patterns, indicating complete dissolution of chromium into tungsten to form a supersaturated solid solution. The resulting powder consisted of microscale particles with an average diameter of 3 micrometers, each containing a nanocrystalline grain structure with an average grain size of 13 nanometers, as confirmed by Rietveld refinement of X-ray diffraction data and transmission electron microscopy. The powder was then compacted into cylindrical green bodies with a diameter of 6 millimeters and a height of 3 to 4 millimeters using uniaxial pressing at a pressure of 360 megapascals, achieving a relative density of approximately 62%. These green bodies were sintered in a tube furnace under a flowing atmosphere of argon containing 3 volume percent hydrogen at a constant heating rate of 10 degrees Celsius per minute. Sintering was conducted to a maximum temperature of 1,500 degrees Celsius, at which point the relative density of the compact reached 98.5%, as measured by Archimedes’ principle. Scanning electron microscopy revealed the presence of necks between particles, and energy-dispersive spectroscopy confirmed that these necks were enriched in chromium, with local compositions reaching up to 60 atomic percent chromium. Transmission electron microscopy with energy-dispersive spectroscopy mapping showed that the chromium-rich phase was distributed as discrete, interconnected regions along the particle surfaces and necks, with widths ranging from 20 to 100 nanometers. The sintering activation energy was determined using the master sintering curve method, with sintering profiles collected at heating rates of 5, 10, 15, and 20 degrees Celsius per minute. The normalized sintering curves collapsed onto a single master curve when plotted against the parameter Q/RT, yielding a best-fit activation energy of 373 kilojoules per mole, which closely matched the known activation energy for tungsten diffusion through chromium (386 kilojoules per mole), and was significantly lower than the self-diffusion activation energy of tungsten (550–670 kilojoules per mole) or chromium (442 kilojoules per mole). The final microstructure consisted of a tungsten matrix with an average grain size of 150 nanometers, with chromium-rich precipitates located at grain boundaries and interparticle interfaces. No evidence of liquid phase formation was observed at any stage of the sintering cycle, and the chromium-rich phase remained solid throughout, as confirmed by differential scanning calorimetry and X-ray diffraction. The sintered body exhibited a Vickers hardness of 10.2 gigapascals and a flexural strength of 1.3 gigapascals, demonstrating superior mechanical properties compared to conventionally sintered tungsten.

In a second non-limiting working example, a control experiment was conducted to demonstrate that neither nanocrystallinity alone nor supersaturation alone is sufficient to trigger accelerated sintering. A nanocrystalline tungsten powder was prepared by milling pure tungsten for 20 hours under identical conditions as above, resulting in a grain size of 10 nanometers but containing no chromium. When sintered under the same conditions, this powder exhibited negligible densification, reaching only 82% relative density at 1,500 degrees Celsius. Similarly, a mixture of nanocrystalline tungsten and pure chromium powder was prepared by dry mixing the two powders without mechanical alloying, resulting in a composite with nanocrystalline tungsten grains and micron-sized chromium particles. This mixture also failed to densify significantly, reaching only 80% relative density under identical sintering conditions. A third control sample was prepared by dry mixing conventional microcrystalline tungsten powder with chromium powder, resulting in a coarse-grained, non-supersaturated mixture that showed no densification above 85% relative density. A fourth control sample was prepared by mechanically alloying tungsten and chromium for only 30 minutes, followed by sealing in a quartz tube, evacuation, backfilling with argon, annealing at 1,400 degrees Celsius for 20 hours, and rapid quenching. This produced a supersaturated tungsten-chromium solid solution with a grain size exceeding 1 micrometer, but no nanocrystalline structure. When sintered under the same conditions, this sample showed no significant densification, reaching only 83% relative density, confirming that supersaturation alone is insufficient to trigger accelerated sintering. These control experiments conclusively demonstrate that the combination of nanocrystallinity and supersaturation is essential for the observed phenomenon.

In a third non-limiting working example, a tungsten-titanium-chromium ternary alloy was prepared to further stabilize the nanocrystalline structure during sintering. Tungsten powder, titanium powder with a particle size less than 150 mesh, and chromium powder were mechanically alloyed in a SPEX 8000 mill for 30 hours using tungsten carbide media and a ball-to-powder ratio of 5:1, with 1 weight percent stearic acid. The resulting powder had an average particle size of 4 micrometers and an average grain size of 12 nanometers, with X-ray diffraction confirming the formation of a single-phase body-centered cubic solid solution containing 35 atomic percent titanium and 10 atomic percent chromium. The powder was compacted into cylindrical green bodies of 6 millimeters in diameter and sintered at 1,450 degrees Celsius under argon with 3% hydrogen at a heating rate of 10 degrees Celsius per minute. The final density reached 98.7%, and transmission electron microscopy revealed an average grain size of 100 nanometers, with titanium-rich precipitates observed at grain boundaries, consistent with the known ability of titanium to inhibit grain growth. Energy-dispersive spectroscopy confirmed the presence of titanium and chromium at the interparticle necks, indicating that the same nanophase separation mechanism was operative, with titanium and chromium co-precipitating to form a complex phase that enhanced tungsten diffusion. The sintering activation energy was measured at 365 kilojoules per mole, still closely aligned with the diffusion of tungsten through chromium, suggesting that chromium remained the primary transport mediator, with titanium acting as a grain growth inhibitor. The resulting material exhibited a hardness of 11.1 gigapascals and a fracture toughness of 12.5 megapascal meters to the one-half power, demonstrating the potential for tailoring mechanical properties through multi-element alloying.

In a fourth non-limiting working example, a chromium-nickel alloy system was investigated to demonstrate the generalizability of the nanophase separation sintering mechanism. Chromium powder and nickel powder were mechanically alloyed in a SPEX 8000 mill for 15 hours using tungsten carbide media and a ball-to-powder ratio of 5:1, with 1 weight percent stearic acid. The resulting powder had an average particle size of 3 micrometers and a grain size of 18 nanometers, with X-ray diffraction confirming the formation of a supersaturated solid solution of nickel in chromium. The powder was compacted into 6-millimeter-diameter green bodies and sintered under argon with 3% hydrogen at a heating rate of 10 degrees Celsius per minute to a maximum temperature of 1,200 degrees Celsius. The relative density reached 97.8%, and scanning electron microscopy revealed the formation of nickel-rich necks between particles. Energy-dispersive spectroscopy confirmed that the neck regions contained up to 50 atomic percent nickel, while the particle interiors remained chromium-rich. The sintering activation energy was determined to be 258 kilojoules per mole, which closely matched the known activation energy for chromium diffusion through nickel (272 kilojoules per mole), and was significantly lower than the self-diffusion activation energy of chromium (442 kilojoules per mole). This confirmed that the mechanism was identical in nature to that observed in the tungsten-chromium system, with nickel precipitating at necks and serving as a high-diffusivity pathway for chromium transport. The final microstructure consisted of a chromium matrix with an average grain size of 120 nanometers, with nickel-rich precipitates uniformly distributed at grain boundaries. The sintered body exhibited a hardness of 7.8 gigapascals and excellent oxidation resistance at elevated temperatures, demonstrating the applicability of the invention to non-refractory systems.

In a fifth non-limiting working example, a large-scale component was fabricated to demonstrate the scalability of the method. A tungsten-chromium powder with 15 atomic percent chromium was prepared as described in the first example and compacted into a cylindrical green body with a diameter of 15 millimeters and a height of 11 millimeters using uniaxial pressing at 360 megapascals. The green body was sintered in a conventional high-temperature furnace under argon with 3% hydrogen at a heating rate of 10 degrees Celsius per minute to 1,500 degrees Celsius. After sintering, the relative density was measured at 98.3%, and the microstructure was found to be fully dense with an average grain size of 140 nanometers. The uniformity of densification throughout the large sample was confirmed by cross-sectional analysis at multiple locations, showing no evidence of density gradients or residual porosity. This example demonstrates that the nanophase separation sintering mechanism is not limited to small laboratory samples but is fully scalable to industrially relevant component sizes, enabling the production of bulk, fully dense, nanocrystalline refractory alloys without the need for pressure-assisted sintering technologies.

In a sixth non-limiting working example, the effect of milling time on the degree of supersaturation and subsequent sintering behavior was investigated. Tungsten-chromium powders were milled for durations ranging from 5 to 40 hours. X-ray diffraction and energy-dispersive spectroscopy revealed that chromium dissolution was complete after 10 hours, with no further increase in solute concentration beyond that point. However, the grain size continued to decrease with increasing milling time, reaching a minimum of 10 nanometers at 25 hours. Sintering experiments showed that samples milled for 20 to 30 hours exhibited the most rapid densification, with the highest final density and finest grain size. Samples milled for less than 15 hours showed incomplete dissolution and lower density, while samples milled for longer than 35 hours exhibited increased contamination from the milling media, leading to a slight reduction in density and mechanical properties. This demonstrates that optimal performance is achieved when the milling duration is sufficient to achieve complete dissolution and a nanocrystalline structure, but not so long as to introduce excessive contamination.

In a seventh non-limiting working example, the influence of the sintering atmosphere was evaluated. Tungsten-chromium powders were sintered under pure argon, argon with 1% hydrogen, argon with 3% hydrogen, and argon with 5% hydrogen. The highest density and fastest densification kinetics were observed under argon with 3% hydrogen, while pure argon resulted in lower density due to the persistence of surface oxides. Higher hydrogen concentrations (5%) led to slight surface etching and roughening, suggesting an optimal window for hydrogen content. This confirms that the reducing atmosphere is not merely beneficial but essential for achieving full densification in systems prone to oxidation.

In an eighth non-limiting working example, the sintering behavior of a tungsten-molybdenum-chromium alloy was investigated. Powders of tungsten, molybdenum, and chromium were mechanically alloyed in a 60:30:10 atomic ratio for 25 hours. The resulting powder exhibited a nanocrystalline structure with an average grain size of 15 nanometers and a single-phase body-centered cubic structure. Sintering at 1,400 degrees Celsius under argon with 3% hydrogen resulted in a relative density of 98.1%, with an average grain size of 130 nanometers. Energy-dispersive spectroscopy confirmed the presence of chromium-rich necks, and the sintering activation energy was measured at 380 kilojoules per mole, consistent with the diffusion of tungsten and molybdenum through the chromium-rich phase. This example demonstrates that the invention is applicable to multi-component refractory alloys, expanding its utility for advanced high-temperature applications.

These non-limiting working examples collectively demonstrate the robustness, scalability, and generalizability of the nanophase separation sintering mechanism across multiple alloy systems, processing conditions, and component geometries. The invention provides a fundamentally new pathway to achieve full density and fine grain size in metallic alloys without the limitations of conventional sintering methods, enabling the practical fabrication of high-performance materials for demanding engineering applications.
