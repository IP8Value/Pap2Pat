# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The detection of fetal genetic abnormalities during pregnancy has long been a cornerstone of prenatal care, with the goal of identifying life-threatening or severely disabling conditions early enough to enable informed decision-making and timely clinical intervention. For decades, invasive procedures such as amniocentesis and chorionic villus sampling have served as the gold standard for diagnosing chromosomal aneuploidies and monogenic disorders, despite their associated risks of miscarriage and maternal morbidity. The advent of non-invasive prenatal screening (NIPS) marked a transformative shift in this landscape, leveraging the presence of cell-free fetal DNA (cfDNA) in maternal plasma to infer fetal genetic status without direct access to fetal tissues. This breakthrough, grounded in the observation that placental trophoblast cells release fragmented DNA into the maternal bloodstream, has enabled the widespread adoption of screening for common aneuploidies such as trisomy 21, trisomy 18, and trisomy 13. The clinical utility of NIPS has been further extended to include chromosomal microdeletion and microduplication syndromes, such as DiGeorge syndrome and 1p36 deletion, as well as select dominant monogenic disorders like achondroplasia and Noonan syndrome. However, despite these advances, the current generation of NIPS technologies remains fundamentally limited in their ability to simultaneously and reliably detect multiple classes of genetic abnormalities—chromosomal, subchromosomal, and single-gene variants—within a single assay. These limitations arise not merely from technical constraints but from inherent biological and analytical challenges that have not been adequately addressed by existing methodologies.

One of the most persistent challenges in NIPS is the extremely low fraction of fetal DNA present in maternal plasma, particularly during the first trimester when screening is most clinically desirable. On average, fetal cfDNA constitutes only approximately ten percent of the total cell-free DNA in maternal circulation, with concentrations often falling below five percent in early gestation. This low fetal fraction introduces a formidable signal-to-noise problem, wherein the subtle allelic imbalances or copy number deviations attributable to fetal genetic abnormalities must be discerned against a background dominated by maternal DNA. Conventional NIPS approaches have attempted to overcome this challenge by either increasing the depth of sequencing coverage across the entire genome or by targeting a large number of single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) loci to detect deviations in allelic ratios. However, these strategies are inherently inefficient, requiring the interrogation of tens of thousands of loci to achieve acceptable sensitivity and specificity. Moreover, they are vulnerable to a host of confounding factors that introduce false signals or obscure true fetal variants. Maternal copy number variations (CNVs), which are relatively common in the general population, can mimic fetal aneuploidies by altering read depth profiles or allelic fractions in a manner indistinguishable from fetal chromosomal gains or losses. Similarly, regions of absence of heterozygosity (AOH) in the maternal genome eliminate the ability to detect fetal heterozygous SNPs, thereby rendering entire chromosomal segments uninformative for SNP-based analyses. Multiple gestations, including dizygotic twins and vanishing twin scenarios, further complicate interpretation by introducing additional sources of genetic material with distinct allelic compositions, often leading to ambiguous or misleading results. These confounders are not merely statistical noise; they represent biological realities that, if unaccounted for, can lead to catastrophic clinical consequences, including unnecessary invasive diagnostic procedures or, worse, the failure to detect a life-threatening fetal condition.

Another critical limitation of current NIPS platforms lies in their inability to distinguish between maternal and fetal genotypes with sufficient precision to enable accurate variant calling. Most existing methods rely on either read depth (RD) analysis, which measures the relative abundance of sequencing reads mapping to specific chromosomal regions, or allelic fraction (AF) analysis, which quantifies the proportion of sequencing reads carrying a variant allele at heterozygous SNP loci. While RD-based methods are robust to maternal genotype and can detect large-scale copy number changes, they are blind to the parental origin of the aberration and cannot resolve cases involving maternal CNVs or AOH. Conversely, AF-based methods are sensitive to fetal genotype and can identify the parental origin of aneuploidy, but they are rendered ineffective in regions where the mother is homozygous or where allelic hybridization bias distorts the measured allelic ratios. This hybridization bias, an artifact inherent to liquid-phase capture methods, arises because oligonucleotide probes designed to perfectly complement the reference allele bind more efficiently than those containing a mismatched variant allele, thereby systematically under-representing the alternative allele in sequencing data. This bias is particularly detrimental in NIPS, where the fetal signal is already attenuated by low fetal fraction, and even minor deviations in allelic representation can lead to false-negative or false-positive results. Furthermore, existing methods do not exploit the distinctive biophysical properties of fetal cfDNA, such as its consistently shorter fragment length compared to maternal cfDNA, which has been consistently observed across multiple studies but remains underutilized as a diagnostic cue. The failure to integrate this information into analytical pipelines represents a missed opportunity to enhance specificity and reduce false positives.

Beyond chromosomal and subchromosomal abnormalities, the detection of monogenic disorders presents an even greater analytical challenge. Unlike aneuploidies, which involve alterations in the dosage of hundreds or thousands of genes across entire chromosomes, monogenic variants are confined to single nucleotide positions or small indels within individual genes. Detecting such variants requires not only high sequencing depth but also exquisite precision in distinguishing true fetal mutations from sequencing errors, maternal contamination, or artifacts introduced during library preparation. Current approaches to monogenic NIPS rely on statistical models that estimate the likelihood of a variant being of fetal origin based on its allelic frequency and the fetal fraction, but these models are highly susceptible to false positives arising from low-level maternal mosaicism, PCR amplification bias, or sequencing noise. Moreover, the absence of a reliable method to independently verify the fetal origin of a variant—beyond its allelic abundance—leaves clinicians with uncertain results that are difficult to interpret and even more difficult to act upon. The clinical utility of monogenic NIPS is therefore severely constrained by its poor positive predictive value, which in many cases remains below 50 percent, rendering it unsuitable for population-wide screening.

The biological origins of aneuploidy, particularly those arising from maternal meiotic nondisjunction, remain incompletely understood, largely due to the inaccessibility of human oocytes and the ethical and practical limitations of studying meiosis directly in human tissue. While it is well established that advanced maternal age is a major risk factor for trisomy 21 and other common aneuploidies, and that aberrant meiotic recombination patterns—such as reduced or displaced crossovers—are strongly associated with these events, the precise molecular mechanisms linking recombination errors to chromosome mis-segregation remain elusive. The inability to detect meiotic recombinants in fetal cfDNA has prevented researchers from validating hypotheses derived from invasive prenatal samples and from studying recombination patterns at a population scale. This gap in knowledge not only impedes our understanding of human reproductive biology but also limits the development of more sophisticated analytical models that could leverage recombination signatures as additional diagnostic evidence for aneuploidy. In essence, current NIPS technologies operate with a narrow set of inputs—read depth and allelic fraction—while ignoring a wealth of potentially diagnostic information embedded in the structure, length, and linkage patterns of fetal cfDNA. The consequence is a screening paradigm that, despite its widespread adoption, remains fundamentally reactive rather than predictive, and limited in its scope to a narrow subset of conditions.

There is therefore an urgent and unmet need for a comprehensive, integrated, and biologically informed NIPS platform capable of simultaneously detecting chromosomal aneuploidies, microdeletion/microduplication syndromes, and dominant monogenic disorders with high sensitivity and specificity, while simultaneously resolving maternal confounders such as CNVs, AOH, and multiple gestations. Such a platform must transcend the limitations of conventional methods by incorporating multiple, orthogonal dimensions of genomic information—including allelic hybridization bias correction, multidimensional read depth and allelic fraction analysis, fragment length profiling, and meiotic recombination detection—into a unified analytical framework. It must be capable of genetically deconvoluting the maternal-fetal cfDNA admixture with sufficient precision to distinguish fetal variants from maternal background noise, even at fetal fractions below five percent. It must also be able to identify the parental and meiotic origin of aneuploidies, characterize recombination breakpoints associated with nondisjunction, and validate the fetal origin of monogenic variants through independent biophysical criteria. Only by addressing these challenges in an integrated and systematic manner can the next generation of NIPS fulfill its promise as a truly comprehensive, non-invasive, and clinically actionable tool for prenatal genetic screening.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention disclosed herein comprises a novel, integrated system for non-invasive prenatal screening that enables the concurrent detection of chromosomal aneuploidies, microdeletion and microduplication syndromes, and dominant monogenic disorders through the comprehensive analysis of cell-free DNA in maternal plasma. This system is distinguished by its multi-dimensional analytical framework, which synergistically combines a proprietary probe design strategy, advanced computational algorithms, and biophysical filtering techniques to overcome the fundamental limitations of existing NIPS platforms. At its core is a method termed coordinative allele-aware target enrichment sequencing (COATE-seq), which fundamentally redefines the process of hybridization-based target enrichment by eliminating allelic bias through the deliberate design of probes that do not preferentially bind to either the reference or alternative allele at a given SNP locus. Unlike conventional probes, which are engineered to maximize complementarity with the reference genome and thereby introduce systematic under-representation of variant alleles, COATE probes are intentionally designed to have near-identical hybridization equilibrium constants for both alleles. This is achieved by selecting, at each target SNP position, a nucleotide that minimizes the difference in melting temperature between the probe-target duplexes formed with the reference and alternative alleles, as calculated using the nearest neighbor thermodynamic model. The resulting probes exhibit minimal discrimination between alleles, thereby preserving the true allelic ratio in the sequencing data and enabling accurate quantification of fetal allelic fractions even at low fetal fractions. This innovation alone reduces the number of required SNP loci by more than 80 percent compared to conventional SNP-based NIPS, significantly lowering sequencing costs and increasing assay throughput without sacrificing sensitivity.

The COATE-seq method is integrated into a broader analytical pipeline that concurrently interrogates four distinct dimensions of cfDNA biology: read depth, allelic fraction, fragment length, and SNP linkage patterns. Each of these dimensions provides independent, orthogonal information that, when combined, allows for the precise genetic deconvolution of maternal and fetal contributions to the cfDNA mixture. Read depth analysis is employed to detect large-scale copy number variations by comparing the normalized sequencing coverage of target chromosomes against a panel of reference chromosomes. To mitigate technical artifacts and batch effects, the system employs a principal component analysis-based normalization strategy that removes systematic biases unrelated to copy number, followed by a Hampel filter to eliminate outlier loci with aberrant read depth. Allelic fraction analysis, enabled by the suppression of hybridization bias through COATE-seq, is used to detect deviations from the expected 50:50 allelic ratio at heterozygous maternal loci, which indicate the presence of fetal aneuploidy. Crucially, the system distinguishes between maternal and fetal contributions by calculating fetal fraction independently using both SNP-based and Y-chromosome-based methods, and by evaluating the consistency of allelic fraction patterns across multiple loci. This dual-approach not only enhances the accuracy of fetal fraction estimation but also enables the detection of complex scenarios such as dizygotic twin pregnancies and maternal mosaic CNVs, which would otherwise confound single-dimension analyses.

A critical innovation of this system is its ability to detect and characterize meiotic recombination events associated with aneuploidy, a capability previously unattainable in non-invasive settings. By analyzing the spatial patterns of fetal homozygosity and heterozygosity at maternal heterozygous SNP loci, the system identifies transitions between regions consistent with maternal meiosis I and meiosis II nondisjunction, which are indicative of crossover events. The presence of extended stretches of fetal homozygosity at loci where the mother is heterozygous is diagnostic of maternal meiosis II nondisjunction, while the absence of such stretches, coupled with the presence of alternating heterozygous and homozygous patterns, is diagnostic of meiosis I nondisjunction with recombination. The system employs a probabilistic model to compute the likelihood of different meiotic error states (maternal meiosis I, maternal meiosis II, paternal meiosis I, paternal meiosis II) at each SNP locus, and then aggregates these likelihoods across the entire chromosome to determine the most probable aneuploidy state and its associated recombination breakpoints. This allows for the first time the non-invasive characterization of the parental origin and meiotic mechanism underlying aneuploidy, providing not only diagnostic information but also insights into the biological origins of chromosomal disorders.

For the detection of monogenic variants, the system implements a two-stage filtering strategy that leverages both allelic abundance and fragment length characteristics. The first stage, termed Allele Count Distribution (ACD) filtering, employs a beta-binomial statistical model to calculate the probability that an observed variant allele count is consistent with a fetal origin, given the total sequencing depth, the fetal fraction, and the expected distribution of allele counts under a null hypothesis of maternal origin. Variants that fall within a defined likelihood threshold are retained for further analysis. The second stage, Fetal-Maternal Insert-size Distribution (FMID) filtering, exploits the well-documented observation that fetal cfDNA fragments are, on average, approximately ten base pairs shorter than maternal fragments. For each candidate variant, the system performs a nearest-neighbor insert-size calibration, in which each read carrying the variant allele is matched with the closest read carrying the reference allele in terms of fragment length. The reference allele reads with the most similar lengths are then excluded, under the assumption that they are likely of maternal origin. The remaining reference allele reads are presumed to be maternal, while the variant allele reads are presumed to be fetal. The system then performs a statistical comparison of the fragment length distributions of the remaining variant and reference allele reads using multiple non-parametric tests, including the Mann-Whitney U-test, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and the Kruskal-Wallis test. A variant is considered confirmed only if the variant allele fragments are significantly shorter than the reference allele fragments and if the minimum p-value across all tests is below a stringent threshold. This dual-filtering approach, which requires both statistical evidence of fetal origin and biophysical evidence of fetal fragment length, achieves a specificity exceeding 99.9 percent, effectively eliminating the vast majority of false positives that plague conventional monogenic NIPS methods.

The system further incorporates a comprehensive quality control framework that identifies and excludes samples with confounding factors prior to variant calling. Maternal copy number variations are detected by simultaneously analyzing both read depth and allelic fraction profiles; a region exhibiting elevated read depth coupled with allelic fractions deviating from 0.5 is flagged as a potential maternal duplication, while a region with reduced read depth and allelic fractions near 0.75 or 0.25 is flagged as a potential maternal deletion. Absence of heterozygosity is identified by detecting long contiguous stretches of homozygous SNPs exceeding a defined threshold, typically seventy-five consecutive loci, which are indicative of maternal uniparental disomy or identity-by-descent. Dizygotic twin pregnancies are detected by observing an unusually high number of fetal SNPs and increased variability in allelic fractions across the genome, consistent with the presence of two genetically distinct fetuses. In all cases, samples flagged by these quality control filters are excluded from further analysis, ensuring that only high-confidence results are reported to clinicians. The entire analytical pipeline is implemented on a high-performance computing infrastructure capable of processing hundreds of samples simultaneously, with secondary analysis—including alignment, deduplication, variant calling, and annotation—completed in less than six hours per batch. The system is designed to be scalable, modular, and adaptable, allowing for the addition of new target regions, genes, or analytical modules without requiring fundamental changes to the core methodology.

The invention also encompasses a novel probe design algorithm that enables the efficient and cost-effective targeting of genomic regions associated with a broad spectrum of genetic disorders. Probes are designed to cover the entire mappable regions of chromosomes 1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 13, 15, 18, 21, 22, X, and Y, as well as critical subregions associated with microdeletion syndromes such as 1p36 deletion, 2q33.1 deletion, Wolf-Hirschhorn syndrome, Cri du Chat syndrome, Langer-Giedion syndrome, Jacobsen syndrome, Prader-Willi/Angelman syndrome, and DiGeorge syndrome. In addition, probes are designed to target the coding exons and splice sites of genes associated with dominant monogenic disorders, including FGFR3, FGFR2, COL1A1, COL1A2, COL2A1, PTPN11, SOS1, RAF1, and RIT1. Each probe is optimized for hybridization efficiency by ensuring that the GC content falls within a range of 30 to 70 percent and that the probe length is sufficient to ensure specificity but short enough to allow efficient capture and amplification. The probe design algorithm is fully automated and can be updated as new disease-associated loci are identified, enabling the system to evolve in response to advances in human genetics and clinical needs. The probes are synthesized as a single, pooled library and used in a hybridization-based target enrichment protocol that is compatible with standard next-generation sequencing workflows, ensuring broad compatibility with existing laboratory infrastructure.

The system further includes a robust data interpretation and reporting module that integrates clinical context, analytical confidence scores, and population frequency data to generate clinically actionable reports. For chromosomal aneuploidies, the system reports not only the presence or absence of trisomy but also the likely parental origin and meiotic mechanism, along with a confidence score derived from the aggregate likelihood of the observed data under different aneuploidy models. For monogenic variants, the system reports the specific nucleotide change, its predicted functional impact, its classification according to ACMG guidelines, and the likelihood that it is of fetal origin, based on the combined evidence from ACD and FMID filtering. The system also includes built-in decision support tools that assist clinicians in interpreting results in the context of ultrasound findings, maternal age, and family history, and that provide guidance on follow-up testing and genetic counseling. The entire system is designed to be validated, reproducible, and compliant with regulatory standards for clinical diagnostic assays, with built-in controls for intra-assay, inter-assay, and inter-operator variability. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in non-invasive prenatal screening, moving from a fragmented, condition-specific approach to a unified, comprehensive, and biologically grounded platform capable of detecting the full spectrum of genetic disorders in a single, reliable, and scalable test.

### Computer System

The computer system that implements the analytical pipeline of this invention is a high-performance computing infrastructure specifically architected to handle the massive data throughput, complex algorithmic computations, and stringent data integrity requirements of non-invasive prenatal screening at scale. The system is composed of a distributed architecture comprising a front-end sample management interface, a high-throughput sequencing data ingestion layer, a parallelized computational processing engine, and a secure, auditable reporting and storage module. All components are integrated through a standardized application programming interface and operate within a secure, encrypted environment compliant with HIPAA, GDPR, and other relevant data protection regulations. The system is designed to process batches of up to one hundred and forty-four samples simultaneously, with each sample generating approximately ten gigabytes of raw sequencing data in paired-end 100-base format. The ingestion layer is responsible for receiving FASTQ files from the sequencing platform, performing initial quality control checks, demultiplexing samples based on unique molecular identifiers, and assigning metadata such as gestational age, maternal age, and clinical indication to each sample. This layer also performs error correction on the unique molecular identifiers to mitigate the effects of PCR amplification artifacts and sequencing errors, ensuring that each read can be accurately traced to its original DNA molecule.

The computational processing engine is the core of the system and is implemented on a dual-processor server equipped with two AMD EPYC 7713 processors, each featuring 64 cores and 128 threads, for a total of 128 physical cores and 256 logical threads. The server is provisioned with 768 gigabytes of DDR4 ECC memory and utilizes high-speed NVMe solid-state drives for temporary data storage and caching, ensuring that I/O bottlenecks do not impede the processing of large datasets. The engine executes a series of modular, parallelized software modules that are optimized for multi-threaded execution and memory efficiency. The first module performs alignment of sequencing reads to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using a modified version of the BWA-MEM algorithm, which has been tuned for the specific characteristics of cfDNA, including its short fragment length and high rate of fragmentation. Following alignment, a unique molecular identifier (UMI)-based deduplication module removes PCR duplicates by grouping reads that originate from the same DNA molecule based on their UMI sequence and alignment coordinates. This step is critical for eliminating amplification bias and ensuring that read depth measurements accurately reflect the true abundance of each genomic region.

The next module performs the COATE-seq probe-based target enrichment analysis, wherein the system identifies all reads that map within the predefined probe regions and calculates the number of reads supporting each allele at every SNP locus. This module employs a specialized algorithm that corrects for background sequencing noise and estimates the effective sequencing depth at each locus, accounting for variations in probe hybridization efficiency and local GC content. The allelic fraction at each SNP is then calculated as the proportion of reads supporting the alternative allele, and this value is used as input for subsequent statistical analyses. Concurrently, a read depth normalization module calculates the median read depth across all target chromosomes and reference chromosomes, and applies a principal component analysis-based correction to remove systematic biases introduced during library preparation and sequencing. The corrected read depth values are then used to compute Z-scores for each chromosome, which are compared against a reference distribution derived from a large cohort of euploid pregnancies to identify potential aneuploidies.

The meiotic recombination detection module operates on the allelic fraction data to identify transitions between regions consistent with maternal meiosis I and meiosis II nondisjunction. This module employs a hidden Markov model that assigns a probability to each SNP locus of belonging to one of four states: maternal meiosis I, maternal meiosis II, paternal meiosis I, or paternal meiosis II. The transition probabilities between states are informed by empirical data on the frequency and distribution of crossover events in human meiosis, and the emission probabilities are derived from the observed allelic fractions and fetal fraction estimates. The model outputs the most probable sequence of meiotic states along each chromosome, along with the location of crossover breakpoints, which are defined as the positions where the most likely state changes from one meiotic mechanism to another. These breakpoints are then validated using a statistical test that compares the likelihood of the observed data under a model with one or two breakpoints versus a model with no breakpoints.

The monogenic variant detection module operates independently of the aneuploidy detection modules and is responsible for identifying single nucleotide variants and small insertions or deletions within the targeted genes. This module first applies a repeat region filter to exclude variants located within repetitive elements annotated by RepeatMasker, which are prone to misalignment and false positives. It then applies the Allele Count Distribution filter, which uses a beta-binomial model to calculate the probability that the observed number of variant allele reads is consistent with a fetal origin, given the total read depth, the fetal fraction, and the expected distribution of allele counts under a null hypothesis of maternal origin. Variants that fall within a likelihood threshold are retained for further analysis. The module then applies the Fetal-Maternal Insert-size Distribution filter, which performs a nearest-neighbor insert-size calibration for each variant read. For each read carrying a variant allele, the system identifies the closest read carrying a reference allele in terms of fragment length and excludes it from the reference allele pool. This process is repeated iteratively until all variant reads have been matched, leaving behind a pool of reference allele reads that are presumed to be of maternal origin. The system then performs a statistical comparison of the fragment length distributions of the remaining variant and reference allele reads using four non-parametric tests: the Mann-Whitney U-test, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, the Kruskal-Wallis H-test, and the Welch’s t-test. A variant is considered confirmed only if the minimum p-value across all four tests is less than 0.001 and if the median fragment length of the variant allele reads is significantly shorter than that of the reference allele reads.

The final module is the quality control and reporting engine, which integrates all analytical results into a unified clinical report. This module first evaluates the sample for the presence of maternal confounders, including copy number variations, absence of heterozygosity, and multiple gestations, using the criteria described in the previous section. Samples that fail any of these quality control checks are flagged as inconclusive and excluded from further analysis. For samples that pass quality control, the module computes an overall confidence score for each detected abnormality, based on the aggregate likelihood of the observed data under the most probable genetic model. This score is expressed as a probability ranging from 0 to 1, with values above 0.99 considered high confidence. The module then generates a structured report that includes the detected abnormality, the specific genomic coordinates, the predicted functional consequence, the parental origin and meiotic mechanism (where applicable), the fetal fraction, the confidence score, and a recommendation for confirmatory testing. The report is formatted in accordance with standards established by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and is delivered through a secure web portal accessible to authorized clinicians. All data is stored in a secure, encrypted database with full audit trails, and the system is designed to support retrospective re-analysis as new clinical or scientific evidence emerges.

### Other Embodiments

While the primary embodiment of this invention is a comprehensive non-invasive prenatal screening platform for the detection of chromosomal aneuploidies, microdeletion and microduplication syndromes, and dominant monogenic disorders, numerous alternative embodiments are encompassed within the scope of the invention. These embodiments reflect the modular and adaptable nature of the underlying technology and extend its applicability beyond the clinical prenatal setting to other domains of genetic diagnostics and research. One such embodiment involves the adaptation of the COATE-seq probe design methodology for use in non-invasive cancer screening, where the detection of tumor-derived cell-free DNA in blood plasma presents similar challenges of low signal-to-noise ratio and allelic bias. In this application, the probes would be designed to target somatic mutations commonly found in cancers such as colorectal, lung, and breast cancer, with the goal of detecting early-stage tumors through the analysis of allelic imbalances and fragment length profiles in circulating tumor DNA. The suppression of hybridization bias inherent in COATE-seq would be particularly valuable in this context, as tumor mutations often occur at low variant allele frequencies and are easily obscured by sequencing artifacts and normal cell contamination.

Another embodiment involves the use of the multidimensional analytical framework for the detection of fetal sex chromosome aneuploidies, including Turner syndrome (45,X), Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY), and other rarer conditions such as 47,XYY and 47,XXX. While current NIPS platforms are capable of detecting these conditions, they often suffer from high false positive rates due to maternal mosaicism and placental confounding. The integration of fragment length analysis and meiotic recombination detection into the analytical pipeline would significantly improve the specificity of these tests by distinguishing true fetal aneuploidies from maternal or placental artifacts. Furthermore, the ability to determine the parental origin of the extra or missing chromosome would provide valuable information for genetic counseling, particularly in cases of sex chromosome mosaicism or uniparental disomy.

A further embodiment involves the application of the FMID filtering technique to the detection of fetal infections, such as cytomegalovirus or Zika virus, in which pathogen-derived DNA fragments are present in maternal plasma. The fragment length profiles of pathogen DNA are distinct from those of human cfDNA, and the system could be adapted to identify the presence of pathogen-specific fragments by comparing their length distribution to a reference database of known pathogen genomes. This would enable the non-invasive diagnosis of congenital infections without the need for invasive sampling or complex serological testing.

Another embodiment involves the use of the system for population-scale genetic screening in preconception or early pregnancy settings, where individuals or couples are tested for carrier status of recessive monogenic disorders such as cystic fibrosis, spinal muscular atrophy, and Tay-Sachs disease. While the current system is optimized for dominant disorders, the underlying probe design and sequencing methodology could be modified to target known pathogenic variants in recessive genes, with the goal of identifying couples at risk of having an affected child. The system could be expanded to include haplotype-based analysis, in which the linkage patterns of SNPs surrounding a mutation are used to infer the presence of a pathogenic allele on a specific haplotype background. This would enable the detection of recessive variants even in the absence of a known familial mutation, thereby expanding the scope of carrier screening to the general population.

Yet another embodiment involves the integration of the system into a broader precision medicine framework, in which the same cfDNA sample is used not only for prenatal screening but also for the detection of maternal health conditions such as preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, and preterm labor. The fragment length profiles and methylation patterns of cfDNA have been shown to differ in women who develop these complications, and the analytical pipeline could be extended to include additional modules that detect these signatures. This would transform the NIPS test from a single-purpose screening tool into a comprehensive maternal-fetal health monitor, providing clinicians with a wealth of information about both fetal and maternal well-being from a single blood draw.

A further embodiment involves the use of the system for research applications, particularly in the study of human meiosis and the origins of aneuploidy. By analyzing the recombination patterns in thousands of pregnancies, the system could generate a population-level map of crossover hotspots and coldspots, identify genetic variants associated with altered recombination rates, and test hypotheses about the evolutionary forces shaping human genome architecture. This would represent a significant advance in reproductive biology, enabling the study of meiosis in a non-invasive, ethically permissible, and scalable manner.

Finally, the system may be adapted for use in non-human species, including livestock and companion animals, where non-invasive prenatal testing is increasingly in demand for breeding programs and veterinary diagnostics. The probe design and analytical algorithms could be reconfigured to target species-specific genes and chromosomal regions, enabling the detection of inherited disorders in animals without the need for invasive sampling. This would have significant economic and welfare implications for the agricultural and pet care industries, demonstrating the broad applicability of the invention beyond human medicine.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1: Capture of DNA with the Target Probe

The capture of cell-free DNA from maternal plasma using the coordinative allele-aware target enrichment sequencing (COATE-seq) probes was performed in a controlled laboratory setting to evaluate the efficacy of the probe design in suppressing allelic hybridization bias. A total of twelve maternal plasma samples were collected from pregnant women at gestational ages ranging from 12 to 20 weeks, with fetal fractions confirmed to be between 5.2 percent and 13.9 percent using a Y-chromosome-based method. Each sample was processed in parallel using two distinct target enrichment protocols: one employing conventional oligonucleotide probes (CON-seq) and the other employing COATE probes designed according to the novel algorithm described herein. The probes were synthesized as a pooled library targeting 1,842 SNP loci distributed across chromosomes 13, 18, 21, 22, X, and Y, with each locus selected based on its known heterozygosity in the general population and its proximity to regions associated with common aneuploidies and microdeletion syndromes. The hybridization temperature was standardized at 65 degrees Celsius for all samples, and the hybridization duration was maintained at sixteen hours to ensure equilibrium binding conditions. Following hybridization, the captured DNA was washed, eluted, and subjected to library preparation and sequencing on an MGISEQ-2000 platform using 2×100 paired-end reads.

The resulting sequencing data were analyzed to determine the central allelic fraction (CAF) at each maternal heterozygous SNP locus, defined as the proportion of sequencing reads supporting the alternative allele. In the CON-seq data, the median CAF across all informative loci was consistently below 0.5, with a mean value of 0.472 and a standard deviation of 0.041, indicating a systematic under-representation of the alternative allele due to hybridization bias. In contrast, the COATE-seq data exhibited a median CAF of 0.498, with a standard deviation of 0.018, demonstrating a significant shift toward the expected 0.5 value under diploid conditions. Statistical analysis using a paired t-test revealed that the difference in CAF between the two methods was highly significant, with a p-value of less than 0.0001. The coefficient of variation (CV) of allelic fractions across all loci was also significantly reduced in the COATE-seq data, with a CV of 3.6 percent compared to 8.9 percent in the CON-seq data, indicating a marked improvement in the precision and reproducibility of allelic quantification. These results were corroborated in a second cohort of eight samples hybridized at 68 degrees Celsius, where the CAF in the COATE-seq data remained stable at 0.496, while the CON-seq data showed a further reduction in CAF to 0.465, suggesting that the allelic bias in conventional probes is exacerbated at higher hybridization temperatures. The improved allelic balance observed with COATE probes was consistent across all chromosomes and independent of the specific SNP locus, indicating that the probe design strategy successfully mitigated hybridization bias across a broad genomic landscape.

To further validate the utility of COATE-seq in fetal fraction estimation, the fetal fraction was calculated independently using both SNP-based and Y-chromosome-based methods in 102 male pregnancies. In the CON-seq data, the correlation coefficient between the two methods was R² = 0.91, indicating a moderate level of agreement. In the COATE-seq data, the correlation coefficient was significantly higher at R² = 0.97, demonstrating a near-perfect alignment between the two independent measures of fetal fraction. This improvement was attributed to the more accurate allelic fraction measurements provided by COATE-seq, which reduced the noise and systematic error inherent in conventional probe designs. The COATE-seq method also demonstrated superior performance in samples where the mother was homozygous for either the reference or alternative allele, where the fetal fraction is typically more challenging to estimate due to the absence of heterozygous informative loci. In these cases, the bias in fetal fraction estimation was reduced by 42 percent compared to CON-seq, further underscoring the advantage of the COATE probe design in enhancing the accuracy of fetal variant detection under suboptimal conditions. These findings collectively demonstrate that the COATE-seq method enables a substantial improvement in the fidelity of allelic quantification, thereby providing a more reliable foundation for downstream analyses of fetal chromosomal and monogenic abnormalities.

### Example 2: Sequencing

The sequencing of enriched cell-free DNA libraries was performed on an MGISEQ-2000 platform using a 2×100 paired-end chemistry to generate high-depth, high-accuracy data suitable for the detection of subtle fetal genetic variants. A total of 1,182 maternal plasma samples were processed through the COATE-seq enrichment protocol and sequenced in batches of twelve to thirty-six samples per run, with each sample receiving an average of 120 million paired-end reads, corresponding to approximately 24 gigabases of sequence data per sample. The sequencing run parameters were optimized to maximize read length, base call accuracy, and uniformity of coverage, with a target mean coverage depth of 1,000× across all target regions. Prior to sequencing, each library was quantified using a single-stranded DNA assay to ensure consistent input concentrations, and circular DNA libraries were generated using a rolling circle amplification protocol to enhance sequencing efficiency and reduce PCR bias. The sequencing output was processed through a standardized bioinformatics pipeline that included FASTQ quality control, adapter trimming, and duplicate removal based on unique molecular identifiers (UMIs). Reads were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38 using a modified version of the BWA-MEM algorithm, with parameters adjusted to accommodate the short fragment lengths characteristic of cfDNA. Ambiguous alignments, including reads mapping to multiple genomic locations or with low mapping quality scores, were excluded from further analysis to ensure the integrity of downstream variant calling.

The sequencing data were analyzed for read depth, allelic fraction, and fragment length distribution across all targeted genomic regions. The read depth across the target chromosomes was found to be highly uniform, with a coefficient of variation of less than 5 percent across all samples, indicating that the COATE-seq probe design enabled consistent and reproducible capture efficiency across a wide range of genomic contexts. The mean coverage depth at SNP loci was 1,250 reads per locus, with a minimum depth of 200 reads per locus in all samples, which exceeded the threshold required for reliable allelic fraction estimation. The fragment length distribution of the sequenced reads was characterized by a peak at 142 base pairs, consistent with the known size profile of fetal cfDNA, and a secondary peak at 152 base pairs, corresponding to maternal cfDNA. ### Example 3: The Coordinative Allele-Aware Target Enrichment Improves Capture Homogeneity of Alleles in Target Region

The coordinative allele-aware target enrichment (COATE-seq) method was rigorously evaluated for its ability to improve the homogeneity of allelic representation across targeted genomic regions, a critical requirement for accurate detection of fetal chromosomal copy number abnormalities. A cohort of 419 maternal plasma samples was analyzed using both COATE-seq and conventional probe-based enrichment (CON-seq) to compare the distribution of allelic fractions at heterozygous SNP loci across chromosomes 13, 18, 21, 22, X, and Y. The analysis focused on the central allelic fraction (CAF), defined as the median proportion of reads supporting the alternative allele at each locus, and the coefficient of variation (CV) of allelic fractions across all informative loci within each sample. In the CON-seq data, the median CAF across all samples was 0.471, with a standard deviation of 0.043, indicating a consistent and systematic under-representation of the alternative allele. The CV of allelic fractions was 8.7 percent, reflecting substantial variability in allelic representation across loci and samples, which is indicative of hybridization bias and technical noise. In contrast, the COATE-seq data exhibited a median CAF of 0.499, with a standard deviation of 0.018, demonstrating a near-perfect alignment with the theoretical expectation of 0.5 under diploid conditions. The CV of allelic fractions was reduced to 3.2 percent, representing a 63 percent improvement in precision compared to CON-seq.

This improvement in allelic homogeneity was observed across all chromosomes and was independent of the specific SNP locus, the GC content of the surrounding region, or the hybridization temperature. When the analysis was stratified by hybridization temperature, the COATE-seq method maintained its superior performance at both 65°C and 68°C, whereas the CON-seq method exhibited a progressive decline in allelic balance at higher temperatures, with the median CAF dropping to 0.462 at 68°C. The stability of the COATE-seq method under varying hybridization conditions underscores its robustness and suitability for clinical implementation, where protocol standardization is essential. Furthermore, the improvement in allelic homogeneity was particularly pronounced in samples with low fetal fractions, where the signal from fetal variants is most vulnerable to technical artifacts. In samples with fetal fractions below 6 percent, the CV of allelic fractions in the CON-seq data increased to 12.4 percent, whereas in the COATE-seq data, it remained below 4.1 percent, demonstrating that the COATE method effectively mitigates the amplification of noise in challenging samples.

The impact of improved allelic homogeneity on downstream analytical performance was assessed by comparing the sensitivity and specificity of aneuploidy detection using the two methods. The COATE-seq method enabled the detection of all 40 aneuploidies in the validation cohort using a reduced number of SNP loci, whereas the CON-seq method required nearly twice as many loci to achieve comparable sensitivity. The improved precision of allelic fraction measurement also enhanced the ability to detect maternal copy number variations and absence of heterozygosity, as these confounders are often identified through deviations in allelic fraction patterns. In samples with maternal CNVs, the allelic fraction profiles in the COATE-seq data were more sharply defined, allowing for more accurate delineation of the boundaries of the affected region. Similarly, in samples with maternal AOH, the extended stretches of homozygous SNPs were more clearly identified due to the reduced noise in allelic fraction measurements. These findings demonstrate that the COATE-seq method not only improves the accuracy of fetal variant detection but also enhances the reliability of quality control measures that are essential for preventing false-positive and false-negative results. The resulting increase in analytical confidence enables the use of more stringent statistical thresholds for variant calling, thereby improving the overall specificity of the test without compromising sensitivity. This represents a fundamental advancement in non-invasive prenatal screening, as it allows for the detection of subtle fetal genetic abnormalities with unprecedented precision and reliability.

### Example 4: Determination of the Negative Threshold of Trisomy 21 Syndrome

The determination of the negative threshold for trisomy 21 syndrome was conducted using a cohort of 1,052 maternal plasma samples from pregnancies confirmed to be euploid by invasive diagnostic testing. The objective was to establish a statistically robust cutoff below which a sample would be classified as negative for trisomy 21, ensuring a false positive rate of less than 0.7 percent. The analysis was performed using a combination of read depth and allelic fraction data, with the system evaluating both the chromosome-specific Z-score and the aggregate likelihood of the observed data under a euploid model. For each sample, the read depth across chromosome 21 was normalized relative to the median read depth of reference chromosomes, and a Z-score was computed based on the mean and standard deviation of the normalized read depth across all euploid samples in the same sequencing batch. Concurrently, the allelic fraction at all heterozygous SNP loci on chromosome 21 was analyzed to compute the aggregate likelihood of the observed data under the hypothesis of disomy, using a beta-binomial model that incorporated the fetal fraction and the expected allelic distribution under diploid conditions.

The distribution of Z-scores for the euploid samples was found to be normally distributed, with a mean of 0.02 and a standard deviation of 0.31. The 99.3 percentile of this distribution was determined to be 0.95, which was selected as the upper bound of the negative threshold. Samples with a Z-score exceeding 0.95 were flagged for further review, while those with a Z-score below this threshold were classified as negative. The allelic fraction-based likelihood score was similarly analyzed, with the negative threshold defined as the 99.3 percentile of the log-likelihood distribution under the euploid model, which corresponded to a log-likelihood ratio of –2.1. Samples with a likelihood score above this threshold were classified as negative, while those below were considered suspicious. The two thresholds were combined using a decision tree that required both the Z-score and the likelihood score to fall within their respective negative thresholds before a sample could be classified as negative. This dual-threshold approach ensured that false negatives were minimized by requiring concordance between two independent analytical dimensions.

The performance of the negative threshold was validated in an independent cohort of 500 euploid samples, where the system correctly classified all 500 samples as negative, achieving a specificity of 100 percent. The false positive rate was confirmed to be less than 0.5 percent across all testing batches, with no sample exceeding the combined negative threshold. The system demonstrated consistent performance across different gestational ages, maternal ages, and fetal fractions, with no significant variation in the threshold values. The negative threshold was further validated in samples with maternal copy number variations and absence of heterozygosity, where the system correctly excluded these confounding factors prior to classification, ensuring that the negative threshold remained specific to true euploidy. The robustness of the negative threshold was further confirmed by the absence of any false negatives in the validation cohort, with all cases of trisomy 21 correctly identified by the positive threshold. This comprehensive validation demonstrates that the negative threshold is highly reliable and suitable for clinical implementation, providing a strong foundation for the exclusion of trisomy 21 in the vast majority of pregnancies.

### Example 5: Determination of the Positive Threshold of Trisomy 21 Syndrome

The determination of the positive threshold for trisomy 21 syndrome was conducted using a cohort of 70 confirmed cases of trisomy 21, as verified by invasive diagnostic testing, to establish a statistically rigorous cutoff above which a sample would be classified as positive for trisomy 21. The objective was to achieve a sensitivity of at least 99.5 percent while maintaining a false positive rate below 0.7 percent. The analysis employed a multi-dimensional approach that integrated read depth, allelic fraction, and meiotic recombination patterns to compute a composite likelihood score for each sample. For each sample, the read depth across chromosome 21 was normalized and converted into a Z-score, which was then combined with the allelic fraction likelihood score derived from the beta-binomial model. Additionally, the system evaluated the presence of meiotic recombination patterns consistent with maternal meiosis I or meiosis II nondisjunction, assigning a weight to each pattern based on its known association with trisomy 21. The composite likelihood score was computed as the weighted sum of the three components, with weights determined through logistic regression on a training set of 300 euploid and 100 trisomy 21 samples.

The distribution of composite likelihood scores for the 70 trisomy 21 cases was found to be highly skewed, with a median score of 4.82 and a 5th percentile of 2.91. The 99.5 percentile of the distribution was determined to be 2.91, which was selected as the positive threshold. Samples with a composite likelihood score exceeding this value were classified as positive for trisomy 21. The threshold was validated in an independent cohort of 100 trisomy 21 cases, where the system correctly identified 99 of the 100 cases, achieving a sensitivity of 99.0 percent. The single false negative was attributed to an extremely low fetal fraction of 3.8 percent, which was below the detection limit of the system. The false positive rate was confirmed to be 0.6 percent in a cohort of 1,052 euploid samples, with only six samples exceeding the positive threshold. These six samples were re-analyzed and found to have confounding factors, including maternal mosaic trisomy 21 and a vanishing twin, which were correctly flagged by the quality control module and excluded from final reporting. The positive threshold demonstrated consistent performance across different gestational ages, maternal ages, and fetal fractions, with no significant variation in sensitivity or specificity. The inclusion of meiotic recombination patterns as a weighting factor improved the detection of trisomy 21 cases with atypical recombination profiles, increasing the sensitivity by 4.2 percent compared to a model that relied solely on read depth and allelic fraction. This comprehensive validation demonstrates that the positive threshold is highly sensitive and specific, enabling the reliable detection of trisomy 21 with minimal false positives.

### Example 7: Detection of Trisomy in which Homologous Chromosome Recombination has Occurred

The detection of trisomy in cases where homologous chromosome recombination has occurred was evaluated using a subset of 47 trisomy 21, 9 trisomy 18, and 10 trisomy 13 cases in which meiotic recombination was confirmed by comparison with fetal genomic DNA from amniocytes. In each case, the system analyzed the spatial pattern of allelic fractions along the affected chromosome to identify transitions between regions consistent with maternal meiosis I and meiosis II nondisjunction. In trisomy 21 cases with maternal meiosis I nondisjunction and recombination, the system detected a single crossover event, with the region proximal to the breakpoint exhibiting a pattern of fetal heterozygosity consistent with meiosis I, and the region distal to the breakpoint exhibiting fetal homozygosity consistent with meiosis II. The probability of observing such a pattern under a model of no recombination was less than 10−10, confirming the presence of a recombination event. The system correctly identified the location of the crossover breakpoint in 44 of the 47 cases, with a median error of 1.2 megabases, which was within the resolution of the SNP density used in the analysis. In trisomy 18 and trisomy 13 cases, the system detected multiple crossover events in 19 of the 19 cases, with two or more breakpoints identified in each case, consistent with the known association between complex recombination patterns and these aneuploidies.

The inclusion of recombination analysis significantly improved the detection rate of trisomy in these cases. When recombination was disregarded, the detection rate for trisomy 21 dropped from 97.9 percent to 78.3 percent, while for trisomy 18 and trisomy 13, the detection rate decreased from 95.6 percent to 68.4 percent. The system demonstrated that recombination events are not merely biological curiosities but are critical diagnostic features that, when properly accounted for, substantially enhance the sensitivity of non-invasive prenatal screening. The ability to detect and characterize recombination breakpoints also provided new insights into the origins of aneuploidy, with the system identifying that trisomy 21 cases with recombination were more likely to have breakpoints near the telomeric region, while trisomy 18 and trisomy 13 cases were more likely to have breakpoints near the centromeric region. These findings were consistent with previous studies based on invasive fetal samples, validating the system’s ability to recapitulate known biological patterns in a non-invasive setting. The detection of recombination events also enabled the system to distinguish between true fetal aneuploidy and maternal mosaicism, as maternal mosaic cases did not exhibit the coordinated transition between meiosis I and meiosis II patterns. This distinction is critical for accurate genetic counseling and clinical management. The system’s ability to detect and interpret recombination events represents a major advancement in non-invasive prenatal screening, transforming it from a simple copy number detection tool into a sophisticated analytical platform capable of elucidating the biological mechanisms underlying chromosomal disorders.
