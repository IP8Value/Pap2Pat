# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

### 1. Field

The present invention relates to optical imaging systems and, more specifically, to a novel method and apparatus for achieving high-speed, aberration-free axial re-focusing in microscopy and laser-based imaging systems without mechanical movement of the objective or the sample. The invention enables rapid, precise, and diffraction-limited control of the focal plane along the optical axis by converting lateral scanning motion into axial displacement of the focus through an optically conjugated remote focusing architecture. This technology is particularly suited for applications requiring volumetric imaging at high temporal resolution, including but not limited to live-cell fluorescence microscopy, two-photon intravital imaging, light-sheet microscopy, neuroscientific studies of neural activity dynamics, cardiac imaging in small animal models, and high-precision laser machining where rapid axial targeting is essential. The invention overcomes fundamental limitations of existing technologies—such as electrically tuneable lenses, acoustic gradient index lenses, deformable mirrors, and reverberation-based methods—by eliminating spherical aberrations across the entire axial scan range while simultaneously achieving scan rates orders of magnitude faster than conventional mechanical or piezoelectric approaches. The system leverages commercially available high-speed galvanometric mirror scanners, which are already widely deployed for lateral scanning in confocal and two-photon microscopes, thereby enabling seamless integration into existing imaging platforms with minimal modification.

### 2. Discussion of Related Art

In conventional optical microscopy, axial re-focusing has historically been accomplished through mechanical translation of either the objective lens or the sample stage along the optical axis. While this approach provides high fidelity and excellent depth resolution, it is fundamentally constrained by the inertia of the moving components, resulting in scan rates typically limited to tens of hertz at best. This limitation is particularly detrimental in applications involving dynamic biological processes that occur on millisecond or even microsecond timescales, such as action potential propagation in neurons, calcium signaling in cardiac myocytes, or vesicular trafficking within living cells. To circumvent these mechanical constraints, researchers have developed a variety of alternative strategies for non-mechanical axial scanning. Among the most prominent are electrically tuneable lenses (ETLs), tuneable acoustic gradient index of refraction (TAG) lenses, and deformable mirrors (DMs). ETLs operate by applying an electric field to a liquid-filled chamber to induce a curvature in the lens surface, thereby altering its focal length. TAG lenses exploit acoustic waves to generate a refractive index gradient within a transparent medium, effectively creating a lens whose focal point can be shifted by modulating the acoustic frequency. Both technologies have demonstrated impressive axial scan rates, with resonant TAG lenses reaching frequencies in excess of 100 kHz. However, both ETLs and TAG lenses introduce a quadratic phase profile to the incoming wavefront, which approximates only a simple defocus term and fails to correct for higher-order aberrations such as spherical aberration, coma, and astigmatism that inevitably arise when imaging beyond the nominal focal plane of the primary objective. As a result, image quality degrades significantly with increasing axial displacement, rendering these technologies unsuitable for high-resolution applications requiring diffraction-limited performance across extended depth ranges.

Deformable mirrors offer greater wavefront control by allowing spatially resolved modulation of surface height, enabling correction of complex aberrations. Nevertheless, their utility for axial scanning is severely restricted by the inherent trade-off between actuator stroke and bandwidth. High-speed DMs, capable of operating at kilohertz frequencies, typically exhibit stroke amplitudes on the order of a single wavelength of light—insufficient for meaningful axial re-focusing in most imaging scenarios. Conversely, DMs engineered for large stroke—necessary for axial ranges exceeding 50 micrometers—suffer from drastically reduced bandwidth, often falling below 100 Hz, which renders them incompatible with the demands of high-speed volumetric imaging. Furthermore, the complexity of driving and calibrating multi-actuator DMs introduces significant practical challenges, including hysteresis, nonlinearity, and thermal drift, which compromise reproducibility and long-term stability.

An alternative paradigm, known as aberration-free remote focusing, has emerged as a promising solution to these limitations. This method relies on the precise optical conjugation between two objectives: a remote-focusing objective and the primary imaging objective. By placing a movable mirror at the focal plane of the remote objective and translating it axially, the focus formed by the primary objective can be shifted in the sample plane while maintaining diffraction-limited performance, provided the pupils of the two objectives are matched in size and position. This technique has been shown to preserve resolution and contrast over axial ranges exceeding 200 micrometers, far surpassing the capabilities of ETLs and TAG lenses. However, the axial motion of the mirror remains a mechanical constraint, typically implemented using piezoelectric actuators or voice coils, which are inherently slow, with maximum scan rates rarely exceeding 1 kHz. Consequently, while aberration-free, this approach remains bottlenecked by the speed of its actuator, preventing its adoption in applications demanding sub-millisecond axial switching.

More recently, a technique known as reverberation microscopy has been proposed, which exploits multiple reflections within an optical cavity to generate a series of axially displaced focal spots from a single laser pulse. By introducing a feedback loop into the laser path, the beam is reflected multiple times, each reflection forming a focus at a different axial position. Although this method can achieve axial scanning on the nanosecond timescale, it is fundamentally limited by the number of resolvable focal planes, typically capped at approximately ten due to exponential decay in pulse intensity with each reflection. Moreover, reverberation microscopy imposes a fixed, discrete set of focal planes, each separated by a scattering mean-free path, which in biological tissues is approximately 100 micrometers—far too coarse for high-resolution imaging of subcellular structures. The technique also suffers from severe non-uniform illumination across the focal planes, with the earliest reflections receiving the highest intensity and the later reflections being exponentially dimmer. This renders it unsuitable for quantitative imaging or applications requiring consistent excitation intensity. Furthermore, like ETLs and TAG lenses, reverberation microscopy introduces only a quadratic phase function, resulting in significant spherical aberration beyond the nominal focal plane, thereby degrading lateral resolution and contrast.

Other approaches have attempted to circumvent axial scanning altogether by employing projection imaging or spatial encoding of depth information. Projection imaging collapses the three-dimensional structure into a two-dimensional projection, sacrificing volumetric information entirely. Spatial encoding methods, such as structured illumination or coded aperture techniques, require sparse samples and are incompatible with dense, complex biological tissues. These methods may be useful in specific contexts but fail to address the fundamental need for high-resolution, three-dimensional, time-resolved imaging of dynamic processes in intact biological specimens.

Thus, despite decades of innovation, no existing technology simultaneously satisfies the critical requirements of high axial scan rate (>1 kHz), large axial scan range (>50 µm), diffraction-limited resolution across the entire scan volume, compatibility with standard imaging objectives, and ease of integration into existing microscopy platforms. The field remains in urgent need of a novel solution that decouples axial scanning speed from mechanical inertia, eliminates aberrations across the entire depth range, and leverages the mature, high-performance lateral scanning technologies already embedded in modern microscopes. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a transformative paradigm: the conversion of lateral scanning motion into axial re-focusing through a precisely engineered optical architecture that maintains aberration-free performance while enabling unprecedented scan speeds.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a novel optical system and method for achieving high-speed, aberration-free axial re-focusing in imaging systems by transforming lateral scanning motion into controlled axial displacement of the focal plane. The invention comprises a dual-arm optical configuration in which a collimated laser beam is first directed through a remote-focusing arm containing a high-speed galvanometric scanning mirror and a remote objective lens, and subsequently through an illumination arm containing a primary imaging objective lens. The remote-focusing arm is optically conjugated to the illumination arm such that the back focal plane of the remote objective is imaged onto the back focal plane of the primary objective, ensuring pupil matching and enabling aberration-free image formation. A reflective element is positioned at the focal plane of the remote objective, and the galvanometric mirror is used to scan the incident laser beam laterally across the surface of this reflective element. When the beam strikes the reflective element at its nominal focal point, the reflected beam remains collimated, resulting in a focus at the nominal focal plane of the primary objective. When the beam is scanned to a different lateral position on the reflective element, the angle of incidence changes, causing the reflected beam to converge or diverge, thereby introducing a controlled amount of defocus that is transmitted through the conjugated optical train and manifested as an axial shift of the focus in the sample plane. Crucially, because the galvanometric mirror is also used to de-scan the returning beam, any lateral displacement introduced by the reflective element is canceled out on the return path, resulting in pure axial motion of the focal spot without lateral drift.

The invention encompasses two distinct implementations of this principle: a discrete axial scanning mode and a continuous axial scanning mode. In the discrete mode, the reflective element is a multi-step mirror comprising a series of planar surfaces at different heights, each corresponding to a distinct axial focal position. By sequentially directing the laser beam to each step, the system generates a set of discrete, well-defined focal planes with arbitrary axial spacing, limited only by the physical dimensions of the mirror and the field of view of the remote objective. This configuration enables the system to achieve large axial displacements—on the order of hundreds of micrometers—with high fidelity and without aberration, making it ideal for applications requiring precise, stepwise axial sampling such as volumetric imaging of thick tissues or multi-plane stimulation protocols. In the continuous mode, the reflective element is a single, slightly tilted planar mirror. The remote objective is laterally offset such that the incident laser focus is incident normal to the mirror surface. As the galvanometric mirror scans the beam laterally across the tilted surface, the point of reflection moves along the mirror, causing the angle of reflection to vary linearly with lateral position. This results in a continuous, linear variation of the wavefront curvature, producing a smoothly varying axial focus over a finite range. The axial scan range is determined by the tilt angle of the mirror and the lateral field of view of the remote scanning system, and the system maintains diffraction-limited performance across the entire range, provided the numerical aperture of the remote objective exceeds that of the primary objective to accommodate the angular spread of the tilted beam.

The invention further enables resonant axial scanning by driving the galvanometric mirror with a sinusoidal waveform at frequencies up to 12 kHz, thereby generating rapid, periodic axial oscillations of the focal plane. The system does not require synchronized modulation of laser power or complex feedback control to achieve uniform axial intensity distribution; instead, the intensity profile is passively balanced by adjusting the scan amplitude to match the dwell time of the resonant motion across the desired axial range. In the continuous mode, this allows for homogeneous illumination over a linear portion of the sinusoidal trajectory, while in the discrete mode, the relative dwell times on each step can be equalized by tuning the scan amplitude to center the motion over the steps. The invention is fully compatible with standard imaging modalities, including two-photon microscopy, light-sheet microscopy, and confocal microscopy, and can be retrofitted into existing systems with minimal modification. The use of commercially available, high-speed galvanometric mirrors ensures scalability to even higher frequencies, potentially reaching megahertz rates when paired with acousto-optic deflectors. The system’s ability to maintain diffraction-limited resolution across the entire axial scan range, combined with its speed, simplicity, and compatibility, represents a paradigm shift in axial scanning technology, enabling previously unattainable temporal resolution in live biological imaging and high-precision laser processing applications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Experimental Setup

The experimental system is composed of two optically conjugated arms arranged orthogonally to one another, each containing a high-precision objective lens, a set of relay optics, and a galvanometric scanning mirror. The first arm, designated as the remote-focusing arm, is responsible for generating and modulating the axial position of the focal spot, while the second arm, designated as the illumination arm, delivers the focused beam to the sample and forms the final image. A collimated laser beam, typically from a continuous-wave or pulsed laser source operating in the visible or near-infrared spectrum, is directed into the system via a polarizing beam splitter, which transmits the incident beam and reflects it into the remote-focusing arm. Within this arm, the beam is first expanded and collimated to ensure uniform illumination across the aperture of the galvanometric scanning mirror. The mirror, which is a high-speed, low-inertia galvanometer driven by a resonant or deterministic waveform, is positioned such that its rotational axis is perpendicular to the plane of incidence of the laser beam. The mirror is imaged into the back focal plane of a remote objective lens, which is typically an air objective with a moderate numerical aperture, such as 0.7 or 0.8, and a long working distance to accommodate the reflective element placed at its focal plane. The remote objective is followed by a 4F telescope composed of two telecentric tube lenses, which ensures that the pupil of the remote objective is precisely conjugated to the pupil of the primary imaging objective in the illumination arm. This conjugation is critical to the operation of the system, as it enables the transfer of wavefront aberrations from the remote arm to the illumination arm in a manner that cancels out any introduced distortions.

At the focal plane of the remote objective, a reflective element is positioned. This element is either a multi-step mirror or a single, planar mirror tilted at a small angle relative to the optical axis. In the discrete axial scanning configuration, the reflective element is fabricated as a microfabricated staircase with a series of planar steps, each separated by a precisely controlled vertical displacement. These steps are arranged in a linear array across the field of view of the remote objective, with the height difference between adjacent steps determining the axial displacement of the focal spot in the sample plane. The steps are coated with a highly reflective material, such as aluminum or gold, to maximize reflectivity and minimize absorption losses. The galvanometric mirror is then driven to scan the laser beam laterally across these steps, with each lateral position corresponding to a specific step and, consequently, a specific axial focus. When the beam strikes a step that lies exactly at the nominal focal plane of the remote objective, the reflected beam remains collimated, and the focus formed by the primary objective lies at its nominal focal plane. When the beam strikes a step that is displaced axially from this nominal plane, the reflected beam becomes either convergent or divergent, depending on whether the step is above or below the focal plane. This change in wavefront curvature is preserved through the 4F telescope and conjugated to the back focal plane of the primary objective, resulting in an axial shift of the focus in the sample plane. Crucially, because the galvanometric mirror is also used to de-scan the returning beam, any lateral displacement introduced by the step mirror is canceled out on the return path, ensuring that the final focus in the sample plane exhibits pure axial motion with no lateral drift.

In the continuous axial scanning configuration, the multi-step mirror is replaced by a single, planar mirror that is tilted at a small angle—typically between 5° and 10°—with respect to the image plane. The remote objective is laterally offset from the optical axis such that the incident laser focus is oriented normal to the surface of the tilted mirror. This alignment ensures that the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, preserving the symmetry of the optical path. As the galvanometric mirror scans the beam laterally across the mirror, the point of reflection moves along the surface, causing the angle of reflection to vary linearly with lateral position. This variation translates into a linear change in the convergence or divergence of the reflected beam, resulting in a continuous, linear shift of the focal plane along the optical axis. The axial scan range is determined by the product of the lateral field of view of the remote scanning system and the tangent of the mirror tilt angle. For example, with a 7.5° tilt and a lateral scan range of 3 millimeters, an axial scan range of approximately 40 micrometers can be achieved. The system is designed such that the numerical aperture of the remote objective exceeds that of the primary objective to ensure that the full angular spread of the tilted beam is captured and transmitted without vignetting. This design choice is essential to maintaining the numerical aperture and, therefore, the lateral resolution of the final image. The use of a tilted planar mirror eliminates the need for discrete steps, allowing for continuous, smooth axial motion, and enables the system to operate in a manner analogous to a mechanical axial translator, but with the speed and precision of a galvanometric scanner.

The illumination arm contains a water immersion objective, typically with a high numerical aperture of 0.8 to 1.05, which is matched in pupil size and position to the remote objective through a second 4F telescope. The demagnification between the two arms is carefully calculated to ensure that the pupil diameters are matched, which is necessary for aberration-free remote focusing. For example, if the remote objective has a numerical aperture of 0.8 in air, and the primary objective has a numerical aperture of 0.8 in water, the demagnification must be set to approximately 1.33 to account for the refractive index difference between air and water. This demagnification ensures that the angular spectrum of the beam exiting the remote objective is correctly scaled to match the acceptance angle of the primary objective, thereby preserving the diffraction-limited performance of the system. The beam then passes through the primary objective and is focused onto the sample, which may be a biological specimen such as a live cell, tissue slice, or zebrafish embryo, embedded in a suitable medium such as agarose or methylcellulose. The sample is illuminated from below, and fluorescence emission is collected by a detection objective oriented orthogonally to the illumination axis, which is typically a water immersion objective with a high numerical aperture and a tube lens coupled to a scientific CMOS camera or a photomultiplier tube. The detection path includes appropriate optical filters to block excitation light and transmit only the fluorescence signal, ensuring high signal-to-noise ratio and minimal background.

To characterize the performance of the system, transmission measurements of the focal spot are acquired using a tertiary imaging system that consists of a high-precision objective mounted on a piezoelectric stage. This objective is aligned along the optical axis of the primary imaging objective and is used to image the laser focus in transmission as the axial position is varied. The piezoelectric stage is stepped in increments of 160 nanometers to acquire three-dimensional stacks of the focal spot, allowing for precise measurement of the point spread function in all three dimensions. These measurements are used to quantify the lateral and axial resolution, assess the presence of aberrations, and validate the diffraction-limited nature of the focus across the entire scan range. In the case of resonant scanning, where the galvanometric mirror operates at high frequencies, the three-dimensional stack is acquired as a time-averaged intensity distribution, since the focal spot moves too rapidly for the camera to resolve individual positions. The resulting data are then processed to determine the full width at half maximum in both the lateral and axial directions, which are compared to theoretical diffraction limits to confirm the performance of the system. For two-photon microscopy applications, the system is integrated into a standard two-photon raster-scanning microscope by replacing the conventional axial actuator with the remote-focusing arm, while retaining the lateral galvanometric scanners and the detection path. The laser source is typically a Ti:sapphire oscillator tuned to 920 nanometers, with pulse durations of 100 femtoseconds and a repetition rate of 80 megahertz. The laser power is controlled via a Pockels cell to ensure consistent excitation intensity across the axial scan range, and the fluorescence signal is collected by a photomultiplier tube and digitized using a field-programmable gate array for real-time image acquisition.

The system is controlled by a custom software interface that synchronizes the galvanometric mirror waveform with the camera acquisition, the laser power modulation, and the piezoelectric stage positioning. For discrete scanning, the mirror is driven with a stepwise waveform that corresponds to the positions of the steps on the mirror, while for continuous scanning, a triangular or sinusoidal waveform is employed to generate linear or oscillatory axial motion. The system is calibrated using fluorescent nanospheres of known size, which are embedded in a gel matrix and scanned through the axial range to measure the point spread function at each position. The calibration procedure ensures that the axial displacement per unit angular deflection of the galvanometric mirror is accurately known, allowing for precise control of the focal plane position. The entire system is mounted on a vibration-isolated optical table and enclosed in a temperature-controlled chamber to minimize thermal drift and mechanical noise. The use of telecentric lenses throughout the optical train ensures that the chief rays remain parallel to the optical axis, minimizing field curvature and distortion, particularly at the edges of the scan range. The system is designed to be modular, allowing for easy replacement of the reflective element, the galvanometric mirror, or the objective lenses to adapt to different experimental requirements, such as varying axial ranges, scan speeds, or numerical apertures. The invention thus provides a robust, scalable, and highly versatile platform for high-speed, aberration-free axial re-focusing that can be applied across a broad spectrum of scientific and industrial imaging applications.
