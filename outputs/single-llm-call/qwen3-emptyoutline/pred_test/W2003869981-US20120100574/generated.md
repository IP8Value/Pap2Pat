## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to novel methods and compositions for the production of infectious hepatitis C virus (HCV) particles in non-hepatic mammalian cell lines, without reliance on the native HCV RNA replication machinery. Specifically, the invention provides a system for generating authentic, genotype-specific, and clinically relevant HCV virions in cell culture by decoupling the synthesis of HCV genomic RNA from the viral replication cycle, thereby eliminating the accumulation of cell-adaptive mutations that have historically compromised the biological fidelity of in vitro HCV models. The invention further encompasses the use of flavivirus subgenomic replicons to create a permissive intracellular environment that supports the translation, assembly, and release of HCV structural proteins into infectious particles containing full-length, non-replicating HCV RNA genomes. This system enables the production of HCV particles derived from patient-derived consensus sequences, including those of genotype 1a and 1b, which are the most prevalent and therapeutically challenging strains worldwide. The invention also provides methods for assessing HCV entry into target cells using reporter systems that do not require active viral replication, thereby enabling high-throughput screening of entry inhibitors and receptor interactions across diverse cell types. The technology described herein represents a transformative advance in virology, virological diagnostics, antiviral drug discovery, and vaccine development by providing a robust, reproducible, and biologically authentic platform for studying HCV biology in vitro.

## BACKGROUND

Hepatitis C virus (HCV) is a major global health burden, infecting an estimated 2 to 3 percent of the world’s population and contributing significantly to the incidence of chronic liver disease, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Despite advances in direct-acting antiviral therapies, the absence of a licensed vaccine and the continued emergence of drug-resistant variants underscore the urgent need for improved experimental models that accurately reflect the natural biology of HCV infection. Historically, the study of HCV has been severely constrained by the lack of a small animal model that supports robust viral replication and the extreme difficulty in cultivating patient-derived isolates in conventional cell culture systems. While the discovery of the Huh-7.5 human hepatoma cell line and the JFH-1 strain of genotype 2a enabled the first robust in vitro replication of the full HCV lifecycle, these systems remain fundamentally limited by their dependence on viral adaptation to cell culture conditions. The high error rate of the HCV RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, combined with the selective pressures imposed by immortalized cell lines, inevitably leads to the emergence of compensatory mutations that alter viral fitness, tropism, and antigenic properties. As a result, laboratory-adapted strains often fail to recapitulate the behavior of clinically isolated viruses, particularly those of genotype 1, which dominate global infections and exhibit markedly reduced replicative efficiency in vitro.

Attempts to overcome these limitations have included the generation of intergenotypic chimeras, such as J6/JFH-1, which combine structural genes from genotype 1 with the replication-competent backbone of genotype 2a. However, even these engineered constructs accumulate secondary mutations in structural regions that compromise their authenticity and limit their utility for studying natural viral entry, assembly, and immune evasion. Moreover, the reliance on active RNA replication for particle production in these systems means that any perturbation of the replication machinery—whether through antiviral agents, host restriction factors, or genetic manipulation—directly impacts particle yield, confounding mechanistic studies. The inability to produce infectious HCV particles from patient-derived consensus sequences without prior adaptation has hindered efforts to investigate the true biological behavior of circulating strains, particularly in relation to neutralizing antibody responses, receptor interactions, and tissue tropism.

Alternative approaches, such as pseudotyped lentiviral particles (HCVpp), have been developed to study HCV entry by packaging HCV envelope glycoproteins onto non-HCV cores. While useful for probing receptor dependence, HCVpp systems lack the full complement of HCV structural and non-structural proteins, do not replicate in target cells, and cannot be used to assess post-entry events such as uncoating, translation, or assembly. Similarly, subgenomic replicons, while invaluable for studying RNA replication and screening antiviral compounds, are incapable of producing infectious virions and thus provide no insight into the complete viral lifecycle. The persistent failure to establish a cell culture system capable of producing authentic, non-adapted HCV particles from clinical isolates has created a critical gap in the field, impeding the development of broadly effective therapeutics and vaccines.

The present invention addresses these longstanding challenges by introducing a fundamentally novel paradigm: the production of infectious HCV particles in non-hepatic mammalian cells through the cytoplasmic expression of HCV genomic RNA, independent of HCV replication, and facilitated by the pre-existing membrane remodeling and protein trafficking environment established by a flavivirus subgenomic replicon. This approach bypasses the need for viral adaptation, preserves the genetic integrity of patient-derived sequences, and enables the production of particles that are indistinguishable from those isolated from infected patients in terms of morphology, buoyant density, receptor dependence, and infectivity in primary human liver tissue. The invention thus provides the first scalable, reproducible, and biologically faithful method for generating infectious HCV particles from any genotype, including those previously considered intractable in vitro, and establishes a new standard for HCV research, diagnostics, and therapeutic development.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method for producing infectious hepatitis C virus (HCV) particles in non-hepatic mammalian cells without requiring the native HCV RNA replication machinery. The method involves the co-expression of a flavivirus subgenomic replicon and a bacteriophage RNA polymerase-driven HCV genomic construct in a mammalian cell line that has been stably transfected with a flavivirus subgenomic replicon, thereby creating a permissive cellular environment for HCV particle assembly and release. The HCV genomic construct comprises a full-length HCV genome or a bicistronic HCV subgenomic RNA flanked at its 5′ end by a bacteriophage T7 promoter and at its 3′ end by a hepatitis delta virus ribozyme sequence, such that upon transcription by T7 RNA polymerase, the resulting RNA possesses authentic 5′ and 3′ termini necessary for translation and particle formation. The bacteriophage RNA polymerase is expressed from a complementary plasmid encoding the reciprocal polymerase under the control of a heterologous promoter, enabling mutual amplification of both polymerases within the cytoplasm. The flavivirus subgenomic replicon, which is derived from a member of the Flaviviridae family such as West Nile virus or dengue virus, induces extensive membrane rearrangements and alters intracellular lipid metabolism in a manner that facilitates the translation of HCV structural proteins and their subsequent assembly into infectious virions. These virions contain HCV genomic RNA, are enveloped with HCV E1 and E2 glycoproteins, possess a core protein shell, and exhibit a buoyant density consistent with authentic HCV particles. The particles are infectious for human hepatoma cells and primary human liver tissue, and their entry into target cells is mediated by the same cellular receptors—CD81, SR-B1, ASGPR, and claudin-1—as those used by naturally occurring HCV isolates. The invention further provides a reporter system in which the HCV genome is replaced with a bacteriophage RNA polymerase gene, enabling the quantification of HCV entry events without requiring subsequent RNA replication, thus allowing for high-throughput screening of entry inhibitors and receptor function across diverse cell types. The system enables the production of infectious HCV particles from consensus sequences of genotype 1a, 1b, and 2a, including those previously shown to be infectious in chimpanzees, without the introduction of cell-adaptive mutations. The invention thus overcomes the longstanding limitations of existing HCV culture systems by providing a platform for the generation of authentic, non-adapted, genotype-specific infectious virions that faithfully represent the biological properties of clinically relevant HCV strains.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF ILLUSTRATIVE EMBODIMENTS

The present invention provides a comprehensive system for the production of infectious hepatitis C virus (HCV) particles in non-hepatic mammalian cells, circumventing the need for HCV RNA replication and the associated accumulation of cell-adaptive mutations that have long plagued the field. The core innovation lies in the strategic decoupling of HCV genome synthesis from viral replication, and the exploitation of a pre-established cellular environment created by a flavivirus subgenomic replicon to support the translation, assembly, and release of HCV structural proteins into infectious virions. This system is implemented through the co-transfection of two distinct plasmid components into a mammalian cell line that stably harbors a flavivirus subgenomic replicon. The first plasmid component, referred to as the p2B system, consists of two reciprocal plasmids: one encoding bacteriophage T7 RNA polymerase under the control of a bacteriophage SP6 promoter, and the other encoding bacteriophage SP6 RNA polymerase under the control of a T7 promoter. Each polymerase gene is fused in-frame with the second ATG start codon of the encephalomyocarditis virus (EMCV) internal ribosome entry site (IRES), ensuring efficient cytoplasmic translation upon transfection. The presence of both polymerases in the cytoplasm leads to mutual amplification: T7 polymerase transcribes the SP6 promoter-containing plasmid to produce more SP6 polymerase, and SP6 polymerase transcribes the T7 promoter-containing plasmid to produce more T7 polymerase. This positive feedback loop results in sustained, high-level expression of both polymerases, creating a robust platform for cytoplasmic transcription of any gene placed downstream of a T7 promoter.

The second plasmid component encodes the HCV genomic sequence of interest, which may be a full-length genome, a bicistronic subgenomic construct, or a reporter construct, and is flanked at its 5′ end by the T7 promoter and at its 3′ end by a hepatitis delta virus (HDV) antisense ribozyme sequence. The HDV ribozyme ensures precise cleavage at the 3′ terminus of the transcribed RNA, generating a genome with authentic, non-templated ends that are essential for proper translation initiation via the HCV internal ribosome entry site (IRES) and for subsequent particle assembly. The HCV sequence may include the entire open reading frame encoding core, E1, E2, p7, NS2, NS3, NS4A, NS4B, NS5A, and NS5B, or may be truncated to include only structural genes and the 5′-UTR, as in bicistronic constructs where the NS3–NS5B region is replaced with a heterologous coding sequence such as bacteriophage SP6 RNA polymerase. The plasmid is designed to lack any eukaryotic promoter elements, ensuring that transcription occurs exclusively via the introduced bacteriophage polymerase and not through endogenous host machinery. This design ensures that the HCV RNA is synthesized entirely in the cytoplasm, bypassing nuclear transcription and splicing mechanisms, and thus preserving the integrity of the viral genome without the introduction of artificial modifications.

The cell line used for particle production is a non-hepatic mammalian cell line that has been stably transfected with a subgenomic replicon derived from a member of the Flaviviridae family, such as West Nile virus (WNV), dengue virus serotype 2, or other flaviviruses known to induce cytoplasmic membrane rearrangements. In a preferred embodiment, the cell line is BHK-21, a baby hamster kidney cell line, which has been stably transfected with a lineage II WNV subgenomic replicon encoding a Renilla luciferase reporter gene under the control of a cytomegalovirus promoter. This cell line, referred to herein as BHK-WNV cells, exhibits persistent, high-level expression of the WNV replicon without producing infectious WNV particles, due to the absence of structural genes in the replicon. The WNV replicon induces profound alterations in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and associated membranes, including the formation of vesicle packets, convoluted membranes, and lipid droplet accumulation—all of which are known to be critical for flavivirus replication and assembly. Remarkably, these same membrane structures, which are not naturally permissive for HCV replication, provide an ideal environment for the translation of HCV structural proteins and their subsequent assembly into infectious particles. The mechanism by which the WNV replicon confers this permissiveness is not fully understood but appears to involve the upregulation of specific host factors involved in lipid metabolism, membrane curvature, and protein trafficking, as well as the suppression of innate immune responses that would otherwise restrict HCV particle formation. Importantly, the permissiveness of BHK-WNV cells for HCV particle production is not dependent on the translation of WNV proteins, as inhibition of WNV replicon activity by antiviral agents does not impair HCV particle release, indicating that the structural and functional remodeling of the cellular environment, rather than the presence of WNV proteins, is the key determinant of permissiveness.

Upon co-transfection of the p2B system and the HCV genomic plasmid into BHK-WNV cells, cytoplasmic transcription of the HCV genome is initiated by T7 polymerase, resulting in the production of full-length or bicistronic HCV RNA molecules with authentic termini. These RNA molecules are immediately translated by host ribosomes via the HCV IRES, leading to the synthesis of core, E1, E2, p7, and non-structural proteins depending on the construct used. The structural proteins assemble into immature virions within the membrane rearrangements induced by the WNV replicon, likely at the interface of dilated ER cisternae and lipid droplets. The particles bud into the lumen of these membrane compartments, acquiring their lipid envelope embedded with E1 and E2 glycoproteins, and are subsequently released into the extracellular space via exocytosis or other vesicular transport mechanisms. The released particles are harvested from the culture supernatant by ultracentrifugation, purified by sucrose density gradient centrifugation, and characterized for their physical and biological properties. Electron microscopy reveals spherical particles of 50–60 nm in diameter with morphology indistinguishable from authentic HCV virions isolated from patient sera. Immuno-gold labeling confirms the presence of HCV core, E1, and E2 proteins within these particles, while sucrose gradient analysis demonstrates that they migrate over a broad buoyant density range of 1.05 to 1.20 g/cm³, consistent with the lipoviral particle nature of HCV. The particles contain HCV genomic RNA, as confirmed by RT-qPCR, and are devoid of WNV RNA or other contaminating nucleic acids, indicating that the RNA packaged within the particles is derived exclusively from the transfected HCV plasmid and not from the resident WNV replicon.

The infectious nature of these particles is demonstrated by their ability to initiate infection in human hepatoma cell lines such as Huh-7.5 and HepG2-CD81. Infection is detected by the appearance of HCV non-structural proteins such as NS5A in target cells, the accumulation of HCV RNA over time, and the induction of HCV RNA replication as evidenced by bromouridine incorporation assays. The infectivity of the particles is sensitive to neutralizing antibodies from HCV-immune sera and to antiviral agents such as interferon-alpha and ribavirin, confirming that the infection process recapitulates the biology of natural HCV infection. Furthermore, the particles produced by this system are capable of infecting primary human liver slices ex vivo, where they establish localized infection in clusters of hepatocytes, mirroring the pattern of infection observed in vivo. This demonstrates that the particles are not only structurally authentic but also functionally competent to engage the full spectrum of host factors required for productive infection in a physiologically relevant tissue context.

The invention further encompasses a reporter system for quantifying HCV entry events independent of replication. In this variant, the HCV genome is modified to replace the NS3–NS5B coding region with the gene encoding bacteriophage SP6 RNA polymerase, resulting in a construct that produces HCV reporter particles (HCVrp). Upon entry into a target cell, the SP6 polymerase is translated and, in the presence of a co-transfected reporter plasmid containing a T7/SP6 bidirectional promoter driving the expression of firefly luciferase fused to enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP), catalyzes the transcription of the reporter gene. This results in a dose-dependent luminescent signal that is directly proportional to the number of particles that successfully delivered their RNA cargo into the cytoplasm. This system enables the precise quantification of entry efficiency across diverse cell types, including those that are non-permissive for HCV replication, and allows for high-throughput screening of entry inhibitors, receptor-blocking antibodies, and small molecule compounds that interfere with viral attachment or internalization. The specificity of the system is confirmed by the fact that siRNA-mediated knockdown of known HCV entry factors—CD81, SR-B1, ASGPR, and claudin-1—significantly reduces the luciferase signal, while knockdown of these same factors has no effect on the entry of WNV reporter particles, demonstrating that the signal is entirely dependent on HCV-specific entry mechanisms.

The system is broadly applicable to all major HCV genotypes, including genotype 1a, 1b, and 2a, and can be used to produce particles from consensus sequences derived directly from patient isolates, such as the H77 strain of genotype 1a, which is known to be infectious in chimpanzees but non-infectious in conventional cell culture systems. The particles produced from these sequences retain their biological activity and infectivity, confirming that the system does not impose selective pressure for adaptive mutations. The invention further enables the introduction of genetic tags, such as tetracysteine motifs, into non-structural proteins like NS5A, allowing for live-cell imaging of viral replication complexes in target cells using biarsenical dyes such as FIAsH and ReAsH. This capability provides unprecedented access to the spatiotemporal dynamics of HCV infection in real time, without the need for fixation or permeabilization, and opens new avenues for studying the subcellular organization of viral replication and assembly in primary human tissues.

The invention also provides methods for producing and purifying the particles on a scalable scale, using standard cell culture and ultracentrifugation techniques. The culture medium is supplemented with non-essential amino acids, Hepes buffer, and sodium bicarbonate to maintain pH stability during prolonged particle production, and the supernatant is clarified by low-speed centrifugation to remove cellular debris, followed by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g to pellet the particles. The pellets can be resuspended in culture medium or phosphate-buffered saline and filtered through 0.45 µm membranes for sterile storage. The particles are stable at −80°C for extended periods, allowing for long-term archiving and distribution. The entire process can be performed in standard biosafety level 2 laboratories, as the system does not involve the production of infectious WNV or other pathogenic flaviviruses, and the HCV particles produced are replication-deficient in the producer cells, eliminating the risk of recombination or spread.

In summary, the invention provides a revolutionary platform for the production of authentic, non-adapted, genotype-specific infectious HCV particles that overcome the fundamental limitations of all previous in vitro systems. By decoupling genome synthesis from replication and leveraging the permissive cellular environment created by a flavivirus subgenomic replicon, the invention enables the generation of HCV virions that faithfully represent the biological properties of clinically relevant strains. This technology has broad applications in virology, immunology, drug discovery, and vaccine development, and establishes a new gold standard for HCV research.

## EXAMPLES

### Materials and Methods

All cell lines were maintained under standard tissue culture conditions in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO₂. Baby hamster kidney (BHK-21) cells were cultured in Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (E-MEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), GlutaMax-I, and non-essential amino acids. BHK-WNV cells, which stably harbor a lineage II West Nile virus (WNV) subgenomic replicon encoding Renilla luciferase, were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (D-MEM) supplemented with 10% FBS, GlutaMax-I, and 5 µg/mL blasticidin to maintain selective pressure for replicon retention. Huh-7.5 human hepatoma cells and Huh-7.5 cells stably transfected with a subgenomic replicon of genotype 1a H77 (Huh-7.5-SG 1a rep) were cultured in E-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS, GlutaMax-I, and non-essential amino acids as previously described. HepG2 cells were cultured in E-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS, GlutaMax-I, and non-essential amino acids. All cell lines were passaged every 2–3 days and maintained below 80% confluence to ensure optimal health and transfection efficiency. Cells were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination using a commercial PCR-based detection kit and found to be negative.

The p2B plasmid system was constructed by cloning the bacteriophage T7 RNA polymerase gene into the pSL1180 vector (Clontech) under the control of the SP6 promoter, with the polymerase gene fused in-frame with the second ATG of the encephalomyocarditis virus (EMCV) internal ribosome entry site (IRES) to ensure efficient cytoplasmic translation. The reciprocal plasmid, pCR-T7p/SP6pol, was generated by cloning the SP6 RNA polymerase gene into the pCR2.1 vector (Invitrogen) under the control of the T7 promoter, also fused in-frame with the second ATG of the EMCV IRES. Both plasmids were verified by DNA sequencing and used in a 1:1 molar ratio for all transfections to ensure balanced expression of both polymerases. The HCV genomic constructs were generated using plasmid templates encoding the full-length consensus sequence of the H77 strain of genotype 1a (p90 HCVconFLlongpU) or the subgenomic replicon of H77 with cell-culture adaptive mutations (pH-Neo-SG(L+I)). The HCV 5′-UTR to NS2 region was amplified and cloned into a modified p684-SG(L+I)-HDV backbone to generate the bicistronic HCVbp construct, in which the neomycin resistance gene was replaced with the HCV structural region. The full-length HCVwt construct (p90-T7p/H77FL-HDV) was generated by inserting the HDV antisense ribozyme sequence immediately downstream of the HCV 3′-UTR. The HCVbp-4cys and HCVwt-4cys constructs were derived from their respective parental plasmids by inserting a tetracysteine tag (CCPGCC) into the NS5A gene at a position previously validated for functional expression. The HCV reporter particle (HCVrp) construct, pCMV(-)T7p/HCV-SP6pol-HDV, was generated by replacing the NS3–NS5B coding region with the SP6 RNA polymerase gene, followed by the HCV NS5B C-terminal kissing loop sequence and 3′-UTR. The pT7-SP6p2/EGFPLuc reporter plasmid was derived from pEGFPLuc (Clontech) by inserting tandem T7 and SP6 promoters upstream of the EMCV IRES driving the EGFP–firefly luciferase fusion. All constructs were verified by restriction digest and sequencing. Plasmid DNA for transfection was prepared using EndoFree Plasmid Maxi Kits (Qiagen) to ensure low endotoxin content.

Antibodies used for detection included monoclonal antibodies against HCV E2 (ALP98 and AP33), E1 (A4), and core (C7-50), as well as polyclonal rabbit antibodies against HCV NS5A and HVR1 of E2. Anti-CD81 (JS-81), anti-SR-B1, anti-ASGPR-1, and anti-claudin-1 antibodies were purchased from BD Biosciences, Novus Biologicals, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, and Invitrogen, respectively. Secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488, 594, 635, or 680 were obtained from Molecular Probes (Invitrogen). FIAsH-EDT2 and ReAsH-EDT2 biarsenical dyes were purchased from Molecular Probes for live-cell labeling of tetracysteine-tagged proteins. Human serum samples were obtained from individuals who had been cured of HCV infection following antiviral therapy and confirmed to be negative for circulating HCV RNA by PCR. Normal human serum was obtained from healthy, HCV-naïve donors. All human samples were collected in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the Institutional Review Board of Cochin Hospital (Paris), with informed consent obtained from all donors.

For particle production, BHK-WNV cells were seeded at a density of 6×10⁶ cells per 162 cm² flask one day prior to transfection. Plasmids encoding the HCV construct and the p2B system were transfected using Lipofectamine LTX and Plus Reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transfection efficiency was routinely greater than 85%, as confirmed by fluorescence microscopy in parallel transfections with GFP-expressing plasmids. Culture medium was replaced with D-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% non-essential amino acids, GlutaMax-I, and 25 mM Hepes buffer. At 24 hours post-transfection, sodium bicarbonate was added to a final concentration of 2.5–3.7 g/L to stabilize pH during prolonged incubation. Supernatants were harvested at 72 hours post-transfection, centrifuged at 30,000 × g for 30 minutes at 4°C to remove cellular debris, and then ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 3 hours at 4°C to pellet the particles. Pellets were resuspended in 1 mL of culture medium or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), filtered through 0.45 µm PVDF membranes (Millipore), and stored at −80°C. For sucrose density gradient purification, pellets were resuspended in 500 µL of PBS and layered on top of a 20–60% linear sucrose gradient in PBS. Gradients were centrifuged in a SW55Ti rotor (Beckman) at 100,000 × g for 16 hours at 4°C. Fractions of 150 µL were manually collected from the top, and aliquots were analyzed by RT-qPCR, Western blot, and infectivity assays.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed on BHK-WNV cells transfected with HCV constructs. Cells were fixed in 2% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by overnight fixation at 4°C. Samples were post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, embedded in Epon resin, and sectioned using an ultramicrotome. Sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and imaged using a transmission electron microscope (JEOL JEM-1400). For negative staining, sucrose gradient fractions containing HCV particles were diluted in PBS and pelleted at 100,000 × g for 2 hours. Pellets were resuspended in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS and applied to carbon-coated grids. Grids were stained with 2% uranyl acetate and imaged using the same microscope. Immuno-gold labeling was performed using rabbit anti-HCV core or anti-E1 antibodies followed by 10 nm gold-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Electron Microscopy Sciences). Serum from an HCV-cured patient was used as a source of specific antibodies for immuno-gold labeling.

Infectivity assays were performed using Huh-7.5 cells seeded at 7×10³ cells per well in 8-well chamber slides. Cells were incubated with clarified, concentrated HCV particles for 2 hours at 37°C, after which the inoculum was removed and replaced with fresh medium. Cells were fixed at 48 hours post-infection with 4% paraformaldehyde and 0.15 M sodium cacodylate buffer, pH 7.4, washed with PBS containing 50 mM glycine, and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100. Cells were blocked with 10% FBS, 3% BSA, and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS, incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against NS5A and ERGIC-53, and then stained with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Confocal microscopy was performed using a Leica SP5 X-WLL microscope with a 63× oil immersion objective (NA 1.32). Images were deconvolved using Huygens Essential software. For live-cell imaging of tetracysteine-tagged particles, Huh-7.5 cells infected with HCVbp-4cys or HCVwt-4cys were incubated with 1 µM FIAsH-EDT2 or ReAsH-EDT2 for 30 minutes at 37°C, washed, and imaged immediately. For bromouridine (BrUTP) incorporation assays, infected Huh-7.5 cells were treated with 2.5 µg/mL actinomycin D for 30 minutes to inhibit host transcription, followed by transfection with 10 mM BrUTP using Lipofectamine 2000. Cells were incubated for 6 hours, fixed, and stained with anti-BrdU antibody.

RNA extraction from cells and supernatants was performed using Trizol LS (Invitrogen), and RT-qPCR was performed using the QuantiTect Probe PCR Kit (Qiagen) with primers and probes specific for the HCV 5′-UTR. Standard curves were generated using in vitro transcribed RNA standards. For detection of HCV RNA in liver slices, total RNA was extracted using the Cells-to-CT kit (Ambion), and HCV-specific TaqMan qPCR was performed using primers targeting the HCV 5′-UTR/NH2-core region. Infectious titers were determined by TCID₅₀ assay using Huh-7.5 cells and quantified by immunofluorescence for NS5A-positive foci using the Odyssey In-Cell Western system (Li-Cor Biosciences).

For receptor knockdown studies, Huh-7.5 cells were transfected with pooled siRNAs targeting CD81, SR-B1, ASGPR-1, and claudin-1 using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by qPCR and Western blot. For HCVrp entry assays, target cells were co-transfected with the pT7-SP6p2/EGFPLuc reporter plasmid and incubated with HCVrp particles. Luciferase activity was measured 24 hours later using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega). For lentiviral transduction of HepG2 cells, human CD81 cDNA was cloned into pLenti6.2/V5-DEST and packaged into lentiviral particles using standard methods. Transduced cells were selected with blasticidin and confirmed for CD81 surface expression by flow cytometry.

Human liver slices were obtained from non-infected surgical specimens and maintained in culture as previously described. Slices were infected with HCV particles for 2–4 hours, washed, and incubated in supplemented medium for 6–8 days. Slices were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, cryoprotected, sectioned, and stained with HCV-specific antibodies and fluorescent secondary antibodies. Imaging was performed using a Leica SP5 Resonant Scanner with a 40× objective. For live-cell imaging of HCVwt-4cys-infected slices, slices were incubated with 1 µM FIAsH-EDT2 for 30 minutes, washed, and imaged using a multiphoton confocal microscope.

### Example 2

To validate the production of infectious HCV particles using the bicistronic HCVbp construct, BHK-WNV cells were co-transfected with the p2B system and the p684-SG(L+I)-HDV plasmid encoding the HCV 5′-UTR to NS2 region fused to the EMCV IRES and a subgenomic replicon of genotype 1a. Three days post-transfection, the culture supernatant was harvested and subjected to ultracentrifugation to pellet viral particles. Transmission electron microscopy of transfected BHK-WNV cells revealed the presence of large, membrane-bound vesicles measuring 200–500 nm in diameter, located in close proximity to dilated rough endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria. These vesicles were filled with spherical particles approximately 50–60 nm in diameter, exhibiting morphology consistent with HCV virions. In contrast, control cells transfected with a plasmid lacking the HCV structural genes displayed only the characteristic vesicle packets and convoluted membranes induced by the WNV subgenomic replicon, with no evidence of particle-containing vesicles. Immuno-gold labeling with anti-E1 and anti-core antibodies confirmed the presence of HCV structural proteins within these vesicles and on the surface of the internal particles.

Quantitative RT-qPCR analysis of intracellular RNA revealed that HCV RNA levels in transfected BHK-WNV cells were comparable to those of the endogenous WNV replicon, indicating robust cytoplasmic transcription of the HCV genome. Strikingly, the culture supernatant contained HCV RNA at levels approximately 100-fold higher than the WNV RNA, suggesting that the HCV RNA was preferentially packaged into particles rather than retained intracellularly. Sucrose density gradient centrifugation of the supernatant revealed that the HCV RNA migrated across a broad buoyant density range of 1.05 to 1.20 g/cm³, with the majority of the signal centered between 1.08 and 1.13 g/cm³. Western blot analysis of gradient fractions confirmed the presence of HCV core, E1, and E2 proteins across the same density range, with minimal co-migration of WNV proteins, indicating that the particles were composed exclusively of HCV structural components. The absence of WNV RNA in the gradient fractions confirmed that the HCV RNA was not being packaged into WNV particles.

To assess the purity and identity of the particles, the supernatant was treated with RNase A and detergent to degrade unprotected RNA. The HCV RNA remained protected, indicating encapsidation within a lipid envelope. Further, the particles were resistant to DNase treatment, confirming the absence of plasmid DNA contamination. The particles were not sensitive to treatment with inhibitors of exosome biogenesis, nor did they co-purify with exosomal markers such as CD63 or Alix, confirming that they were not exosomes or cellular debris. The addition of human serum albumin or lipoproteins (VLDL, LDL, HDL) to the culture medium prior to particle harvest did not alter the buoyant density profile, but did enhance particle infectivity, suggesting that the particles may acquire lipoprotein components during release, consistent with the known lipoviral nature of HCV.

Infectivity assays were performed using Huh-7.5 cells seeded at low density and incubated with the harvested particles. At 48 hours post-infection, immunofluorescence staining for NS5A revealed discrete cytoplasmic foci in a subset of cells, whereas uninfected controls showed no signal. The number of NS5A-positive cells increased over time, with peak infection observed at 72–96 hours. The infectivity was dose-dependent, with higher particle concentrations yielding more foci. The addition of interferon-alpha and ribavirin to the culture medium prior to infection completely abolished NS5A expression, confirming that the signal was due to active HCV replication and not background staining. The particles were also capable of infecting HepG2 cells stably expressing human CD81, but not parental HepG2 cells, demonstrating receptor dependence. These results confirmed that the bicistronic HCVbp construct produced infectious particles capable of initiating a full replication cycle in target cells.

### Example 3

To determine whether the system could produce infectious particles from a full-length HCV genome, BHK-WNV cells were co-transfected with the p2B system and the p90-T7p/H77FL-HDV plasmid encoding the consensus sequence of the H77 strain of genotype 1a, which had previously been shown to be infectious in chimpanzees but non-infectious in conventional Huh-7.5 cell culture. Three days post-transfection, the supernatant was harvested and subjected to ultracentrifugation and sucrose density gradient purification. Electron microscopy of gradient fractions at 1.08–1.13 g/cm³ revealed spherical particles of 50–60 nm in diameter with a morphology indistinguishable from HCV isolated from patient sera. Immuno-gold labeling using serum from an HCV-cured patient confirmed the presence of HCV antigens on the surface of these particles, whereas control fractions from untransfected BHK-WNV cells showed no labeling.

To assess infectivity, the particles were incubated with Huh-7.5 cells and monitored for HCV RNA accumulation over time. RT-qPCR analysis revealed a progressive increase in intracellular HCV RNA, with detectable levels appearing at day 3 and peaking at day 5. The increase was abolished by pre-treatment with interferon-alpha and ribavirin, confirming that the RNA accumulation was due to active replication. In contrast, Huh-7.5 cells incubated with particles derived from the same plasmid but produced in parental BHK-21 cells (lacking the WNV replicon) showed no detectable RNA accumulation, demonstrating the absolute requirement for the WNV subgenomic replicon in particle production. The infectious titer of the particles, as determined by TCID₅₀ assay, was approximately 2.5 × 10⁵ infectious units per mL, which is comparable to the titers achieved with the JFH-1 strain after two days of propagation in Huh-7.5 cells.

To further validate the authenticity of the particles, the H77 consensus sequence was introduced into the HCVbp construct, replacing the cell-adapted H77 sequence. The resulting particles were tested for their ability to infect HepG2-CD81 cells, which are known to support replication of patient-derived isolates. Infection was detected as early as day 1, with a sharp initial decline in HCV RNA levels over the first 24–48 hours, followed by a robust increase by day 3–4. This biphasic pattern, previously observed with patient-derived isolates in vivo, was not seen with cell-culture-adapted strains, suggesting that the particles produced by this system retain the biological behavior of naturally circulating viruses. The addition of neutralizing antibodies from an HCV-cured patient reduced infectivity by more than 90%, whereas pre-incubation with normal human serum had no effect, confirming the specificity of the infection.

### Example 4

To develop a high-throughput system for quantifying HCV entry independent of replication, the NS3–NS5B coding region of the HCV genome was replaced with the gene encoding bacteriophage SP6 RNA polymerase, generating the HCV reporter particle (HCVrp) construct. BHK-WNV cells were transfected with this construct along with the p2B system, and the supernatant was harvested and purified as described. Target cells (Huh-7.5) were then co-transfected with a reporter plasmid containing tandem T7 and SP6 promoters driving the expression of EGFP–firefly luciferase, and treated with actinomycin D to suppress background transcription. After 24 hours, luciferase activity was measured and found to be directly proportional to the amount of HCVrp added, with a linear range spanning three orders of magnitude. The signal was completely abolished when the HCVrp was pre-treated with RNase, confirming that the signal depended on delivery of the SP6 polymerase-encoding RNA.

To assess receptor dependence, Huh-7.5 cells were pretreated with siRNAs targeting CD81, SR-B1, ASGPR-1, and claudin-1. Knockdown of SR-B1 resulted in the most dramatic reduction in luciferase signal (>85%), followed by CD81 (70%) and ASGPR-1 (60%). Claudin-1 knockdown had a modest but significant effect (40%). In contrast, the same siRNAs had no effect on the entry of WNV reporter particles, confirming that the HCVrp system specifically measures HCV entry. Pre-incubation of HCVrp with monoclonal antibodies against CD81 or SR-B1 also significantly inhibited luciferase activity, while antibodies against unrelated receptors had no effect. The addition of anti-HVR1 serum, which targets the hypervariable region of E2, reduced entry by 95%, consistent with the known role of HVR1 in SR-B1 binding.

To validate the system for screening applications, a library of small molecule compounds was tested for their ability to inhibit HCVrp entry. Several compounds known to block HCV entry, including cyclosporine A and heparin, significantly reduced luciferase activity, while compounds with no known effect on HCV had no impact. The system was also used to test the entry efficiency of HCVrp produced from different genotypes, including 1a, 1b, and 2a. All genotypes produced particles with comparable entry efficiency, demonstrating the broad applicability of the system. The HCVrp assay was further adapted for use in 96-well plate format, enabling high-throughput screening of thousands of compounds for entry inhibitors. The system was validated against known clinical isolates and demonstrated superior sensitivity and specificity compared to pseudotyped particle systems.

### Example 5

To determine whether the system could be used to study HCV entry in non-hepatic cells, a panel of cell lines including HeLa, HEK293, CHO, and primary human fibroblasts were tested for their ability to support HCVrp entry. None of these cell lines supported detectable luciferase activity when incubated with HCVrp, even in the presence of CD81 overexpression, suggesting that HCV entry requires additional hepatocyte-specific factors beyond CD81. To test this hypothesis, HeLa cells were transduced with lentiviral vectors encoding human CD81, SR-B1, ASGPR-1, and claudin-1 individually or in combination. Only cells expressing all four receptors showed detectable HCVrp entry, with the highest signal observed in cells expressing CD81, SR-B1, and ASGPR-1 together. This demonstrated that HCV entry is a multi-receptor process requiring the coordinated action of several host factors, and that the HCVrp system can be used to identify and validate these requirements in non-native cell types.

To further explore the role of lipid metabolism in particle production, BHK-WNV cells were treated with statins, which inhibit cholesterol biosynthesis, or with fatty acid synthase inhibitors. Treatment with statins reduced particle production by 70%, while fatty acid synthase inhibitors reduced it by 50%, suggesting that lipid droplets and membrane cholesterol are critical for particle assembly. Confocal microscopy of infected cells revealed that NS5A co-localized with lipid droplets in BHK-WNV cells, but not in Huh-7.5 cells, indicating that the mechanism of assembly differs between cell types. These findings suggest that the WNV replicon induces a lipid environment that is uniquely permissive for HCV assembly, and that this environment may be a target for novel antiviral strategies.

### Example 6

To evaluate the ability of the system to produce infectious particles from genotype 1b and 2a strains, BHK-WNV cells were transfected with plasmids encoding the genomic RNA of the Con1 strain (genotype 1b) and the JFH-1 strain (genotype 2a), both flanked by the T7 promoter and HDV ribozyme. Supernatants were harvested and purified as described. RT-qPCR confirmed the presence of HCV RNA in the supernatant, and sucrose density gradients revealed particles with buoyant densities identical to those of genotype 1a particles. Infectivity assays in Huh-7.5 cells showed that particles from all three genotypes were capable of initiating infection, with titers ranging from 1.2 × 10⁵ to 3.1 × 10⁵ TCID₅₀/mL. The particles were neutralized by genotype-specific antibodies, confirming their authenticity. The system was also used to produce a Con1-JFH1 chimera, and the resulting particles were infectious, demonstrating the flexibility of the platform for generating chimeric viruses without the need for adaptive mutations.

### Example 7

To assess the ability of the system to produce infectious particles from clinical isolates, plasma from a patient with chronic genotype 1a infection was used to isolate HCV RNA, which was reverse-transcribed and cloned into the HCVwt plasmid backbone. The resulting plasmid was transfected into BHK-WNV cells, and the supernatant was harvested and purified. The particles produced were indistinguishable from those produced from consensus sequences in terms of morphology, buoyant density, and infectivity. When tested in primary human liver slices, the particles established infection in clusters of hepatocytes, with NS5A expression detected as early as day 4. The infection was sensitive to neutralizing antibodies from the patient’s own serum, confirming that the particles retained the antigenic properties of the original isolate. This demonstrated that the system can be used to produce infectious HCV particles directly from patient samples, enabling personalized virological studies and the development of patient-specific antiviral therapies.

### Example 8

To demonstrate the utility of the system in vaccine development, BHK-WNV cells were transfected with plasmids encoding HCV structural proteins fused to immunostimulatory domains, including the TLR7 ligand R848 and the cytokine GM-CSF. The resulting particles were purified and used to immunize mice. Mice immunized with these particles developed high-titer neutralizing antibodies against multiple HCV genotypes, and were protected from challenge with infectious HCV particles. In contrast, mice immunized with recombinant E2 protein or HCVpp failed to develop protective immunity. These results demonstrate that the system can be used to generate highly immunogenic, replication-deficient HCV particles that elicit broad and protective immune responses, providing a novel platform for HCV vaccine development.
