# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### Technical Field

The present invention relates generally to non-volatile memory devices and, more specifically, to spin-current-based magnetic memory elements and arrays that utilize spin-orbit torque for magnetization switching without direct current passage through the magnetic tunnel junction. The invention is particularly directed to a novel class of memory architectures that achieve ultra-high write endurance exceeding ten trillion write cycles while maintaining stable tunnel magnetoresistance characteristics, thereby enabling their deployment in edge artificial intelligence systems, embedded computing platforms, and next-generation memory hierarchies where power efficiency, longevity, and data retention are critical. The invention further encompasses the structural and material configurations of spin current magnetization rotational elements that isolate write current pathways from the tunnel barrier region of the magnetic tunnel junction, thus eliminating the primary degradation mechanism that has historically limited the endurance of conventional spin-transfer-torque magnetic random-access memory devices.

### Description of Related Art

The rapid proliferation of Internet of Things (IoT) systems and edge artificial intelligence applications has placed unprecedented demands on memory technologies to deliver low power consumption, high endurance, non-volatility, and fast access times. Traditional complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS)-based memory architectures, including static random-access memory (SRAM) and embedded flash, are increasingly inadequate to meet these requirements due to their inherent power inefficiencies, limited write endurance, or volatility. In response, magnetic random-access memory (MRAM) has emerged as a leading candidate for next-generation memory solutions, particularly in its spin-transfer-torque (STT)-MRAM variant, which leverages spin-polarized currents to switch the magnetization state of a free magnetic layer within a magnetic tunnel junction (MTJ). STT-MRAM offers non-volatility, scalability, and reasonable write speeds, and has already begun to replace embedded flash in certain applications due to its improved endurance and lower write current compared to flash memory.

However, fundamental physical limitations have constrained the scalability and reliability of STT-MRAM. In particular, the write operation in STT-MRAM requires the passage of current directly through the thin insulating tunnel barrier of the MTJ, which inevitably induces Joule heating and dielectric breakdown over repeated cycling. This degradation mechanism manifests as progressive thinning, pinhole formation, or chemical diffusion within the tunnel barrier, leading to a gradual reduction in tunnel magnetoresistance (TMR) ratio and eventual catastrophic failure. Even with the adoption of perpendicular magnetic anisotropy and optimized tunnel barrier materials such as magnesium oxide (MgO), the write endurance of STT-MRAM devices remains fundamentally capped below 10¹² cycles under practical operating conditions, falling short of the endurance required to replace SRAM in high-performance cache hierarchies.

To circumvent these limitations, researchers have proposed alternative switching mechanisms that decouple the write current path from the tunnel barrier. Among these, spin-orbit torque (SOT)-based magnetization switching has garnered significant attention. SOT-MRAM architectures utilize the spin Hall effect or Rashba-Edelstein effect in a heavy metal layer adjacent to the magnetic free layer to generate a spin current that exerts a torque on the magnetization of the free layer, thereby enabling switching without direct current flow through the MTJ. This three-terminal architecture—comprising a separate spin current generation line, a magnetic tunnel junction, and a read path—promises to eliminate the primary source of tunnel barrier degradation, thereby enabling theoretically unlimited write endurance. Furthermore, SOT switching has been demonstrated to operate on sub-nanosecond timescales, offering superior speed compared to STT-MRAM, particularly at low pulse widths.

Despite these theoretical advantages, prior art has failed to provide experimental validation of the endurance potential of SOT-MRAM at the scale required for commercial deployment. Most reported SOT-MRAM devices have been limited to demonstration of switching functionality under idealized conditions, with endurance tests rarely extending beyond 10⁹ cycles. Even in cases where endurance beyond 10¹⁰ cycles was claimed, the integrity of the MTJ structure was not rigorously verified post-cycling, leaving open the possibility that degradation occurred elsewhere in the device structure. Moreover, the materials and geometries employed in prior SOT-MRAM devices were not optimized for long-term reliability under high-current-density operation. In particular, the use of high-resistivity spin current generation lines—often tungsten or tantalum layers deposited under conditions that enhance spin Hall efficiency—has introduced a new failure mode: resistive heating-induced structural failure of the spin current line itself. This has resulted in two previously unreported and unaddressed failure mechanisms: electrical open circuits caused by localized melting and rupture of the spin current line, and electrical short circuits caused by metal migration and bridging between the spin current line and the underlying or adjacent magnetic layers.

Furthermore, prior designs have not systematically addressed the asymmetry in switching probability between opposite current polarities, which arises from stray magnetic fields induced by the synthetic antiferromagnetic (SAF) pinned layer or from interfacial Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interactions. These asymmetries lead to write errors and necessitate higher write currents to ensure reliable switching, inadvertently accelerating the degradation of the spin current line. Additionally, no prior work has demonstrated a fully integrated, functional array of SOT-based memory elements with end-to-end write/read circuitry capable of sustaining high-cycle endurance under realistic operating conditions. The absence of such integrated demonstrations has left a critical gap between theoretical promise and practical viability.

Consequently, there exists a long-felt but unmet need for a spin-current-type memory device that achieves write endurance exceeding 10¹² cycles while preserving the integrity of the magnetic tunnel junction, that identifies and mitigates the newly discovered failure modes associated with the spin current generation line, and that demonstrates scalable array operation with robust read/write circuitry under ambient conditions. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel spin current magnetization rotational element architecture that decouples the write current path from the tunnel barrier, employs a precisely engineered heavy metal spin current line with controlled resistivity and geometry to suppress Joule heating, and incorporates structural and material innovations that prevent electrical open and short failures. The invention further demonstrates, for the first time, the feasibility of a fully functional 8×8 array of such elements operating with sustained endurance beyond ten trillion cycles, thereby establishing a new paradigm for non-volatile memory design in edge AI and high-reliability computing systems.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

### SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a spin current magnetization rotational element for non-volatile memory applications, comprising a magnetic tunnel junction and a spin current generation line disposed in close proximity but electrically isolated from the magnetic tunnel junction, wherein the spin current generation line is configured to carry a write current that generates a spin current via spin-orbit torque, and wherein the spin current is directed perpendicularly to the magnetization axis of the free layer of the magnetic tunnel junction, thereby inducing magnetization switching without the passage of write current through the tunnel barrier of the magnetic tunnel junction. The magnetic tunnel junction includes a free magnetic layer, a tunnel barrier layer, and a synthetic antiferromagnetic pinned layer, each arranged in a stacked configuration such that the magnetization of the free layer lies in-plane and is orthogonal to the direction of the write current in the spin current generation line. The spin current generation line is formed from a heavy metal material with a resistivity of at least 350 microohm-centimeters and a thickness of less than five nanometers, and is patterned into a linear structure with a length no greater than one micrometer and a width no greater than four hundred nanometers, such that the current density during write operations is sufficient to induce magnetization switching but insufficient to cause Joule heating-induced structural failure. The magnetic tunnel junction exhibits a tunnel magnetoresistance ratio of at least eighty percent after one hundred billion write cycles and maintains a stable resistance state with no measurable degradation of the tunnel barrier interface, as confirmed by cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy. The spin current generation line exhibits no evidence of electrical open or electrical short failure after one trillion write cycles under ambient conditions, and the magnetic tunnel junction remains structurally intact with preserved interfacial clarity between the free layer and the tunnel barrier, and between the tunnel barrier and the pinned layer. The invention further includes a memory array comprising a plurality of such spin current magnetization rotational elements arranged in a two-dimensional grid, each element independently addressable via a dedicated spin current generation line and a shared read path, wherein the array is operated under a write protocol that alternates write current polarity to achieve balanced switching probability, and wherein the array sustains a survival rate of at least ninety-five percent of elements after one hundred billion write cycles. The invention further comprises a write/read circuit system integrated with the array, comprising a pulse generator configured to deliver write pulses with widths between one nanosecond and twenty nanoseconds, a read circuit configured to measure tunnel magnetoresistance using a direct current bias below one microampere, and a control unit configured to execute a write endurance protocol that cycles between write and read phases with a cycle time of at least one hundred nanoseconds, wherein the write endurance protocol is executed for a minimum of one trillion cycles without degradation of the tunnel magnetoresistance ratio or the structural integrity of the spin current generation line. The invention thus enables a new class of non-volatile memory devices that achieve unprecedented write endurance, eliminate tunnel barrier degradation, and provide a scalable architecture suitable for integration into edge AI processors and high-reliability embedded systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Spin Current Magnetization Rotational Element

The spin current magnetization rotational element of the present invention represents a fundamental departure from conventional magnetic memory architectures by entirely decoupling the mechanism of magnetization switching from the physical integrity of the magnetic tunnel junction. Unlike prior art devices in which the write current must traverse the thin insulating tunnel barrier, thereby inducing irreversible damage through Joule heating and electromigration, the present invention confines the write current to a dedicated spin current generation line that is physically and electrically separated from the magnetic tunnel junction. This separation is not merely a geometric arrangement but a structural and functional innovation that redefines the operational principles of spin-based memory. The spin current generation line, fabricated from a heavy metal material such as tungsten, tantalum, or a tungsten-tantalum alloy, is positioned adjacent to the free magnetic layer of the magnetic tunnel junction, with a separation distance of less than two nanometers, ensuring efficient spin current injection via the spin Hall effect. The heavy metal material is selected not merely for its high spin Hall angle but for its specific resistivity, which is precisely controlled during deposition to lie between 350 and 450 microohm-centimeters. This resistivity range is critical: if the resistivity is too low, the spin Hall efficiency is insufficient to generate the required spin current density for reliable switching; if it is too high, the resulting Joule heating during write operations leads to thermal runaway, causing structural failure of the spin current line. Through extensive experimentation, it has been determined that a resistivity of approximately 400 microohm-centimeters strikes the optimal balance between spin current generation efficiency and thermal stability under high-current-density operation.

The spin current generation line is patterned into a narrow, elongated structure with a thickness of three nanometers, a length of seven hundred nanometers, and a width of three hundred sixty nanometers. This geometry is not arbitrary; it is the result of a systematic optimization process that minimizes the total resistance of the line while maximizing the current density delivered to the interface with the free layer. The length of the line is constrained to be less than one micrometer to ensure that the thermal diffusion length during a write pulse does not exceed the lateral dimensions of the line, thereby preventing heat accumulation at the ends. The width is selected to be greater than three hundred nanometers to ensure that the current density remains below the threshold for electromigration-induced void formation, yet narrow enough to enable high-density array integration. The orientation of the spin current generation line is aligned such that the direction of the write current flows perpendicular to the easy axis of magnetization of the free layer. This orthogonal configuration is essential for maximizing the torque efficiency of the injected spin current, as the spin angular momentum vector generated by the spin Hall effect is oriented perpendicular to both the charge current and the crystallographic axis of the heavy metal. In this configuration, the spin current exerts a torque that rotates the magnetization vector of the free layer around an axis perpendicular to both the spin current direction and the magnetization direction, thereby enabling deterministic switching between parallel and antiparallel states without the need for an external magnetic field.

The magnetic tunnel junction itself is constructed as a top-pinned structure, comprising a free magnetic layer, a tunnel barrier layer, and a synthetic antiferromagnetic pinned layer, all deposited sequentially without breaking vacuum to preserve interfacial purity. The free magnetic layer is composed of a cobalt-iron-boron alloy with a thickness of approximately one point five nanometers, chosen to provide sufficient magnetic volume for thermal stability while maintaining in-plane magnetic anisotropy. The in-plane anisotropy is further enhanced by the elliptical shape of the free layer, which is patterned into an ellipse with a major axis of three hundred sixty nanometers and a minor axis of one hundred twenty nanometers. This shape anisotropy, combined with the intrinsic magneto-crystalline anisotropy of the CoFeB alloy, ensures that the magnetization remains locked in the plane of the film, eliminating the need for perpendicular magnetic anisotropy and its associated complexity. The tunnel barrier layer is formed from a single crystalline magnesium oxide film with a thickness of one point two nanometers, deposited by reactive sputtering under ultra-high vacuum conditions to minimize oxygen vacancies and defect density. The synthetic antiferromagnetic pinned layer consists of a stack of cobalt-iron-boron and ruthenium layers, designed to produce a net zero magnetic moment while maintaining a strong exchange bias with the antiferromagnetic reference layer, which is composed of iridium-manganese. The iridium-manganese layer is deposited at a temperature of three hundred degrees Celsius to ensure strong antiferromagnetic ordering, and is capped with a tantalum layer to prevent oxidation during subsequent processing.

The critical innovation of the present invention lies in the structural integrity of the interfaces between the spin current generation line and the free layer, and between the free layer and the tunnel barrier. Cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy performed after one trillion write cycles reveals no evidence of interfacial diffusion, delamination, or amorphization. The boundary between the tungsten spin current line and the cobalt-iron-boron free layer remains atomically sharp, with no indication of alloying or intermixing. Similarly, the interface between the free layer and the magnesium oxide tunnel barrier exhibits no signs of oxidation, void formation, or roughening. This is in stark contrast to conventional STT-MRAM devices, where even after one hundred billion cycles, the tunnel barrier typically exhibits measurable thinning and increased interfacial roughness. The preservation of these interfaces is attributed to the complete absence of write current flow through the tunnel barrier. In STT-MRAM, the write current must pass through the tunnel barrier, generating localized hot spots that accelerate atomic migration and defect formation. In the present invention, the write current flows entirely within the spin current generation line, and the spin current—composed of spin-polarized electrons—interacts with the free layer through spin transfer torque, without any direct charge injection into the tunnel barrier. This fundamental distinction eliminates the primary degradation mechanism that has plagued magnetic memory for decades.

The switching mechanism of the spin current magnetization rotational element is governed by the spin-orbit torque generated at the interface between the heavy metal and the free magnetic layer. When a write pulse is applied to the spin current generation line, a charge current flows along the length of the line, generating a transverse spin current due to the spin Hall effect. This spin current, composed predominantly of electrons with spin angular momentum aligned perpendicular to the direction of charge flow, is injected into the free layer, where it exerts a torque on the magnetization vector. The torque can be described by the equation τ = ħ/2e × θ_SH × (J_c × m), where τ is the torque density, ħ is the reduced Planck constant, e is the elementary charge, θ_SH is the spin Hall angle of the heavy metal, J_c is the charge current density, and m is the unit vector of the magnetization. For tungsten, with a spin Hall angle of approximately 0.1, a charge current density of six point seven times ten to the seventh amperes per square centimeter generates a spin current density sufficient to overcome the magnetic anisotropy energy barrier of the free layer and induce a full 180-degree magnetization reversal. The switching time is determined by the pulse width of the write current, with reliable switching achieved at pulse widths as short as one nanosecond. The switching probability is highly symmetric for positive and negative current polarities when the stray magnetic field from the synthetic antiferromagnetic pinned layer is minimized. In the present invention, the shift field caused by the stray field from the SAF-pinned layer is measured to be approximately negative sixty oersteds. This asymmetry, if uncorrected, leads to unequal switching probabilities between the parallel-to-antiparallel and antiparallel-to-parallel transitions, requiring higher write currents to ensure reliable switching of the less favorable direction. To mitigate this, the composition and thickness of the SAF-pinned layer are precisely engineered to reduce the net stray field, and the write protocol is designed to alternate current polarity in a balanced manner, ensuring that each magnetic state receives equal exposure to write stress.

The endurance performance of the spin current magnetization rotational element has been rigorously tested under accelerated conditions. A write endurance protocol was implemented in which a write pulse of ten nanoseconds duration was applied at a current density of six point seven times ten to the seventh amperes per square centimeter, followed by a one hundred nanosecond wait period, resulting in a total cycle time of one hundred ten nanoseconds. This protocol was repeated for one hundred billion, two hundred billion, five hundred billion, and one trillion write cycles. At each interval, the tunnel magnetoresistance ratio was measured using a direct current bias of five hundred nanoamperes, and the resistance state was verified to remain stable. The TMR ratio, which was initially measured at ninety-two percent, remained above ninety percent even after one trillion cycles, with no measurable degradation in the magnitude or stability of the resistance states. The resistance drift observed over time was minimal, less than two percent, and was attributed to minor thermal fluctuations rather than structural degradation. In contrast, when the write current density was increased to eight point three times ten to the seventh amperes per square centimeter to accelerate testing, a new failure mode emerged. At this higher current density, the spin current generation line exhibited two distinct failure mechanisms: electrical open and electrical short. Electrical open failures were characterized by a sudden and permanent increase in the resistance of the spin current line, accompanied by a complete loss of switching capability. Cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy of these failed devices revealed a clear rupture in the tungsten line, with a visible void and fractured edges, indicating localized melting due to Joule heating. The failure occurred not at the center of the line but near the ends, where current crowding and thermal accumulation were greatest. Electrical short failures, on the other hand, were characterized by a sudden and permanent decrease in resistance, resulting in a low-resistance path between the spin current line and the underlying free layer. Cross-sectional imaging revealed a metallic bridge extending from the side wall of the tungsten line to the free layer, suggesting that the tungsten material had migrated under the influence of high temperature and current density, forming a conductive filament. These failure modes were never observed in devices operated at or below six point seven times ten to the seventh amperes per square centimeter, even after one trillion cycles. This demonstrates that the present invention operates within a safe current density window that avoids the thermal and electromigration thresholds of the spin current generation line.

The implications of these findings are profound. Prior art SOT-MRAM devices have assumed that the magnetic tunnel junction is the primary source of endurance limitation, and therefore focused their efforts on improving the tunnel barrier or the free layer. The present invention demonstrates that, in fact, the spin current generation line is the critical component determining long-term reliability. By optimizing the material, geometry, and operating conditions of the spin current generation line, it is possible to achieve endurance levels that were previously thought impossible. The invention further demonstrates that the magnetic tunnel junction, when isolated from write current, is inherently stable over trillions of cycles. This is a revolutionary insight that overturns the conventional wisdom in magnetic memory design. The invention thus provides a clear path forward for the development of non-volatile memory devices that can replace SRAM in cache hierarchies, serve as universal memory in embedded systems, and enable persistent computing in edge AI applications where power and longevity are paramount.

The scalability of the invention is further demonstrated by the successful fabrication and operation of an eight-by-eight array of spin current magnetization rotational elements. Each element in the array is independently addressable via its own dedicated spin current generation line, while the read path is shared through a common bit line connected to the magnetic tunnel junctions. The array is integrated with a custom analog write/read circuit controlled by a field-programmable gate array, which generates precise write pulses with programmable width and amplitude, and measures the resistance state of each element using a low-current read protocol. The write endurance test was conducted on the entire array, with each element subjected to the same one-trillion-cycle protocol. The survival rate of the array was measured at ninety-six percent after one trillion cycles, with only two elements failing due to electrical open in the spin current line. These failures occurred at the same current density that caused failure in the single-element device, confirming that the failure mechanism is intrinsic to the material and geometry of the spin current line, and not a result of array-level parasitics or crosstalk. The remaining sixty-two elements exhibited no degradation in TMR ratio, no increase in read error rate, and no change in switching threshold. This demonstrates that the invention is not merely a single-device curiosity but a scalable architecture capable of supporting large-scale memory arrays.

The invention further includes a method of operation that ensures long-term reliability by balancing the write stress on each magnetic state. Rather than applying a fixed current polarity for each write operation, the protocol alternates the polarity of the write pulse in a cyclic manner: for every ten billion write cycles, five billion are applied with positive current density to switch from parallel to antiparallel, and five billion are applied with negative current density to switch from antiparallel to parallel. This balanced approach ensures that neither magnetic state is subjected to disproportionate stress, thereby minimizing the risk of asymmetric degradation. The protocol further includes a periodic refresh cycle, in which each element is read and, if necessary, rewritten to its intended state, to correct for any minor drift caused by thermal fluctuations. This refresh cycle is executed once every ten million write cycles and requires no additional power beyond the read current, making it compatible with ultra-low-power operation.

In summary, the spin current magnetization rotational element of the present invention represents a paradigm shift in non-volatile memory design. It eliminates the fundamental degradation mechanism of tunnel barrier failure by decoupling the write current path from the magnetic tunnel junction. It introduces a new set of design rules for the spin current generation line, defining the precise material, geometry, and operating conditions required to achieve unprecedented endurance. It demonstrates, for the first time, the feasibility of a fully functional memory array operating beyond one trillion write cycles. And it establishes a new benchmark for reliability in memory technology, one that is not based on theoretical projections but on empirical, reproducible, and scalable experimental evidence. The invention thus provides the foundation for a new generation of memory devices that will enable the next wave of artificial intelligence, edge computing, and ubiquitous sensing applications.
