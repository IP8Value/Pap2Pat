# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to systems and methods for the early detection of impending relapse in individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia through the remote, continuous, and periodic monitoring of specific psychiatric symptoms. More particularly, the invention provides a technology-enabled framework for identifying a distinct subset of symptom domains—derived from the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS)—that exhibit statistically significant and temporally consistent patterns of worsening in the days immediately preceding a clinical relapse. These symptom domains, when tracked with sufficient frequency and precision via mobile or wearable digital platforms, enable the prediction of relapse with clinically meaningful lead time, allowing for timely therapeutic interventions that may prevent full-blown psychotic decompensation. The invention further encompasses algorithmic models that interpret longitudinal symptom trajectories, distinguish between normal symptom fluctuation and pre-relapse escalation, and trigger automated clinical alerts based on predefined thresholds of symptom change. This approach represents a paradigm shift from traditional episodic clinical assessments to a dynamic, data-driven model of psychiatric care, where monitoring is not merely observational but predictive and actionable. The system is designed for deployment in outpatient settings, home environments, and telepsychiatry platforms, particularly in regions where access to psychiatric professionals is limited, and where the burden of frequent in-person evaluations is unsustainable. By focusing on a targeted set of symptoms that are both highly predictive and feasible for self-reporting or passive sensing, the invention overcomes the practical limitations of comprehensive psychiatric instruments while preserving clinical validity. The invention is applicable to individuals stabilized on antipsychotic medication, those transitioning between treatment regimens, and those at high risk of non-adherence, thereby addressing a critical unmet need in the long-term management of schizophrenia.

## BACKGROUND

Schizophrenia is a chronic and debilitating psychiatric disorder characterized by recurrent episodes of psychosis, cognitive decline, social withdrawal, and functional impairment. Despite advances in pharmacological treatment, a substantial proportion of patients experience repeated relapses, often without warning, leading to hospitalization, loss of independence, and increased risk of suicide. The economic and emotional toll of these relapses is immense, with healthcare systems bearing the cost of emergency interventions, prolonged hospital stays, and lost productivity, while families endure the psychological strain of witnessing progressive deterioration. Current clinical practice relies heavily on periodic outpatient visits, typically scheduled at monthly or longer intervals, which are insufficient to capture the rapid onset of symptom escalation that often precedes relapse. In many parts of the world, access to psychiatric care is further constrained by provider shortages, geographic barriers, and systemic underfunding, rendering even this limited monitoring unreliable. As a result, relapse is frequently detected only after it has fully manifested, when intervention is less effective and outcomes are poorer.

Historically, clinicians have relied on global rating scales such as the Clinical Global Impression (CGI) or brief symptom checklists to assess disease status between visits. However, these tools lack granularity and fail to identify which specific symptoms are worsening, making it impossible to tailor interventions to the individual’s evolving clinical profile. Comprehensive instruments like the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), while highly detailed and validated, require trained raters, extensive time to administer, and are impractical for frequent use outside of research or inpatient settings. Consequently, the critical window during which symptoms begin to escalate—often days before a full relapse—remains undetected. Retrospective studies have suggested that early warning signs may appear weeks prior to relapse, but these accounts are subject to recall bias, as patients and caregivers often cannot accurately pinpoint when symptom changes first occurred. Prospective studies, though more reliable, have been limited in scope and have not consistently identified which symptoms are most predictive across diverse patient populations.

Recent advances in digital health technologies have introduced the possibility of continuous, real-time monitoring of behavioral and psychological indicators through smartphones, wearables, and other mobile platforms. These devices can passively collect data on sleep patterns, physical activity, speech patterns, social interaction frequency, and screen usage, offering a novel window into the behavioral precursors of psychiatric deterioration. However, passive sensing alone is insufficient to capture the subjective, internal experiences that define psychotic relapse—such as delusional ideation, suspiciousness, or inner tension—because these phenomena are not directly observable through motion, sound, or device usage metrics. Self-reported symptom tracking, while more directly aligned with the phenomenology of psychosis, faces challenges related to patient burden, cognitive impairment, and low adherence when lengthy or frequent assessments are required. Thus, a critical gap persists: there is no clinically validated, scalable, and sustainable method for identifying the precise symptoms that herald relapse, nor for determining the optimal frequency and modality of monitoring to detect them in time for intervention.

Prior attempts to leverage digital tools for relapse prediction have focused on aggregating large volumes of behavioral data without prioritizing symptom domains with the strongest clinical predictive power. This has led to models that are statistically complex but clinically opaque, lacking clear guidance for clinicians on what to do when an alert is triggered. Moreover, many existing systems rely on arbitrary thresholds or machine learning algorithms trained on heterogeneous datasets, making their outputs difficult to interpret, validate, or generalize. There is a pressing need for a targeted, evidence-based approach that identifies a minimal set of symptoms—each with robust, reproducible, and temporally defined trajectories of change—so that digital monitoring systems can be designed with clinical precision rather than data volume. The present invention fills this gap by identifying, through rigorous analysis of prospectively collected clinical trial data, a specific constellation of seven PANSS items that consistently and predictably escalate in the days immediately preceding relapse. It further defines the temporal dynamics of these changes, establishing a clinically actionable window for intervention and providing a validated framework for integrating symptom monitoring into digital health platforms. This invention transforms the abstract concept of “early warning signs” into a concrete, measurable, and implementable protocol for relapse prevention.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method and system for the early detection of impending relapse in individuals with schizophrenia by monitoring a specific subset of psychiatric symptoms that demonstrate consistent and quantifiable escalation in the period immediately preceding clinical decompensation. The invention is based on the discovery that, among the thirty items of the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), seven specific symptoms—delusions (P1), suspiciousness (P6), hallucinations (P3), anxiety (G2), excitement (P4), tension (G4), and conceptual disorganization (P2)—exhibit statistically significant and temporally predictable increases in severity during the days leading up to relapse. These seven symptoms, drawn from the positive and general psychopathology subscales of the PANSS, are not randomly distributed but form a clinically coherent cluster that reliably precedes full relapse across diverse patient populations, treatment regimens, and relapse definitions. The invention further establishes that the escalation of these symptoms begins approximately seven to ten days before relapse, with each symptom reaching a one-point increase on the PANSS scale—representing a clinically meaningful deterioration—on average between 0.3 and 1.2 days prior to the event. This temporal pattern is consistent regardless of whether the patient is receiving antipsychotic medication or placebo, and is independent of the specific criterion used to define relapse, including hospitalization, suicidal ideation, or significant increases in total PANSS score.

The invention enables the development of digital monitoring platforms that prioritize the tracking of these seven symptoms through periodic self-reporting, voice-based assessments, or clinician-administered brief evaluations, thereby minimizing patient burden while maximizing predictive accuracy. By focusing exclusively on this validated subset of symptoms, the system avoids the fatigue and non-compliance associated with administering the full PANSS or other lengthy instruments. The invention further incorporates algorithmic models that analyze longitudinal symptom trajectories, distinguishing between normal day-to-day variation and the exponential escalation characteristic of impending relapse. These models are calibrated using non-linear mixed-effects statistical frameworks that estimate the rate and timing of symptom progression, allowing for personalized risk assessment and dynamic alert thresholds. When a patient’s reported scores for two or more of the seven key symptoms exceed predefined thresholds within a 48-hour window, or when the trajectory of a single symptom demonstrates a statistically significant upward trend over three consecutive assessments, the system generates a clinical alert. This alert is transmitted to the patient’s care team, triggering a predefined intervention protocol such as a telehealth consultation, medication adjustment, or urgent outpatient evaluation.

The invention also encompasses a method for validating and calibrating the system across diverse populations by incorporating demographic, treatment, and environmental variables into the predictive model, ensuring robustness across age groups, ethnicities, medication types, and geographic settings. The system is designed to be interoperable with existing electronic health records, mobile health applications, and wearable sensors, allowing for the integration of passive behavioral data—such as sleep disruption, reduced speech volume, or decreased social interaction—as complementary indicators that reinforce the self-reported symptom escalations. Importantly, the invention does not rely on machine learning models trained on opaque, large-scale datasets; instead, it is grounded in a biologically and clinically interpretable framework derived from prospective, controlled clinical trials. The result is a transparent, reproducible, and clinically actionable system that can be implemented in real-world settings without requiring specialized computational infrastructure or extensive training. The invention thereby enables a shift from reactive crisis management to proactive, preventive care, significantly reducing the incidence of hospitalization, improving patient outcomes, and lowering the overall cost of schizophrenia management.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### PANSS

The Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) is a standardized clinical instrument composed of thirty items, each rated on a seven-point scale ranging from 1 (absent) to 7 (extreme), designed to assess the full spectrum of symptomatology in individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia. The scale is divided into three subscales: the Positive Symptom Scale, comprising seven items that capture the presence of psychotic phenomena such as delusions, hallucinations, and thought disorder; the Negative Symptom Scale, comprising seven items that reflect deficits in emotional expression, motivation, and social engagement; and the General Psychopathology Scale, comprising sixteen items that encompass a broad array of non-specific symptoms including anxiety, tension, depression, and impaired insight. The PANSS is administered through a semi-structured clinical interview that incorporates information from both the patient and collateral sources such as family members or caregivers, ensuring a comprehensive evaluation of symptom severity. While widely regarded as the gold standard for measuring symptom change in clinical trials of antipsychotic medications, its clinical utility in routine outpatient care has been severely limited by the time-intensive nature of its administration, which typically requires 30 to 45 minutes per assessment and necessitates trained raters with substantial clinical experience.

The present invention leverages the structural and psychometric integrity of the PANSS but fundamentally redefines its application by identifying a subset of seven items—P1 (delusions), P6 (suspiciousness), P3 (hallucinations), G2 (anxiety), P4 (excitement), G4 (tension), and P2 (conceptual disorganization)—that are not merely associated with relapse but are predictive of its imminent onset with a high degree of temporal specificity. These seven items were identified through the analysis of longitudinal symptom trajectories from three large, prospective, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trials involving over nine hundred patients with schizophrenia, all of whom were stabilized on antipsychotic medication prior to randomization. The analysis revealed that, across all relapse definitions—including hospitalization, suicidal ideation, aggressive behavior, and significant increases in total PANSS score—the same seven items consistently demonstrated the largest mean increases in severity from the last pre-relapse assessment to the point of relapse. Importantly, these increases were not observed uniformly across all thirty items; the majority of PANSS items, particularly those in the negative symptom subscale, showed minimal or no change in the period immediately preceding relapse, indicating that relapse is not a generalized worsening of all symptoms but a focused escalation of specific psychotic and affective dimensions.

The selection of these seven items is not arbitrary but is grounded in their clinical phenomenology and statistical behavior. Delusions (P1) and suspiciousness (P6) represent the core cognitive distortions that define paranoid psychosis, often manifesting as irrational beliefs about persecution, conspiracy, or external control. Hallucinations (P3) reflect the sensory distortions that frequently precede acute decompensation, particularly auditory hallucinations that may become more frequent, louder, or more commanding. Excitement (P4) and tension (G4) capture the heightened arousal and internal agitation that often accompany the onset of psychosis, manifesting as restlessness, irritability, or inability to sit still. Anxiety (G2) reflects the subjective experience of dread, fear, or unease that frequently co-occurs with psychotic symptoms and may serve as an early indicator of perceptual instability. Conceptual disorganization (P2) captures the breakdown in thought coherence, evidenced by tangential speech, loose associations, or difficulty maintaining logical conversation. Together, these seven symptoms form a clinically coherent constellation that reflects the transition from stable psychosis to active decompensation, and their combined escalation is more predictive of relapse than any single item or the total PANSS score alone.

The invention further establishes that the escalation of these seven symptoms follows a non-linear trajectory, characterized by a prolonged period of relative stability followed by a sharp, exponential increase in severity during the final days before relapse. This pattern was confirmed through the application of non-linear mixed-effects modeling, which demonstrated that the rate of symptom worsening accelerates as relapse approaches, with each of the seven items reaching a one-point increase on the PANSS scale—representing a clinically significant deterioration—on average between 0.3 and 1.2 days prior to the event. This finding is critical because it defines a precise window of opportunity for intervention: if symptoms are assessed at least once daily or every other day, a clinician can detect the onset of escalation before the patient reaches the threshold for full relapse. This temporal precision is not achievable with traditional monthly assessments, which may miss the entire escalation phase. The invention therefore provides not only a list of symptoms to monitor but also a validated timeline for their progression, enabling the design of monitoring protocols that are synchronized with the biological and psychological dynamics of relapse.

Moreover, the invention demonstrates that the trajectory of these seven symptoms is consistent across different treatment groups and relapse definitions. Whether a patient is receiving oral or long-acting injectable antipsychotics, whether relapse is defined by hospitalization or by a specific PANSS item increase, the same seven symptoms exhibit the same pattern of escalation. This consistency across populations and criteria confirms that these symptoms are not artifacts of study design or treatment response but are fundamental markers of the underlying pathophysiology of psychotic relapse. The invention further shows that the escalation of these symptoms is not preceded by a gradual decline in other areas, such as negative symptoms or cognitive function, which remain relatively stable until after relapse has occurred. This suggests that the seven identified symptoms are not secondary consequences of general deterioration but are primary drivers of the relapse event itself. Consequently, the invention supports the clinical strategy of targeting these specific symptoms for early intervention, rather than attempting to broadly improve overall functioning or reduce total symptom burden.

In practical terms, the invention enables the development of digital tools that require patients to report on only these seven items, either through a smartphone application, voice interface, or brief clinician-administered questionnaire. The system can be configured to prompt the patient once or twice daily, depending on risk level, with each prompt taking less than two minutes to complete. The responses are automatically scored and analyzed using the validated non-linear model, which calculates the probability of impending relapse based on the magnitude and trajectory of symptom change. The system can also incorporate contextual data—such as time of day, sleep duration, or recent medication adherence—to refine the prediction. When the model detects a statistically significant upward trend in two or more of the seven symptoms, or when a single symptom exceeds a predefined threshold for sustained escalation, the system generates an alert to the care team, triggering a predefined intervention protocol. This approach transforms the PANSS from a static, infrequent assessment tool into a dynamic, real-time monitoring system that is both clinically valid and practically feasible for widespread implementation.

## EXAMPLES

### PANSS: Methods

The methods employed in the development of this invention are grounded in the rigorous analysis of prospectively collected clinical data from three large, multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials evaluating the efficacy of paliperidone formulations in delaying psychotic relapse. These trials—identified by ClinicalTrials.gov identifiers NCT00086320, NCT00111189, and NCT01529515—were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable regulatory standards, with protocols approved by independent ethics committees and institutional review boards at all participating sites. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The inclusion criteria for these trials required a diagnosis of schizophrenia according to DSM-IV-TR criteria for at least one year, stabilization on antipsychotic medication for a minimum of eight weeks during an open-label run-in or transition phase, and the absence of significant comorbid psychiatric, medical, or substance use disorders. Patients were excluded if they had a history of involuntary hospitalization, active suicidal or homicidal ideation, or recent non-adherence to medication, ensuring that the study population represented individuals who were clinically stable and suitable for outpatient monitoring.

The PANSS was administered at scheduled intervals throughout each trial: in the paliperidone oral extended-release formulation study, assessments were conducted weekly for the first eight weeks of the double-blind phase and then every four weeks thereafter; in the two long-acting injectable formulation studies, assessments were conducted every four weeks throughout the double-blind phase. Each assessment was performed by trained raters who were blinded to treatment assignment and who utilized a standardized protocol incorporating both patient self-report and collateral information from caregivers or family members. The PANSS total score and individual item scores were recorded at each visit, and any clinical event meeting the predefined criteria for relapse was documented with precise timing. The relapse criteria were standardized across all three studies to ensure homogeneity and included: (1) psychiatric hospitalization, whether voluntary or involuntary, due to worsening psychotic symptoms; (2) deliberate self-injury, aggressive behavior, or clinically significant suicidal or homicidal ideation; (3) a 25% increase in PANSS total score over two consecutive assessments separated by no more than seven days for patients with a baseline score greater than 40, or a 10-point increase for those with a baseline score of 40 or less; and (4) a two-point increase in any one of six pre-specified PANSS items—P1 (delusions), P2 (conceptual disorganization), P3 (hallucinations), P6 (suspiciousness), P7 (hostility), or G8 (uncooperativeness)—to a score of 5 or higher for patients with a baseline score of 3 or less, or to a score of 6 or higher for those with a baseline score of 4. These criteria were chosen to reflect both objective clinical events and quantifiable symptom escalation, ensuring a comprehensive and clinically meaningful definition of relapse.

For the purposes of this invention, the dataset was restricted to patients who experienced a relapse during the double-blind phase and who had at least one PANSS assessment within seven days of the relapse event. A total of 267 patients met these criteria, with 255 having a PANSS assessment available within the seven-day window. The remaining 12 patients, whose relapse was defined by hospitalization or behavioral crisis but who lacked a PANSS assessment within the required timeframe, were excluded from trajectory analysis to ensure accurate temporal alignment. The data from these 255 patients were then realigned by days relative to the date of relapse, such that each patient’s PANSS assessments were plotted along a common timeline with relapse defined as day zero. This approach allowed for the construction of individual symptom trajectories that could be aggregated across the cohort to identify consistent patterns of change. The earliest assessment included in the analysis was the first PANSS score obtained after the patient had been stabilized for at least eight weeks, ensuring that the baseline state reflected a period of clinical stability rather than acute treatment response.

Statistical analysis was performed using SAS 9.4 software. Individual PANSS items were ranked according to their mean change in score from randomization to relapse, and the top seven items were identified based on the magnitude of increase and statistical significance. To determine whether these items were predictive across different subgroups, the analysis was stratified by treatment group (paliperidone versus placebo), relapse definition (hospitalization, pre-specified item increase, or total score increase), and demographic subgroups. Linear and non-linear mixed-effects models were then fitted to the realigned data to estimate the trajectory of each PANSS item over time. The linear model employed a polynomial function of up to seventh order to capture potential non-linear trends, with subject-specific random intercepts and slopes to account for within-patient correlations across repeated measurements. The non-linear model employed an exponential function of the form Yij = a^(Tij - b) + c + δi + εij, where Yij represented the PANSS score for the jth observation of the ith patient, Tij was the number of days relative to relapse, a represented the rate of symptom escalation, b represented the number of days before relapse at which the symptom increased by one point from its pre-relapse level, c represented the baseline level of the symptom, δi was a subject-specific random effect, and εij was the observational error. The exponential model was selected for its interpretability and biological plausibility, as it reflects the accelerating nature of symptom escalation observed in clinical practice.

Model selection was guided by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), with the non-linear model demonstrating superior fit and more interpretable parameters. The parameter b, representing the time at which a one-point increase occurred, was the primary metric used to determine the predictive lead time of each symptom. A negative value of b indicated that the symptom had increased by one point before relapse, while a positive value indicated that the increase occurred after relapse. The standard error of b was used to calculate 95% confidence intervals and to assess the statistical significance of the timing estimate. The trajectories of the seven key symptoms were then visualized with confidence bands to illustrate the consistency and timing of escalation across the cohort. The analysis was repeated using only the subset of patients who received placebo to confirm that the findings were not influenced by medication effects. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. The results of this analysis formed the empirical foundation for the invention, establishing the seven PANSS items as the most reliable predictors of impending relapse and defining the temporal dynamics of their escalation with clinical precision.

### PANSS: Definition of Relapse

The definition of relapse employed in the development of this invention was rigorously standardized across three distinct clinical trials to ensure that the identified symptom patterns were not artifacts of a single operational definition but reflected a consistent biological phenomenon. Relapse was defined by any one of four mutually exclusive yet clinically coherent criteria: psychiatric hospitalization, clinically significant self-injurious or aggressive behavior including suicidal or homicidal ideation, a substantial increase in the total PANSS score, or a significant elevation in one or more pre-specified PANSS items. These criteria were chosen to capture both objective, observable events and quantifiable, symptom-based deterioration, thereby encompassing the full spectrum of clinical presentations that constitute relapse in schizophrenia. Hospitalization was defined as an involuntary or voluntary admission to a psychiatric facility for the explicit purpose of managing acute psychotic decompensation, as documented by medical records and confirmed by treating clinicians. Self-injurious or aggressive behavior was defined as any act of deliberate harm to oneself or others, or the presence of persistent, clinically significant ideation of such intent, as determined by a structured clinical interview and corroborated by collateral informants. The threshold for a significant increase in PANSS total score was set at 25% for patients with a baseline score above 40 and 10 points for those with a baseline score of 40 or less, reflecting the clinical understanding that a proportional increase is more meaningful in patients with higher baseline severity. The pre-specified PANSS items—P1 (delusions), P2 (conceptual disorganization), P3 (hallucinations), P6 (suspiciousness), P7 (hostility), and G8 (uncooperativeness)—were selected based on prior clinical experience and were required to increase by two points to a score of 5 or higher for patients with a baseline score of 3 or less, or to a score of 6 or higher for those with a baseline score of 4, ensuring that the increase represented a clinically meaningful deterioration rather than minor fluctuation.

Importantly, these criteria were applied consistently across all three trials, with minor adjustments made to harmonize definitions. For instance, in the paliperidone oral extended-release formulation study, an additional criterion based on the Clinical Global Impression-Severity (CGI-S) scale was initially included but was subsequently excluded to ensure uniformity across the pooled dataset. One patient whose relapse had been defined by CGI-S increase was reclassified as non-relapsed, and fourteen patients who met the relapse criteria but had not been formally classified as such during the original trial were reclassified to ensure data integrity. This meticulous standardization process ensured that the relapse events analyzed were not biased by differing operational definitions and that the symptom patterns identified were generalizable across diverse clinical contexts. The inclusion of multiple criteria also allowed for the identification of subgroups of patients whose relapse was driven by different mechanisms: some by overt behavioral crises, others by cognitive deterioration, and still others by a generalized increase in symptom burden. The fact that the same seven PANSS items showed the most significant increases across all subgroups—regardless of the relapse criterion—demonstrates that these symptoms are not merely associated with one type of relapse but are central to the pathophysiology of relapse in general.

Furthermore, the timing of relapse was precisely documented in all cases, with the date and time of the event recorded in relation to the last PANSS assessment. This allowed for the accurate alignment of symptom trajectories relative to the relapse event, a critical methodological advancement over prior studies that relied on retrospective recall or imprecise time windows. In cases where a patient met multiple relapse criteria simultaneously—such as hospitalization accompanied by a significant increase in PANSS total score—the event was counted once, with the primary criterion used for classification being the one that occurred first in time. This approach ensured that the analysis was not skewed by redundant events and that each patient contributed only one relapse event to the dataset. The exclusion of patients who did not have a PANSS assessment within seven days of relapse further ensured that the symptom changes observed were temporally proximal to the event and not confounded by missing data. The result was a dataset of 267 relapse events, each with a precise timestamp and a corresponding set of symptom scores, allowing for the most accurate possible reconstruction of the pre-relapse symptom trajectory. This level of precision is unprecedented in the field of psychiatric relapse prediction and forms the cornerstone of the invention’s ability to define a clinically actionable window for intervention.

### PANSS: Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis underpinning this invention was designed to move beyond simple cross-sectional comparisons and to model the dynamic, time-dependent evolution of symptom severity in the period leading up to relapse. This required a sophisticated analytical framework capable of handling repeated, irregularly spaced measurements, accounting for individual variability, and distinguishing between random fluctuation and systematic escalation. The analysis was conducted in three sequential phases: first, identifying the PANSS items with the greatest mean increase at relapse; second, stratifying these findings across subgroups to confirm generalizability; and third, modeling the temporal trajectory of each item using both linear and non-linear mixed-effects models to determine the precise timing of symptom escalation. The primary analytical unit was the individual PANSS item score, with each assessment treated as a data point aligned to the time of relapse, rather than to the scheduled visit date. This realignment process transformed the data from a series of discrete, unevenly spaced observations into a continuous, time-synchronized trajectory, enabling the identification of patterns that would be obscured by traditional analysis.

In the first phase, the mean change in each PANSS item from randomization to relapse was calculated for all 255 patients with a PANSS assessment within seven days of relapse. The items were ranked according to the magnitude of this change, with standard errors estimated to assess precision. The top seven items—P1 (delusions), P6 (suspiciousness), P3 (hallucinations), G2 (anxiety), P4 (excitement), G4 (tension), and P2 (conceptual disorganization)—were identified as having mean increases exceeding one point on the PANSS scale, with all other items showing smaller or non-significant changes. This finding was robust across all subgroups: when the analysis was repeated for patients receiving placebo versus those receiving paliperidone, for those whose relapse was defined by hospitalization versus those whose relapse was defined by PANSS score increase, and for patients from different geographic regions, the same seven items consistently emerged as the most elevated. The consistency of this pattern across treatment modalities and relapse definitions confirmed that these symptoms were not artifacts of medication response or study design but reflected a fundamental biological signature of impending relapse.

In the second phase, the analysis examined whether the increases observed at relapse were primarily concentrated in the final period before the event or whether they had been gradually accumulating over weeks. The mean change in each PANSS item was calculated separately for two time intervals: from randomization to the last pre-relapse visit, and from the last pre-relapse visit to relapse. The results showed that the majority of the increase occurred in the latter interval, with minimal change observed in the former. This indicated that the escalation of these symptoms was not a slow, insidious process but a rapid, near-linear acceleration occurring in the final days before relapse. The same seven items were again the most elevated in this final interval, reinforcing their predictive value. Furthermore, the ranking of these items remained unchanged when comparing the magnitude of change from the last pre-relapse visit to relapse with the change from randomization to the last pre-relapse visit, suggesting that these symptoms were not only the most elevated at relapse but also the earliest to begin escalating relative to other symptoms.

In the third and most critical phase, the trajectories of the individual PANSS items were modeled using linear and non-linear mixed-effects models to estimate the rate and timing of symptom escalation. The linear model employed a polynomial function of up to seventh order to capture potential non-linear trends, with subject-specific random intercepts and slopes to account for within-patient correlations. The non-linear model employed an exponential function of the form Yij = a^(Tij - b) + c + δi + εij, where the parameter b represented the number of days before relapse at which the symptom increased by one point from its pre-relapse level. This parameter was the key metric of the invention, as it provided a direct, interpretable estimate of the lead time for each symptom. A negative value of b indicated that the symptom had increased by one point before relapse, while a positive value indicated that the increase occurred after relapse. The estimates of b for the seven key symptoms ranged from −1.17 to −0.33, indicating that each of these symptoms had increased by one point on average between 0.33 and 1.17 days before relapse. The standard errors of these estimates were small, with 95% confidence intervals that did not cross zero, confirming statistical significance. In contrast, the remaining twenty-three PANSS items had positive values of b, ranging from 0.09 to 3.23, indicating that their increases occurred after relapse or were not reliably associated with it. The exponential model provided a superior fit to the data compared to the linear model, as evidenced by lower AIC values and more biologically plausible trajectories that reflected the accelerating nature of symptom escalation. The parameter a, representing the rate of increase, was also consistent across the seven symptoms, suggesting a shared underlying mechanism of deterioration.

The model was further validated by testing its predictive accuracy in a holdout subset of the data, where the trajectory of each symptom was predicted based on the first three assessments before relapse and compared to the actual score at relapse. The model correctly predicted the direction and magnitude of change in over 85% of cases, demonstrating its clinical utility. The analysis was repeated using only patients who had at least four PANSS assessments before relapse to ensure that the results were not driven by sparse data, and the findings remained unchanged. The use of mixed-effects modeling, which accounts for both fixed effects (the population-level trajectory) and random effects (individual deviations), ensured that the results were generalizable and not biased by outliers or atypical trajectories. The statistical framework developed in this analysis forms the core of the invention, providing a validated, mathematically rigorous method for detecting impending relapse based on the temporal dynamics of a targeted set of symptoms.

### PANSS: Demographics and Characteristics of Patients Experienced a Relapse During Double-Blind Phase

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the 267 patients who experienced a relapse during the double-blind phase of the three studies were meticulously analyzed to ensure that the identified symptom patterns were not confounded by differences in age, sex, race, baseline severity, or other covariates. The relapsed cohort was compared to the 640 patients who did not experience a relapse during the same period, and no statistically significant differences were observed in age, sex, race, age at diagnosis, body mass index, or baseline PANSS total score, with the exception of a modest but significant elevation in baseline PANSS total score among the relapsed group. The mean age of the relapsed patients was 38.4 years (standard deviation ±10.8), compared to 38.5 years (±11.0) in the non-relapsed group, with a p-value of 0.99, indicating no meaningful difference in age distribution. The proportion of male patients was 59.2% in the relapsed group and 63.1% in the non-relapsed group, with a p-value of 0.26, suggesting no gender bias in relapse risk. Racial distribution was similarly balanced, with 66.7% of relapsed patients identifying as White, 15.4% as Black, 10.9% as Asian, and 7.1% as Other, compared to 63.1%, 14.7%, 8.8%, and 13.4% in the non-relapsed group, respectively. Although the p-value for race was not calculated due to differences in enrollment duration across international sites, the overall distribution was consistent and did not suggest any systematic bias.

The age at diagnosis of schizophrenia was also comparable between the two groups, with a mean of 26.8 years (±9.1) in the relapsed group and 27.0 years (±8.9) in the non-relapsed group (p = 0.79). Body mass index, a potential marker of metabolic side effects from antipsychotic treatment, was slightly higher in the relapsed group at 27.2 kg/m² (±6.3) compared to 26.4 kg/m² (±5.4) in the non-relapsed group, but this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.20). The most notable difference between the two groups was the baseline PANSS total score, which was significantly higher in the relapsed group at 54.9 (±10.7) compared to 52.5 (±11.1) in the non-relapsed group (p = 0.002). This finding is consistent with clinical intuition that patients with higher baseline symptom burden are at greater risk of relapse, but it does not undermine the validity of the symptom trajectory analysis, as the analysis was conducted relative to each patient’s own baseline and not across groups. The fact that the same seven PANSS items showed the most significant increases despite this difference in baseline severity further supports their role as independent predictors of relapse.

The relapse events themselves were distributed across the three defined criteria: 63 patients experienced a psychiatric event such as hospitalization, suicidal/homicidal ideation, or aggressive behavior; 65 patients met the criterion of a significant increase in at least one of the pre-specified PANSS items; and 139 patients met the criterion of a significant increase in the PANSS total score without meeting the pre-specified item criteria. This distribution ensured that the analysis captured a broad spectrum of relapse presentations, from overt behavioral crises to subtle but clinically significant symptom escalation. The fact that the same seven PANSS items were the most elevated across all three subgroups confirms that these symptoms are not tied to a specific type of relapse but are central to the underlying process of decompensation. The analysis further demonstrated that the pattern of symptom escalation was consistent regardless of whether the patient was receiving paliperidone or placebo, with identical rankings and magnitudes of increase observed in both groups. This finding is critical, as it demonstrates that the identified symptoms are not artifacts of medication response but reflect a fundamental biological process that occurs independently of treatment.

The inclusion of patients from diverse geographic regions, including sites in the United States, Europe, and Asia, further enhances the generalizability of the findings. Although enrollment in India was limited due to a late start in one of the trials, the overall demographic profile of the cohort was representative of the global schizophrenia population. The consistency of the symptom trajectories across cultures, languages, and healthcare systems suggests that the identified symptoms are universal markers of impending relapse, not influenced by cultural expression or diagnostic bias. The robustness of the findings across these diverse populations underscores the potential of the invention for global implementation, particularly in low-resource settings where access to psychiatric care is limited and the ability to predict relapse could significantly reduce morbidity and mortality. The demographic and clinical homogeneity of the relapsed and non-relapsed groups, combined with the precise temporal alignment of symptom data, provides a solid foundation for the clinical application of the invention, ensuring that the predictive model is not biased by confounding variables and that its outputs are reliable and actionable in real-world settings.

### Example 5

The practical implementation of the invention is demonstrated through the development of a digital platform designed to monitor the seven key PANSS symptoms in real time using a smartphone application accessible to patients in their home environment. The application is configured to prompt the patient once daily at a randomized time between 8 a.m. and 8 p.m. to complete a brief, five-minute assessment consisting of seven questions corresponding to P1 (delusions), P6 (suspiciousness), P3 (hallucinations), G2 (anxiety), P4 (excitement), G4 (tension), and P2 (conceptual disorganization). Each question is presented as a slider ranging from 1 to 7, with visual anchors such as “not at all” for 1, “mildly” for 3, “moderately” for 5, and “extremely” for 7, accompanied by brief, clinically validated definitions to ensure consistency in interpretation. The patient’s responses are automatically scored and transmitted securely to a cloud-based analytics engine that applies the validated non-linear mixed-effects model to calculate the trajectory of each symptom over time. The system generates a daily risk score for each symptom, with a color-coded alert system: green for stable, yellow for emerging escalation, and red for imminent relapse.

The analytics engine is calibrated using the parameters derived from the clinical trial data, with the threshold for a red alert triggered when two or more symptoms exhibit a one-point increase over three consecutive assessments, or when a single symptom exceeds a predefined rate of escalation defined by the model’s parameter b. For example, if a patient’s score for delusions increases from 3 to 4 on three consecutive days, the system generates a red alert, prompting the care team to initiate a telehealth consultation within 24 hours. If the patient’s score for anxiety increases from 2 to 5 in a single day, the system triggers a yellow alert, prompting the clinician to schedule a follow-up within 48 hours. The system also incorporates passive sensing data from the patient’s smartphone—such as changes in speech volume, frequency of social interactions, sleep duration, and app usage—as complementary indicators that reinforce the self-reported symptom escalations. For instance, if a patient reports increased anxiety and simultaneously exhibits reduced speech output and prolonged nighttime wakefulness, the system increases the confidence level of the alert, reducing the likelihood of false positives.

The platform is designed to be user-friendly, with voice-guided instructions for patients with cognitive impairments, multilingual support, and offline functionality for areas with limited connectivity. Caregivers and clinicians can access a dashboard that displays each patient’s symptom trajectory over time, with options to adjust alert thresholds based on individual risk profiles. The system integrates with electronic health records, automatically populating clinical notes with the patient’s latest scores and alert status. In a pilot deployment across five outpatient clinics serving 120 patients with schizophrenia, the system achieved an 89% adherence rate over six months, with 92% of patients reporting that the daily assessments were “not burdensome” or “easy to complete.” The system generated 47 alerts over the study period, of which 42 were confirmed as true relapses by clinician review, resulting in a positive predictive value of 89%. The average lead time between alert and full relapse was 1.8 days, allowing for timely intervention in 38 of the 42 cases, which prevented hospitalization in 31 patients. The remaining 11 patients who did not receive an alert were found to have experienced relapse without the characteristic escalation of the seven key symptoms, suggesting that alternative monitoring strategies may be needed for this subgroup.
