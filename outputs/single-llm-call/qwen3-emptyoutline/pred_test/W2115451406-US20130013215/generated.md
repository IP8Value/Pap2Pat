# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The prediction of protein three-dimensional structure from amino acid sequence remains one of the most enduring and consequential challenges in molecular biology, biochemistry, and structural bioinformatics. For decades, researchers have pursued methods to determine how a linear chain of amino acids folds into a compact, functional, and often highly intricate native conformation under physiological conditions. The significance of this problem extends far beyond academic curiosity; accurate structural prediction enables rational drug design, elucidation of disease mechanisms rooted in misfolding, engineering of synthetic enzymes, and understanding of cellular signaling pathways governed by protein interactions. Despite remarkable advances in computational biology, the fundamental physical principles underlying protein folding—particularly in the absence of evolutionary homologs or experimental templates—have not been fully harnessed in a predictive framework that is both accurate and computationally tractable on standard hardware.

Historically, protein structure prediction has evolved along two dominant paradigms: homology-based modeling and ab initio simulation. Homology-based approaches, exemplified by tools such as MODELLER and SWISS-MODEL, rely on the assumption that evolutionary conservation implies structural conservation. When a sufficiently similar sequence (typically with >30% identity) exists in the Protein Data Bank, these methods can generate highly accurate models by threading the target sequence onto a known template. However, this approach is fundamentally limited by the availability of homologous structures. For many proteins—particularly those from uncharacterized organisms, rapidly evolving pathogens, or proteins with low sequence conservation across species—no suitable template exists, rendering homology-based methods ineffective. In such cases, ab initio prediction becomes the only viable path forward.

Ab initio methods attempt to predict structure from first principles, using physical laws and energy functions to guide the folding process without reliance on evolutionary information. Early ab initio approaches, such as those based on Monte Carlo sampling or simplified lattice models, were constrained by coarse-grained representations and unrealistic energy landscapes, resulting in poor accuracy and an inability to reproduce native-like folds. More recent efforts, including those employing molecular dynamics (MD) simulations with all-atom force fields like AMBER, CHARMM, or GROMOS, have achieved notable successes, particularly for small proteins. However, these simulations are computationally prohibitive, often requiring weeks or months of supercomputer time to sample the conformational space of even modestly sized proteins. Furthermore, MD simulations are highly sensitive to initial conditions, force field parameterization, and solvent models, and they frequently become trapped in local energy minima that do not correspond to the native state.

The advent of deep learning—most notably through AlphaFold2 and RoseTTAFold—has revolutionized the field by achieving near-experimental accuracy in both template-based and template-free predictions. These systems leverage massive datasets of known structures and co-evolutionary signals extracted from multiple sequence alignments to infer spatial constraints between residues. While extraordinarily powerful, these methods operate as black boxes: they provide accurate predictions but offer no mechanistic insight into the physical forces driving folding. They cannot be easily adapted to non-physiological conditions, such as altered pH, temperature, ionic strength, or the presence of denaturants, because they are trained exclusively on structures determined under standard laboratory conditions. Moreover, they require extensive computational infrastructure and training data that are not universally accessible. For many applications—particularly in environmental microbiology, synthetic biology, or point-of-care diagnostics—there is a critical need for a method that is physically interpretable, computationally lightweight, and responsive to environmental perturbations.

Existing physical models have largely failed to bridge the gap between accuracy and efficiency. Early physics-based models often oversimplified the energy landscape, neglecting key interactions such as the dielectric displacement force arising from water polarization around charged residues, or misrepresenting the role of entropy in guiding conformational transitions. Others attempted to incorporate solvent effects through generalized Born models or implicit solvent approximations, but these were either too computationally expensive or too inaccurate to be used in real-time simulations. Furthermore, most models treated protein folding as a global optimization problem, attempting to minimize a single, aggregated energy function over the entire chain simultaneously. This approach ignores the hierarchical nature of folding, in which local secondary structure elements form rapidly and independently, acting as nucleation sites for subsequent tertiary assembly.

A critical limitation of prior physical models is their inability to reconcile the competing influences of electrostatic, hydrophobic, and entropic forces in a dynamic, residue-resolved, time-dependent manner. While Coulombic interactions between charged side chains and hydrophobic collapse driven by the hydrophobic effect are well-documented, the interplay between these forces—particularly how the polarization of surrounding water molecules generates an additional, repulsive force that actively prevents hydrophobic aggregation in the presence of charged residues—has been largely overlooked in computational frameworks. Similarly, the role of thermal fluctuations as a directional, rather than purely random, force influencing the probability of conformational transitions has not been systematically quantified or integrated into folding algorithms. Most simulations treat thermal motion as a noise term to be averaged out, rather than as a physical driver that modulates mobility and determines the likelihood of specific transitions under non-equilibrium conditions.

Moreover, no existing method has successfully combined a physically grounded, first-principles description of residue-level forces with an algorithmic framework that identifies and locks secondary structure elements as they form, thereby reducing the dimensionality of the search space and enabling rapid convergence to native-like tertiary folds. The notion that secondary structure formation is not merely a consequence of local sequence preferences but a direct outcome of a delicate, dynamic balance between electrostatic repulsion and hydrophobic attraction—mediated by the dielectric properties of the surrounding medium—has never been operationalized into a predictive algorithm. The idea that the motion of a protein during folding can be modeled as a drift-diffusion process governed by a mobility function derived from the gradient of free energy, incorporating entropy, electrostatics, and hydrophobic effects as competing terms, has remained theoretical.

This invention addresses these longstanding limitations by introducing a novel, ab initio protein structure prediction method grounded entirely in physical kinetics and non-equilibrium thermodynamics. The method operates on a desktop computer, requires no homologous templates, and predicts both secondary and tertiary structure with accuracy rivaling state-of-the-art machine learning models, while simultaneously providing a transparent, interpretable trajectory of the folding process. It is the first method to explicitly model the drift and diffusion of protein substructures under the simultaneous influence of four distinct physical forces: Coulombic electrostatic interactions, dielectric displacement forces arising from water polarization, thermal diffusion forces, and global entropy gradients. These forces are integrated into a single mobility function that determines the velocity of each backbone pivot point at every time step, allowing the protein to evolve toward lower free energy states in a physically realistic manner. Secondary structure elements are identified in real time by detecting regions where hydrophobic attraction and electrostatic repulsion reach a near-balance, and once identified, these elements are frozen and treated as rigid units during tertiary folding, dramatically accelerating convergence. The entire process—from linear chain to folded tertiary structure—is simulated in under a minute on a standard personal computer, without any statistical training, machine learning, or pre-existing structural templates. This represents a paradigm shift: protein folding is no longer treated as a black-box optimization problem or a statistical inference task, but as a physical process governed by measurable, calculable, and interpretable forces.

The implications of this invention extend far beyond structural prediction. Because the model is driven by explicit physical parameters—charge, hydrophobicity, dielectric environment, and temperature—it can simulate how proteins fold under non-standard conditions, such as low pH, high salt, or the presence of osmolytes. It can predict the effects of point mutations on folding pathways, not merely final structure, enabling mechanistic insights into disease-causing variants. It can model the folding of intrinsically disordered proteins, membrane-associated peptides, and protein fragments that lack homologs in existing databases. It can be integrated into real-time biosensors or diagnostic platforms where rapid, on-device structural prediction is required. Most importantly, it provides a mechanistic explanation for why proteins fold the way they do—not just what they look like, but how and why they arrived at that conformation. This level of physical transparency is unprecedented in the field and opens new avenues for both fundamental biological discovery and applied biotechnology.

## SUMMARY

This invention discloses a novel, ab initio method for predicting the three-dimensional structure of a protein from its amino acid sequence using a physically grounded, drift-diffusion-based simulation that operates entirely on first principles without reliance on homologous templates, statistical models, or machine learning. The method simulates the folding process as a dynamic, time-resolved physical process in which each backbone pivot point—corresponding to the alpha carbon of each amino acid residue—moves under the influence of four distinct, quantifiable forces: electrostatic Coulombic forces between charged side chains, dielectric displacement forces induced by the polarization of surrounding water molecules, thermal diffusion forces arising from Brownian motion, and global entropy gradients that modulate the mobility of the protein chain in response to changes in conformational disorder. These forces are integrated into a single mobility function derived from non-equilibrium thermodynamics, which determines the velocity and direction of motion for each pivot point at every discrete time step during the simulation. The simulation begins with a fully extended linear chain of amino acids and progresses through a series of incremental rotational movements about backbone dihedral angles, guided by the net torque generated by the summation of these four forces acting on each pivot bond. The method uniquely identifies secondary structure elements—alpha helices and beta sheets—in real time by detecting regions where hydrophobic attraction and electrostatic repulsion are nearly balanced, and upon identification, these elements are frozen as rigid structural units, effectively reducing the conformational search space and accelerating convergence to the native tertiary fold. The hydrophobicity and partial charge of each amino acid side chain are assigned to the nearest backbone atom, enabling precise calculation of forces at the residue level. The dielectric displacement force, a previously unincorporated physical term, is calculated based on the energy change associated with the redistribution of water molecules around nonpolar regions in the presence of an electric field, and is shown to be of comparable magnitude to the Coulombic force at sub-nanometer distances. Thermal forces are not treated as random noise but as a directional component proportional to the square root of the Boltzmann constant times temperature, scaled by an inverse mobility factor that reflects the resistance to motion under the prevailing energy landscape. Global entropy is modeled as a decreasing function of protein compaction, and its gradient is incorporated into the mobility function to bias motion toward more ordered, lower-entropy conformations, consistent with the thermodynamic drive toward folding. The simulation proceeds iteratively, with each time step allowing only the pivot bond experiencing the greatest net torque to rotate, ensuring that the most energetically favorable local rearrangements occur first. After tertiary structure formation is achieved through this drift-diffusion process, a final, rapid energy relaxation step is performed using a standard molecular dynamics engine with default parameters to refine local atomic clashes and optimize hydrogen bonding networks, without altering the global fold. The entire process, from sequence input to final 3D structure output, requires less than one minute of computation time on a standard desktop computer and achieves backbone root-mean-square deviation values relative to experimentally determined structures that are among the most accurate reported for any ab initio method, with an average deviation of 4.9 angstroms across a diverse set of proteins ranging from 30 to 157 residues, and a deviation of approximately 8 angstroms for a 320-residue human protein. The method does not require any prior knowledge of protein structure, evolutionary relationships, or training data, and it is fully deterministic and physically interpretable, enabling researchers to trace the exact sequence of conformational changes that lead to the final fold. This invention thus provides the first computationally efficient, physically transparent, template-free, and environmentally responsive framework for protein structure prediction, enabling applications in drug discovery, protein engineering, disease mechanism analysis, and real-time structural biology that were previously inaccessible due to computational or conceptual limitations.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The detailed implementation of this invention comprises a sequence of interrelated computational procedures that collectively simulate the physical folding of a protein from its linear amino acid sequence into its native three-dimensional conformation. The method is implemented as a software system executing on a general-purpose computing device, such as a standard desktop or laptop computer equipped with a multi-core processor and sufficient memory to store the atomic coordinates and force parameters of the protein chain. The system does not require specialized hardware, high-performance computing clusters, or cloud-based resources, and it operates entirely offline, ensuring accessibility and privacy for sensitive biological data. The input to the system is a string of amino acid residues, represented by their one-letter codes, and optionally, environmental parameters such as temperature, pH, and ionic strength, which influence the charge state of ionizable side chains and the dielectric properties of the surrounding medium. The output is a three-dimensional coordinate set describing the positions of all backbone atoms—specifically the nitrogen, alpha carbon, and carbonyl carbon atoms of each residue—in a conformation that closely matches the experimentally observed native structure, as validated by root-mean-square deviation metrics against structures deposited in the Protein Data Bank.

The first phase of the method involves the assignment of physical properties to each amino acid residue. Each residue is characterized by its hydrophobicity index, its net charge at the specified pH, and its partial atomic charges, which are derived from established physicochemical scales and force field parameterizations. The hydrophobicity index for each residue is obtained from a standardized scale such as the Kyte-Doolittle scale or a pH-adjusted variant, and is assigned to the nearest backbone atom, specifically the alpha carbon, to ensure that the force calculations are localized to the protein’s structural framework. The net charge of each residue is determined by protonation state modeling based on the pKa values of ionizable side chains—such as lysine, arginine, histidine, aspartic acid, glutamic acid, tyrosine, and cysteine—at the specified environmental pH. These pKa values are adjusted according to the local dielectric environment using a modified Henderson-Hasselbalch equation that accounts for the proximity of neighboring charged residues, ensuring that the charge state reflects the actual electrostatic context rather than an isolated, bulk-solution assumption. Partial atomic charges for each atom within the side chain are computed using the AMBER10 force field parameter set, which provides a consistent and experimentally validated framework for electrostatic interactions. These charges are then projected onto the alpha carbon atom to simplify the force calculation while preserving the physical influence of the side chain. This assignment strategy ensures that all forces acting on the protein are calculated with respect to the backbone pivot points, which serve as the rotational degrees of freedom during folding.

With the physical properties assigned, the simulation initiates with the protein in a fully extended, linear conformation, with all backbone dihedral angles set to values that produce a straight chain. The simulation proceeds in discrete time steps, each corresponding to a fixed interval of physical time, typically on the order of femtoseconds to picoseconds, chosen to ensure numerical stability and physical realism. At each time step, the net force acting on each backbone pivot bond is computed as the vector sum of four distinct physical contributions. The first contribution is the electrostatic Coulombic force, which arises from the interaction between charged side chains. This force is calculated using Coulomb’s law, with the dielectric constant of water set to 78 times the vacuum permittivity, consistent with the assumption that the majority of the protein is solvated by aqueous medium. The distance between each pair of charged residues is computed as the Euclidean distance between their assigned alpha carbon atoms, and the force is calculated as the product of the charges divided by the square of this distance, scaled by the dielectric constant and the Coulombic constant. The second contribution is the dielectric displacement force, a novel term introduced by this invention that accounts for the movement of water molecules in response to an electric field. This force arises because polar water molecules are attracted to regions of high electric field, thereby displacing nonpolar, hydrophobic residues away from these regions. The magnitude of this force is derived from the gradient of the electric field energy stored in the dielectric medium, as described by Jackson’s formalism for dielectric polarization. The energy change associated with the reorganization of water around a nonpolar region is approximated as proportional to the square of the charge magnitude divided by the fourth power of the distance between the charged and nonpolar regions, and the resulting force is obtained by taking the negative spatial derivative of this energy with respect to distance. This term is shown to be significant at distances below one nanometer, where the local dielectric environment deviates from bulk water, and it acts to repel hydrophobic residues from charged regions, thereby preventing inappropriate aggregation and guiding the formation of correctly folded secondary structures.

The third contribution is the thermal diffusion force, which represents the influence of Brownian motion on the protein’s conformational dynamics. Unlike previous models that treat thermal motion as a stochastic perturbation, this invention treats it as a deterministic, directional force that modulates the probability of conformational transitions. The thermal force is calculated as the square root of the product of the Boltzmann constant, the temperature in Kelvin, and an inverse mobility factor that reflects the resistance to motion under the prevailing energy landscape. This mobility factor is derived from the same function that governs the drift velocity of the protein, ensuring consistency between the drift and diffusion components of the motion. The thermal force is not applied uniformly but is scaled by the lever arm length of the pivot bond, meaning that motions involving larger torsional angles are more strongly influenced by thermal fluctuations. This ensures that the simulation captures the entropic tendency of the protein to explore conformational space, while still being biased toward lower-energy states by the other forces.

The fourth and final contribution is the global entropy gradient, which represents the thermodynamic drive toward increased order as the protein folds. This term is not a direct force but is incorporated into the mobility function that governs the velocity of each pivot point. The mobility is defined as the ratio of the diffusivity of the protein segment to the gradient of the effective free energy, which includes contributions from electrostatic energy, hydrophobic energy, and the product of global entropy and temperature. The global entropy is estimated by comparing the current conformation of the protein to a reference state of maximum disorder, such as the fully extended chain, and is computed as a function of the number of intramolecular contacts, the compactness of the chain, and the degree of secondary structure formation. As the protein folds and becomes more compact, the global entropy decreases, and this reduction in entropy increases the effective free energy gradient, thereby reducing the mobility of the protein and slowing its motion. This creates a self-regulating feedback mechanism: as the protein approaches its native state, its mobility decreases, preventing over-folding and allowing the structure to settle into a stable conformation. The entropy term is calibrated against experimental observations of folding kinetics and is adjusted to ensure that the simulated folding trajectory matches the observed trend of decreasing energy fluctuations over time.

Once the four forces are computed for each pivot bond, the net torque acting on each bond is determined by multiplying the net force vector by the lever arm length—the distance from the pivot point to the point of force application, which is approximated as the center of mass of the side chain. The pivot bond with the largest net torque is selected for rotation in the current time step. This selection mechanism ensures that the most energetically favorable local rearrangement occurs first, mimicking the hierarchical nature of protein folding, in which the strongest interactions form first and guide subsequent structural transitions. The rotation angle is determined by the magnitude of the torque and the time step duration, and the new conformation is computed using standard rigid-body rotation matrices applied to the atoms downstream of the pivot point. After the rotation, the positions of all atoms are updated, and the forces are recalculated for the new conformation. This iterative process continues until the protein reaches a state of dynamic equilibrium, characterized by a plateau in the total energy of the system and a reduction in the magnitude of the net torque below a predefined threshold.

During this process, the method simultaneously identifies secondary structure elements in real time. An alpha helix is detected when a contiguous sequence of six or more residues contains a hydrophilic residue at both ends and a sufficient number of hydrophobic residues in between, such that the summation of hydrophobic character exceeds a threshold value while the summation of net charge remains below a critical limit. This balance between hydrophobic attraction and electrostatic repulsion is the key determinant of helix formation: if the electrostatic repulsion is too strong, the helix cannot form; if it is too weak, the hydrophobic residues collapse into an irregular aggregate. The method uses a hierarchical decision tree to classify the type of secondary structure based on the relative magnitudes of these forces, as defined by a set of empirically derived inequalities. For example, if the product of the total charge and the hydrophobic sum is less than a certain value, and the hydrophobic sum exceeds another threshold, an alpha helix is assigned. If the hydrophobic sum is insufficient but the charge distribution is favorable for extended conformation, a beta sheet is predicted. These rules are applied sequentially along the sequence, and once a secondary structure is identified, the corresponding segment is frozen in place, meaning that its backbone dihedral angles are fixed and no further rotational motion is permitted for those residues. This freezing mechanism dramatically reduces the conformational search space, as the protein now folds around pre-formed rigid units rather than a fully flexible chain. The frozen secondary structures are then treated as single pseudo-residues during the tertiary folding phase, with their summed hydrophobicity and charge values propagated to their respective alpha carbon atoms to maintain force continuity.

The tertiary folding phase proceeds as described above, with the remaining unstructured regions continuing to drift and diffuse under the influence of the four forces. The frozen secondary structures act as nucleation sites, guiding the orientation and packing of the remaining segments. As the simulation progresses, the energy of the system decreases, and the fluctuations in torque diminish, indicating convergence toward a stable fold. Once the net torque falls below a predefined threshold for a sustained number of consecutive time steps, the simulation terminates, and the resulting structure is passed to a final refinement step. This step employs a standard molecular dynamics engine, such as AMBER10, with default parameters for the dielectric constant, van der Waals interactions, and hydrogen bonding, to perform a brief energy minimization and short-time dynamics simulation lasting no more than a few hundred picoseconds. This step corrects minor atomic clashes, optimizes hydrogen bond geometries, and refines side chain rotamers, but it does not alter the global fold determined by the drift-diffusion simulation. The result is a high-fidelity three-dimensional structure that retains the physical realism of the primary simulation while achieving the atomic precision of conventional MD methods.

The method has been validated across a diverse set of proteins, including small, medium, and large globular proteins, with lengths ranging from 30 to 320 amino acids. The accuracy of the predicted structures was assessed using the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) between the simulated backbone atoms and the experimentally determined structures from the Protein Data Bank. For proteins under 150 residues, the average RMSD was 4.9 angstroms, with a standard deviation of 1.08 angstroms, placing this method among the most accurate ab initio predictors reported in the literature. For the largest protein tested—a 320-residue human tyrosine phosphatase—the RMSD was approximately 8 angstroms, which is comparable to or better than other ab initio methods and significantly superior to early physics-based models. The method correctly predicted the presence and location of alpha helices and beta sheets with an accuracy of approximately 70 to 75 percent at the residue level, and even higher accuracy for core secondary structure elements. The inclusion of the dielectric displacement force was shown to be critical for preventing the collapse of hydrophobic regions in the presence of charged residues, a common failure mode in previous models. The thermal force was found to be essential for enabling the protein to escape local energy minima and explore conformational space without becoming trapped. The entropy gradient ensured that the folding trajectory followed a physically realistic path, with energy fluctuations decreasing over time and the system approaching a state of dynamic equilibrium rather than complete stasis.

The method is fully deterministic and reproducible: given the same input sequence and environmental conditions, it will always produce the same structure. It does not rely on training data, statistical correlations, or machine learning algorithms, and therefore it is not subject to bias from the composition of existing structural databases. It can be applied to any protein sequence, regardless of evolutionary history, and it can simulate folding under non-standard conditions, such as low pH, high temperature, or the presence of denaturants, by simply adjusting the charge states and dielectric parameters. The simulation generates a complete folding trajectory, allowing researchers to observe the sequence of structural transitions that lead to the final fold, providing unprecedented mechanistic insight into the folding process. This capability is particularly valuable for studying misfolding diseases, protein aggregation, and the effects of mutations on folding pathways. The method is also highly efficient, requiring less than one minute of computation time on a standard desktop computer, making it suitable for high-throughput screening, real-time analysis, and integration into portable diagnostic devices. The software implementation is modular, with clear separation between the force calculation, mobility determination, secondary structure detection, and refinement modules, allowing for future expansion and customization. The method can be extended to include additional physical effects, such as disulfide bond formation, metal ion coordination, or membrane embedding, by incorporating additional force terms and boundary conditions. The invention thus represents a fundamental advance in protein structure prediction, offering a physically accurate, computationally efficient, and universally applicable method for determining protein conformation from sequence alone.
