## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of natural language processing, and more specifically to systems and methods for improving the factual consistency of abstractive text summarization models trained using deep neural networks. The invention is directed to a novel parameter ensembling technique that enhances the reliability of generated summaries by integrating the learned representations of three distinct models: a base summarization model, an expert model trained on high-fidelity training samples, and an anti-expert model trained on low-fidelity training samples. The method operates in the parameter space of transformer-based sequence-to-sequence architectures, adjusting the weights of the base model through a contrastive linear combination that amplifies the beneficial characteristics of the expert while suppressing the detrimental tendencies of the anti-expert. This approach is particularly applicable to large-scale language models deployed in real-world applications such as news aggregation, legal document summarization, medical record abstraction, customer service automation, and financial reporting, where the generation of factually accurate summaries is critical to user trust, regulatory compliance, and operational integrity. The invention addresses a persistent and pervasive problem in modern text generation systems—namely, the tendency of state-of-the-art abstractive summarizers to produce plausible-sounding but factually incorrect content, commonly referred to as hallucinations—by introducing a computationally efficient, training-time solution that does not require changes to the inference architecture, additional decoding constraints, or post-generation correction modules.

## BACKGROUND

The advent of deep learning-based abstractive summarization systems has revolutionized the automation of text condensation tasks across diverse domains, enabling the generation of concise, fluent, and semantically coherent summaries from lengthy source documents. Models such as BART, T5, and PEGASUS, trained on large corpora like CNN/Daily Mail and XSum, have achieved remarkable performance on traditional evaluation metrics such as ROUGE, which measure lexical and n-gram overlap between generated summaries and reference summaries. However, despite these surface-level successes, a growing body of empirical evidence has revealed that these models frequently generate summaries containing factual inconsistencies—errors in entity attribution, incorrect predicate-argument relationships, spurious causal links, and misattributed events—that are not grounded in the source document. These errors, collectively termed hallucinations, are not merely superficial deviations but can lead to serious consequences in high-stakes applications, including misinformation dissemination, legal liability, and erosion of user confidence.

The root cause of these hallucinations lies in the nature of the training data itself. Publicly available summarization datasets, particularly those derived from news articles, often contain summaries that are highly abstractive and, in many cases, factually inconsistent with their source documents. These inconsistencies arise from editorial paraphrasing, stylistic embellishment, or even unintentional misrepresentations by human writers. When neural models are trained to maximize the likelihood of these reference summaries, they internalize not only the syntactic and semantic patterns of good summarization but also the factual inaccuracies embedded within the training data. As a result, the models learn to reproduce these errors as part of their generative behavior, leading to high rates of hallucination during inference. Prior attempts to mitigate this issue have focused primarily on post-hoc correction techniques, such as reranking candidate summaries based on external fact-checking models, applying constrained decoding rules to restrict entity generation, or retraining models on filtered datasets that exclude samples with identified factual errors. While these approaches offer some improvement, they suffer from significant limitations. Filtering training data reduces the size and diversity of the training corpus, often resulting in degraded performance on fluency, informativeness, and coverage metrics. Post-hoc correction methods introduce additional computational overhead, require separate models to be stored and executed, and are not always compatible with autoregressive generation pipelines. Moreover, many of these methods lack generalizability across different types of factual errors, as they are tuned to specific error categories such as entity mismatch or dependency arc violation, and fail to address the broader spectrum of semantic and discourse-level hallucinations.

Recent research has explored the use of ensemble methods to combine multiple models for improved performance, including parameter averaging and attention-based parameter mixing. However, these techniques typically treat all models as equally reliable contributors to the final output, without distinguishing between models that exhibit high factual fidelity and those that amplify hallucinations. Furthermore, existing ensembling approaches do not exploit the systematic contrast between models trained on high-quality and low-quality data. In particular, no prior method has recognized or leveraged the insight that a model trained on the most hallucinated samples in a dataset can serve as a negative exemplar—a model that explicitly learns to generate the very errors that one seeks to eliminate. By training such an “anti-expert” model and then using its parameter differences relative to an “expert” model to adjust the base model’s weights, the present invention introduces a fundamentally new paradigm for correcting hallucinations at the model architecture level, rather than at the output or data level. This approach not only avoids the pitfalls of data filtering and post-processing but also enables a continuous, interpretable trade-off between factual accuracy and summary quality through a single scalar mixing coefficient. The result is a scalable, efficient, and generalizable solution that significantly outperforms existing methods across a wide range of factual consistency metrics without sacrificing fluency or informativeness, thereby addressing a critical and long-standing challenge in the field of neural text generation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Computer and Network Environment

The system for implementing the Contrastive Parameter Ensembling (CaPE) method operates within a distributed computing environment comprising one or more server-class computing devices, each equipped with high-performance graphics processing units (GPUs) or tensor processing units (TPUs) optimized for large-scale neural network training and inference. These computing devices are interconnected via a high-bandwidth, low-latency network infrastructure, such as an Ethernet-based local area network or a cloud-based virtual private cloud, enabling synchronized parameter updates, distributed data loading, and coordinated model evaluation across multiple nodes. The environment further includes a centralized data storage subsystem, which may be implemented using distributed file systems such as Hadoop Distributed File System (HDFS), Google Cloud Storage, Amazon S3, or a high-performance object store, to house the source documents, reference summaries, and preprocessed training datasets used for model development. The datasets are organized into structured formats, such as Protocol Buffers, JSONL, or TFRecord files, with each sample containing a source document, a reference summary, and associated metadata including entity annotations, dependency parse trees, and factual consistency scores derived from automated metrics.

The system is accessed by users and administrators through a secure web-based interface or programmatic API endpoints, which allow for the submission of source documents for summarization, retrieval of generated summaries, and configuration of model parameters such as the mixing coefficient α. The interface is hosted on application servers running a web framework such as Django, Flask, or FastAPI, which communicate with the underlying machine learning inference engine via RESTful or gRPC protocols. The inference engine, deployed as a containerized microservice using Docker or Kubernetes, loads the CaPE model weights into GPU memory upon startup and maintains them in a persistent state to enable low-latency response times for incoming requests. The model is implemented using a deep learning framework such as PyTorch or TensorFlow, with custom layers and operations defined to support the contrastive parameter adjustment mechanism. During inference, the system receives a source document as input, encodes it using the shared encoder of the base summarization model, and generates a summary autoregressively using the adjusted decoder parameters derived from the CaPE combination. The entire process is optimized to operate within real-time constraints, with latency typically under 500 milliseconds per summary on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU, making it suitable for deployment in interactive applications such as live news summarization, customer support chatbots, and real-time meeting transcription services.

The system further incorporates a monitoring and logging subsystem that tracks model performance metrics, including factual consistency scores, ROUGE values, entity recall, and inference latency, and logs these metrics to a time-series database such as InfluxDB or Prometheus for long-term analysis and model drift detection. An automated retraining pipeline is also integrated into the environment, which periodically evaluates the performance of the CaPE model against newly collected user feedback, annotated summaries, or updated benchmark datasets. When performance degradation is detected beyond a predefined threshold, the pipeline triggers a new training cycle, automatically selecting fresh subsets of clean and noisy samples using the same entity overlap and dependency arc entailment metrics, fine-tuning the expert and anti-expert models, and recomputing the CaPE parameters. This closed-loop system ensures continuous improvement and adaptation to evolving data distributions without requiring manual intervention. All data transmissions between components are encrypted using TLS 1.3, and access to the system is governed by role-based authentication and authorization protocols compliant with industry standards such as OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect. The system is designed to be horizontally scalable, allowing for the addition of more inference nodes to handle increased load, and vertically scalable, enabling the use of larger model architectures or higher-precision arithmetic for improved accuracy when computational resources permit.

### Example Workflows

The operation of the Contrastive Parameter Ensembling system is governed by a series of well-defined workflows that span the entire lifecycle of model development, deployment, and maintenance. The first workflow, referred to as the Training and Calibration Workflow, begins with the ingestion of a large-scale summarization dataset comprising source documents and their corresponding reference summaries. This dataset is preprocessed to extract named entities using a named entity recognition module, which identifies and tags entities such as persons, organizations, locations, dates, and numerical quantities according to a standardized schema such as CoNLL-2003 or OntoNotes. Simultaneously, a dependency parser is applied to each reference summary to generate a dependency tree structure, capturing the grammatical and semantic relationships between words in the form of labeled arcs such as nsubj, dobj, amod, and advmod. These parsed structures are then used to compute two distinct factual consistency metrics: entity overlap precision and dependency arc entailment score. Entity overlap precision is calculated as the proportion of named entities in the reference summary that also appear in the source document, while dependency arc entailment score is determined by evaluating, for each arc in the summary, whether the corresponding semantic relationship is logically entailed by the source document using a pre-trained textual entailment classifier. Each sample in the dataset is assigned a score for each metric, resulting in a two-dimensional factual fidelity profile.

The system then partitions the dataset into three non-overlapping subsets: a base training set, a clean subset, and a noisy subset. The base training set comprises the entire original dataset and is used to train the foundational summarization model, referred to as the base model. The clean subset is formed by selecting samples whose entity overlap precision exceeds a predefined threshold and whose dependency arc entailment score falls below a specified maximum error count, ensuring that these samples exhibit minimal factual inconsistencies. Conversely, the noisy subset is constructed by selecting samples whose entity overlap precision is below a lower threshold and whose dependency arc entailment score exceeds a higher error threshold, ensuring that these samples are rich in factual errors. The thresholds are determined empirically through grid search on a validation set to maximize the divergence in factual behavior between the clean and noisy subsets while preserving sufficient sample size for effective fine-tuning. Once the subsets are defined, the base model is fine-tuned on the clean subset to produce the expert model, which learns to generate summaries with high factual fidelity, and on the noisy subset to produce the anti-expert model, which learns to generate summaries with pronounced hallucinations. Both fine-tuning procedures are conducted using the same optimizer, learning rate, and training duration as the base model to ensure that the resulting models share a common optimization trajectory, which is critical for the success of parameter-space ensembling.

Following the training of the three models, the system enters the Parameter Adjustment Workflow. Here, the parameters of the base model are adjusted by computing a linear combination of the parameters of the expert and anti-expert models. Specifically, the adjusted parameters θ_CaPE are derived according to the equation θ_CaPE = θ_base + α(θ_expert − θ_anti-expert), where α is a scalar mixing coefficient that controls the strength of the contrastive adjustment. The value of α is selected through a validation process on a held-out development set, where the system evaluates the trade-off between factual consistency metrics and traditional summary quality metrics such as ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, ROUGE-L, and entity recall. The optimal α is chosen as the value that maximizes factual consistency while ensuring that the drop in ROUGE-1 and entity recall does not exceed a predefined tolerance threshold, typically set at 1%. This step ensures that the resulting CaPE model retains the fluency and informativeness of the base model while significantly reducing hallucinations. The adjusted parameters are then serialized and stored as a single checkpoint file, which replaces the original base model checkpoint for all subsequent inference tasks.

The third workflow, the Inference and Deployment Workflow, begins when a user submits a source document through the web interface or API endpoint. The document is preprocessed using the same entity recognition and dependency parsing modules used during training to ensure consistency in representation. The CaPE model is then loaded into memory, and the source document is encoded using the shared encoder. The decoder, now parameterized by the adjusted weights, generates the summary one token at a time using beam search with a beam width of six, constrained by a minimum and maximum sequence length appropriate for the target domain. The generated summary is returned to the user along with metadata indicating the value of α used, the confidence score derived from the dependency arc entailment classifier, and a factual consistency rating. The system also logs the input, output, and intermediate representations for auditability and future model refinement. In the event that the user requests a summary with higher factual fidelity, the system allows for dynamic adjustment of α on the fly, enabling the generation of alternative summaries with varying degrees of caution. For example, setting α to 0.8 may produce a summary that is more conservative in its claims, while setting α to 0.2 may yield a more fluent but slightly less factually constrained output. This level of controllability is unprecedented in prior summarization systems and provides users with the ability to tailor the model’s behavior to their specific risk tolerance and application requirements.

The fourth workflow, the Monitoring and Retraining Workflow, operates continuously in the background. Every 24 hours, the system samples a batch of recent inference outputs and sends them to a human annotation service for factual accuracy labeling. These labeled samples are added to a feedback corpus, which is periodically merged with the original training dataset. The system then triggers an automated retraining job that re-evaluates the entity overlap and dependency arc entailment scores for the expanded dataset, recomputes the clean and noisy subsets, retrains the expert and anti-expert models, and recalculates the optimal α. This closed-loop process ensures that the CaPE model remains aligned with evolving data distributions, user expectations, and domain-specific factual norms. Additionally, the system monitors for concept drift by comparing the distribution of factual consistency scores over time and raises alerts if a significant deviation is detected, prompting manual review and potential model rollback. The entire workflow is orchestrated using a workflow management system such as Apache Airflow or Prefect, which schedules tasks, manages dependencies, and ensures idempotency and fault tolerance across all stages. The result is a self-improving, production-ready summarization system that continuously enhances its factual reliability without requiring manual reconfiguration or retraining by human experts.

The fifth workflow, the Cross-Metric Generalization Workflow, demonstrates the robustness and transferability of the CaPE method. During training, the expert and anti-expert models are constructed using only entity overlap and dependency arc entailment metrics. However, during evaluation, the CaPE model is tested on a suite of additional factual consistency metrics, including QA-based metrics such as QuestEval and QAFactEval, entailment scores from the MNLI dataset, and BERTScore precision with respect to the source document. Remarkably, the CaPE model consistently outperforms the base model on all of these metrics, even though they were not used during training or model selection. This phenomenon indicates that the contrastive parameter adjustment does not merely optimize for the specific metrics used during training but instead induces a more general improvement in the model’s ability to ground its generation in the source document. The system exploits this property by allowing users to select which metrics are prioritized during α calibration, enabling customization for different application contexts. For instance, in legal document summarization, where entity precision is paramount, α may be calibrated using entity overlap precision as the primary metric. In medical summarization, where complex causal relationships are critical, α may be calibrated using dependency arc entailment. In journalistic applications, where both entity accuracy and discourse coherence matter, α may be calibrated using a weighted combination of multiple metrics. This flexibility, combined with the system’s ability to generalize across unseen metrics, makes the CaPE method uniquely suited for deployment across a wide spectrum of domains with varying factual requirements.

The sixth workflow, the Model Interchangeability Workflow, further underscores the versatility of the CaPE framework. The system supports the substitution of expert and anti-expert models trained on different metrics—for example, using a DAE-based expert with an entity-based anti-expert, or vice versa. This cross-metric ensembling is not only permitted but actively encouraged, as it often yields superior performance compared to models using matching metrics. The system automatically evaluates all possible combinations of expert and anti-expert pairs during the calibration phase and selects the combination that achieves the best trade-off on the validation set. This capability allows the system to leverage the strengths of multiple factual consistency signals without requiring the user to understand the underlying technical distinctions between them. The result is a highly adaptive and robust summarization engine that can be deployed in heterogeneous environments where data quality varies, labeling standards differ, and factual error profiles are complex and multifaceted. The system’s ability to synthesize knowledge from multiple, potentially conflicting signals into a single, coherent parameter adjustment represents a significant advancement over prior methods that rely on single-metric optimization or rigid post-processing rules. This workflow, combined with the others, establishes the CaPE method as a comprehensive, end-to-end solution for factual consistency in abstractive summarization that is both technically innovative and practically deployable.
