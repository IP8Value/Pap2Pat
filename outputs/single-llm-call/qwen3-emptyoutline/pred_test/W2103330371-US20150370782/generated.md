# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The medical domain is characterized by an immense and rapidly expanding corpus of structured and unstructured knowledge, encompassing clinical guidelines, peer-reviewed journals, electronic health records, medical textbooks, and public health databases. This knowledge is critical to the practice of evidence-based medicine, yet its sheer volume and dynamic nature present formidable challenges to clinicians who must synthesize, recall, and apply relevant information during patient care. Traditional methods of knowledge acquisition—such as manual literature review, consultation of printed references, or reliance on memory—are increasingly inadequate in the face of daily exposure to thousands of new research findings and evolving treatment protocols. The cognitive burden imposed by this information overload contributes to diagnostic errors, delayed treatment decisions, and suboptimal clinical outcomes. To address this challenge, automated systems capable of extracting, organizing, and contextualizing medical knowledge from textual sources have become essential. However, existing approaches to medical information extraction are constrained by fundamental limitations in scalability, accuracy, and adaptability to real-world conditions.

One of the most significant obstacles in automated medical knowledge extraction lies in the identification and interpretation of semantic relationships between medical entities. While ontologies such as the Unified Medical Language System (UMLS) provide a comprehensive framework for categorizing medical concepts—encompassing over 2.7 million distinct concepts drawn from more than 160 source vocabularies—they offer only partial solutions to the problem of relation extraction. UMLS contains structured relationships between concepts, but these relationships are often incomplete, inconsistently annotated, and insufficiently granular to support the nuanced reasoning required in clinical decision-making. Furthermore, the majority of medical knowledge resides in natural language text, where relationships are expressed implicitly, ambiguously, or contextually, and cannot be reliably captured by rule-based systems or static knowledge bases alone. Existing relation extraction systems, particularly those developed for benchmark datasets such as i2b2, assume the prior availability of perfectly annotated entity mentions with a limited set of predefined semantic categories—typically restricted to “problem,” “treatment,” and “test.” This assumption is fundamentally misaligned with the reality of clinical text, where entity recognition must operate over a vast, heterogeneous, and noisy space of possible concepts, many of which are polysemous, context-dependent, or inconsistently named. Tools such as MetaMap, while widely used, generate an overwhelming number of candidate concept mappings for every term in a text, often assigning multiple conflicting UMLS Concept Unique Identifiers (CUIs) to a single word or phrase. For example, the term “hepatitis B” may be mapped to both “Disease or Syndrome” and “Pharmacologic Substance,” reflecting the dual usage of the term to refer to the infection itself or the vaccine used to prevent it. This ambiguity introduces significant noise into downstream relation extraction tasks, rendering traditional supervised learning methods—reliant on clean, labeled training data—highly susceptible to overfitting and poor generalization.

Moreover, the scale of the medical text corpus necessitates computational efficiency that many state-of-the-art relation extraction methods cannot achieve. Modern medical corpora, such as those derived from MEDLINE abstracts, clinical notes, and biomedical literature repositories, often contain hundreds of millions of sentences, totaling tens of gigabytes of raw text. Parsing such volumes using traditional dependency parsers optimized for general English—such as Stanford Parser or Berkeley Parser—is computationally prohibitive. Even when specialized parsers are employed, the use of non-linear classifiers, kernel-based methods, and tree kernels, while effective in controlled environments, introduce prohibitive computational complexity that scales poorly with corpus size. These methods often require iterative optimization, expensive kernel computations, and extensive hyperparameter tuning, making them unsuitable for real-time or large-scale deployment in clinical information systems. Additionally, the cost of obtaining high-quality labeled examples for training is exorbitant, requiring domain experts to manually annotate thousands of sentence-level instances for each relation type. Given the scarcity of such labeled data, most systems are forced to rely on distant supervision techniques, which automatically generate training examples by aligning known concept pairs from knowledge bases with co-occurring terms in text. However, this approach inevitably introduces a high rate of false positives: a sentence containing two entities known to be related in a knowledge base does not necessarily express that relation. For instance, the sentence “A recent study examined the association between antibiotics and Lyme disease” contains the entities “antibiotics” and “Lyme disease,” which are known to be linked by a “treats” relationship in UMLS, yet the sentence itself does not assert that antibiotics treat Lyme disease—it merely references their co-occurrence in research. Without a mechanism to distinguish between syntactic co-occurrence and semantic entailment, distant supervision leads to training sets dominated by noise, which severely degrades model performance.

Existing approaches to mitigating this noise have been largely heuristic. Some systems attempt to filter out noisy examples using linguistic patterns or rule-based filters, but these rules are brittle, require manual engineering, and fail to generalize across diverse text genres. Others employ ensemble methods or probabilistic models to estimate label confidence, but these methods typically require additional labeled data for calibration or rely on assumptions about noise distribution that do not hold in practice. Furthermore, most relation extraction models treat labeled and unlabeled data as disjoint sets, failing to exploit the underlying geometric structure of the data manifold—the intrinsic low-dimensional structure formed by the distribution of similar examples in feature space. This oversight is particularly detrimental in medical domains, where subtle variations in phrasing, context, or syntactic construction can distinguish a true relation from a false one, and where unlabeled examples often contain valuable structural information about the relationships between entities that is not captured by isolated labeled instances. As a result, models trained solely on small, noisy labeled datasets exhibit poor robustness, low recall, and high variance across different clinical contexts.

The limitations of current systems are further compounded by the lack of a unified framework that simultaneously addresses entity ambiguity, data scarcity, computational efficiency, and relational complexity. While some systems incorporate semantic types from UMLS to constrain candidate relations, they do not systematically weigh the influence of multiple semantic types assigned to a single entity, nor do they account for the varying degrees of confidence associated with different label assignments. Similarly, while kernel-based methods have demonstrated strong performance on small, curated datasets, they are ill-suited for deployment in large-scale, real-time environments due to their computational overhead. Linear models, while efficient, are often outperformed by non-linear methods in terms of accuracy, leading to a trade-off between speed and precision that has not been satisfactorily resolved. There exists, therefore, a critical gap in the field: a need for a scalable, robust, and efficient relation extraction system that can operate directly on raw, unannotated medical text, leverage both labeled and unlabeled data to overcome annotation bottlenecks, respect the intrinsic structure of the medical knowledge space, and deliver high-precision relational knowledge at a scale and speed compatible with clinical workflow demands. The invention described herein addresses this gap by introducing a novel, closed-form manifold-based learning framework that integrates semantic typing, dependency parsing, and topological preservation into a unified, computationally efficient architecture capable of extracting high-confidence medical relations from massive, noisy corpora without reliance on hand-crafted rules or exhaustive manual annotation.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a novel system and method for extracting semantic relations between medical entities from large-scale, unstructured clinical text using a manifold-based learning framework that simultaneously leverages labeled and unlabeled data, respects the intrinsic geometric structure of the data manifold, and enables scalable, high-precision relation extraction without reliance on hand-crafted rules or perfect entity annotation. The system operates on a corpus of medical text containing hundreds of millions of sentences, automatically identifying candidate relations through the co-occurrence of concept pairs derived from a comprehensive medical ontology, and then employs a computationally efficient, closed-form optimization algorithm to assign confidence scores to each candidate relation based on a combination of semantic, syntactic, and contextual features. Unlike conventional approaches that treat labeled and unlabeled examples as separate entities or rely on iterative optimization techniques, the invention formulates relation extraction as a global optimization problem over a graph Laplacian regularized feature space, where the objective function balances the fidelity of predicted labels to known annotations with the preservation of local neighborhood relationships among all examples—labeled and unlabeled alike. This formulation ensures that examples with similar feature representations, regardless of their labeling status, are assigned similar scores, thereby mitigating overfitting and enhancing generalization in the presence of sparse and noisy training data.

The invention introduces a novel feature representation that integrates multiple layers of medical knowledge, including UMLS semantic types, dependency path structures, topic distributions derived from latent semantic indexing, and bag-of-words patterns from the immediate context of entity pairs. Each relation instance is encoded as a high-dimensional vector that captures not only the identity of the entities involved but also their grammatical roles, semantic categorizations, and contextual usage within the sentence. The system employs a domain-specific parser optimized for medical language, enabling rapid and accurate dependency parsing of the entire corpus with minimal computational overhead. Semantic types are assigned to each entity via a two-step process: first, the parser identifies candidate terms and maps them to UMLS Concept Unique Identifiers (CUIs), and second, each CUI is expanded into one or more UMLS semantic types, which are then aggregated into a binary feature vector representing the entity’s conceptual profile. This approach accommodates the inherent ambiguity of medical terminology, where a single term may map to multiple, sometimes conflicting, semantic categories, by preserving all possible interpretations and allowing the learning algorithm to infer the most salient interpretation based on context.

The core innovation of the invention lies in its manifold regularization algorithm, which computes a closed-form solution to a constrained optimization problem that minimizes a cost function composed of two terms: a supervised term that penalizes deviations between predicted scores and known labels, and an unsupervised term that penalizes differences in scores between neighboring examples in the feature space. The algorithm incorporates a weight matrix derived from the pairwise Euclidean distances between all examples, ensuring that similar instances are constrained to have similar outputs, and a diagonal label weight matrix that allows for the assignment of varying confidence levels to individual labeled examples. This feature is particularly valuable in scenarios where training labels are derived from distant supervision or crowdsourced annotation, as it enables the system to down-weight low-confidence labels and emphasize high-confidence ones without requiring additional manual curation. The resulting mapping function is computed in a single step using matrix algebra, eliminating the need for iterative training, gradient descent, or kernel approximation, thereby achieving computational efficiency comparable to linear regression while retaining the representational power of non-linear models. The closed-form nature of the solution guarantees global optimality with respect to the defined cost function, ensuring reproducibility and stability across deployments.

The invention further enables the construction of a scalable, dynamic medical relation knowledge base that complements and extends existing ontologies such as UMLS. By applying the trained relation detectors to the entire corpus, the system extracts millions of high-confidence relations in the form of triples (relation type, entity 1, entity 2, confidence score), where the confidence score is derived from the output of the manifold model and calibrated by the frequency of co-occurrence across the corpus. This knowledge base is not static but continuously expandable, as new text can be processed using the same model without retraining, enabling real-time updates to reflect emerging medical evidence. The system demonstrates superior performance over state-of-the-art baselines—including support vector machines with linear and tree kernels, and linear regression—achieving significant improvements in F1 score across seven clinically critical relation types, including “treats,” “contraindicates,” “causes,” “has symptom,” “has finding,” “has location,” and “has manifestation.” When evaluated on a real-world question-answering task using a set of 742 clinical dilemmas, the knowledge base generated by the invention outperformed the UMLS Metathesaurus in recall by a substantial margin at all levels of answer retrieval, demonstrating its superior coverage of clinically relevant relationships. The system’s computational efficiency allows for the processing of an 11-gigabyte corpus on a modest cluster of 16 machines in under eight hours, making it feasible for deployment in institutional settings with limited computational resources. The invention thus provides a transformative solution to the longstanding challenges of medical relation extraction, enabling the automated, scalable, and reliable extraction of clinically actionable knowledge from unstructured text, with direct applications in clinical decision support, knowledge base augmentation, automated literature synthesis, and personalized medicine.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention relates to a system and method for extracting semantic relations between medical entities from large-scale, unstructured clinical text using a manifold-based learning framework that integrates labeled and unlabeled data, preserves the intrinsic geometric structure of the medical knowledge space, and enables scalable, high-precision relation extraction without reliance on hand-crafted rules or perfect entity annotation. The system is designed to operate on a corpus of medical text comprising hundreds of millions of sentences, derived from a diverse array of sources including peer-reviewed journals, clinical guidelines, electronic health records, biomedical textbooks, MEDLINE abstracts, and publicly accessible medical encyclopedias such as Wikipedia. The corpus, in its entirety, contains approximately eighty million sentences and eleven gigabytes of pure textual content, representing a comprehensive snapshot of contemporary medical knowledge. The system does not assume the prior availability of perfectly annotated entity mentions or a restricted set of semantic categories; instead, it operates directly on raw text, automatically identifying candidate entities through a domain-specific parsing and concept mapping pipeline that leverages the Unified Medical Language System (UMLS) as its foundational knowledge source.

The first component of the system is the entity recognition and semantic typing module. This module employs a specialized parser adapted for the medical domain, known as MedicalESG, which is an optimized extension of the English Slot Grammar (ESG) framework. Unlike general-purpose parsers, MedicalESG incorporates a comprehensive medical lexicon derived from the UMLS 2012 Release, enabling it to recognize and disambiguate medical terminology with high precision while maintaining computational efficiency. The parser processes each sentence in the corpus and generates a dependency parse tree that encodes the grammatical relationships between words, including subject-predicate-object structures, modifier attachments, and prepositional dependencies. Simultaneously, the parser identifies all contiguous text spans that correspond to potential medical concepts and maps each span to one or more UMLS Concept Unique Identifiers (CUIs) using a fast lookup mechanism. For example, the term “antibiotics” is mapped to CUI C0003232, while “Lyme disease” is mapped to CUIs C0024198 and C0717360, reflecting its dual representation as both a disease entity and a potential diagnostic entity. Each CUI is then expanded into one or more UMLS semantic types, which are drawn from a standardized set of 133 categories that classify medical concepts into broad domains such as “Disease or Syndrome,” “Pharmacologic Substance,” “Organic Chemical,” “Anatomical Structure,” and “Immunologic Factor.” The semantic type assignment is not deterministic; rather, it is probabilistic and context-sensitive, allowing for the coexistence of multiple semantic interpretations for a single term. For instance, the term “Hepatitis B” may be associated with both “Disease or Syndrome” and “Pharmacologic Substance,” reflecting its usage in clinical discourse to refer either to the infectious condition or to the vaccine used for its prevention. Rather than selecting a single type, the system retains all associated types and encodes them as a binary vector of length 133, where each entry corresponds to a UMLS semantic type and is set to 1 if the entity is associated with that type, and 0 otherwise. This encoding preserves the ambiguity inherent in medical language and allows the subsequent learning algorithm to resolve it based on contextual evidence.

The second component of the system is the relation candidate generation module. This module identifies potential relation instances by scanning the parsed corpus for sentences in which two entities, each associated with one or more CUIs, co-occur within a predefined syntactic proximity—typically within the same sentence or adjacent sentences. The candidate relations are not assumed to be true; rather, they are hypothesized based on the co-occurrence of concept pairs known to be related in the UMLS Metathesaurus. For example, if the UMLS knowledge base contains a known “may treat” relationship between CUI C0003232 (“antibiotics”) and CUI C0024198 (“Lyme disease”), then every sentence in the corpus containing both of these CUIs is flagged as a candidate for the “treats” relation. This approach, known as distant supervision, generates a vast number of candidate instances—on the order of tens of millions—but inevitably introduces a high degree of noise, as many of these co-occurrences do not express the intended relation. For instance, the sentence “A recent study investigated the relationship between antibiotics and Lyme disease” contains both entities but does not assert that antibiotics treat Lyme disease; it merely references their co-occurrence in research. To mitigate this noise, the system employs a clustering-based filtering mechanism that reduces the candidate set to a manageable size for manual annotation. Specifically, for each relation type, the system performs K-medoids clustering on the set of candidate sentences, using a similarity metric defined as the bag-of-words similarity of the dependency paths connecting the two entities. The dependency path is extracted from the parse tree as the minimal sequence of grammatical relations linking the two entities—for example, the path between “antibiotics” and “Lyme disease” in the sentence “Antibiotics are the standard therapy for Lyme disease” may be represented as “subj → be → pred → standard therapy → for → obj.” The similarity between two dependency paths is computed as the cosine similarity of their word frequency vectors, normalized to account for syntactic variation. The K-medoids algorithm identifies the most representative sentences—those closest to the cluster centers—as candidates for manual annotation, reducing the candidate set from millions to a few thousand per relation. These representative sentences are then presented to domain experts for binary labeling as either positive (the relation is expressed) or negative (the relation is not expressed). The remaining sentences, which are not selected for annotation, are retained as unlabeled data for use in the learning phase.

The third component of the system is the feature extraction module, which transforms each relation instance—whether labeled or unlabeled—into a high-dimensional feature vector suitable for machine learning. Each feature vector is constructed from eight distinct feature groups, each capturing a different aspect of the linguistic and semantic context surrounding the entity pair. The first group consists of the UMLS semantic types of the first entity, encoded as a 133-dimensional binary vector. The second group consists of the UMLS semantic types of the second entity, encoded similarly. The third group captures syntactic features derived from the dependency path between the two entities, including the sequence of grammatical relations such as “subj,” “pred,” “mod,” “nprep,” and “objprep,” which are discretized and encoded as a sparse feature vector. The fourth group models the incoming and outgoing links of each entity in the dependency tree, capturing directional information that helps determine whether the relation flows from entity one to entity two or vice versa. The fifth and sixth groups capture topic-based features derived from latent semantic indexing (LSI), a technique that projects the words in the dependency path and the entire sentence onto a 100-dimensional latent topic space constructed from the entire corpus. These topic features encode the semantic themes of the context surrounding the entity pair, allowing the model to generalize beyond exact word matches. The seventh and eighth groups capture bag-of-words features derived from the dependency path and the sentence, respectively, but only include words that have appeared in the positive training examples, thereby focusing the model on discriminative vocabulary. The resulting feature vector for each relation instance is a concatenation of all eight groups, yielding a high-dimensional representation that encodes the entity identities, their semantic interpretations, their grammatical roles, and their contextual usage.

The fourth and most critical component of the system is the manifold-based relation extraction model, which learns a mapping function that assigns a confidence score to each relation instance based on its feature vector. The model is formulated as a constrained optimization problem that seeks to minimize a cost function composed of two terms: a supervised term that penalizes deviations between predicted scores and known labels, and an unsupervised term that penalizes differences in scores between neighboring examples in the feature space. The supervised term is defined as the weighted sum of squared errors between the predicted score for each labeled example and its true label, where the weight assigned to each labeled example is determined by a diagonal matrix ∆, whose entries reflect the confidence in the label. This weight matrix allows the system to assign higher confidence to labels derived from expert annotation and lower confidence to labels derived from distant supervision or crowdsourcing. The unsupervised term is defined as a graph Laplacian regularization term, which encourages similar examples to have similar scores by penalizing large differences in predicted scores between examples that are close in the feature space. The similarity between two examples is modeled using a weight matrix W, where each entry W_ij is computed as the exponential of the negative squared Euclidean distance between the feature vectors of examples i and j. This formulation ensures that examples with similar feature representations are constrained to have similar outputs, thereby preserving the local geometry of the data manifold. The overall cost function is given by C(f) = (f^T X A X^T f - 2 f^T X A y + y^T y) + µ (f^T X L X^T f), where f is the mapping function to be learned, X is the feature-instance matrix, A is a diagonal matrix of label weights, y is the vector of true labels, L is the graph Laplacian matrix derived from W, and µ is a regularization parameter that controls the trade-off between fitting the labeled data and preserving the manifold structure. The solution to this optimization problem is derived in closed form using matrix algebra, yielding f = (X(A + µL)X^T)^+ X A y^T, where ^+ denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse. This closed-form solution guarantees global optimality with respect to the cost function and can be computed in a single step, eliminating the need for iterative training, gradient descent, or kernel approximation. The resulting mapping function is computationally efficient at inference time, requiring only a matrix-vector multiplication to assign a confidence score to any new relation instance, making it suitable for real-time deployment.

The fifth component of the system is the knowledge base construction and application module. Once the manifold model is trained on the labeled and unlabeled data, it is applied to the entire corpus to extract a comprehensive set of medical relations. For each candidate relation instance, the model outputs a confidence score between -1 and 1, where positive scores indicate a high likelihood that the relation is expressed, and negative scores indicate the opposite. These scores are then aggregated across all occurrences of each relation type and entity pair to compute a final confidence score for each triple (relation type, entity 1, entity 2), taking into account both the model’s prediction and the frequency of co-occurrence in the corpus. The resulting knowledge base contains approximately 3.4 million such entries, covering all seven clinically critical relation types identified in the system: “treats,” “contraindicates,” “causes,” “has symptom,” “has finding,” “has location,” and “has manifestation.” This knowledge base is stored in a structured format that supports rapid querying and can be updated incrementally as new text is added to the corpus. The system is evaluated on a real-world clinical question-answering task using a set of 742 Doctor Dilemma questions, each of which represents a clinical scenario requiring the identification of a specific medical relationship. For each question, the system identifies the focus of the question (e.g., the disease or treatment being asked about) and the non-focus argument (e.g., the entity being queried), then uses the relation detector to identify the most likely relation between them. It then queries the knowledge base to retrieve candidate answers based on the relation type and the non-focus argument. For example, if the question asks “What is the standard treatment for Lyme disease?”, the system identifies “Lyme disease” as the focus and “treats” as the relation, then retrieves all entities that are linked to “Lyme disease” via the “treats” relation. The retrieved candidates are ranked by their confidence scores, and the top p candidates are returned as potential answers. When evaluated against the UMLS Metathesaurus, the knowledge base generated by the invention significantly outperforms UMLS in recall at all levels of answer retrieval, demonstrating its superior coverage of clinically relevant relationships. The UMLS knowledge base, while precise, is static and incomplete, containing only a fraction of the relations present in the literature. In contrast, the invention’s knowledge base is dynamic, scalable, and continuously expandable, enabling it to capture emerging medical knowledge as it is published.

The system’s computational efficiency is a key differentiator. The entire pipeline—from parsing and concept mapping to feature extraction and relation extraction—is optimized for speed and scalability. The MedicalESG parser processes text at a rate ten times faster than MetaMap, enabling the parsing of the entire 11-gigabyte corpus in a reasonable time frame. The closed-form nature of the manifold model ensures that inference is as fast as linear regression, requiring no iterative optimization or kernel computation. The system was deployed on a cluster of 16 machines, each equipped with four 2.8 GHz cores, and completed the extraction of 3.4 million relations from the entire corpus in just eight hours. This performance makes the system suitable for deployment in institutional settings with limited computational resources, including hospitals, research laboratories, and public health agencies. The system does not require continuous retraining; once the model is trained on a representative corpus, it can be applied to new text without modification, enabling real-time knowledge acquisition and updating. The system’s ability to integrate both labeled and unlabeled data, respect the geometric structure of the medical knowledge space, and deliver high-precision relations at scale represents a fundamental advancement in the field of medical information extraction. It overcomes the limitations of rule-based systems, kernel-based methods, and linear models by providing a unified, closed-form solution that is both accurate and efficient, enabling the automated extraction of clinically actionable knowledge from the vast and ever-expanding corpus of medical text.
