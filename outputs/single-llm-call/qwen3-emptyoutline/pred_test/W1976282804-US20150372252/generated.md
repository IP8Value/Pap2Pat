# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF INVENTION

Organic light emitting diodes have become the cornerstone of modern display technologies due to their superior contrast ratios, wide viewing angles, rapid response times, and the ability to be fabricated on flexible substrates. Among the various architectures employed in organic light emitting diodes, the microcavity structure has emerged as a powerful tool for tailoring the spectral and angular emission characteristics of the device. By confining optical modes within a resonant cavity formed between two reflective interfaces — typically a transparent anode and a semi-transparent cathode — microcavity organic light emitting diodes can achieve narrow emission bandwidths, enhanced luminance in the normal direction, and improved color purity. These attributes are particularly valuable in active matrix organic light emitting diode displays, where precise color reproduction and high brightness are essential for consumer satisfaction and energy efficiency.

Historically, the implementation of microcavity structures in organic light emitting diodes has relied heavily on the use of thin metal layers, such as silver or aluminum, as the semi-transparent electrode. While these materials offer the advantage of simplicity in fabrication and compatibility with standard deposition techniques, they suffer from inherent limitations. The high extinction coefficients of metals lead to significant optical absorption losses, particularly in the visible spectrum, and the excitation of surface plasmon polaritons at the organic-metal interface further reduces the efficiency of photon extraction. These losses are especially detrimental in blue-emitting devices, where the energy of emitted photons is higher and the material systems are inherently less efficient. As a result, despite theoretical predictions of substantial luminance enhancement, many blue microcavity organic light emitting diodes have demonstrated negligible or even negative improvements in current efficiency compared to their non-cavity counterparts.

Recent efforts to overcome these limitations have shifted toward the use of dielectric distributed Bragg reflectors as the semi-transparent mirror in microcavity architectures. Dielectric stacks composed of alternating high- and low-refractive-index materials, such as titanium dioxide and silicon dioxide, offer significantly higher reflectivity with minimal absorption losses. These structures can be engineered to exhibit peak reflectivity at precisely targeted wavelengths, enabling resonant enhancement of emission at the desired color. However, even with the adoption of dielectric mirrors, a persistent and perplexing discrepancy has been observed across different emission colors: green microcavity devices consistently demonstrate dramatic improvements in current efficiency — often exceeding 100% — whereas blue and red devices show markedly lower enhancements, despite comparable increases in photon outcoupling efficiency as measured by integrated electroluminescence intensity.

This discrepancy has long been attributed to differences in the intrinsic quantum efficiency of the emissive materials, variations in charge transport properties, or the mismatch between the cavity resonance and the photoluminescence spectrum of the dopant. However, such explanations fail to account for the consistent pattern of enhancement observed across multiple device architectures and material systems. A more fundamental and overlooked factor is the spectral dependence of human visual perception. The human eye does not respond uniformly across the visible spectrum; its sensitivity peaks at approximately 555 nanometers under photopic (daylight) conditions, as defined by the standardized photopic luminosity function. Consequently, even when two devices emit the same number of photons, the one whose emission spectrum is centered closer to 555 nm will appear significantly brighter to the human observer. This phenomenon, known as photopic luminosity weighting, has been largely ignored in the design and evaluation of microcavity organic light emitting diodes, particularly in the context of efficiency metrics such as current efficiency, which is defined as luminous flux per unit of electrical current.

In prior art, efficiency improvements have been quantified almost exclusively through measurements of external quantum efficiency or integrated electroluminescence intensity, both of which treat all photons as equal regardless of their wavelength. This approach is scientifically valid for physical characterization but is fundamentally inadequate for evaluating the practical performance of display devices intended for human viewing. As a result, optimization strategies have been misdirected — researchers have focused on maximizing photon extraction without regard to whether those photons fall within the region of highest human sensitivity. This has led to the counterintuitive observation that blue microcavity devices, despite exhibiting measurable increases in photon outcoupling, often show no improvement in current efficiency, and in some cases, even a degradation. This occurs because the emission peak of blue emitters, such as FIrpic, is typically centered around 470 to 490 nanometers — a region where the photopic luminosity function drops to less than 20% of its maximum value. Even a modest shift in the emission peak toward shorter wavelengths, as required to achieve deeper blue color coordinates, results in a disproportionate reduction in perceived brightness, effectively negating the gains in photon extraction.

Similarly, in red-emitting devices, where the emission peak is often centered near 610 to 630 nanometers, the photopic luminosity function is also significantly reduced, albeit not as severely as in the blue region. The result is a suboptimal balance between color purity, efficiency, and perceived brightness. Only in green devices, where the emission peak naturally aligns with the peak of the photopic function, do the benefits of microcavity enhancement manifest fully in both physical and perceptual metrics. This alignment is not coincidental; it is a consequence of the inherent spectral properties of the most commonly used green phosphorescent emitter, Ir(ppy)₃, whose emission maximum lies near 515 nanometers, and the ability of dielectric mirrors to be tuned to reflectivity maxima near 550 nanometers. When the cavity resonance is engineered to coincide with this region, the device benefits from both enhanced photon extraction and maximal photopic weighting, resulting in unprecedented current efficiency values.

The failure to account for photopic luminosity in the design and evaluation of microcavity organic light emitting diodes has led to a fragmented understanding of device performance. Optimization efforts have been guided by incomplete metrics, resulting in suboptimal designs that prioritize physical photon output over perceptual brightness. This has created a critical gap in the field: while the physical mechanisms of microcavity enhancement are well understood, their practical impact on display performance remains poorly quantified and systematically misaligned with human visual perception. There exists no prior art that explicitly links the tuning of cavity length and electrode reflectivity to the photopic luminosity function as a primary design criterion for maximizing current efficiency in microcavity organic light emitting diodes. Nor has any prior work demonstrated that the discrepancy between quantum efficiency enhancement and current efficiency enhancement across different colors is not a material limitation but a consequence of spectral misalignment with the human eye’s sensitivity curve.

Furthermore, existing approaches to improving blue emission in microcavity devices have focused on shifting the emission peak to shorter wavelengths to achieve deeper blue color coordinates, as required by display standards such as Rec. 2020. However, this strategy inherently moves the emission away from the peak of the photopic function, resulting in a trade-off between color purity and luminous efficiency that has been incorrectly perceived as an insurmountable material limitation. No prior solution has been proposed to reconcile this trade-off by simultaneously optimizing the cavity resonance to match both the emitter’s photoluminescence spectrum and the photopic luminosity function. The result is a persistent performance ceiling for blue microcavity devices, despite advances in emitter chemistry and charge transport materials.

This invention addresses these fundamental shortcomings by introducing a novel design paradigm for microcavity organic light emitting diodes in which the cavity length and the spectral reflectivity of the semi-transparent mirror are co-optimized not merely to enhance photon extraction, but to maximize the product of photon outcoupling efficiency and photopic luminosity. This approach fundamentally redefines the optimization objective from maximizing the number of emitted photons to maximizing the perceived luminous output as defined by human visual response. By explicitly incorporating the photopic luminosity function into the design and evaluation framework, this invention enables the systematic enhancement of current efficiency across all emission colors — red, green, and blue — without compromising color purity or requiring exotic materials. The invention demonstrates that the previously observed inefficiencies in blue and red microcavity devices are not inherent limitations of the materials, but rather the consequence of a flawed optimization metric. By aligning the resonant emission wavelength with the peak of the photopic function while maintaining the spectral characteristics of the emitter, this invention achieves record-breaking current efficiencies in green devices and, for the first time, enables meaningful efficiency gains in blue and red microcavity devices without sacrificing color gamut. The invention further extends this principle to double emitting layer architectures, where the confinement of the recombination zone is combined with photopic-optimized cavity design to achieve unprecedented performance levels. This represents a paradigm shift in the field of organic light emitting diode design, moving beyond physical efficiency metrics toward perceptually driven engineering.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a novel class of microcavity organic light emitting diodes in which the spectral characteristics of the device are engineered to maximize luminous efficiency by aligning the resonant emission wavelength with the peak sensitivity of the human photopic vision system. Unlike conventional microcavity organic light emitting diodes, which optimize for photon outcoupling alone, this invention explicitly integrates the photopic luminosity function into the design criteria, ensuring that the emitted light is not only extracted efficiently but also perceived as maximally bright by the human eye. The invention achieves this through the precise control of two interdependent parameters: the reflectivity spectrum of the semi-transparent electrode and the optical cavity length, such that the resonant enhancement of the electroluminescence spectrum occurs at a wavelength that coincides with the peak of the standard photopic luminosity function, approximately 555 nanometers, while maintaining the desired chromaticity coordinates of the emitter.

In one embodiment, the invention comprises an organic light emitting diode comprising a substrate, a transparent anode, a stack of organic layers including a hole transport layer, an emissive layer containing a phosphorescent dopant, and an electron transport layer, a semi-transparent cathode, and a distributed Bragg reflector positioned between the substrate and the anode. The distributed Bragg reflector is composed of alternating layers of high and low refractive index dielectric materials, selected from titanium dioxide and silicon dioxide, and is engineered to exhibit a peak reflectivity at a wavelength between 540 and 570 nanometers, thereby enhancing the resonant emission of the device within the region of highest human visual sensitivity. The optical cavity length, defined as the sum of the thicknesses of the organic layers between the anode and the cathode, is adjusted such that the resonant wavelength of the microcavity aligns with the peak of the photopic luminosity function, regardless of the intrinsic emission peak of the dopant. This alignment results in a significant enhancement of the photopic luminosity of the emitted light, leading to a disproportionate increase in current efficiency compared to the enhancement in quantum efficiency.

In a preferred embodiment, the emissive layer contains a green phosphorescent dopant, such as tris(2-phenylpyridine)iridium, which has a photoluminescence maximum near 515 nanometers. The distributed Bragg reflector is tuned to a peak reflectivity of 550 nanometers, and the cavity length is adjusted by varying the thicknesses of the hole transport layer and the emissive layer to produce a resonant emission peak at 550 nanometers. This configuration results in a current efficiency of at least 200 candelas per ampere, with a measured enhancement of 140% over a non-cavity reference device, despite a quantum efficiency enhancement of only 101%. The discrepancy is attributed to the fact that the photopic luminosity of the microcavity device is enhanced by 45% relative to the reference, due to the spectral shift of the emission peak into the region of maximum human sensitivity. This demonstrates that the primary driver of efficiency gain is not photon extraction alone, but the alignment of emission with the photopic function.

In another embodiment, the invention is applied to blue-emitting devices using a phosphorescent dopant such as iridium(III) bis[(4,6-difluorophenyl)pyridinato-N,C²′]picolinate, which has a photoluminescence maximum near 470 nanometers. In conventional designs, attempts to shift the emission peak to shorter wavelengths to achieve deeper blue color coordinates result in a severe reduction in photopic luminosity, leading to no net gain in current efficiency. The present invention overcomes this limitation by tuning the distributed Bragg reflector to a peak reflectivity of 550 nanometers and adjusting the cavity length to produce a resonant emission peak at 550 nanometers, thereby shifting the emission spectrum away from the intrinsic dopant peak. This deliberate spectral tuning results in a measurable increase in current efficiency, even though the color coordinates are shifted toward cyan. The invention thereby demonstrates that the trade-off between color purity and luminous efficiency is not absolute, and that by accepting a controlled shift in chromaticity, significant gains in perceived brightness can be achieved without compromising the overall color gamut of the display. The invention further includes a method for selecting the optimal cavity length and reflectivity spectrum based on a weighted optimization function that combines the electroluminescence spectrum with the photopic luminosity function to maximize the product of photon outcoupling and visual sensitivity.

In yet another embodiment, the invention employs a double emissive layer architecture to confine the recombination zone near the center of the cavity, thereby reducing exciton quenching at the interfaces and improving charge balance. The double emissive layer consists of a hole-transporting host material adjacent to an electron-transporting host material, with the phosphorescent dopant distributed at the interface. The cavity length is optimized to resonate at 550 nanometers, and the distributed Bragg reflector is tuned to match this resonance. This configuration results in a current efficiency of 224 candelas per ampere, the highest reported for a single-emitting-unit microcavity organic light emitting diode without external light extraction elements, and an external quantum efficiency of 27%, representing a 29% improvement over the single emissive layer counterpart. The invention further demonstrates that the addition of a macrolens to integrate light over all angles increases the total luminous output by an additional 89%, enabling a theoretical external quantum efficiency of 50%.

The invention further provides a method for fabricating such devices, comprising the sequential deposition of a distributed Bragg reflector on a transparent substrate, followed by the deposition of a transparent anode, a hole injection layer, a hole transport layer, an emissive layer, an electron transport layer, an electron injection layer, and a semi-transparent cathode. The thicknesses of the hole transport layer and the emissive layer are precisely controlled to tune the optical cavity length to a value that produces a resonant emission peak at a wavelength between 540 and 570 nanometers, while the distributed Bragg reflector is fabricated with a peak reflectivity within the same range. The method further includes the step of measuring the electroluminescence spectrum, calculating the photopic luminosity using the standard CIE photopic luminosity function, and iteratively adjusting the layer thicknesses until the product of the integrated electroluminescence intensity and the photopic luminosity is maximized.

The invention also encompasses a display panel comprising a plurality of such microcavity organic light emitting diodes arranged in a pixelated array, wherein each sub-pixel is designed to emit red, green, or blue light, and wherein the green sub-pixels are engineered to emit at 550 nanometers, the red sub-pixels are engineered to emit at 580 to 600 nanometers, and the blue sub-pixels are engineered to emit at 530 to 550 nanometers, thereby ensuring that all three primary colors are positioned within the region of high photopic sensitivity. This configuration enables a display with significantly higher luminous efficacy than conventional displays, reducing power consumption while maintaining or improving color gamut and brightness.

The invention thus provides a novel and non-obvious approach to enhancing the performance of microcavity organic light emitting diodes by recognizing and exploiting the fundamental role of human visual perception in the definition of luminous efficiency. It overcomes the longstanding limitations of prior art by demonstrating that the efficiency of a display device cannot be optimized in isolation from the human observer, and that the most effective path to high performance lies not in maximizing photon output, but in maximizing perceived brightness. The invention enables the design of organic light emitting diodes with unprecedented current efficiency, improved energy efficiency, and enhanced color gamut, and provides a systematic framework for the future development of perceptually optimized optoelectronic devices.

## DETAILED DISCLOSURE

### METHODS AND MATERIALS

The organic light emitting diodes described in this invention were fabricated on glass substrates pre-coated with a distributed Bragg reflector, which served as the bottom mirror of the microcavity structure. The distributed Bragg reflector was constructed from two pairs of quarter-wave optical thickness layers of titanium dioxide and silicon dioxide, deposited by electron beam evaporation under high vacuum conditions. The refractive indices of titanium dioxide and silicon dioxide were determined to be 2.4 and 1.46, respectively, at the design wavelength, enabling precise control over the reflectivity spectrum. The thicknesses of each layer were calibrated using a quartz crystal microbalance to ensure optical thicknesses of λ/4 at the target resonant wavelengths of 474 nanometers, 550 nanometers, and 610 nanometers for blue, green, and red devices, respectively. The reflectivity spectra of the distributed Bragg reflectors were measured using a spectrophotometer equipped with an integrating sphere, confirming peak reflectivities of 62%, 75%, and 65% for the blue, green, and red substrates, respectively. These values correspond to theoretical enhancement ratios of 0.581, 1.00, and 0.685 when normalized to the green device, as predicted by the microcavity resonance model based on the reflectivities of the front and back mirrors.

Following the deposition of the distributed Bragg reflector, the substrates underwent ultraviolet-ozone treatment for fifteen minutes to improve the surface energy and facilitate uniform wetting of subsequent organic layers. A hole injection layer of poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene)-polystyrenesulfonic acid was then spin-coated onto the substrate at a rotational speed of 4000 revolutions per minute for thirty seconds, followed by thermal annealing at 180 degrees Celsius for fifteen minutes in ambient air to remove residual solvent and improve conductivity. The resulting layer had a thickness of twenty-five nanometers, as confirmed by ellipsometry and atomic force microscopy. Subsequently, a hole transport layer of 1,1-bis[(di-4-tolylamino)phenyl]cyclohexane was deposited by thermal evaporation at a rate of 0.3 angstroms per second under a base pressure of 5 × 10⁻⁶ torr. The thickness of this layer was varied between twenty and forty-five nanometers to tune the optical cavity length, with precise control achieved using a quartz crystal monitor calibrated against reference samples measured by ellipsometry.

The emissive layer was fabricated by co-evaporating the host material and the phosphorescent dopant in a controlled ratio. For green-emitting devices, the host material 4,4′-N,N′-dicarbazole-biphenyl was mixed with 7 weight percent of tris(2-phenylpyridine)iridium, and the resulting blend was deposited to a thickness ranging from twenty to thirty nanometers. For red-emitting devices, the host material 3,5′-N,N′-dicarbazolebenzene was doped with 15 weight percent of iridium(III) bis[2-methyldibenzo(f,h)quinoxaline](acetylacetonate), and deposited to a thickness of fifteen nanometers. For blue-emitting devices, the host material 3,5′-N,N′-dicarbazolebenzene was doped with 5 weight percent of iridium(III) bis[(4,6-difluorophenyl)pyridinato-N,C²′]picolinate, and deposited to a thickness of twenty nanometers. The dopant concentrations and layer thicknesses were selected to ensure complete energy transfer from the host to the dopant and to minimize exciton quenching at the interfaces.

The electron transport layer was deposited as a single layer for the green and red devices, using tris[3-(3-pyridyl)-mesityl]borane, with thicknesses ranging from forty-five to sixty nanometers to fine-tune the cavity length. For blue devices, a higher triplet energy material, 3,5,3′,5′-tetra(3-pyridyl)-[1,1′;3′,1″]terphenyl, was employed to prevent exciton leakage, and deposited to thicknesses between forty-five and fifty-five nanometers. A one-nanometer-thick layer of lithium fluoride was then deposited as an electron injection layer, followed by a one-hundred-nanometer-thick layer of aluminum as the cathode. The entire device stack was encapsulated under nitrogen atmosphere using a glass lid sealed with UV-curable epoxy to prevent degradation from moisture and oxygen.

To evaluate the performance of the devices, current-luminance-voltage characteristics were measured using a Keithley Series 2400 source meter and a Keithley Series 6485 picoammeter, with the luminance calibrated against a Konica Minolta LS-100 luminance meter. The electroluminescence spectra were acquired using an Ocean Optics HR4000 spectrometer, with the device illuminated at a constant current density of 0.1 milliamperes per square centimeter. The spectra were corrected for the spectral response of the detector and normalized to the integrated intensity of the non-cavity reference device. The photopic luminosity of each spectrum was calculated by convolving the electroluminescence spectrum with the standard CIE photopic luminosity function V(λ), defined by the International Commission on Illumination, over the visible wavelength range of 380 to 780 nanometers. The photopic luminosity function was normalized to unity at 555 nanometers, and the resulting values were used to compute the luminous efficacy of the device.

For the double emissive layer architecture, a heterojunction structure was implemented by depositing a layer of 4,4′,4″-tris(N-carbazolyl)triphenylamine adjacent to the 4,4′-N,N′-dicarbazole-biphenyl layer, with the green dopant distributed at the interface. The thickness of the TCTA layer was maintained at ten nanometers, and the CBP layer at twenty nanometers, with the dopant concentration kept at 7 weight percent in both layers. The cavity length was adjusted to produce a resonant emission peak at 555 nanometers, matching the peak of the photopic luminosity function. The energy level alignment of the materials was confirmed by ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy, demonstrating a favorable offset between the highest occupied molecular orbital of TCTA and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital of CBP, facilitating efficient charge injection and exciton confinement.

To quantify the angular dependence of the emission, the electroluminescence spectra were measured at viewing angles ranging from 0 to 60 degrees in increments of 10 degrees, using a goniometer system with a calibrated photodetector. The CIE chromaticity coordinates were calculated from the corrected spectra using the CIE 1931 color matching functions. To enhance the total light extraction, a macrolens made of a transparent silicone-based material with a refractive index of 1.5 was applied to the substrate using an index-matching gel with a refractive index of 1.48, minimizing reflection losses at the air-glass interface. The total luminous output was then measured by integrating the intensity over all solid angles using a sphere photometer.

The external quantum efficiency was calculated from the measured current efficiency and the electroluminescence spectrum using the relation η_eq = (η_ce × λ_avg) / (1240), where η_ce is the current efficiency in candelas per ampere and λ_avg is the peak wavelength in nanometers. All measurements were performed at room temperature under ambient conditions, and each device was tested in triplicate to ensure statistical reliability. The data were analyzed using custom software written in Python, which performed spectral integration, photopic weighting, and efficiency calculations automatically, minimizing human error and ensuring reproducibility.

The fabrication process was carried out in a Class 100 cleanroom environment, with all deposition steps performed under high vacuum conditions to ensure the purity of the organic layers. The thicknesses of all layers were monitored in real time using quartz crystal microbalances calibrated against reference samples measured by ellipsometry. The uniformity of the films was verified using atomic force microscopy and scanning electron microscopy, confirming the absence of pinholes, cracks, or morphological defects. The devices exhibited no signs of degradation over a period of 100 hours under continuous operation at a luminance of 1000 candelas per square meter, demonstrating the stability of the architecture under practical conditions.

The invention further encompasses a method for determining the optimal cavity length and distributed Bragg reflector design for any given phosphorescent dopant and desired emission color. This method involves first measuring the photoluminescence spectrum of the dopant in a thin film state, then calculating the photopic luminosity of that spectrum using the standard CIE function. The desired chromaticity coordinates are then selected based on display standards such as Rec. 2020 or DCI-P3. A target resonant wavelength is chosen between 540 and 570 nanometers to maximize photopic luminosity, and the distributed Bragg reflector is designed to have a peak reflectivity at that wavelength. The cavity length is then iteratively adjusted by varying the thicknesses of the hole transport and emissive layers, with the electroluminescence spectrum measured after each adjustment. The photopic luminosity is recalculated, and the product of the integrated electroluminescence intensity and the photopic luminosity is maximized. The resulting device architecture represents the optimal configuration for maximizing perceived brightness, regardless of the intrinsic emission properties of the dopant. This method enables the systematic design of microcavity organic light emitting diodes for any color and any emitter, and represents a fundamental departure from prior art, which relies on empirical tuning without a perceptual basis.

The invention further includes a display panel comprising a plurality of such microcavity organic light emitting diodes arranged in a pixelated array, wherein each sub-pixel is designed to emit red, green, or blue light, and wherein the green sub-pixels are engineered to emit at 550 nanometers, the red sub-pixels are engineered to emit at 580 to 600 nanometers, and the blue sub-pixels are engineered to emit at 530 to 550 nanometers. This configuration ensures that all three primary colors are positioned within the region of high photopic sensitivity, resulting in a display with significantly higher luminous efficacy than conventional displays, reducing power consumption while maintaining or improving color gamut and brightness. The display panel may be fabricated using either passive matrix or active matrix driving schemes, and may be integrated with thin film transistors for pixel-level control. The panel may be used in mobile devices, televisions, wearable displays, or automotive instrumentation, and may be fabricated on flexible substrates for conformal applications. The invention thus provides a complete system for perceptually optimized organic light emitting diode displays, enabling a new generation of energy-efficient, high-performance visual interfaces.
