# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of cancer immunotherapy has evolved significantly over the past several decades, with a growing emphasis on harnessing the body’s own immune system to recognize and eliminate malignant cells. Among the most promising strategies are dendritic cell (DC)-based vaccines, which aim to prime and activate tumor-specific T cell responses by presenting tumor-associated antigens in the context of appropriate co-stimulatory and inflammatory signals. While the FDA-approved vaccine Sipuleucel-T demonstrated a survival benefit in patients with metastatic prostate cancer, its clinical utility remains limited by its reliance on a single tumor antigen, prostatic acid phosphatase, and the logistical complexity of personalized ex vivo processing of patient-derived peripheral blood mononuclear cells. This constraint renders such approaches impractical for cancers lacking well-defined, immunogenic antigens—particularly aggressive, heterogeneous malignancies such as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), where neoantigen burden is low and HLA-restricted epitopes are difficult to predict and validate. Alternative strategies, including the use of irradiated tumor cells engineered to secrete granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), as exemplified by GVAX, have shown limited success due to uncontrolled cytokine release, which paradoxically promotes the expansion of immunosuppressive myeloid-derived suppressor cells and impairs dendritic cell maturation. Similarly, tumor lysate-based vaccines, while capable of presenting a broad repertoire of antigens, often fail to induce robust immune activation without additional adjuvants, and their immunogenicity can be further diminished by the presence of tolerogenic molecules within the lysate. More recent innovations involving mRNA delivery to dendritic cells have demonstrated encouraging results in personalized cancer vaccines, particularly when encoding poly-neo-epitopes, yet these methods remain prohibitively expensive, time-intensive, and dependent on high-quality tumor sequencing and bioinformatic prediction pipelines that are not universally accessible. Furthermore, the instability of mRNA and the need for specialized delivery vehicles, such as lipid nanoparticles, introduce additional variables that complicate manufacturing scalability and regulatory approval. In parallel, efforts to enhance antigen presentation through the use of particulate vaccine platforms have gained traction, with evidence suggesting that particles in the size range of 100 to 1000 nanometers are optimally internalized by dendritic cells and efficiently trafficked to lymphoid tissues, thereby promoting robust T cell priming. Tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs), derived from the plasma membranes of tumor cells through ultracentrifugation and density gradient purification, represent a naturally occurring particulate antigen source that retains the full complement of tumor-associated surface proteins, including glycoproteins, receptors, and immune-modulatory molecules. These vesicles, typically 200 to 600 nanometers in diameter, mirror the antigenic profile of the parent tumor and are inherently phagocytosable by antigen-presenting cells. However, despite their structural advantages, unmodified TMVs often lack sufficient immunostimulatory capacity to overcome the immunosuppressive microenvironment characteristic of established tumors. To address this limitation, prior approaches have sought to chemically conjugate or genetically engineer immunostimulatory ligands onto the surface of TMVs, but these methods frequently suffer from low efficiency, instability, or non-physiological orientation of the appended molecules. A more elegant and biologically relevant solution lies in the use of glycosyl phosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored immunostimulatory molecules (GPI-ISMs), which, due to their lipid tail, can spontaneously integrate into the phospholipid bilayer of TMVs through a process known as protein transfer. This mechanism allows for the stable, oriented, and functional incorporation of cytokines such as GM-CSF and IL-12 directly onto the vesicle surface without the need for chemical crosslinking or recombinant fusion proteins. GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-IL-12, when incorporated into TMVs, not only enhance dendritic cell activation through receptor-mediated signaling but also promote the internalization of the vesicle itself, creating a synergistic platform in which antigen and adjuvant are delivered simultaneously in a single, physiologically relevant particle. This innovation enables the generation of a potent, off-the-shelf, tumor-antigen-rich vaccine that can be produced from archived or minimally invasive tumor samples, including biopsy material, thereby overcoming the critical limitation of tissue availability that has hindered the clinical translation of many autologous tumor-derived therapies. Moreover, the ex vivo pulsing of autologous dendritic cells with these GPI-ISM-adjuvanted TMVs provides a controlled, scalable, and reproducible method for generating therapeutic vaccines that bypass the need for systemic cytokine administration, thereby minimizing off-target toxicity while maximizing tumor-specific immune activation. The ability to generate such a vaccine from frozen tumor tissue further enhances its utility in clinical settings where fresh tumor material is unavailable, such as in cases of recurrent or metastatic disease following prior resection. This approach thus represents a transformative advancement in cancer immunotherapy, merging the antigenic breadth of whole-tumor-derived vesicles with the precision and potency of GPI-anchored cytokine delivery and the controlled delivery dynamics of dendritic cell-based vaccination.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a novel dendritic cell-based therapeutic vaccine platform for the treatment of cancer, comprising autologous dendritic cells that have been pulsed ex vivo with tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs) incorporating glycosyl phosphatidylinositol-anchored immunostimulatory molecules (GPI-ISMs) on their surface. The TMVs are derived from tumor tissue, whether obtained from primary tumors, metastatic lesions, or archived frozen samples, and are purified to a size range of approximately 200 to 600 nanometers, thereby facilitating efficient uptake by dendritic cells. The GPI-ISMs, selected from the group consisting of GPI-GM-CSF, GPI-IL-12, or a combination thereof, are incorporated onto the TMV surface via spontaneous protein transfer, resulting in stable, functional, and orientationally correct presentation of these immunostimulatory cytokines without the need for chemical conjugation or genetic fusion. The dendritic cells, preferably bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) generated from the host subject, are cultured ex vivo and exposed to the GPI-ISM-incorporated TMVs for a duration sufficient to enable internalization and activation, typically ranging from 12 to 48 hours. Following pulsing, the dendritic cells are washed to remove unbound vesicles and administered to the subject via subcutaneous, intradermal, or intranodal injection. The invention provides a method for inhibiting tumor growth, reducing metastatic burden, and enhancing tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte populations in subjects with solid tumors, including but not limited to breast cancer, melanoma, ovarian cancer, and lung cancer. The therapeutic efficacy of the vaccine is achieved through the synergistic enhancement of dendritic cell uptake, maturation, and cytokine production induced by the GPI-ISMs, which together promote the priming of tumor-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses. Notably, the invention is effective even in tumor models lacking a defined, single tumor antigen, such as triple-negative breast cancer, where the broad antigenic repertoire presented by the TMVs enables immune recognition of multiple tumor-associated epitopes. The invention further provides a method for generating the TMVs from frozen tumor tissue, enabling their use in clinical scenarios where fresh tumor material is unavailable, such as in recurrent or metastatic disease. The GPI-ISM incorporation process is scalable, reproducible, and compatible with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards, allowing for the production of consistent, well-characterized vaccine batches. The dendritic cell pulsing and administration protocol is designed to minimize systemic cytokine exposure, thereby reducing the risk of off-target immunotoxicity while maximizing local immune activation at the tumor site. The resulting vaccine induces a durable anti-tumor immune response characterized by increased infiltration of activated dendritic cells and cytotoxic T lymphocytes into the tumor microenvironment, reduced tumor volume, decreased metastatic spread, and prolonged survival in preclinical models. This invention thus provides a broadly applicable, clinically translatable, and highly effective dendritic cell-based immunotherapy that overcomes the limitations of prior antigen-specific, cytokine-driven, or lysate-based approaches by integrating the antigenic diversity of whole-tumor membranes with the potent, localized immune stimulation conferred by GPI-anchored cytokines.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Tumor Membrane Vesicles (TMVs)

Tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs) are subcellular, membrane-bound structures derived from the plasma membrane of tumor cells, possessing a diameter ranging from approximately 200 to 600 nanometers, a size range that has been empirically demonstrated to optimize uptake by professional antigen-presenting cells, particularly dendritic cells and macrophages. These vesicles retain the full complement of surface proteins, glycoproteins, lipids, and other molecular signatures present on the original tumor cell membrane, thereby serving as a comprehensive and physiologically authentic representation of the tumor’s antigenic profile. Unlike lysates or purified peptides, TMVs preserve the native conformation and post-translational modifications of tumor-associated antigens, including those that are poorly immunogenic or conformationally dependent, such as glycosylated receptors and transmembrane proteins. The preparation of TMVs begins with the procurement of tumor tissue, which may be obtained from fresh surgical resections, core biopsies, or archived frozen samples stored at temperatures below −80°C. The tissue is mechanically homogenized using a tissue grinder or similar device in a buffered isotonic solution to disrupt cellular integrity while preserving membrane fragments. The resulting homogenate is subjected to a series of differential centrifugation steps to remove nuclei, cellular debris, and large organelles, followed by ultracentrifugation over a discontinuous sucrose density gradient, typically containing a 41% sucrose layer, to isolate the plasma membrane-derived vesicles. The TMV-containing fraction is collected, washed multiple times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual sucrose and soluble contaminants, and concentrated via ultrafiltration or additional ultracentrifugation. The final preparation is characterized for particle size distribution using dynamic light scattering, which consistently reveals a mean diameter of 200 to 600 nanometers with a polydispersity index below 0.4, indicating a relatively homogeneous population suitable for biological delivery. Surface marker expression is confirmed via flow cytometry using antibodies specific to tumor-associated antigens, such as HER2 in HER2-positive breast cancer models, or pan-tumor markers such as EpCAM or MUC1 in other contexts. The absence of intracellular contaminants, such as endoplasmic reticulum or mitochondrial markers, is verified by negative staining for calnexin, cytochrome c, or other organelle-specific proteins. The integrity of the vesicle membrane is assessed by electron microscopy, which reveals a closed, spherical, bilayered structure resembling the plasma membrane of the parent tumor cell. Importantly, TMVs derived from frozen tissue retain the same antigenic and structural properties as those derived from fresh tissue, enabling their use in clinical settings where tumor material is not immediately available, such as in cases of recurrent disease following prior resection or in patients who have undergone neoadjuvant therapy. The ability to store TMVs at ultra-low temperatures without loss of antigenicity or membrane integrity further enhances their utility as an off-the-shelf therapeutic product. The vesicles are non-replicative, non-infectious, and do not contain nuclear material, rendering them inherently safe for clinical administration. Their particulate nature ensures that they are efficiently captured by dendritic cells in lymphoid tissues, promoting antigen processing and presentation via both MHC class I and MHC class II pathways, thereby enabling the activation of both CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and CD4+ helper T cells. The broad antigenic content of TMVs circumvents the need for prior identification of immunodominant epitopes, making this approach particularly advantageous for heterogeneous, antigenically diverse tumors such as triple-negative breast cancer, where traditional neoantigen-based vaccines have demonstrated limited efficacy. The TMVs thus serve as a natural, self-adjuvanting antigen source that captures the complexity of the tumor immune landscape without requiring prior knowledge of specific targets.

### TMV Preparation, Characterization, and GPI-ISM Incorporation

The preparation of tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs) incorporates a series of meticulously controlled steps designed to ensure reproducibility, purity, and functional integrity of the final product. Following homogenization and ultracentrifugation over a 41% sucrose gradient, the TMV fraction is collected and subjected to buffer exchange via tangential flow filtration or repeated ultracentrifugation in phosphate-buffered saline to eliminate residual sucrose and other solutes that may interfere with downstream applications. The concentration of TMVs is quantified by protein assay, typically using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) method, with a standard curve generated from purified bovine serum albumin. Particle concentration is further confirmed using nanoparticle tracking analysis or tunable resistive pulse sensing, ensuring that the final preparation contains a defined number of vesicles per microgram of protein. Characterization of the TMVs is performed using multiple orthogonal methods, including dynamic light scattering to determine hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index, transmission electron microscopy to visualize morphology and membrane integrity, and flow cytometry to assess the presence of tumor-associated surface antigens. For example, in HER2-positive models, TMVs are stained with fluorescently labeled anti-HER2 antibodies and analyzed for fluorescence intensity relative to control vesicles derived from non-transfected cells. The absence of contamination by non-plasma membrane components is confirmed by negative staining for intracellular markers such as calnexin, GRP78, or cytochrome c oxidase. Once characterized, the TMVs are prepared for the incorporation of glycosyl phosphatidylinositol-anchored immunostimulatory molecules (GPI-ISMs). These molecules, which include GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-IL-12, are produced recombinantly in mammalian cell expression systems, such as HEK293 or CHO cells, and purified via affinity chromatography using tags such as His6 or FLAG, followed by endotoxin removal using polymyxin B resin. The purified GPI-ISMs are then incubated with the TMVs at a molar ratio of approximately 25 µg of GPI-ISM per milligram of TMV protein, in a volume of phosphate-buffered saline supplemented with 1 mM calcium chloride to enhance membrane fluidity and facilitate lipid insertion. The mixture is incubated at 37°C under constant rotation for a period of four hours, allowing the GPI anchor to spontaneously integrate into the phospholipid bilayer of the TMV membrane through hydrophobic interactions. This process, known as protein transfer, results in the stable, oriented, and functional display of the cytokine moiety on the external surface of the vesicle, preserving its receptor-binding conformation and biological activity. Following incubation, unbound GPI-ISMs are removed by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for one hour, and the resulting TMV pellet is washed three times with PBS to ensure complete removal of free cytokine. The successful incorporation of GPI-ISMs is confirmed by flow cytometry using fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies specific to GM-CSF or IL-12, with a clear shift in fluorescence intensity observed in GPI-ISM-incorporated TMVs compared to control vesicles lacking the cytokine. Functional validation is performed by incubating the GPI-ISM-TMVs with dendritic cells and measuring downstream signaling events, such as STAT3 phosphorylation for GM-CSF or STAT4 activation for IL-12, confirming that the cytokines remain biologically active following incorporation. The stability of the GPI-ISM-TMV complex is assessed over time, with no significant loss of cytokine signal observed after 72 hours of storage at 4°C, and minimal degradation after freeze-thaw cycles, indicating suitability for cryopreservation and clinical logistics. The final product is sterile-filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane, aliquoted, and stored at −80°C until use. The entire preparation process is conducted under aseptic conditions, and endotoxin levels are measured using the Limulus amebocyte lysate (LAL) assay, with all final preparations required to contain less than 0.1 EU per microgram of protein to meet regulatory standards for in vivo administration. The resulting GPI-ISM-incorporated TMVs constitute a novel, self-adjuvanting vaccine platform that simultaneously delivers tumor antigens and innate immune signals in a single, physiologically coherent particle, enabling a coordinated and potent activation of the adaptive immune response.

### TMVs Containing the Tumor Antigen HER2 and Protein Transferred GPI-ISMs on Their Surface

The tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs) described herein are uniquely configured to carry both tumor-specific antigens and immunostimulatory cytokines on their surface, creating a dual-function therapeutic entity that enhances antigen presentation while simultaneously promoting dendritic cell maturation. In the context of HER2-positive breast cancer, TMVs derived from the D2F2/E2 cell line, which is stably transfected with the full-length human ErbB-2 gene, retain high levels of HER2 protein expression on their membrane surface, as confirmed by flow cytometric analysis using anti-HER2 monoclonal antibodies. The expression level of HER2 on the TMVs closely mirrors that observed on the parent D2F2/E2 cells, indicating that the vesiculation process does not selectively exclude or degrade this critical tumor antigen. This preservation of HER2 is essential for the induction of HER2-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses, which are known to correlate with improved clinical outcomes in HER2-positive breast cancer patients. In parallel, the TMVs are modified to incorporate glycosyl phosphatidylinositol-anchored immunostimulatory molecules (GPI-ISMs), specifically GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-IL-12, via the protein transfer mechanism. Flow cytometric analysis reveals that unmodified TMVs exhibit no detectable signal for GM-CSF or IL-12, whereas TMVs incubated with purified GPI-ISMs demonstrate a significant and dose-dependent increase in cytokine surface expression, with fluorescence intensity levels comparable to those observed on cytokine-expressing cell lines. The incorporation of GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-IL-12 is not merely structural but functional, as evidenced by the ability of these vesicles to induce phosphorylation of STAT3 and STAT4, respectively, in dendritic cells upon co-incubation. The presence of both cytokines on the same vesicle surface creates a synergistic signaling environment, where GM-CSF promotes dendritic cell survival, proliferation, and antigen uptake, while IL-12 drives the differentiation of naïve T cells into Th1 effector cells and enhances cytotoxic T lymphocyte activity. Importantly, the spatial co-localization of HER2 and the GPI-ISMs on the same vesicle ensures that dendritic cells encountering the vesicle are simultaneously exposed to the tumor antigen and the adjuvant signals, promoting the formation of a stable immunological synapse and enhancing the efficiency of antigen presentation. This co-delivery mechanism is critical for overcoming the immunosuppressive microenvironment often found in established tumors, where dendritic cells may be tolerized or rendered anergic by the absence of appropriate co-stimulatory signals. The vesicles are further characterized for their ability to activate dendritic cells in vitro, with increased expression of co-stimulatory molecules such as CD86 and MHC class II observed only in cells exposed to TMVs containing GPI-ISMs, but not in those exposed to plain TMVs or free cytokines. This indicates that the GPI-anchored configuration is superior to soluble cytokine administration, as it prevents systemic diffusion and ensures localized, receptor-targeted signaling. The combination of HER2 antigen and GPI-ISMs on the same vesicle also enables the induction of a broad, polyclonal T cell response against multiple HER2 epitopes, rather than a narrow, single-epitope response that may be susceptible to immune escape. The stability of this configuration under physiological conditions, including serum exposure and temperature fluctuations, further supports its suitability for clinical translation. The resulting construct represents a novel class of therapeutic vaccine that integrates the antigenic diversity of whole-tumor membranes with the precision of GPI-anchored cytokine delivery, offering a powerful platform for the treatment of HER2-positive cancers and potentially other malignancies expressing well-defined tumor antigens.

### Uptake of TMVs by DCs is Potentiated by GPI-ISMs

The efficiency with which dendritic cells internalize tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs) is a critical determinant of vaccine efficacy, as internalization is a prerequisite for antigen processing, MHC loading, and subsequent T cell priming. The present invention demonstrates that the incorporation of glycosyl phosphatidylinositol-anchored immunostimulatory molecules (GPI-ISMs) onto the surface of TMVs significantly enhances their uptake by dendritic cells, both in terms of the percentage of cells that internalize the vesicles and the quantity of vesicles internalized per cell. To quantify this effect, TMVs were labeled with pHrodo Deep Red, a fluorogenic dye that remains non-fluorescent at neutral pH but emits strong red fluorescence upon acidification in the phagolysosome, thereby providing a direct and reliable readout of vesicle internalization. Dendritic cells, cultured ex vivo from murine bone marrow, were exposed to pHrodo-labeled TMVs—either plain, GPI-GM-CSF-incorporated, GPI-IL-12-incorporated, or a combination of both—for varying durations ranging from 30 to 180 minutes. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that even at the earliest time point of 30 minutes, dendritic cells exposed to GPI-ISM-containing TMVs exhibited a significantly higher percentage of pHrodo-positive cells compared to those exposed to plain TMVs. By 180 minutes, the proportion of internalizing dendritic cells increased by more than threefold in the presence of GPI-GM-CSF and by over twofold in the presence of GPI-IL-12, with the greatest effect observed when both cytokines were incorporated simultaneously. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the pHrodo signal, which reflects the number of vesicles internalized per cell, was similarly elevated in dendritic cells exposed to GPI-ISM-TMVs, with the highest MFI observed in cells treated with TMVs containing both GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-IL-12. This enhancement in uptake is not attributable to increased vesicle aggregation or non-specific binding, as control experiments using non-GPI-anchored soluble cytokines failed to induce similar increases in internalization. The mechanism underlying this potentiation is believed to involve receptor-mediated endocytosis triggered by the interaction of GPI-GM-CSF with its cognate receptor, GM-CSFR, and GPI-IL-12 with the IL-12 receptor complex, both of which are expressed on the surface of dendritic cells. The engagement of these receptors not only facilitates clathrin-dependent internalization but also promotes actin remodeling and membrane ruffling, increasing the efficiency of phagocytic cup formation. Furthermore, the presence of GPI-ISMs may alter the biophysical properties of the TMV membrane, increasing its fluidity or curvature, thereby facilitating membrane invagination. The enhanced uptake observed with GPI-ISM-TMVs translates directly into improved antigen processing, as evidenced by increased MHC class I and II presentation of tumor-derived peptides on the dendritic cell surface. This phenomenon is particularly significant in the context of tumor immunity, where dendritic cells are often rendered inefficient in antigen capture due to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. By enhancing uptake, GPI-ISMs effectively convert the TMVs from passive antigen carriers into active immunological triggers, ensuring that even low doses of vesicles can elicit a robust dendritic cell response. This property enables the use of lower, more clinically feasible doses of TMVs in therapeutic applications, reducing manufacturing burden and potential off-target effects. The ability to potentiate dendritic cell uptake without the need for exogenous adjuvants or chemical transfection agents represents a major advancement in vaccine design, offering a self-contained, biologically integrated mechanism for enhancing immunogenicity.

### DC Vaccine Therapy Reduces Tumor Growth

The therapeutic efficacy of the dendritic cell (DC) vaccine platform described herein is demonstrated through its ability to significantly inhibit tumor growth in murine models of breast cancer. In a HER2-positive model using the D2F2/E2 cell line, mice were subcutaneously challenged with tumor cells and allowed to establish tumors for seven days prior to vaccination. At this time point, dendritic cells, generated ex vivo from the bone marrow of the same mouse strain, were pulsed with tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs) either devoid of immunostimulatory molecules (plain TMVs) or enriched with GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-IL-12 (TMV vaccine). Following a 24-hour pulsing period, the dendritic cells were washed to remove unbound vesicles and injected subcutaneously into the contralateral flank. Control groups received either phosphate-buffered saline, unpulsed dendritic cells, or dendritic cells pulsed with plain TMVs. Tumor growth was monitored twice weekly using caliper measurements, and survival was tracked over time. Mice in the control groups, including those receiving unpulsed dendritic cells or plain TMV-pulsed dendritic cells, exhibited rapid tumor progression, with all animals reaching humane endpoints by day 35 post-tumor inoculation. In stark contrast, mice treated with dendritic cells pulsed with GPI-ISM-incorporated TMVs demonstrated a marked suppression of tumor growth, with tumor volumes remaining significantly smaller throughout the observation period. By day 57, a substantial proportion of the vaccinated mice remained tumor-free or exhibited only minimal residual disease, whereas all control animals had succumbed to tumor burden. Survival analysis confirmed a statistically significant improvement in overall survival for the DC vaccine group, with a median survival extended by more than 60% compared to controls. This therapeutic effect was not attributable to non-specific immune activation, as dendritic cells pulsed with TMVs lacking GPI-ISMs failed to confer any survival advantage. The inhibition of tumor growth was accompanied by the induction of tumor-specific T cell responses, as evidenced by increased frequencies of HER2-reactive CD8+ T cells in the spleen and tumor-draining lymph nodes of vaccinated mice. The absence of systemic toxicity, including weight loss, cytokine storm, or organ infiltration, further supports the safety profile of this approach. The efficacy of the vaccine is dependent on the co-delivery of antigen and adjuvant on the same vesicle, as separate administration of soluble cytokines and plain TMVs failed to replicate the therapeutic outcome. This underscores the importance of spatial and temporal coordination between antigen presentation and co-stimulatory signaling in dendritic cell activation. The ability of the DC vaccine to induce durable anti-tumor immunity suggests the establishment of immunological memory, as re-challenge of cured mice with the same tumor cell line resulted in rapid rejection, whereas naïve mice developed progressive tumors. These findings establish the DC vaccine platform as a potent and specific therapeutic strategy capable of inducing long-lasting tumor control in a clinically relevant model of HER2-positive breast cancer.

### DC Vaccine Inhibits 4T1 Tumor Growth, Reduces Lung Metastasis, and Improved Immune Profile of Tumors

The therapeutic utility of the dendritic cell (DC) vaccine platform extends beyond tumors expressing defined antigens, as demonstrated by its efficacy in the highly aggressive and antigenically heterogeneous 4T1 triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) model, which lacks a single, immunodominant tumor antigen. In this model, mice were challenged with 4T1 cells and allowed to establish tumors for five days before receiving a single intradermal injection of dendritic cells pulsed with 4T1-derived tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs) incorporating GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-IL-12. Control groups received either phosphate-buffered saline, unpulsed dendritic cells, or dendritic cells pulsed with plain TMVs lacking GPI-ISMs. Tumor growth kinetics revealed that only the group receiving DCs pulsed with GPI-ISM-TMVs exhibited significant inhibition of primary tumor progression, with tumor volumes remaining substantially lower than those in all control groups throughout the study period. This effect was not observed in any other group, confirming that the therapeutic benefit is dependent on the presence of GPI-ISMs on the TMVs. Given the spontaneous metastatic nature of the 4T1 model, the burden of lung metastases was quantified after 20 days of tumor inoculation. Lungs were harvested, digested with collagenase, and treated with 6-thioguanine to selectively eliminate fibroblasts while preserving tumor cells. The number of viable 4T1 cells in the lungs was then enumerated using automated cell counting. Mice treated with the DC vaccine exhibited a two- to threefold reduction in metastatic burden compared to all control groups, demonstrating that the vaccine not only suppresses primary tumor growth but also impedes the dissemination and colonization of distant sites. This reduction in metastasis is likely mediated by the systemic activation of tumor-specific T cells capable of recognizing and eliminating circulating tumor cells and micrometastases. To further characterize the immune response elicited by the vaccine, tumors were resected and processed into single-cell suspensions for flow cytometric analysis of the tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) population. Analysis revealed a significant increase in the proportion of MHC class II-positive, CD11c-positive dendritic cells within the tumor microenvironment of vaccinated mice, indicating enhanced recruitment and/or local maturation of antigen-presenting cells. Concurrently, there was a marked increase in the frequency of tumor-infiltrating CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, with a corresponding elevation in the CD8+/Treg ratio, a key biomarker of anti-tumor immune competence. The presence of activated dendritic cells within the tumor parenchyma suggests that the vaccine induces a self-sustaining immune response capable of recruiting and activating additional immune cells at the site of disease. This remodeling of the tumor immune landscape—from an immunosuppressive, T cell-excluded phenotype to an inflamed, T cell-infiltrated state—is a hallmark of successful immunotherapy and is strongly associated with improved clinical outcomes in human breast cancer. The fact that these effects were observed in the absence of a defined tumor antigen underscores the power of the TMV platform to present a broad array of tumor-associated antigens, enabling the immune system to recognize multiple epitopes and reducing the likelihood of immune escape. The combination of primary tumor inhibition, metastatic suppression, and immune microenvironment remodeling establishes the DC vaccine as a comprehensive therapeutic strategy for aggressive, antigenically diverse cancers such as triple-negative breast cancer.

### Bone Marrow-Derived Dendritic Cell (BMDC) Production

The generation of functional, mature dendritic cells for use in the therapeutic vaccine platform is achieved through a standardized, reproducible protocol for the ex vivo differentiation of bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs). Bone marrow cells are harvested from the femurs and tibias of healthy, age-matched mice under sterile conditions, with surrounding muscle and connective tissue carefully removed. The bones are flushed with RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin using a 22-gauge needle and syringe to dislodge the marrow cells. The resulting cell suspension is passed through a 70-micrometer cell strainer to remove bone fragments and clumps, followed by erythrocyte lysis using ammonium-chloride-potassium (ACK) buffer to eliminate red blood cells. The remaining nucleated cells are resuspended in complete RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 20 ng/mL recombinant murine granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (rmGM-CSF), a cytokine essential for the differentiation of myeloid progenitors into dendritic cells. Cells are seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per milliliter in non-tissue culture-treated petri dishes to prevent adherence and promote suspension growth. The culture medium is replenished with fresh rmGM-CSF on days 3, 6, and 8 to maintain optimal cytokine concentration and support dendritic cell maturation. By day 10, the majority of cells exhibit the characteristic dendritic morphology, with elongated, branched processes and high expression of CD11c, MHC class II, and CD86, as confirmed by flow cytometry. At this stage, the cells are harvested by gentle scraping, washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline, and resuspended in sterile PBS at a concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells per dose for administration. The purity and maturity of the BMDC population are rigorously assessed prior to use, with a minimum threshold of 85% CD11c positivity required for inclusion in therapeutic experiments. The cells are non-adherent, viable, and free of microbial contamination, as confirmed by negative results in mycoplasma testing and endotoxin assays. The use of autologous BMDCs ensures compatibility with the host immune system and avoids alloreactivity, while the ex vivo pulsing protocol allows for precise control over antigen loading and activation state. This method is scalable, cost-effective, and fully compatible with Good Manufacturing Practice standards, enabling the production of clinical-grade dendritic cell products for human application. The resulting BMDCs are highly phagocytic, capable of migrating to lymphoid tissues upon injection, and possess the capacity to present both exogenous and cross-presented antigens, making them ideal vehicles for the delivery of tumor membrane vesicles.

### 4T1 Lung Metastasis Assay

The assessment of metastatic burden in the 4T1 breast cancer model is conducted using a standardized, quantitative lung metastasis assay designed to accurately enumerate viable tumor cells that have disseminated from the primary site to the lungs. Following 20 days of tumor inoculation, mice are euthanized humanely, and the lungs are aseptically excised and placed in sterile culture medium. Each lung is minced into small fragments using sterile scalpels and transferred to a digestion solution containing 1 mg/mL collagenase IV and 100 µg/mL DNase I in RPMI-1640 medium. The tissue is then incubated at 37°C under continuous rotation for a period of two hours to facilitate enzymatic dissociation of the extracellular matrix and release of individual cells. The resulting suspension is filtered through a 70-micrometer cell strainer to remove undigested tissue and debris, and the filtrate is centrifuged at 300 × g for five minutes to pellet the cells. The cell pellet is resuspended in selection medium composed of complete DMEM supplemented with 60 µM 6-thioguanine, a purine analog that selectively inhibits the proliferation of host-derived fibroblasts and stromal cells while sparing tumor cells, which are inherently resistant to its cytotoxic effects due to their elevated expression of hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase. The cells are then plated in 96-well plates and incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂ for 72 hours to allow for the outgrowth of viable tumor cells. Wells are visually inspected for confluency, and once a single well reaches confluence, all wells are harvested, trypsinized, and counted using an automated cell counter equipped with fluorescent viability staining to distinguish live from dead cells. The total number of viable 4T1 cells per lung is calculated and normalized to the number of lungs analyzed per group. This assay provides a sensitive, reproducible, and quantitative measure of metastatic progression, enabling the comparison of therapeutic efficacy across treatment groups. The use of 6-thioguanine selection ensures that the enumerated cells are of tumor origin and not contaminating host cells, thereby eliminating false positives and enhancing the specificity of the readout. The results obtained from this assay directly correlate with survival outcomes and tumor burden, validating its use as a primary endpoint in preclinical studies of metastatic breast cancer. The consistency and reliability of this method make it an indispensable tool for evaluating the ability of the dendritic cell vaccine to suppress systemic tumor dissemination and establish durable anti-metastatic immunity.
