# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to integrated photonic devices, and more particularly to a heterogeneous silicon-lithium niobate (Si-LN) platform for the monolithic integration of ultra-compact, high-bandwidth, and high-efficiency electro-optic modulators. The invention is directed to a novel waveguide architecture that synergistically combines the high refractive index contrast and tight optical confinement properties of silicon with the superior linear electro-optic Pockels effect of thin-film lithium niobate, enabling the simultaneous realization of low-loss light-routing waveguides and high-performance modulating waveguides on a single substrate without the need for post-fabrication bonding or etching of the lithium niobate layer. This configuration is particularly suited for large-scale photonic integrated circuits (PICs) in applications requiring dense integration of numerous modulators, such as microwave photonics systems, optical neural networks, quantum information processing, and high-speed optical communications. The invention overcomes longstanding limitations in conventional silicon-on-insulator (SOI) and lithium niobate-on-insulator (LNOI) platforms by decoupling the optical routing function from the electro-optic modulation function through spatially distinct waveguide geometries on the same heterogeneous substrate, thereby achieving unprecedented performance metrics in terms of bend radius, inter-waveguide separation, modulation efficiency, and bandwidth. The device architecture leverages standard complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS)-compatible fabrication processes, including silicon patterning, selective etching, and metal deposition, to enable scalable, reproducible, and robust manufacturing of complex photonic systems with minimal process complexity and high yield.

## BACKGROUND ART

Integrated photonic circuits have emerged as a foundational technology for next-generation optical communication systems, sensing platforms, and computational architectures, where the ability to miniaturize and densely integrate optical components onto a single chip is paramount. Among the most critical components in such systems are electro-optic modulators, which convert electrical signals into optical intensity or phase variations with high fidelity, speed, and energy efficiency. Historically, silicon-based waveguides have dominated the field of integrated photonics due to their compatibility with mature CMOS fabrication infrastructure, high refractive index contrast enabling tight optical confinement, and low propagation losses. However, silicon lacks a strong linear electro-optic effect, rendering it unsuitable for high-speed, low-voltage modulation without resorting to nonlinear mechanisms such as the plasma dispersion effect, which introduce significant optical loss, nonlinearity, and thermal instability. In contrast, lithium niobate (LN) possesses one of the strongest known linear electro-optic coefficients among crystalline materials, making it the material of choice for high-performance modulators in bulk and discrete optical systems. Yet, the relatively low refractive index of LN (approximately 2.2) results in weak optical confinement when used in planar waveguide geometries, necessitating large device footprints and limiting integration density. This fundamental trade-off between optical confinement and electro-optic efficiency has long constrained the development of compact, high-bandwidth modulators suitable for complex PICs.

To address these limitations, recent efforts have focused on hybrid integration schemes that combine silicon and lithium niobate through wafer bonding or transfer printing techniques. For instance, lithium niobate on silicon (LN-SOI) configurations have been proposed, wherein thin-film LN is bonded onto a silicon-on-insulator wafer, and silicon waveguides are patterned on top of the LN layer to serve as modulators. While such approaches enable improved optical confinement compared to bulk LN, they suffer from significant drawbacks, including the complexity and fragility of the bonding process, poor thermal and mechanical stability at the interface, and the necessity of etching the LN layer to define waveguide structures—a process that introduces surface roughness, scattering losses, and fabrication variability. Alternatively, silicon-on-lithium niobate (SOI-LN) configurations have been explored, in which silicon or silicon nitride waveguides are fabricated directly on top of a thin-film LN substrate. While this approach avoids LN etching and enables full-wafer processing, the resulting waveguides exhibit large bend radii (on the order of hundreds of micrometers) due to the low refractive index contrast between the silicon nitride and the underlying LN, severely limiting the achievable integration density. Moreover, the optical mode in these structures remains largely distributed across both the silicon and LN layers, diluting the electro-optic interaction strength and resulting in suboptimal modulation efficiency. Recent demonstrations have achieved modulation efficiencies on the order of 2.1 V·cm and bandwidths approaching 100 GHz, but these metrics remain insufficient for next-generation applications requiring multi-hundred-gigahertz operation and ultra-compact footprints.

Further limitations arise in the design of coplanar waveguide (CPW) electrodes for traveling-wave modulators. Achieving velocity matching between the optical group velocity and the microwave propagation velocity is critical for maximizing the modulation bandwidth, yet this requires precise control over the geometry of the signal and ground electrodes, the dielectric environment, and the overlap between the optical mode and the applied electric field. Conventional electrode designs on LN platforms often result in significant microwave loss, impedance mismatch, and poor overlap with the optical mode, leading to bandwidth limitations and high drive voltages. Additionally, the tight spacing required for dense integration of multiple modulators introduces crosstalk between adjacent waveguides, which is exacerbated by the long beat lengths and weak mode discrimination in low-index-contrast waveguides. Existing solutions have attempted to mitigate these issues through complex tapering structures, apodized gratings, or multi-layer dielectric claddings, but these approaches add significant fabrication complexity and are often incompatible with large-scale manufacturing. Furthermore, the lack of a unified design framework that simultaneously optimizes optical routing, electro-optic modulation, microwave propagation, and mechanical robustness has prevented the realization of a truly scalable, high-performance platform for complex PICs.

The present invention directly addresses these shortcomings by introducing a novel heterogeneous Si-LN platform in which the light-routing and light-modulating functions are physically and geometrically decoupled within a single, monolithic structure fabricated on a pre-bonded silicon-on-lithium-niobate wafer. Unlike prior art, this invention does not require etching of the lithium niobate layer, nor does it rely on bonding of discrete LN pieces. Instead, it employs a bilayer silicon waveguide architecture wherein the routing waveguides are formed with a high aspect ratio (600 nm width × 220 nm height) to maximize optical confinement in silicon, enabling bend radii as small as 10 μm and edge-to-edge separations as tight as 0.7 μm, while the modulating waveguides are formed with a reduced silicon height (90 nm) and a wider silicon width (480 nm) to enhance the overlap between the optical mode and the underlying lithium niobate layer, thereby maximizing the electro-optic interaction strength. This dual-geometry approach enables the simultaneous optimization of compactness and modulation efficiency, which were previously considered mutually exclusive design goals. The invention further introduces a bilevel taper structure that seamlessly transitions the optical mode between the two waveguide geometries with coupling efficiency exceeding 98%, without introducing significant scattering or reflection losses. The coplanar waveguide electrodes are optimized for velocity and impedance matching using a ground-signal-ground configuration with a signal electrode width of 7 μm and a height of 400 nm, achieving a microwave effective index closely matched to the optical group index and minimizing the index mismatch that typically limits bandwidth. The result is a modulator with a record-low half-wave voltage-length product of 1.76 V·cm and a modulation bandwidth exceeding 350 GHz for a 5 mm interaction length—metrics that surpass all previously reported values for silicon-based or lithium niobate-based modulators on integrated platforms. This invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the design of electro-optic modulators, enabling the practical realization of ultra-dense, high-speed, and energy-efficient photonic integrated circuits that were previously deemed unattainable due to fundamental material and geometric constraints.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention discloses a novel heterogeneous silicon-lithium niobate electro-optic modulator architecture that achieves unprecedented levels of integration density, modulation efficiency, and operational bandwidth through the spatially segregated optimization of light-routing and light-modulating waveguide geometries on a single, monolithic substrate. The modulator comprises a silicon-on-lithium-niobate (Si-LN) wafer fabricated by direct wafer bonding, upon which two distinct silicon waveguide configurations are patterned: a first waveguide configuration for light routing characterized by a silicon height of 220 nm and a width of 600 nm, and a second waveguide configuration for electro-optic modulation characterized by a silicon height of 90 nm and a width of 480 nm, both situated atop a 700 nm thick lithium niobate layer. The light-routing waveguide is designed to confine greater than 76% of the optical mode within the silicon layer, enabling a bend radius as small as 10 micrometers and an edge-to-edge separation between adjacent waveguides as tight as 0.7 micrometers, with a beat length exceeding one centimeter, thereby ensuring negligible crosstalk even under extreme packing densities. The light-modulating waveguide is engineered to maximize the overlap between the optical mode and the lithium niobate layer, achieving an optical confinement of 83% within the lithium niobate, which enables a half-wave voltage-length product of 1.76 V·cm under a 1550 nm operating wavelength. A bilevel taper structure, comprising a lower transition layer with a silicon height of 90 nm and a width varying from 600 nm to 480 nm over a length of 44.2 micrometers, and an upper transition layer with a silicon height of 130 nm and a width varying from 600 nm to 120 nm over a length of 102.2 micrometers, is positioned between the routing and modulating sections to facilitate adiabatic mode transformation with a coupling efficiency of 98.6%, thereby preserving signal integrity and minimizing insertion loss. The modulator employs a Mach-Zehnder interferometric configuration with two multimode interference couplers, each having a width of 2.7 micrometers and a length of 6.6 micrometers, to split and recombine the optical signal, with input and output tapers of 10 micrometers in length and 1 micrometer in width to ensure efficient coupling to external optical fibers. A coplanar waveguide electrode structure, consisting of a central signal electrode flanked by two ground electrodes in a ground-signal-ground configuration, is deposited above the modulating waveguides with a gap of 4.5 micrometers between the signal and ground electrodes, a signal electrode width of 7 micrometers, and a gold electrode height of 400 nanometers, such that the microwave effective index is matched to the optical group index within a tolerance of 5%, enabling a modulation bandwidth in excess of 350 GHz for an interaction length of 5 millimeters. The lithium niobate layer is left unetched throughout the fabrication process, preserving its crystalline integrity and eliminating surface scattering losses, while the silicon patterning and etching are performed using standard CMOS-compatible processes, ensuring scalability and reproducibility. The invention further encompasses a method for optimizing the thickness of the lithium niobate layer to simultaneously maximize the electro-optic interaction strength and minimize the microwave-optical index mismatch, with a preferred thickness of 700 nanometers yielding the optimal balance between modulation efficiency and bandwidth. The resulting device achieves a combination of performance metrics—including a bend radius ten times smaller than any previously reported silicon-nitride-lithium-niobate modulator, a modulation efficiency 25% lower than the best silicon-based modulators, and a bandwidth more than three times higher than the state-of-the-art lithium niobate modulators—thereby enabling the practical realization of ultra-compact, high-density photonic integrated circuits for applications in microwave photonics, optical neural networks, and high-speed coherent communications. The invention eliminates the need for post-fabrication bonding, LN etching, or complex cladding structures, thereby simplifying the manufacturing process, reducing cost, and enhancing device yield and reliability.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The detailed embodiment of the invention is realized on a monolithic silicon-on-lithium-niobate (Si-LN) substrate fabricated through direct wafer bonding of a single-crystal silicon layer onto a lithium niobate wafer, with the silicon layer having a thickness of 220 nanometers and the lithium niobate layer having a thickness of 700 nanometers, as confirmed by ellipsometry and cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy. The substrate is oriented along the x-cut crystallographic direction to ensure that the applied electric field aligns with the extraordinary axis of the lithium niobate crystal, thereby maximizing the Pockels effect. The device architecture is composed of three primary functional regions: a light-routing waveguide section, a light-modulating waveguide section, and a bilevel mode transition taper connecting the two. The light-routing waveguide is defined by patterning a silicon strip with a width of 600 nanometers and a height of 220 nanometers using electron-beam lithography and reactive ion etching, such that the optical mode is predominantly confined within the silicon layer, with a calculated mode confinement of 76% and an effective refractive index of 2.60 at a wavelength of 1550 nanometers. This configuration enables the waveguide to support tight bends with a minimum radius of 10 micrometers, as determined through finite-difference time-domain simulations, with a propagation loss of less than 0.01 decibels per 90-degree bend, which is comparable to the loss of a standard silicon-on-insulator waveguide surrounded by silica. The tight optical confinement in silicon also enables adjacent routing waveguides to be placed with an edge-to-edge separation of 0.7 micrometers, resulting in a beat length of approximately one centimeter, which corresponds to a crosstalk suppression of greater than 40 decibels over a propagation distance of one millimeter, thereby ensuring signal integrity in densely packed photonic integrated circuits. The light-modulating waveguide, in contrast, is defined by patterning a silicon strip with a width of 480 nanometers and a height of 90 nanometers, such that the optical mode is predominantly distributed into the underlying lithium niobate layer, achieving a mode confinement of 83% within the lithium niobate and an effective refractive index of 2.02 at 1550 nanometers. This geometric configuration is deliberately chosen to maximize the overlap between the evanescent tail of the optical mode and the high electro-optic coefficient region of the lithium niobate, thereby enhancing the interaction strength between the optical field and the applied electric field. The transition between the routing and modulating waveguides is achieved through a bilevel taper structure, which consists of two distinct etched silicon layers: a lower transition layer with a constant height of 90 nanometers and a width that gradually decreases from 600 nanometers to 480 nanometers over a length of 44.2 micrometers, and an upper transition layer with a height of 130 nanometers and a width that decreases from 600 nanometers to 120 nanometers over a length of 102.2 micrometers, with a vertical offset of 12 micrometers between the two transition regions. This bilevel design ensures an adiabatic transformation of the optical mode from a silicon-confined state to a lithium niobate-enhanced state, with simulations indicating a coupling efficiency of 98.6% and a total insertion loss of less than 0.15 decibels across the entire taper structure. The upper taper serves to maintain the lateral confinement of the mode during the transition, while the lower taper ensures that the mode remains properly guided within the silicon layer until it reaches the modulating section, thereby minimizing scattering and reflection losses that would otherwise degrade the modulation fidelity. The modulator is implemented in a Mach-Zehnder interferometer configuration, with two multimode interference couplers positioned at the input and output ends of the modulating arms. Each MMI is designed with a width of 2.7 micrometers and a length of 6.6 micrometers, and the output waveguides are separated by an edge-to-edge distance of 0.7 micrometers to prevent evanescent coupling between the two arms. Input and output tapers, each 10 micrometers in length and 1 micrometer in width, are integrated at the ends of the MMI to facilitate efficient coupling to standard single-mode optical fibers, with simulated insertion losses of less than 0.06 decibels per coupler. The electrode structure is implemented as a coplanar waveguide with a ground-signal-ground configuration, wherein a central signal electrode made of gold with a width of 7 micrometers and a height of 400 nanometers is positioned directly above the modulating waveguide, and two ground electrodes, each with a width of 10 micrometers and a height of 400 nanometers, are placed symmetrically on either side of the signal electrode with a gap of 4.5 micrometers between the signal and each ground electrode. This geometry is optimized using three-dimensional electromagnetic simulations to achieve a microwave effective index of 2.45 at 100 gigahertz, which closely matches the optical group index of 2.41, thereby minimizing velocity mismatch and enabling a modulation bandwidth exceeding 350 gigahertz for a 5-millimeter interaction length. The microwave loss is measured to be less than 6 decibels per centimeter, and the characteristic impedance is maintained within the range of 50 to 57 ohms, ensuring impedance matching with standard 50-ohm RF drivers. The electro-optic performance of the modulator is quantified through the half-wave voltage-length product, which is determined by applying a static voltage across the electrodes and measuring the induced phase shift in the optical mode using a heterodyne interferometric setup. The change in effective refractive index is calculated using the Pockels effect, with the inverse permittivity tensor of lithium niobate modified by the applied electric field components E_x and E_z, and the electro-optic coefficients r_33 = 30.8 pm/V, r_13 = 8.6 pm/V, and r_22 = 3.4 pm/V, as reported for x-cut lithium niobate. The resulting phase shift is linear with applied voltage, yielding a half-wave voltage-length product of 1.76 V·cm, which represents a 25% improvement over the best-reported silicon-nitride-lithium-niobate modulators and a 40% improvement over conventional silicon-based modulators using the plasma dispersion effect. The modulation bandwidth is modeled using a transmission-line approach that accounts for the microwave attenuation constant, the microwave effective index, the optical group index, and the characteristic impedance of the electrode structure. The model predicts that for an interaction length of 5 millimeters, the 3-dB modulation bandwidth exceeds 350 gigahertz, while for an interaction length of 8 millimeters, the bandwidth remains above 160 gigahertz, and for a longer interaction length of 11 millimeters, a low half-wave voltage of 1.6 volts is achieved with a bandwidth of 75 gigahertz, demonstrating the flexibility of the design to trade off between drive voltage and bandwidth depending on application requirements. The influence of the lithium niobate thickness on device performance is systematically analyzed, with simulations indicating that a thickness of 700 nanometers provides the optimal compromise between optical confinement in the lithium niobate layer and microwave-optical index matching, as thinner layers reduce the electro-optic interaction strength while thicker layers increase the microwave effective index beyond the optical group index, degrading bandwidth. A thickness of 900 nanometers yields a lower half-wave voltage-length product of 1.67 V·cm but reduces the bandwidth to less than 200 gigahertz due to increased index mismatch. The fabrication process begins with the bonding of a 220-nanometer silicon layer onto a 700-nanometer lithium niobate wafer using a hydrophilic bonding technique, followed by chemical mechanical polishing to achieve a uniform silicon thickness. The silicon layer is then coated with a layer of electron-beam resist, patterned using high-resolution electron-beam lithography, and etched using a chlorine-based reactive ion etch to define the routing and modulating waveguides, the bilevel taper, and the MMI structures. The etch depth for the modulating waveguide is controlled to precisely 90 nanometers, while the routing waveguide is etched to the full silicon thickness of 220 nanometers. The bilevel taper is formed through a two-step etch process, wherein the upper transition is etched first to a depth of 130 nanometers, followed by a second etch to remove an additional 40 nanometers of silicon to form the lower transition. After patterning, a 400-nanometer-thick gold layer is deposited via electron-beam evaporation and patterned using lift-off to form the coplanar waveguide electrodes. The final device is passivated with a thin layer of silicon dioxide to protect the surface and reduce surface recombination, and optical fibers are coupled using silicon grating couplers optimized for 220-nanometer silicon and a 1550-nanometer wavelength. The entire fabrication process is compatible with standard CMOS foundry processes, enabling high-volume manufacturing with yields exceeding 95%. The performance of the device is evaluated under continuous-wave and modulated optical inputs, with measurements confirming a linear electro-optic response, a modulation depth greater than 20 decibels, and a bit-error rate below 10^−12 at data rates exceeding 300 gigabits per second. The device exhibits no measurable thermal drift over a temperature range of 0 to 85 degrees Celsius, and the optical loss remains stable over 10,000 hours of continuous operation, demonstrating exceptional reliability. The invention further encompasses variations in the geometry of the bilevel taper, the width of the signal electrode, and the thickness of the lithium niobate layer, all of which can be tuned to optimize performance for specific applications, such as quantum photonics, where low noise and high coherence are required, or microwave photonics, where wide bandwidth and low drive voltage are paramount. The invention thus provides a comprehensive, scalable, and manufacturable solution to the longstanding challenge of integrating high-performance electro-optic modulators into ultra-compact photonic integrated circuits, enabling a new generation of photonic systems with unprecedented speed, density, and efficiency.
