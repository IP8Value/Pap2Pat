# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging has long been challenged by the need to capture high-fidelity, real-time cine sequences during periods of physiological stress, particularly in the immediate aftermath of exercise. Myocardial wall motion abnormalities induced by stress—such as ischemia or regional contractile dysfunction—are transient phenomena that typically resolve within seconds to minutes following cessation of physical exertion. This transient nature necessitates imaging protocols that can acquire sufficient temporal and spatial resolution without requiring breath-holding or ECG triggering, both of which are impractical or impossible in the post-exercise state due to tachypnea, tachycardia, and patient fatigue. As a result, real-time cine imaging has emerged as the only viable clinical approach for capturing stress-induced cardiac dysfunction, yet it comes at a substantial cost to image quality. The inherent trade-offs in real-time acquisition—namely, the reduction in k-space sampling density to achieve high temporal frame rates—lead to significant degradation in signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), increased susceptibility to motion artifacts, and diminished spatial resolution. These limitations are further exacerbated during post-exercise imaging, where irregular breathing patterns, elevated heart rates, and involuntary body movements introduce additional sources of phase inconsistency and ghosting in the acquired data.

Traditional methods for accelerating magnetic resonance imaging, such as parallel imaging techniques like SENSE and GRAPPA, have been widely adopted in clinical practice to mitigate the effects of undersampling. These methods rely on spatially varying coil sensitivity profiles to reconstruct full-field-of-view images from under-sampled k-space data. However, in the context of real-time exercise stress imaging, these conventional approaches suffer from critical shortcomings. First, the assumption of static coil sensitivity profiles over time is fundamentally invalid during dynamic physiological states, where respiratory motion, cardiac displacement, and changes in torso geometry cause the relative positioning of the receiver coil elements to vary significantly from frame to frame. Second, the use of auto-calibration signal (ACS) lines, typically derived from averaged low-frequency k-space data, becomes unreliable under conditions of rapid motion and irregular breathing, leading to inaccurate kernel estimation and the propagation of reconstruction errors. Third, the absence of temporal consistency constraints in standard parallel imaging reconstructions permits the accumulation of noise and artifacts across consecutive frames, particularly in regions of high motion such as the lateral and inferior walls of the left ventricle. These limitations collectively result in images that are often too noisy or artifact-laden to permit confident clinical interpretation, especially when subtle wall motion abnormalities are the diagnostic target.

Attempts to improve real-time cine reconstruction have included the use of temporal filtering, compressed sensing, and model-based reconstructions that incorporate prior knowledge of cardiac motion. While these approaches have demonstrated improvements in certain contexts, they often require extensive computational resources, lengthy reconstruction times, or assumptions about cardiac motion that are not universally valid across patient populations. Moreover, many of these methods are not designed to handle the unique combination of high temporal variability, low SNR, and non-periodic motion that characterizes post-exercise cardiac imaging. The SPIRIT reconstruction method, which employs a kernel-based approach to model the relationship between under-sampled k-space data and full k-space through spatially localized calibration kernels, represents a significant advancement over traditional parallel imaging techniques by offering improved robustness to motion and greater flexibility in handling variable acceleration factors. However, even SPIRIT assumes that coil sensitivity profiles remain constant across the entire acquisition window, an assumption that is demonstrably violated during real-time exercise stress imaging. Furthermore, SPIRIT does not incorporate explicit spatial regularization to suppress noise and stabilize the inversion process under extreme undersampling conditions.

The need for a reconstruction framework capable of simultaneously addressing temporal variability in coil sensitivity, noise amplification due to undersampling, and motion-induced artifacts has remained unmet in the clinical domain. Existing methods either sacrifice temporal fidelity to improve spatial resolution, or preserve temporal resolution at the expense of diagnostic image quality. There exists a critical gap in the art for a reconstruction technique that can dynamically adapt to time-varying coil sensitivities while enforcing spatial coherence and noise suppression in a computationally feasible manner. Such a method would enable the acquisition of high-quality, clinically interpretable cine images during the narrow window of post-exercise cardiac assessment, thereby transforming the diagnostic utility of stress cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging. The present invention fulfills this unmet need by introducing a novel extension of the SPIRIT reconstruction framework that incorporates temporal coil sensitivity estimation and spatial regularization, thereby enabling a substantial and statistically significant improvement in image signal-to-noise ratio without increasing ghosting artifacts or compromising temporal fidelity.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a novel method for reconstructing real-time cardiac cine images during exercise stress conditions, wherein image quality is substantially enhanced through the integration of temporal coil sensitivity estimation and spatial regularization within a kernel-based reconstruction framework. The method operates on under-sampled k-space data acquired during free-breathing, real-time cine imaging following physical exertion, and is specifically designed to overcome the limitations of conventional parallel imaging techniques that assume static coil sensitivity profiles and lack explicit noise suppression mechanisms. The invention introduces a temporal sensitivity estimation module that dynamically computes time-varying coil sensitivity maps for each frame of the cine sequence by leveraging temporal correlations in the k-space data, thereby accounting for respiratory and cardiac motion-induced changes in coil positioning. These time-varying sensitivity maps are then incorporated into a modified SPIRIT reconstruction kernel estimation process, enabling more accurate modeling of the relationship between under-sampled and fully sampled k-space data on a frame-by-frame basis. To further enhance image fidelity, a spatial regularization term is introduced into the reconstruction optimization problem, which enforces local smoothness and structural coherence in the reconstructed image domain while preserving fine anatomical details and edge integrity. The combined optimization problem is solved using a non-linear conjugate gradient algorithm that iteratively minimizes the difference between the estimated and measured k-space data while penalizing spatially inconsistent solutions, resulting in images with significantly improved signal-to-noise ratio and reduced artifact burden. The method is computationally efficient, compatible with standard clinical MRI hardware and acquisition protocols, and requires no modification to the imaging sequence itself, making it readily translatable to existing clinical workflows. When applied to real-time exercise stress cine imaging, the invention achieves an average signal-to-noise ratio improvement of greater than 38 percent relative to conventional reconstruction techniques, without any measurable increase in ghosting artifacts or temporal blurring. The reconstructed images exhibit superior delineation of myocardial walls, more accurate assessment of regional motion, and enhanced diagnostic confidence in detecting stress-induced abnormalities, thereby enabling more reliable and timely clinical decision-making in the critical post-exercise imaging window.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE DRAWINGS AND PRESENTLY PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The present invention relates to a method and system for reconstructing real-time cardiac cine images acquired during exercise stress conditions, wherein image quality is substantially enhanced through the synergistic integration of temporal coil sensitivity estimation and spatial regularization within a kernel-based reconstruction framework. The method is designed to operate on under-sampled k-space data collected using a multi-channel radiofrequency coil during free-breathing, real-time cine acquisition following physical exertion, and is particularly suited for applications in cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging where rapid heart rate, irregular respiration, and dynamic torso motion render conventional reconstruction techniques inadequate. The invention does not require modifications to the imaging pulse sequence, nor does it necessitate additional hardware or specialized acquisition protocols; instead, it operates as a post-processing reconstruction engine that transforms raw, under-sampled k-space data into high-fidelity, clinically interpretable cine images. The preferred embodiment of the invention comprises a sequence of computational steps that are executed in a structured, iterative manner to produce a final reconstructed image series with optimized signal-to-noise ratio, spatial resolution, and temporal consistency.

The process begins with the acquisition of under-sampled k-space data using a multi-channel receiver coil array, wherein each frame of the cine sequence is sampled at an acceleration factor of four or greater using a time-interleaved sampling scheme. The acquisition parameters are selected to ensure sufficient temporal resolution for capturing cardiac motion during peak stress, typically employing a balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) sequence with a repetition time (TR) of approximately 1.09 milliseconds, an echo time (TE) of 0.9 milliseconds, a flip angle of 58 degrees, and a bandwidth of 1420 Hz. The image matrix is configured to provide a spatial resolution of approximately 2.44 mm in both the frequency- and phase-encoding directions, with a field of view sufficient to encompass the entire left ventricle across multiple short-axis and long-axis slices. The under-sampled k-space data for each frame is stored in a four-dimensional dataset comprising spatial dimensions (kx, ky), temporal dimension (frame index), and coil dimension (channel index). This raw dataset forms the input to the reconstruction pipeline and is processed without prior averaging or filtering to preserve the full temporal dynamics of the cardiac cycle.

The first computational step in the reconstruction process involves the extraction of auto-calibration signal (ACS) lines from the under-sampled k-space data. These ACS lines, which correspond to the central, low-frequency region of k-space, are used to estimate the spatial relationships between the coil channels and to calibrate the reconstruction kernels. In conventional parallel imaging methods, these ACS lines are averaged across multiple frames to produce a single, static set of calibration coefficients. However, in the context of real-time exercise stress imaging, such averaging introduces temporal smearing and inaccuracies due to the dynamic nature of cardiac and respiratory motion. To address this limitation, the present invention employs a novel approach in which the ACS lines are extracted for each individual frame and then subjected to a Karhunen-Loève transform (KLT) filtering procedure. The KLT, also known as the proper orthogonal decomposition, is applied to the temporal sequence of ACS lines to identify the dominant modes of variation in the coil sensitivity profiles across time. This transformation decomposes the temporal ACS data into a set of orthogonal basis functions, each representing a distinct pattern of sensitivity variation, and associated temporal coefficients that describe the amplitude of each mode at each frame. By retaining only the most significant modes—those that capture the majority of the variance in the sensitivity profiles—the method effectively denoises the ACS data while preserving the essential temporal dynamics. The filtered ACS lines are then used to compute a time-varying set of calibration kernels for each frame, thereby enabling a frame-specific estimation of the coil sensitivity profiles that accurately reflect the instantaneous position and orientation of the body relative to the coil array.

The second step of the reconstruction process involves the estimation of temporal coil sensitivity maps for each frame. Building upon the filtered ACS lines, the method computes a spatially resolved sensitivity profile for each coil channel at each time point by solving a constrained inverse problem that relates the measured k-space data to the underlying image domain. This is accomplished by formulating a linear system in which the unknowns are the sensitivity maps, and the measurements are the under-sampled k-space data, with the calibration kernels serving as the system matrix. To ensure numerical stability and physical plausibility, the sensitivity estimation is regularized using a spatial smoothness constraint that penalizes abrupt changes in sensitivity across neighboring pixels, while preserving the overall structure of the coil response. The resulting sensitivity maps are not assumed to be static but are instead allowed to evolve dynamically across the cine sequence, reflecting the true physiological motion of the torso and heart. These time-varying sensitivity maps are then incorporated into the SPIRIT reconstruction framework, replacing the conventional static sensitivity assumptions. In the modified SPIRIT formulation, the kernel estimation for each frame is performed using the corresponding time-varying sensitivity map, ensuring that the kernel accurately models the spatial relationships between the coil channels under the current physiological state. This temporal adaptation of the sensitivity profiles enables the reconstruction to compensate for motion-induced phase inconsistencies and coil misalignment, which are the primary sources of ghosting and blurring in conventional real-time cine imaging.

The third step of the reconstruction process involves the construction of the kernel-based reconstruction model. For each frame, a set of calibration kernels is estimated by solving a linear system that relates the under-sampled k-space data to the fully sampled k-space data, using the time-varying sensitivity maps as a prior. Each kernel is a small, localized operator that maps a neighborhood of under-sampled k-space data to a corresponding neighborhood of fully sampled k-space data. The size of the kernel neighborhood is selected to balance spatial resolution with computational efficiency, typically spanning a region of 7×7 k-space points in the frequency domain. The kernel estimation is performed independently for each frame, using the corresponding time-varying sensitivity map, and is constrained to ensure that the kernels are spatially smooth and physically consistent with the underlying coil geometry. The resulting kernels are then used to predict the missing k-space data for each frame by convolving the under-sampled k-space with the estimated kernels. However, this prediction alone is insufficient to produce high-quality images, as the under-sampling factor is high and the noise amplification inherent in the inversion process remains substantial. To address this, the invention introduces a spatial regularization term into the reconstruction optimization problem, which enforces structural coherence and noise suppression in the image domain.

The fourth step of the reconstruction process involves the application of spatial regularization to the image reconstruction. This is accomplished by formulating the reconstruction as a non-linear optimization problem in which the objective function comprises two primary components: a data fidelity term and a regularization term. The data fidelity term measures the discrepancy between the reconstructed k-space data and the measured under-sampled k-space data, weighted by the estimated kernels and time-varying sensitivity maps. The regularization term penalizes solutions that exhibit high spatial gradients or lack structural coherence, thereby suppressing noise and stabilizing the inversion process. The regularization is implemented using a total variation (TV) penalty, which promotes piecewise-smooth solutions while preserving sharp edges and fine anatomical details. The TV penalty is computed as the L1 norm of the spatial gradient of the reconstructed image, encouraging solutions that are locally smooth except at boundaries between tissues of differing signal intensity. This choice of regularization is particularly well-suited to cardiac imaging, where the myocardium, blood pool, and epicardial fat exhibit distinct intensity profiles and sharp boundaries. The optimization problem is solved iteratively using a non-linear conjugate gradient algorithm, which alternates between updating the image estimate and refining the kernel and sensitivity estimates to minimize the overall objective function. The algorithm is initialized with a preliminary reconstruction obtained using conventional GRAPPA, and successive iterations progressively refine the image by reducing noise, enhancing contrast, and suppressing artifacts. The regularization strength is adaptively adjusted during the optimization process based on the local noise level and the spatial complexity of the image, ensuring that the solution remains faithful to the underlying anatomy while avoiding over-smoothing.

The fifth step of the reconstruction process involves the convergence and final image generation. The iterative optimization continues until the change in the objective function falls below a predefined threshold, indicating that the solution has reached a stable minimum. At this point, the final reconstructed image series is generated by applying an inverse Fourier transform to the fully estimated k-space data for each frame. The resulting cine sequence exhibits significantly improved signal-to-noise ratio, reduced ghosting artifacts, and enhanced delineation of myocardial walls compared to images reconstructed using conventional techniques. The temporal consistency of the reconstructed images is maintained through the dynamic adaptation of the sensitivity profiles and the spatial regularization, which ensures that anatomical structures remain coherent across frames despite the presence of motion. The reconstructed images are then exported in standard DICOM format for clinical review and analysis.

The method of the present invention is implemented as a software module that operates on a standard clinical MRI workstation equipped with sufficient computational resources to handle the iterative reconstruction process. The software is designed to interface with the scanner’s data acquisition system and automatically processes the raw k-space data following image acquisition. The reconstruction pipeline is optimized for parallel computation using multi-threaded processing and graphics processing unit (GPU) acceleration to ensure that the reconstruction time remains clinically acceptable, typically completing within 10 to 15 minutes for a full cine sequence comprising 30 to 50 frames per slice and three cardiac views. The method is compatible with any multi-channel coil array and can be readily adapted to different acceleration factors, field strengths, and pulse sequences without requiring retraining or recalibration. The computational efficiency of the method is achieved through the use of sparse kernel representations, pre-computed Fourier operators, and efficient iterative solvers that minimize memory usage and computational overhead.

The performance of the invention has been validated in a cohort of ten healthy volunteers who underwent free-breathing, real-time exercise stress cine imaging following treadmill exertion. The imaging protocol employed a 1.5 Tesla scanner with a 32-channel receiver coil, and three cardiac views (one short-axis and two long-axis) were acquired for each subject. The reconstructed images were compared against those obtained using conventional time-interleaved GRAPPA (TGRAPPA) reconstruction of the same raw data. Quantitative analysis of the signal-to-noise ratio revealed a mean relative improvement of 38.2 percent in the myocardial wall, with a standard deviation of 17.8 percent, and a statistical significance of p < 1×10⁻⁵. Importantly, the quantification of ghosting artifacts, measured using the peak spatial cross-correlation ratio along the phase-encoding direction, showed no statistically significant difference between the proposed method and conventional TGRAPPA (p = 0.960), indicating that the substantial SNR gain was achieved without introducing additional motion artifacts. Visual assessment by three independent radiologists confirmed the superior image quality of the reconstructed images, with marked improvements in myocardial edge definition, contrast between blood pool and myocardium, and overall diagnostic confidence. The method was found to be robust across all subjects, regardless of body habitus, heart rate, or respiratory pattern, demonstrating its generalizability and clinical utility.

The invention offers several advantages over existing reconstruction techniques. First, by incorporating temporal sensitivity estimation, it explicitly accounts for the dynamic changes in coil positioning that occur during exercise-induced motion, thereby eliminating a major source of reconstruction error that plagues conventional parallel imaging methods. Second, the introduction of spatial regularization provides a powerful mechanism for noise suppression that does not rely on temporal averaging or assumptions of periodic motion, making it uniquely suited for non-periodic, real-time imaging scenarios. Third, the method requires no additional acquisition time, does not increase the specific absorption rate (SAR), and imposes no constraints on the patient’s breathing or heart rate, making it fully compatible with standard clinical workflows. Fourth, the computational framework is modular and extensible, allowing for the incorporation of additional constraints such as cardiac motion models or anatomical priors in future iterations. Finally, the method is scalable and can be applied to other clinical applications involving real-time imaging under motion, such as fetal MRI, lung imaging, or interventional MRI, where similar challenges of motion-induced sensitivity variation and noise amplification exist.

In summary, the present invention provides a novel and highly effective method for reconstructing real-time cardiac cine images during exercise stress conditions by integrating temporal coil sensitivity estimation and spatial regularization into a kernel-based reconstruction framework. The resulting images exhibit a statistically significant and clinically meaningful improvement in signal-to-noise ratio without increasing ghosting artifacts, thereby enabling more accurate and reliable detection of stress-induced myocardial dysfunction. The method is robust, computationally efficient, and fully compatible with existing clinical MRI systems, making it a transformative advancement in the field of cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging. Its implementation promises to expand the diagnostic utility of stress MRI, improve patient outcomes through earlier detection of ischemic heart disease, and reduce the need for repeat imaging or invasive follow-up procedures.
