# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of symmetric key cryptography has long been dominated by the pursuit of efficient, secure, and practical methods for ensuring both confidentiality and authenticity in digital communications. Historically, cryptographic systems have treated encryption and authentication as separate concerns, often requiring two distinct operations to be performed on each data block: one to encrypt the plaintext and another to generate a message authentication code. This dual-pass approach, while conceptually straightforward, introduces significant performance overhead, particularly in high-throughput environments such as secure network communications, embedded systems, and real-time data transmission platforms. The inefficiency of such methods has motivated the development of integrated authenticated encryption schemes that combine both functions into a single, streamlined process. However, many of these integrated solutions have proven vulnerable to cryptanalytic attacks, either due to structural weaknesses in their design or because they rely on assumptions that do not hold under adversarial scrutiny.

Among the most widely studied authenticated encryption modes are CCM, GCM, and EAX, each of which has been adopted in various industry standards. Yet, despite their prevalence, these modes suffer from well-documented limitations. CCM, for instance, requires two passes over the data—one for encryption and another for authentication—making it inherently slower than single-pass alternatives. Moreover, its reliance on counter mode encryption combined with CBC-MAC introduces complexity in implementation and increases the risk of misuse, particularly when initialization vectors are not managed with strict uniqueness guarantees. GCM, while faster and widely deployed, has been shown to be susceptible to forgery attacks when nonces are reused, a failure mode that has led to catastrophic security breaches in real-world systems. EAX, though provably secure under ideal conditions, demands careful key separation and is not inherently resistant to side-channel attacks in hardware implementations.

Beyond these standardized modes, numerous academic proposals have emerged, each attempting to balance performance, security, and simplicity. Phelix, for example, was initially hailed for its elegant design and high throughput, but subsequent analysis revealed structural vulnerabilities that allowed for efficient distinguishing attacks and key recovery under certain conditions. Other constructions, particularly those based on generic composition—such as encrypt-then-MAC or MAC-then-encrypt—require two independent keys, one for encryption and another for authentication. This dual-key requirement introduces substantial operational burden, as key management becomes exponentially more complex in large-scale deployments, especially in environments where devices are resource-constrained or deployed in hostile or untrusted settings. The need for key coordination, distribution, rotation, and storage multiplies the attack surface and increases the likelihood of human or system error.

Furthermore, the cryptographic community has increasingly recognized the importance of algorithmic customization as a defense-in-depth strategy. While public algorithms are subject to exhaustive scrutiny by adversaries, a system that can be uniquely instantiated per user, per device, or per application introduces a layer of obscurity that is not reliant on secrecy of the algorithm itself, but rather on the uniqueness of its parameters. This approach does not violate Kerckhoffs’ principle, as the underlying structure remains publicly known, but it renders large-scale cryptanalytic efforts economically infeasible, since each instantiation must be attacked individually. This is particularly valuable in government, military, and critical infrastructure contexts, where the exposure of a single algorithmic flaw could compromise entire networks. Yet, existing authenticated encryption schemes offer little to no support for such customization without requiring a full re-analysis of security properties for each variant—a prohibitive cost for most organizations.

The design of the underlying permutation function has also remained a critical bottleneck. Most modern symmetric ciphers, including AES, rely on 8-bit substitution boxes (S-boxes) due to their balance between nonlinearity, implementation efficiency, and resistance to differential and linear cryptanalysis. However, the limited size of these S-boxes constrains the degree of algebraic complexity that can be embedded within a single round. As a result, designers are often forced to compensate by increasing the number of rounds, which directly impacts throughput and latency. In hardware implementations, this translates to longer critical paths, higher power consumption, and increased silicon area. While larger S-boxes—such as 16-bit variants—have been explored in theoretical literature, they have been largely dismissed as impractical due to the perceived cost of implementing large lookup tables. Yet, recent advances in finite field arithmetic and circuit optimization have demonstrated that such S-boxes can be implemented without lookup tables, using algebraic expressions derived from multiplicative inversion in finite fields followed by affine transformations. These implementations require only a modest number of logic gates, making them not only feasible but advantageous in terms of both performance and security.

The sponge and duplex constructions have emerged as powerful frameworks for building cryptographic primitives that are both flexible and provably secure under generic assumptions. The sponge construction, popularized by the Keccak algorithm that won the NIST SHA-3 competition, provides a unified structure for hashing, streaming, and authenticated encryption by separating the internal state into an inner and outer portion. The inner state, which is never directly exposed, determines the security level, while the outer state governs the rate at which data is absorbed and squeezed. The duplex construction extends this model by allowing simultaneous absorption and squeezing, eliminating the need to reinitialize the state between operations and enabling a single-pass authenticated encryption scheme. This makes it ideal for stream-like encryption with integrated authentication, where data is processed in real time without buffering.

Despite the theoretical elegance of the duplex construction, its practical deployment has been hindered by the lack of a sufficiently robust and customizable underlying permutation. Existing duplex-based systems often rely on permutations derived from block ciphers or hash functions that were not designed with duplexing in mind, leading to suboptimal diffusion properties, insufficient nonlinearity, or susceptibility to structural attacks. There is therefore a clear and unmet need for a permutation function that is specifically engineered for the duplex construction, capable of delivering high security with minimal rounds, efficient hardware implementation, and extensive configurability—all while maintaining compatibility with the generic security guarantees of the underlying sponge model.

This invention addresses these shortcomings by introducing a novel authenticated encryption algorithm built upon the duplex construction, featuring a custom-designed permutation function that incorporates 16×16 bijective S-boxes, a bitwise permutation with provably optimal diffusion properties, a linear mixing layer with maximal branch number, and a round constant generation mechanism that prevents slide attacks. The algorithm is designed to operate with a fixed rate of 128 bits and a capacity of 384 bits, providing a security level of 2¹²⁸ for a 128-bit key and 2²⁵⁶ for a 256-bit key, meeting or exceeding the security guarantees of NIST-standardized algorithms. Crucially, the permutation is not fixed; it is highly customizable across multiple parameters—including S-boxes, bitwise permutations, mixing matrices, and round constants—without compromising the overall security framework. Each customization option is carefully constrained to preserve the cryptographic properties necessary for resistance against differential, linear, and algebraic attacks, ensuring that any instantiation remains secure as long as the provided guidelines are followed. This design enables organizations to deploy unique, proprietary authenticated encryption variants without the need for costly and time-consuming cryptanalysis of each individual configuration. The algorithm is optimized for hardware implementation, leveraging finite field arithmetic to avoid lookup tables, minimizing gate count, and enabling pipelined, high-throughput operation. It is particularly suited for use in secure embedded systems, military communication devices, IoT platforms requiring low-latency encryption, and any environment where both performance and resistance to large-scale cryptanalysis are paramount.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention disclosed herein is a customizable authenticated encryption algorithm that integrates encryption and authentication into a single, unified process using the duplex construction, with an underlying permutation function that achieves exceptional levels of nonlinearity, diffusion, and algebraic complexity through the use of 16-bit bijective substitution boxes, a bitwise permutation with maximal diffusion properties, a linear mixing layer with optimal branch number, and dynamically generated round constants. The algorithm operates on a 512-bit internal state, partitioned into a 128-bit rate and a 384-bit capacity, enabling it to absorb and squeeze data in 128-bit blocks while maintaining a security level of 2¹²⁸ for a 128-bit key and 2²⁵⁶ for a 256-bit key, thereby satisfying the stringent requirements of high-assurance cryptographic applications. Unlike prior authenticated encryption schemes that require multiple passes over the data or rely on dual-key management, this invention performs both encryption and authentication in a single, continuous pass, eliminating redundant operations and significantly improving throughput while reducing implementation complexity.

The core innovation lies in the design of the permutation function, which consists of multiple rounds, each comprising four distinct operations: a substitution step using 32 identical 16×16 S-boxes, a bitwise permutation that ensures long-range diffusion across the entire state, a mixing layer based on matrix multiplication in a finite field that provides maximal linear and differential branch numbers, and the addition of a unique round constant derived from a cryptographic hash function. The 16×16 S-boxes are constructed using multiplicative inversion in the finite field GF(2¹⁶) modulo the irreducible polynomial x¹⁶ + x⁵ + x³ + x + 1, followed by an affine transformation, resulting in a highly nonlinear, algebraically complex, and hardware-efficient substitution layer that does not require lookup tables. This design reduces the number of logic gates required for implementation to approximately 1,238 XOR gates and 144 AND gates per S-box, making it significantly more efficient than alternative 16-bit S-boxes that rely on memory-based implementations. The substitution step is the sole source of nonlinearity in the permutation, ensuring that all security properties are anchored in this single, well-characterized component.

The bitwise permutation applied after the substitution step is a derangement with no fixed points and an order of 32, ensuring that every bit in the state is permuted in a manner that maximizes the spread of differences across multiple S-boxes in successive rounds. This permutation is defined by an affine function over the bit indices and satisfies five critical properties: all outputs of a given S-box are routed to 16 distinct mixers, the permutation has no fixed points, its order exceeds the number of rounds, all bits share the same order, and the mapping is efficiently implementable as a wire rerouting in hardware. These properties collectively ensure that even a single-bit difference in the input will propagate to a large number of active S-boxes within a few rounds, dramatically increasing the difficulty of constructing differential or linear trails.

The mixing layer follows the bitwise permutation and operates on pairs of 16-bit words using a 2×2 invertible matrix over GF(2¹⁶) modulo the irreducible polynomial x¹⁶ + x⁵ + x³ + x² + 1. This matrix is chosen such that both its linear and differential branch numbers are maximized at three, meaning that any non-zero input difference must affect at least three S-boxes in the next round. This property is formally verified using Boolean satisfiability solvers, which confirm that no input difference of weight one can produce an output difference of weight one, thereby preventing the existence of low-weight differential or linear characteristics. The matrix multiplication is implemented using a combination of field multiplications by x, which require only a single wire rotation and three XOR gates, and additions over the finite field, enabling a compact, high-speed hardware realization.

Round constants are added after each mixing step to disrupt any potential symmetry in the state and to prevent slide attacks. These constants are generated by applying the SHA-3-512 hash function to the ASCII representation of the round index, ensuring that each constant is unique, unpredictable, and highly asymmetric. This method guarantees that no two rounds are identical in structure and that the constants cannot be predicted or manipulated by an adversary without breaking the underlying hash function.

The algorithm supports two key sizes—128 bits and 256 bits—with corresponding round counts of 10 and 16, respectively. The number of rounds is determined through rigorous cryptanalytic analysis to ensure resistance against differential and linear attacks, with a substantial security margin beyond the theoretical minimum. For a 128-bit key, the algorithm resists differential attacks up to a complexity of 2¹²⁸, and for a 256-bit key, it resists attacks up to 2²⁵⁶, matching the security level of the key itself. The permutation is designed to be invertible, but only the forward direction is required for practical use, simplifying implementation and reducing attack surface.

A critical feature of this invention is its extensibility and customizability. While a specific instantiation is provided, the algorithm permits modification of four key components without compromising security: the initial state of the inner portion of the duplex, the 16×16 S-boxes, the bitwise permutation, and the mixing matrix. Each of these components can be altered within well-defined constraints that preserve the cryptographic properties necessary for resistance against known attacks. For instance, alternative S-boxes may be substituted as long as their maximum differential probability does not exceed 2⁻¹⁴ and their linear bias does not exceed 2⁻⁸. Alternative bitwise permutations may be used provided they are derangements with no fixed points, maximal order, and affine definability. Alternative mixing matrices may be employed so long as they are invertible over GF(2¹⁶) and have both linear and differential branch numbers equal to three. Round constants may be replaced with any set of 512-bit values that are unique per round and exhibit high entropy, whether generated pseudorandomly, derived from a different hash function, or selected from a precomputed pool. These customizations allow organizations to generate unique, proprietary variants of the algorithm tailored to their operational needs, without requiring a full re-analysis of the entire system. Each customization is designed to be modular, allowing for incremental changes that do not necessitate a complete redesign of the permutation or revalidation of the entire cryptographic structure.

The algorithm is intended for use in a duplex mode of operation, where a key and optional initialization vector are absorbed through mute calls, header data is authenticated without encryption through additional mute calls, plaintext data is absorbed and simultaneously encrypted via XOR with a keystream generated from the duplex output, and a final blank call produces an authentication tag. Domain separation and padding are assumed to be handled at a higher protocol layer, allowing the core algorithm to remain simple, efficient, and focused on the permutation itself. The algorithm is optimized for hardware implementation, with all operations expressible in terms of bitwise logic, finite field arithmetic, and wire routing, resulting in a design that achieves high throughput with minimal silicon area, low power consumption, and resistance to side-channel attacks. Software implementations are also feasible, though the performance benefits are most pronounced in hardware.

The invention provides a novel and non-obvious solution to the longstanding problem of balancing security, performance, and customization in authenticated encryption. By integrating a high-complexity, hardware-efficient permutation into the duplex framework, it eliminates the need for multiple passes, dual keys, or complex key management, while simultaneously enabling users to generate unique algorithmic variants that resist large-scale cryptanalysis. The use of 16-bit S-boxes, a provably optimal bitwise permutation, and a maximally branched mixing layer represents a significant departure from conventional designs that rely on 8-bit substitutions and simpler diffusion layers. The security analysis demonstrates resistance to differential and linear cryptanalysis at levels exceeding brute-force complexity, and the algebraic complexity of the S-boxes renders algebraic attacks intractable. This invention thus fulfills a critical gap in the cryptographic landscape by offering a secure, efficient, and customizable authenticated encryption algorithm that is uniquely suited for high-assurance, hardware-constrained, and mission-critical applications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The authenticated encryption algorithm described herein is a cryptographic system designed to provide confidentiality and integrity through a single, unified process that operates within the framework of the duplex construction. This construction enables the simultaneous absorption of input data and the squeezing of output data, eliminating the need for separate encryption and authentication phases and thereby achieving superior performance compared to traditional two-pass methods. The algorithm is structured around a permutation function that operates on a 512-bit internal state, which is partitioned into a 128-bit rate and a 384-bit capacity. This partitioning ensures that the algorithm maintains a high level of security while preserving a fast data throughput, as the rate determines the speed of data processing and the capacity governs the resistance to generic attacks. The security level of the algorithm is formally bounded by the capacity, and with a 384-bit capacity, the algorithm provides a security level of 2¹²⁸ for a 128-bit key and 2²⁵⁶ for a 256-bit key, which meets or exceeds the security guarantees of NIST-standardized authenticated encryption schemes such as AES-GCM and ChaCha20-Poly1305.

The algorithm is initialized by constructing a duplex object whose internal state is set to a predefined initial value. In the default instantiation, this initial value is zero for both the rate and capacity portions of the state, but this is not a requirement. The initial state may be set to any 512-bit value, and in customized implementations, the inner 384-bit portion may be initialized to a unique, user-specific value generated during system provisioning. This initialization step is performed before any key or data is absorbed, and it serves as the first point of customization. By selecting a non-zero, unpredictable initial state, an organization can ensure that even if the algorithm’s structure is known, the starting point of the permutation is unique to their deployment, thereby rendering precomputed cryptanalytic tables useless and forcing an adversary to perform a dedicated attack for each individual instance.

Once the state is initialized, the key is absorbed into the duplex object through one or more mute calls. A mute call is defined as a call to the duplexing function where input data is absorbed into the state but no output is produced. The key, which may be either 128 bits or 256 bits in length, is divided into 128-bit blocks and XORed into the rate portion of the state, followed by the application of the permutation function. If the key length exceeds 128 bits, multiple mute calls are performed, each absorbing a subsequent 128-bit segment of the key. The permutation is applied after each absorption, ensuring that the key material is thoroughly mixed into the internal state. This process is repeated until the entire key has been absorbed. The use of mute calls ensures that the key is never directly exposed in the output, preserving its secrecy and preventing any leakage through side channels or output analysis.

Following key absorption, header data may be processed. Header data refers to any metadata or additional authenticated data that must be authenticated but not encrypted. This may include packet headers, timestamps, source and destination identifiers, or protocol-specific control information. Header data is absorbed into the state using additional mute calls, identical in structure to those used for key absorption. Each block of header data is XORed into the rate portion of the state, followed by the application of the permutation function. The absence of output during these calls ensures that the header data remains hidden from external observers, while its influence on the internal state guarantees that any modification to the header will result in a different authentication tag. This mechanism provides strong integrity protection for contextual data without requiring additional encryption overhead.

The body data, which constitutes the actual plaintext to be encrypted, is processed in a different manner. Each block of body data is absorbed into the rate portion of the state through a standard duplexing call, which simultaneously absorbs the input and produces an output. Specifically, the plaintext block is XORed into the rate portion of the state, the permutation function is applied, and then the new rate portion is extracted as a keystream block. This keystream block is then XORed with the plaintext block to produce the corresponding ciphertext block. This process is repeated for each block of plaintext, with the state being updated after every absorption and squeezing operation. The duplex construction ensures that the keystream is dependent on the entire history of absorbed data, including the key, header, and all preceding plaintext blocks, thereby preventing any replay or reordering attacks. The ciphertext blocks are output immediately after generation, allowing for real-time, streaming encryption without buffering.

After all body data has been processed, an authentication tag is generated through a blank call. A blank call is defined as a call to the duplexing function where no input is provided, but output is produced. In this step, the permutation function is applied to the current internal state, and the resulting rate portion is extracted as the authentication tag. This tag is a fixed-length value, typically 128 bits, that serves as a cryptographic checksum of the entire message, including the key, header, and body data. The tag is computed after all inputs have been absorbed, ensuring that any alteration to any part of the input—whether the key, header, or ciphertext—will result in a completely different tag. During decryption, the receiver performs an identical sequence of operations, using the same key and header, and recomputes the tag. If the recomputed tag matches the received tag, the message is accepted as authentic and intact; otherwise, it is rejected.

The permutation function that underlies this entire process is the central innovation of the invention. It is applied after every absorption and squeezing operation and consists of a sequence of four distinct steps: substitution, bitwise permutation, mixing, and round constant addition. These steps are repeated for a predetermined number of rounds, which is ten for a 128-bit key and sixteen for a 256-bit key. Each round is designed to introduce nonlinearity, diffusion, and asymmetry into the state, ensuring that the output is cryptographically unpredictable and resistant to all known forms of cryptanalysis.

The first step in each round is the substitution step, which applies 32 identical 16×16 bijective S-boxes to the 32 16-bit words that compose the state. Each S-box operates independently on one word, transforming it according to a fixed, invertible mapping defined over the finite field GF(2¹⁶). The S-box is constructed using multiplicative inversion in GF(2¹⁶) modulo the irreducible polynomial p(x) = x¹⁶ + x⁵ + x³ + x + 1, followed by an affine transformation. This construction ensures that the S-box is highly nonlinear and algebraically complex, with a maximum differential probability of 2⁻¹⁴ and a maximum linear bias of 2⁻⁸, as determined through exhaustive analysis using specialized evaluation tools. The use of multiplicative inversion eliminates the need for lookup tables, as the inversion can be computed using a sequence of finite field operations that are efficiently implementable in hardware. Specifically, the inversion can be performed using a combination of field multiplications, additions, and conditional bit rotations, requiring only 1,238 XOR gates and 144 AND gates per S-box. This hardware efficiency is a critical advantage over alternative 16-bit S-box designs that rely on memory-based implementations, which would require 64 kilobytes of storage per S-box and introduce latency due to memory access delays. The substitution step is the only nonlinear component in the permutation, and as such, it is the primary source of confusion in the system. The bijective nature of the S-box ensures that no information is lost during the transformation, preserving the entropy of the state and enabling the permutation to be invertible in theory, though inversion is not required for practical use.

Following the substitution step, a bitwise permutation is applied to the entire 512-bit state. This permutation is not a permutation of words, but rather a rearrangement of individual bits, ensuring that the diffusion extends across the entire state rather than being confined to word boundaries. The permutation is defined by an affine function over the bit indices, which maps each bit position to a new position in a deterministic and reversible manner. The specific permutation used in this invention has an order of 32, meaning that after 32 applications, every bit returns to its original position. Importantly, this permutation is a derangement, meaning that no bit remains in its original position after a single application. This property ensures that every bit contributes to the diffusion of differences in every round. Furthermore, the permutation has no low-order bits—every bit has the same order as the overall permutation—ensuring uniform diffusion across all positions. The permutation is also designed such that the 16 output bits of each S-box are routed to 16 distinct mixers in the next round, preventing clustering of diffusion and ensuring that changes propagate evenly across the state. This property is critical for maximizing the number of active S-boxes in differential and linear trails, thereby increasing the complexity of any attack. The permutation is implemented as a simple wire rerouting in hardware, requiring no logic gates and introducing zero latency, making it an ideal component for high-speed implementations.

The third step in each round is the mixing step, which operates on pairs of adjacent 16-bit words using a 2×2 matrix multiplication over the finite field GF(2¹⁶) modulo the irreducible polynomial q(x) = x¹⁶ + x⁵ + x³ + x² + 1. The matrix used in this invention is symmetric and invertible, and its entries are chosen such that both the linear and differential branch numbers are maximized at three. The branch number is a measure of how many input differences propagate to output differences, and a branch number of three means that any non-zero input difference must affect at least three S-boxes in the next round. This property is formally verified using a Boolean satisfiability solver, which confirms that there is no possible input difference of weight one that results in an output difference of weight one. The mixer is implemented using a combination of field multiplications by x, which are trivial to compute using a single left rotation of the 16-bit word and three XOR gates, and field additions, which are performed using standard bitwise XOR. The matrix multiplication is applied to consecutive pairs of words, such that word 0 is mixed with word 1, word 2 with word 3, and so on, ensuring that the diffusion is localized but still sufficient to connect distant parts of the state over multiple rounds. The use of a symmetric matrix ensures that the linear and differential branch numbers are equal, simplifying the security analysis and guaranteeing resistance to both types of cryptanalysis.

The final step in each round is the addition of a round constant, which is a 512-bit value that is XORed into the entire state. This step is essential for preventing slide attacks, which exploit the repetition of identical round functions in ciphers with weak key scheduling. The round constant for round i is generated by taking the ASCII representation of the integer i, hashing it using SHA-3-512, and using the resulting 512-bit digest as the constant. This ensures that each round constant is unique, unpredictable, and highly asymmetric, with no discernible pattern that could be exploited by an adversary. The use of SHA-3-512 guarantees that even small changes in the round index result in completely different constants, and the cryptographic strength of SHA-3 ensures that the constants cannot be reverse-engineered or predicted. Alternative methods of generating round constants may be used, provided they satisfy two conditions: each constant must be unique per round, and each constant must exhibit high entropy and asymmetry. The round constants are not intended to provide cryptographic strength on their own, but rather to break any potential symmetry in the state that might arise from the repetition of the same permutation structure across multiple rounds.

The number of rounds in the permutation is determined through rigorous cryptanalytic analysis. For a 128-bit key, ten rounds are sufficient to ensure that the probability of a successful differential attack exceeds the complexity of a brute-force key search, which is 2¹²⁸. Similarly, for a 256-bit key, sixteen rounds are required to achieve a comparable security margin. The number of rounds is not arbitrary; it is calculated based on the worst-case differential probability of the S-box, the differential branch number of the mixer, and the number of active S-boxes that can be chained together across rounds. The analysis shows that even under the most favorable conditions for an attacker, the probability of a differential characteristic surviving more than six rounds is less than 2⁻¹²⁸, and thus ten rounds provide a substantial security margin. The same reasoning applies to linear cryptanalysis, where the linear bias of the S-box and the linear branch number of the mixer are combined using Matsui’s Piling-Up Lemma to show that the overall bias of any linear approximation becomes negligible after six rounds, necessitating ten rounds for a 128-bit key and sixteen for a 256-bit key. The additional rounds for the 256-bit key are included to maintain a proportional security margin, as the key space is larger and thus requires a correspondingly higher number of rounds to ensure that the algorithm’s resistance scales appropriately.

The algorithm is designed to be fully customizable without compromising its security. The initial state of the inner portion of the duplex may be set to any 384-bit value, allowing each user or organization to generate a unique starting point for the permutation. This customization does not require any change to the algorithm’s structure or any additional cryptanalysis, as the security proof is based on the assumption that the permutation is secure regardless of the initial state, as long as it is not predictable. Similarly, the 16×16 S-boxes may be replaced with any other bijective 16-bit S-box, provided that its maximum differential probability does not exceed 2⁻¹⁴ and its maximum linear bias does not exceed 2⁻⁸. These bounds are derived from the analysis of the original S-box and represent the threshold beyond which the algorithm would become vulnerable to differential or linear attacks. Alternative S-boxes may be constructed using different finite field polynomials or different affine transformations, as long as they meet these criteria. The bitwise permutation may be replaced with any other derangement of the 512-bit state that has no fixed points, an order greater than the number of rounds, and is definable by an affine function. There exist a large number of such permutations, and users may select any one from a precomputed list without requiring further analysis. The mixing matrix may be replaced with any 2×2 invertible matrix over GF(2¹⁶) that has both linear and differential branch numbers equal to three. The existence of such matrices is guaranteed by the mathematical properties of finite fields, and their verification can be performed using SAT solvers or other automated tools. Finally, the round constants may be replaced with any set of 512-bit values that are unique per round and exhibit high entropy, whether generated using SHA-3, a pseudorandom number generator, or a manually selected pool of random values. Each of these customizations can be applied independently or in combination, allowing for the generation of a vast number of unique algorithmic variants, each of which remains secure as long as the constraints are respected.

The algorithm is optimized for hardware implementation, with all operations expressible in terms of bitwise logic, finite field arithmetic, and wire routing. The substitution step requires only 1,238 XOR gates and 144 AND gates per S-box, and with 32 S-boxes, the total gate count for substitution is approximately 39,616 XOR gates and 4,608 AND gates. The bitwise permutation requires no gates at all, as it is implemented as a direct rewiring of signals. The mixing step requires a small number of additional XOR gates for the field multiplications and additions, and the round constant addition is simply a 512-bit XOR operation, which can be implemented with 512 XOR gates. The total gate count for the entire permutation is therefore well under 50,000 gates, making it suitable for implementation on small FPGAs or ASICs with minimal area and power consumption. The algorithm can be pipelined to achieve high throughput, with each round processed in a separate pipeline stage, allowing for the encryption of one block per clock cycle at high frequencies. Software implementations are also possible, though the performance benefits are most pronounced in hardware, where the parallelism and lack of memory accesses enable maximum efficiency.

The algorithm is intended for use in environments where both security and performance are critical, such as military communication systems, secure embedded devices, IoT platforms with constrained resources, and high-speed network appliances. Its ability to be customized without requiring re-analysis makes it ideal for deployment in scenarios where algorithmic uniqueness is desired as a defense-in-depth measure. Unlike public algorithms, which are subject to exhaustive cryptanalysis by adversaries, this algorithm allows organizations to generate proprietary variants that are immune to large-scale attacks, since each variant must be attacked individually. The combination of high security, low latency, minimal hardware footprint, and extensive customization options makes this invention a significant advancement in the field of authenticated encryption, providing a practical and scalable solution to the longstanding challenges of performance, security, and operational flexibility.
