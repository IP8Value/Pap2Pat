# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to a method and system for the continuous, noninvasive estimation of intracranial pressure (ICP) in human subjects using readily available physiological signals, specifically radial arterial blood pressure (rABP) and cerebral blood flow velocity (CBFV), without requiring direct intracranial access or prior calibration to invasive reference measurements. The invention operates within the domain of neurocritical care monitoring, where real-time assessment of intracranial dynamics is essential for the management of traumatic brain injury, hydrocephalus, hemorrhagic stroke, and other neurological conditions associated with altered intracranial compliance and perfusion. The method leverages a physiologically grounded model of cerebral hemodynamics that captures the dynamic relationship between arterial pressure and cerebral blood flow, while accounting for unmeasured physiological variables such as the time delay between arterial and cerebral waveforms and the temporal evolution of mean ICP. By integrating a Bayesian estimation framework with an autoregressive model of ICP dynamics, the invention enables accurate, patient-specific, and clinically interpretable estimation of both mean ICP and ICP pulse pressure from noninvasively acquired signals. The system is designed for deployment in intensive care units, operating rooms, and emergency settings where continuous monitoring is desired but invasive procedures are contraindicated, risky, or impractical. The invention further provides a mechanism for tracking transient changes in ICP over extended periods, reducing dependence on initial prior assumptions and enhancing robustness across heterogeneous patient populations. Importantly, the method does not rely on machine learning models trained on large datasets, nor does it require patient-specific calibration, making it broadly applicable across diverse clinical contexts and demographic groups.

## BACKGROUND

The clinical management of patients with neurological injuries has long been hindered by the invasive nature of gold-standard intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring techniques, which typically involve the surgical placement of catheters or transducers into the ventricular system or parenchyma of the brain. These methods, while accurate, carry significant risks including infection, hemorrhage, and mechanical damage to neural tissue, and are often reserved for critically ill patients in intensive care units due to their procedural complexity and associated complications. As a result, many patients who could benefit from continuous ICP monitoring—such as those with mild to moderate traumatic brain injury, post-hemorrhagic conditions, or acute hydrocephalus—are excluded from such monitoring due to the perceived risk-benefit imbalance. This clinical gap has motivated decades of research into noninvasive alternatives, yet existing approaches have largely failed to achieve the accuracy, reliability, and generalizability required for routine clinical adoption. Many noninvasive methods rely on empirical correlations between surrogate signals such as transcranial Doppler flow velocities, ocular fundus pressure, or tympanic membrane displacement, and ICP, but these correlations are highly variable across individuals and are often confounded by factors such as age, head anatomy, vascular compliance, and pathological state. Other approaches attempt to model the cerebrovascular system using complex multi-compartmental models or transfer functions derived from population averages, but these models frequently require extensive parameter tuning, prior calibration against invasive measurements, or assumptions about cerebrovascular autoregulation that do not hold across diverse patient populations. Furthermore, many methods are sensitive to temporal misalignment between arterial and cerebral waveforms, a common issue arising from differences in signal acquisition sites, transducer response times, and propagation delays through the vascular tree. These misalignments, if unaccounted for, introduce systematic errors that degrade estimation accuracy and render results unreliable. Additionally, existing noninvasive techniques typically provide only a single-point estimate of ICP, offering no insight into the temporal dynamics of intracranial pressure or its pulsatile components, which are critical for detecting pathological phenomena such as plateau waves or autoregulatory failure. While some recent efforts have incorporated Bayesian inference or signal filtering to improve robustness, they remain limited by their reliance on fixed prior distributions that are not adapted to individual patient physiology, or by their inability to distinguish between true physiological changes and measurement noise over prolonged monitoring periods. Moreover, no prior method has successfully combined a simple, interpretable, physiologically based model of cerebral hemodynamics with a real-time tracking mechanism that progressively reduces dependence on initial assumptions while maintaining high accuracy. The absence of a method capable of simultaneously estimating both mean ICP and ICP pulse pressure in a calibration-free, model-based, and temporally adaptive manner represents a critical unmet need in neurocritical care. There exists, therefore, a compelling clinical and technological imperative for a novel system that can deliver continuous, accurate, and reliable noninvasive ICP estimates without the constraints of invasiveness, calibration, or population-specific training, while also providing insight into the pulsatile characteristics of intracranial pressure dynamics.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a novel method for the continuous, noninvasive estimation of intracranial pressure (ICP) and ICP pulse pressure using radial arterial blood pressure (rABP) and cerebral blood flow velocity (CBFV) signals, without requiring any invasive calibration or prior knowledge of individual patient physiology. The method employs a two-stage estimation framework grounded in a simplified, physiologically interpretable model of cerebral hemodynamics that represents the cerebrovascular system as a resistive-capacitive network, where cerebral perfusion pressure is defined as the difference between arterial and intracranial pressures. In the first stage, a baseline estimate of mean ICP is established by analyzing multiple consecutive data windows of rABP and CBFV signals, each spanning approximately twenty cardiac cycles. For each window, a likelihood distribution over possible ICP values and time offsets between the rABP and CBFV waveforms is computed by minimizing the prediction error of the CBFV signal under the assumed hemodynamic model. This likelihood distribution is then combined with a prior probability distribution that assigns elevated probability to physiologically plausible but extreme ICP values, including those below 5 mmHg and above 30 mmHg, to ensure broad applicability across diverse clinical populations. The median of the resulting posterior distribution is taken as the baseline ICP estimate, and this process is repeated across five consecutive windows, with the final baseline being the average of these five estimates. In the second stage, subsequent ICP estimates are generated using a uniform prior distribution to minimize reliance on the initial prior belief, thereby enhancing adaptability to evolving physiological states. However, because uniform priors increase susceptibility to noise-induced estimation errors, a Kalman-filter-like tracking mechanism is employed to fuse these noninvasive estimates with model-predicted changes in ICP derived from a first-order autoregressive model of ICP temporal evolution. This fusion process assigns greater weight to the estimate with lower uncertainty, effectively filtering out spurious fluctuations while preserving true physiological trends. The resulting filtered change is added to the baseline to produce the final noninvasive ICP estimate for each subsequent window. Additionally, the same likelihood distribution used for mean ICP estimation is leveraged to compute noninvasive estimates of ICP pulse pressure by reconstructing the mean-subtracted ICP waveform from the Fourier transform of the CBFV and rABP signals, using only those frequency harmonics that exhibit monotonic spectral decay, thereby avoiding the need for pre-trained models or statistical fitting. The method is fully automated, computationally efficient, and operates in real time on noninvasively acquired signals, requiring no patient-specific calibration or invasive reference measurements. It is robust to variations in signal timing, waveform morphology, and physiological state, and provides both mean ICP and ICP pulse pressure estimates with clinically acceptable accuracy, as demonstrated by Bland-Altman analysis showing a mean bias of less than one millimeter of mercury and a root mean square error below four millimeters of mercury across a large cohort of patients with diverse neurological conditions. The invention further enables continuous, long-term monitoring of ICP dynamics without degradation in accuracy due to prior dependence, making it uniquely suited for applications in neurocritical care, intraoperative monitoring, and emergency neurological triage.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The method of the present invention for noninvasive intracranial pressure estimation is implemented through a sequence of computationally structured, physiologically grounded operations that transform raw arterial and cerebral flow signals into accurate, time-resolved estimates of mean ICP and ICP pulse pressure. The system operates on digitized waveforms of radial arterial blood pressure and transcranial Doppler-measured cerebral blood flow velocity, both of which are routinely acquired in neurocritical care settings using standard clinical equipment. These signals are first preprocessed to remove low-frequency trends and out-of-band noise, ensuring that only the physiologically relevant oscillatory components are retained for analysis. The preprocessing stage involves the application of a moving average filter to extract the slow-varying baseline components of both the rABP and CBFV signals, followed by subtraction of these baselines to yield detrended waveforms. These detrended signals are then passed through a bandpass filter with cutoff frequencies of 0.5 Hz and 16 Hz to eliminate high-frequency noise and low-frequency drift, after which the original baseline components are re-added to restore the absolute pressure and velocity levels. This procedure ensures that the DC components necessary for accurate perfusion pressure calculation are preserved while removing artifacts that could interfere with the model-based estimation. The processed signals are resampled to a common sampling rate of 125 Hz to ensure temporal alignment across all subsequent computational steps. The system then partitions the continuous stream of data into non-overlapping estimation windows, each comprising exactly twenty consecutive cardiac cycles, a duration chosen to balance the need for sufficient signal content to estimate model parameters reliably with the requirement for temporal resolution to capture dynamic changes in intracranial pressure. Within each window, the method begins by defining a range of candidate values for the mean intracranial pressure, beginning at 0 mmHg and increasing in one-millimeter increments up to the mean value of the radial arterial blood pressure observed within that window. This upper bound is selected on the physiological grounds that intracranial pressure cannot exceed the mean arterial pressure in the absence of pathological conditions such as venous outflow obstruction, and negative ICP values are excluded based on clinical evidence indicating their extreme rarity in the target patient population. Simultaneously, a range of time offsets between the rABP and CBFV waveforms is defined, with the bounds of this range determined dynamically for each window based on the requirement that the systolic peaks of the CBFV waveform consistently precede those of the rABP, while the diastolic portions of the two waveforms remain aligned within a tolerance of three sampling intervals, approximately 25 milliseconds. This constraint is motivated by the underlying Windkessel-like model of cerebral hemodynamics, which predicts that cerebral blood flow velocity should rise more rapidly than arterial pressure following the onset of systole, due to the compliance of the cerebral vasculature, and that both signals should return to baseline nearly simultaneously during diastole. Any time offset that satisfies these dual criteria is included in the scan range, thereby accommodating inter-patient variability in signal propagation delays and measurement artifacts without requiring prior calibration. For each combination of candidate mean ICP value and time offset, the method computes estimates of the two model parameters that define the relationship between cerebral perfusion pressure and cerebral blood flow velocity. These parameters, denoted as α and β, are derived by solving a linear least-squares optimization problem in which the predicted CBFV waveform is modeled as a linear combination of the cerebral perfusion pressure waveform and its first-order finite difference approximation. The cerebral perfusion pressure is constructed by subtracting the candidate ICP value from the time-shifted rABP waveform, and the finite difference is computed using a forward Euler approximation with the known sampling rate. The least-squares solution is obtained by forming a design matrix from the perfusion pressure and its difference, and applying the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse to determine the optimal coefficients that minimize the squared error between the predicted and measured CBFV waveforms. This process yields, for each ICP-time offset pair, a set of parameter estimates α and β, along with a corresponding residual error norm that quantifies the discrepancy between the model’s prediction and the actual CBFV signal. These residual norms are then transformed into a likelihood function that assigns higher probability to combinations of ICP and time offset that yield smaller prediction errors, reflecting the assumption that the true physiological state corresponds to the parameter set that best explains the observed data. The likelihood function is normalized to form a probability distribution over the joint space of ICP and time offset, and this two-dimensional distribution is then marginalized over the time offset dimension to produce a one-dimensional likelihood distribution that depends solely on the mean ICP value. This marginalization step effectively integrates out the uncertainty in the temporal alignment of the signals, allowing the method to be robust to the inherent variability in signal propagation delays between the arterial and cerebral measurement sites. The resulting one-dimensional likelihood distribution is then combined with a prior probability distribution that reflects prior knowledge about the likely range of ICP values in the clinical population. This prior distribution is not derived from population averages or historical data, but is instead deliberately designed to be conservative and inclusive, assigning elevated probability to ICP values that are clinically significant but infrequently observed in the training data, specifically those below 5 mmHg and above 30 mmHg. The prior is constructed as a mixture of two truncated Gaussian distributions, one centered at 13.6 mmHg with a standard deviation of 10 mmHg to represent low ICP states, and another centered at 50 mmHg with a standard deviation of 20 mmHg to represent elevated ICP states, with respective weights of 0.8 and 0.2 to reflect the relative prevalence of these extremes in prior clinical literature. This formulation ensures that the method remains sensitive to both hypo- and hypertensive intracranial conditions, even when such states are rare in the dataset, thereby enhancing its generalizability across diverse patient populations and pathological conditions. The product of the likelihood and prior distributions is normalized to form a posterior probability distribution, and the median of this posterior distribution is selected as the baseline estimate of mean ICP for that window. This process is repeated for each of the first five consecutive windows, and the resulting five estimates are averaged to produce a stable, initial baseline ICP value. This baseline serves as the reference point for all subsequent tracking operations and is computed using the full prior distribution to maximize the influence of physiological plausibility in the initial estimation phase. Following the establishment of the baseline, the method transitions into a tracking phase in which subsequent ICP estimates are generated using a uniform prior distribution over the ICP range, effectively removing any influence from the initial prior belief. While this approach increases the method’s adaptability to changing physiological conditions, it also renders the estimates more susceptible to noise and transient artifacts, as the uniform prior provides no regularization to constrain the solution space. To mitigate this vulnerability, a Kalman-filter-like fusion mechanism is employed to combine the noninvasive ICP change estimates derived from the uniform prior with model-predicted changes derived from a first-order autoregressive model of ICP temporal evolution. This autoregressive model assumes that the change in mean ICP from one estimation window to the next is governed by a linear recurrence relation with a single parameter γ, which is set to unity to reflect the possibility of rapid ICP fluctuations. The model also incorporates a white noise term with variance σ²v, which is empirically set to 15 mmHg² to account for the uncertainty in the prediction of ICP dynamics. The model predicts the expected change in ICP for the current window based on the filtered change estimate from the previous window, and computes the associated prediction variance. The noninvasive change estimate, derived from the difference between the current and previous ICP estimates obtained under the uniform prior, is similarly assigned a variance based on the spread of the likelihood distribution. These two estimates—the model-predicted change and the noninvasive change—are then fused using a weighted average in which the weights are inversely proportional to their respective variances, thereby assigning greater confidence to the estimate with lower uncertainty. The resulting filtered change is added to the baseline ICP value to produce the final noninvasive ICP estimate for the current window. This process is repeated iteratively for each successive window, with the baseline remaining fixed and the filtered change accumulating over time to track long-term trends in ICP. This tracking mechanism effectively decouples the estimation from the initial prior distribution, allowing the system to adapt to sustained physiological changes such as those induced by plateau waves or autoregulatory collapse, while simultaneously suppressing short-term noise and measurement artifacts. The method further extends its utility by enabling the noninvasive estimation of ICP pulse pressure, defined as the difference between the peak and trough of the intracranial pressure waveform. This is accomplished by leveraging the same likelihood distribution used for mean ICP estimation, but applying it in a spectral domain. For each estimation window, the mean-subtracted rABP and CBFV waveforms are averaged across individual cardiac cycles to form representative wavelets, with each cycle truncated or extended to a common length defined by the shortest beat duration in the ABP signal. These wavelets are then subjected to a discrete Fourier transform, yielding a set of frequency harmonics that describe the periodic components of the waveforms. The relationship between the Fourier transforms of the CBFV and rABP signals, as derived from the underlying hemodynamic model, is then inverted to solve for the Fourier transform of the ICP waveform. Due to the presence of noise and modeling inaccuracies, reliable estimates are only obtained for the lower harmonics, and a heuristic criterion is applied to retain only those harmonics whose spectral magnitude decreases monotonically with increasing frequency, thereby eliminating spurious higher-frequency components that do not reflect true physiological dynamics. The inverse discrete Fourier transform of the selected harmonics is then computed to reconstruct an approximate ICP waveform, from which the pulse pressure is extracted as the difference between its maximum and minimum values. This procedure is performed independently for each estimation window, yielding a continuous, time-resolved estimate of ICP pulse pressure without requiring any pre-trained models, population-specific calibration, or statistical fitting. The resulting estimates of both mean ICP and ICP pulse pressure are output in real time, accompanied by confidence metrics derived from the variances of the likelihood and posterior distributions, allowing clinicians to assess the reliability of each estimate on a window-by-window basis. The entire system operates in a fully automated, deterministic, and causal manner, requiring no iterative optimization, no machine learning training, and no external calibration. It is implemented using standard digital signal processing techniques and can be deployed on embedded medical devices, bedside monitors, or cloud-based clinical platforms with minimal computational overhead. The method has been validated on a cohort of thirteen pediatric and young adult patients with diverse neurological pathologies, including traumatic brain injury, hydrocephalus, and hemorrhagic stroke, using a total of 1,657 twenty-beat estimation windows spanning over seven hours of continuous data. Bland-Altman analysis demonstrated a mean bias of 0.6 mmHg and a root mean square error of 3.7 mmHg for mean ICP estimation, with limits of agreement ranging from -6.6 to 7.7 mmHg, performance characteristics that are comparable to those reported for invasive micro-transducer devices. Similarly, ICP pulse pressure estimation yielded a mean bias of -1.2 mmHg and a root mean square error of 4.0 mmHg across 1,093 windows, indicating that the method is capable of accurately capturing both the mean and pulsatile components of intracranial pressure. The method’s robustness to the absence of prior information was further confirmed by testing under a uniform prior distribution, which resulted in only a modest degradation in accuracy, underscoring its suitability for use in settings where no prior clinical information is available. The system’s ability to track transient ICP changes was demonstrated by introducing synthetic trends into the CBFV signal, mimicking plateau waves, and observing that the tracking mechanism successfully captured the induced pressure elevations, whereas a static prior approach failed to respond appropriately. The invention thus provides a comprehensive, clinically viable solution for continuous, noninvasive intracranial pressure monitoring that combines physiological interpretability, computational efficiency, and robust performance across heterogeneous patient populations, representing a significant advancement over all prior methods in the field.
