# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND ART

Schizophrenia is a complex and debilitating neuropsychiatric disorder characterized by profound disruptions in thought processes, emotional regulation, and behavioral control, often manifesting in hallucinations, delusions, disorganized speech, and impaired social functioning. Despite decades of clinical and scientific investigation, the precise etiological mechanisms underlying schizophrenia remain incompletely understood, and its diagnosis continues to rely primarily on subjective clinical assessments rather than objective biological markers. The current diagnostic criteria, as outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), are based on symptomatology observed over time, which introduces significant variability in diagnostic accuracy, delays in intervention, and challenges in predicting disease progression or treatment response. This diagnostic ambiguity is compounded by the heterogeneity of the disorder, with multiple phenotypic presentations and overlapping features with other psychiatric conditions such as schizoaffective disorder, bipolar disorder with psychotic features, and severe major depressive disorder with psychosis. Consequently, there exists a critical and unmet need in clinical psychiatry for reliable, quantifiable, and biologically grounded diagnostic tools that can distinguish schizophrenia from other disorders at an early stage, ideally prior to the full emergence of psychotic symptoms.

Historically, the pathophysiology of schizophrenia has been predominantly framed within the context of neurotransmitter dysregulation, particularly involving the dopaminergic system. The dopamine hypothesis, initially proposed in the 1960s and refined over subsequent decades, posits that hyperactivity of mesolimbic dopamine pathways contributes to positive symptoms such as hallucinations and delusions, while hypoactivity of mesocortical pathways underlies negative symptoms and cognitive deficits. This model has been strongly supported by the therapeutic efficacy of antipsychotic medications, which primarily function as antagonists or partial agonists at dopamine D2 receptors. However, the dopamine hypothesis alone fails to account for the full spectrum of neurobiological abnormalities observed in schizophrenia, including structural brain changes, synaptic pruning anomalies, glutamatergic dysfunction, and immune system alterations. Moreover, the temporal relationship between dopamine receptor occupancy and symptom onset remains unclear, and the observation that antipsychotic drugs often require weeks to exert clinical effects despite immediate receptor blockade suggests that downstream neuroadaptive processes, rather than acute receptor modulation, may be more central to therapeutic action.

In parallel with advances in neuropharmacology, emerging evidence has implicated peripheral biological systems in the pathogenesis and manifestation of schizophrenia. Notably, the presence of neurotransmitter receptors, neuropeptides, and associated signaling molecules in peripheral immune cells—particularly lymphocytes—has been documented in multiple studies. These findings challenge the traditional view of the brain as an immunologically isolated organ and suggest that molecular changes occurring in the central nervous system may be mirrored, at least partially, in the periphery. The expression of dopamine receptor subtypes, including D2, D3, and D4, on the surface of peripheral blood lymphocytes has been repeatedly demonstrated, raising the possibility that these cells could serve as accessible surrogates for monitoring central dopaminergic activity. Furthermore, the functional responsiveness of these lymphocytic receptors to dopamine has been shown to influence T-cell adhesion, migration, and cytokine secretion, indicating that dopaminergic signaling in immune cells is not merely an epiphenomenon but may actively contribute to immune modulation in schizophrenia. The observation that certain dopamine receptor gene expression patterns in lymphocytes remain unchanged despite antipsychotic medication further supports the notion that these alterations are intrinsic to the disease process rather than secondary to pharmacological intervention.

Prior investigations into gene expression profiles in schizophrenia have largely focused on postmortem brain tissue, which, while informative, is inherently limited by its static nature, small sample sizes, confounding effects of terminal illness, and inability to capture dynamic changes over the course of illness. Additionally, the ethical and practical constraints associated with obtaining brain tissue from living patients preclude its use for longitudinal monitoring or early diagnostic screening. In contrast, peripheral blood lymphocytes offer a minimally invasive, repeatable, and clinically feasible source of biological material that can be sampled at multiple time points throughout the disease trajectory. Several studies have employed quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) to measure mRNA levels of specific candidate genes in lymphocytes from schizophrenic patients, with some reporting upregulation of the D3 dopamine receptor subtype (DRD3) and downregulation of the α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (α7AChR). However, these studies were typically restricted to a small number of preselected genes, limiting their capacity for unbiased discovery and reducing their potential for identifying novel biomarker panels.

The advent of high-throughput genomic technologies, particularly microarray analysis, has revolutionized the field of molecular psychiatry by enabling the simultaneous assessment of thousands of gene transcripts in a single experiment. This unbiased approach has allowed researchers to move beyond hypothesis-driven investigations toward comprehensive, systems-level analyses of gene expression in psychiatric disorders. Several groups have applied microarray technology to postmortem brain tissue from schizophrenic individuals, identifying dysregulated pathways related to synaptic plasticity, mitochondrial function, myelination, and immune response. However, the translation of these findings into clinically applicable biomarkers has been hampered by the inherent limitations of postmortem tissue and the difficulty of validating findings in living patients. More recently, a handful of studies have explored gene expression profiles in peripheral blood cells, but these efforts have often been confounded by the inclusion of medicated patients, whose gene expression patterns may be significantly altered by antipsychotic drugs. Moreover, many of these studies have relied on single-method validation, such as qRT-PCR using SYBR Green chemistry, which, while cost-effective, is susceptible to amplification artifacts, primer dimer formation, and variability in amplification efficiency, thereby compromising the reliability and reproducibility of results.

There remains a significant gap in the literature regarding the identification of robust, medication-independent, peripheral molecular markers for schizophrenia that are validated using both high-throughput discovery platforms and highly precise, quantitative confirmation methods. Furthermore, no prior study has simultaneously identified and rigorously validated the co-upregulation of the dopamine D2 receptor gene (DRD2) and the inwardly rectifying potassium channel gene Kir2.3 (KCND3) in peripheral blood lymphocytes of drug-naive or drug-free schizophrenic patients using dual-method validation with both SYBR Green and TaqMan probe-based qRT-PCR. The functional interplay between DRD2 and Kir2.3, particularly in the context of neuronal excitability and dopaminergic modulation of potassium currents, suggests a potential mechanistic link between dopaminergic signaling and membrane electrophysiology that may be conserved across central and peripheral tissues. The discovery that both genes are coordinately overexpressed in the peripheral immune cells of untreated schizophrenic patients represents a novel and compelling observation with profound implications for diagnostics, pathophysiological understanding, and therapeutic monitoring. The absence of confounding medication effects in the studied cohort further strengthens the validity of these findings as intrinsic biomarkers of the disease. The development of a diagnostic assay based on the simultaneous quantification of DRD2 and Kir2.3 mRNA levels in peripheral blood lymphocytes would therefore represent a transformative advance in the field of psychiatric diagnostics, enabling earlier, more accurate, and biologically grounded identification of schizophrenia, independent of subjective clinical evaluation.

## BRIEF DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a novel method for the diagnosis, prognosis, and monitoring of schizophrenia through the quantitative measurement of specific gene expression levels in peripheral blood lymphocytes, specifically the upregulation of the dopamine D2 receptor gene (DRD2) and the inwardly rectifying potassium channel gene Kir2.3 (KCND3). This method is based on the groundbreaking discovery that both DRD2 and Kir2.3 exhibit significantly elevated mRNA expression levels in the peripheral blood lymphocytes of individuals with schizophrenia who have not been exposed to antipsychotic medications or other psychotropic agents, and that this upregulation is not observed in healthy control individuals of comparable age and sex. Unlike prior diagnostic approaches that rely exclusively on clinical symptomatology, this invention provides an objective, molecular, and quantifiable biomarker panel that can be measured using standard molecular biology techniques, thereby enabling early detection, differential diagnosis, and longitudinal tracking of disease progression or treatment response.

The invention encompasses a diagnostic assay that measures the relative expression levels of DRD2 and Kir2.3 mRNA in peripheral blood lymphocytes isolated from a subject, compares these levels to a predefined reference range established from a cohort of healthy, medication-free individuals, and determines whether the expression levels of both genes exceed the upper threshold of normal variation observed in the control population. In a preferred embodiment, the assay employs quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) using either SYBR Green dye or TaqMan probe chemistry to precisely quantify mRNA transcripts, with normalization to one or more stable endogenous reference genes such as β-actin or hypoxanthine phosphoribosyltransferase (HPRT). The method further includes the use of a threshold value for each gene, derived from the maximum expression level observed in the control cohort, such that a subject is diagnosed with schizophrenia only if both DRD2 and Kir2.3 expression levels exceed their respective thresholds. This dual-gene requirement significantly enhances diagnostic specificity, reducing the likelihood of false positives that might arise from the dysregulation of a single gene due to unrelated physiological or pathological conditions.

The invention further includes a kit for performing the diagnostic assay, comprising primers and probes specifically designed to amplify and detect DRD2 and Kir2.3 mRNA transcripts, along with reagents for RNA extraction from peripheral blood lymphocytes, reverse transcription, and quantitative real-time PCR. The kit may also include a calibration standard derived from control samples, a reference range table, and instructions for interpreting the results based on the dual-gene criterion. In an alternative embodiment, the invention encompasses a method for stratifying patients with schizophrenia into subgroups based on the magnitude of DRD2 and Kir2.3 upregulation, which may correlate with symptom severity, treatment responsiveness, or disease subtype. For example, individuals exhibiting exceptionally high expression levels of both genes may be identified as having a more biologically distinct form of the disorder, potentially responsive to novel therapeutic strategies targeting dopaminergic-kir channel interactions.

The invention also provides a method for monitoring the efficacy of therapeutic interventions in schizophrenia by serially measuring DRD2 and Kir2.3 mRNA levels in peripheral blood lymphocytes over time. A reduction in the expression levels of both genes following pharmacological or non-pharmacological treatment may serve as an objective indicator of biological response, independent of subjective clinical ratings. This capability is particularly valuable in cases where symptom improvement is ambiguous or delayed, allowing clinicians to make data-driven decisions regarding medication adjustments, augmentation strategies, or alternative treatment modalities. Furthermore, the invention enables the identification of individuals at ultra-high risk for developing schizophrenia by detecting subthreshold elevations in DRD2 and Kir2.3 expression in first-degree relatives or individuals presenting with attenuated psychotic symptoms, thereby facilitating early intervention before the onset of full-blown psychosis.

The method of the invention is distinguished from prior art by its exclusive reliance on drug-naive or drug-free patient populations, thereby eliminating the confounding influence of antipsychotic medications on gene expression profiles. It is further distinguished by the simultaneous validation of two distinct genes using two independent qRT-PCR methodologies—SYBR Green and TaqMan probe-based detection—thereby ensuring robustness, reproducibility, and resistance to technical artifacts. The use of a dual-gene diagnostic criterion, rather than a single biomarker, significantly improves the predictive accuracy and clinical utility of the assay. Unlike previous attempts to identify peripheral biomarkers for schizophrenia, which have often yielded inconsistent or non-replicable results due to small sample sizes, heterogeneous patient populations, or inadequate validation protocols, the present invention provides a method that is grounded in rigorous statistical analysis, stringent control for confounding variables, and biological plausibility derived from the known functional interaction between dopamine D2 receptors and Kir2.3 potassium channels in neuronal and immune cell signaling pathways.

The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the diagnosis of schizophrenia, transitioning from a purely phenomenological model to a molecular, objective, and biologically validated framework. It enables the detection of schizophrenia prior to the emergence of overt psychotic symptoms, facilitates personalized treatment planning, allows for real-time monitoring of therapeutic response, and opens new avenues for understanding the pathophysiological mechanisms linking dopaminergic dysregulation with membrane excitability in both central and peripheral tissues.

## DEFINITIONS

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms shall have the meanings ascribed below, unless the context clearly indicates otherwise. These definitions are intended to provide clarity and consistency in the interpretation of the claims and the detailed description of the invention.

The term “peripheral blood lymphocytes” refers to a population of white blood cells derived from the circulation of a subject, including T cells, B cells, and natural killer cells, isolated from whole blood following the removal of erythrocytes and granulocytes through density gradient centrifugation or selective lysis. These cells are obtained from venous blood drawn into anticoagulant-containing tubes, such as EDTA-treated tubes, and processed under sterile conditions to preserve RNA integrity. Peripheral blood lymphocytes are distinct from other immune cell populations such as monocytes, neutrophils, or dendritic cells, and are selected for analysis due to their established expression of neurotransmitter receptors, including dopamine receptors, and their accessibility for repeated sampling in living subjects.

The term “drug-naive” describes a subject who has never received any form of antipsychotic medication, mood stabilizer, or other psychotropic agent known to alter central or peripheral neurotransmitter receptor expression, including but not limited to typical antipsychotics, atypical antipsychotics, antidepressants, anxiolytics, or lithium. A drug-naive subject may have received non-psychotropic medications for unrelated medical conditions, provided that such medications have no documented effect on dopamine receptor expression or potassium channel function. The term “drug-free” refers to a subject who has discontinued all psychotropic medications for a period of at least six weeks prior to sample collection, sufficient to allow for the clearance of pharmacological agents and their metabolites from systemic circulation and the restoration of baseline gene expression patterns. Both drug-naive and drug-free subjects are included in the scope of this invention to ensure that observed gene expression changes reflect the underlying disease state rather than pharmacological intervention.

The term “dopamine D2 receptor gene” or “DRD2” refers to the human gene located on chromosome 11q23.2 that encodes the dopamine D2 receptor, a G protein-coupled receptor that mediates the inhibitory effects of dopamine on intracellular signaling pathways, particularly through the inhibition of adenylyl cyclase and modulation of ion channel activity. The DRD2 gene gives rise to two major splice variants, D2 short (D2S) and D2 long (D2L), which differ in the inclusion of a 29-amino acid insert in the third intracellular loop and are differentially distributed in the brain and peripheral tissues. The invention encompasses the measurement of total DRD2 mRNA transcripts, including both isoforms, as well as the detection of specific isoform ratios when applicable.

The term “inwardly rectifying potassium channel gene Kir2.3” or “Kir2.3” or “KCND3” refers to the human gene located on chromosome 12q13.13 that encodes the Kir2.3 protein, a member of the inwardly rectifying potassium channel family (Kir2.x) that mediates sustained inward potassium currents at hyperpolarized membrane potentials and contributes to the maintenance of resting membrane potential and cellular excitability. Kir2.3 is known to interact functionally with dopamine D2 receptors in neuronal tissues, and its expression in peripheral lymphocytes has not been previously associated with psychiatric disorders. The invention includes the detection of total Kir2.3 mRNA transcripts, regardless of splice variants, as long as the primers and probes used in the assay are specific to the coding region of the gene and do not cross-react with other Kir2 family members such as Kir2.1 or Kir2.2.

The term “relative mRNA expression level” refers to the quantified amount of a target mRNA transcript (e.g., DRD2 or Kir2.3) normalized to the expression level of one or more stable endogenous reference genes (e.g., β-actin, HPRT, GAPDH, or RPLP0) within the same sample, calculated using a comparative threshold cycle (Ct) method such as the Pfaffl method, which accounts for differences in amplification efficiency between the target and reference genes. The relative expression level is expressed as a fold-change value relative to a control population and is dimensionless, allowing for direct comparison across samples and laboratories.

The term “reference range” or “normal range” refers to the statistically determined upper and lower bounds of relative mRNA expression levels for DRD2 and Kir2.3 in a population of healthy, drug-naive, or drug-free individuals of comparable age, sex, and ethnicity. The upper threshold of the reference range is defined as the highest observed relative expression level in the control cohort, such that any value exceeding this threshold in a test subject is considered abnormally elevated. The reference range is established using a minimum of ten control individuals and validated across multiple independent cohorts to ensure generalizability and reproducibility.

The term “dual-gene diagnostic criterion” refers to the requirement that both DRD2 and Kir2.3 mRNA expression levels must exceed their respective upper thresholds of the reference range in order for a subject to be classified as having schizophrenia. The simultaneous elevation of both genes is necessary for a positive diagnosis, as the elevation of either gene alone is insufficient and may occur in unrelated conditions. This criterion is based on the biological interdependence of DRD2 and Kir2.3 in dopaminergic signaling pathways and is designed to maximize diagnostic specificity and minimize false positives.

The term “quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction” or “qRT-PCR” refers to a molecular biology technique that enables the real-time monitoring of DNA amplification during the polymerase chain reaction process, allowing for the precise quantification of initial template concentration. In the context of this invention, qRT-PCR is performed using either SYBR Green dye, which binds non-specifically to double-stranded DNA and emits fluorescence proportional to the amount of amplified product, or TaqMan probe chemistry, which employs sequence-specific oligonucleotide probes labeled with a fluorescent reporter and a quencher, such that fluorescence is released only upon probe cleavage during amplification. Both methods are validated for sensitivity, specificity, linearity, and reproducibility in the context of low-abundance mRNA transcripts in peripheral blood lymphocytes.

The term “primer” refers to a short, single-stranded oligonucleotide, typically 18 to 25 nucleotides in length, designed to anneal to a specific region of the target mRNA transcript following reverse transcription into complementary DNA (cDNA), and to serve as a starting point for DNA polymerase-mediated amplification. Primers used in this invention are designed to span intron-exon boundaries where possible to prevent amplification of genomic DNA contamination and are validated for specificity using melting curve analysis, sequencing of PCR products, and cross-reactivity testing against related gene family members.

The term “probe” refers to a labeled oligonucleotide, typically 20 to 30 nucleotides in length, designed to hybridize to a specific sequence within the amplified target region during qRT-PCR. In the case of TaqMan probes, the probe is labeled at the 5′ end with a fluorescent reporter dye (e.g., FAM) and at the 3′ end with a quencher dye (e.g., Dabsyl), such that fluorescence is suppressed in the intact probe and released upon cleavage by the 5′ to 3′ exonuclease activity of the DNA polymerase during amplification. Probes used in this invention are designed to be highly specific to the target gene and are validated for efficiency, signal-to-noise ratio, and absence of non-specific binding.

The term “endogenous reference gene” refers to a constitutively expressed gene whose mRNA levels remain stable across the experimental conditions being compared, such as between schizophrenic patients and healthy controls, and which is used to normalize target gene expression levels to account for variations in RNA quantity, quality, and reverse transcription efficiency. Suitable reference genes for this invention include β-actin (ACTB), hypoxanthine phosphoribosyltransferase (HPRT1), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), and ribosomal protein L30 (RPLP0), selected based on their stability across the studied population and confirmed using algorithms such as geNorm or NormFinder.

The term “diagnostic assay” refers to a standardized, reproducible procedure for measuring the relative expression levels of DRD2 and Kir2.3 mRNA in peripheral blood lymphocytes, including all steps from blood collection and lymphocyte isolation to RNA extraction, reverse transcription, qRT-PCR amplification, data normalization, and interpretation according to the dual-gene diagnostic criterion. The assay may be performed manually or automated using robotic platforms and is intended for use in clinical diagnostic laboratories certified under applicable regulatory standards.

The term “biological sample” refers to any biological material obtained from a subject that contains peripheral blood lymphocytes and is suitable for RNA extraction and gene expression analysis. This includes whole blood collected in EDTA, heparin, or citrate tubes, peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) fractions isolated via density gradient centrifugation, or purified lymphocyte populations obtained through magnetic bead separation or flow cytometry. The biological sample must be processed within a defined time window after collection to preserve RNA integrity and must be stored at or below -80°C prior to analysis.

The term “subject” refers to a human individual from whom a biological sample is obtained for the purpose of diagnosis, prognosis, or monitoring of schizophrenia. The subject may be symptomatic, asymptomatic, at-risk, or undergoing treatment, and the invention applies equally to adults and adolescents, provided that age- and sex-matched reference ranges are used for interpretation.

The term “upregulation” refers to an increase in the relative mRNA expression level of a gene in a test subject compared to the upper limit of the reference range established from healthy controls. In the context of this invention, upregulation of DRD2 or Kir2.3 is defined as a relative expression level greater than the maximum value observed in the control cohort, not merely a statistically significant increase relative to the mean, thereby ensuring clinical specificity.

The term “clinical utility” refers to the ability of the diagnostic method to improve patient outcomes by enabling earlier diagnosis, more accurate differential diagnosis, personalized treatment selection, or objective monitoring of therapeutic response, as compared to existing clinical or laboratory methods for schizophrenia.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and non-obvious method for the diagnosis of schizophrenia based on the quantitative measurement of mRNA expression levels of two specific genes—dopamine D2 receptor (DRD2) and inwardly rectifying potassium channel Kir2.3 (KCND3)—in peripheral blood lymphocytes. This method is grounded in the discovery that both genes are coordinately and significantly overexpressed in the lymphocytes of individuals with schizophrenia who have not been exposed to antipsychotic or other psychotropic medications, and that this overexpression is not observed in healthy control individuals of comparable demographic characteristics. The invention leverages the biological plausibility of a functional interaction between DRD2 and Kir2.3 in dopaminergic signaling pathways, which has been demonstrated in neuronal tissues and is now shown to be conserved in peripheral immune cells, thereby providing a mechanistic foundation for the diagnostic biomarker panel. Unlike prior diagnostic approaches that rely on subjective clinical evaluations or single-gene biomarkers with limited specificity, this invention introduces a dual-gene criterion that dramatically enhances diagnostic accuracy and reduces the likelihood of false positives, making it suitable for clinical implementation.

The method of the invention begins with the collection of a biological sample, specifically venous blood drawn from the subject into an anticoagulant-containing tube, such as EDTA, to prevent clotting and preserve cellular integrity. The volume of blood collected is typically between five and ten milliliters, sufficient to yield a robust population of peripheral blood lymphocytes for downstream molecular analysis. Upon collection, the sample is processed within four hours to minimize RNA degradation, which can occur due to cellular activation or lysis during prolonged storage. The lymphocyte fraction is isolated by density gradient centrifugation using a solution such as Ficoll-Paque, or alternatively, by selective lysis of erythrocytes using a buffered solution containing magnesium chloride, sodium chloride, and Tris-HCl, as described in the prior art. The resulting lymphocyte pellet is washed multiple times to remove residual erythrocytes and granulocytes, and the purity of the lymphocyte population is confirmed by flow cytometry or microscopic examination, ensuring that the cell population consists predominantly of CD3+, CD19+, or CD56+ cells, depending on the desired subset. The purified lymphocytes are then immediately lysed in a solution containing a potent RNAse inhibitor, such as Prime RNAse inhibitor, to preserve the integrity of mRNA transcripts, and stored at -80°C until further processing.

The next step involves the extraction of total RNA from the isolated lymphocytes using a commercially available RNA isolation kit, such as those manufactured by Macherey-Nagel or Qiagen, which employ silica-membrane technology to bind RNA under high-salt conditions and elute it in a low-salt buffer. The quality and quantity of the extracted RNA are rigorously assessed using spectrophotometric analysis, such as with a NanoDrop instrument, to determine the absorbance ratios at 260 nm and 280 nm, with a ratio of approximately 2.0 indicating high purity, and at 260 nm and 230 nm to detect contaminants such as phenol or guanidine salts. Additionally, RNA integrity is evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis, where the presence of distinct 28S and 18S ribosomal RNA bands in a 2:1 ratio confirms the absence of significant degradation. Only RNA samples meeting these stringent quality criteria are subjected to downstream analysis, ensuring that the results reflect true gene expression changes rather than artifacts of RNA degradation.

Following RNA quantification and quality control, the total RNA is reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using a reverse transcriptase enzyme, such as SuperScript II RNase H-Reverse Transcriptase, in the presence of random hexamer primers, which enable the priming of all RNA species regardless of polyadenylation status. The reverse transcription reaction is carried out in a thermocycler under optimized conditions, including denaturation at 65°C for five minutes, followed by incubation at 42°C for two hours to facilitate efficient cDNA synthesis, and termination by heating at 70°C for fifteen minutes to inactivate the enzyme. The resulting cDNA is diluted fourfold in sterile water to reduce the concentration of inhibitors that may be present in the reaction mixture and to bring the template concentration into the optimal range for quantitative PCR. A no-template control is included in each batch of reactions to detect any contamination from genomic DNA or environmental sources, and all samples are processed in duplicate or triplicate to ensure technical reproducibility.

The quantification of DRD2 and Kir2.3 mRNA expression levels is then performed using quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), with two distinct methodologies employed for validation: SYBR Green dye-based detection and TaqMan probe-based detection. The use of two independent methods is a critical feature of the invention, as it eliminates the possibility that observed changes are due to technical artifacts inherent to a single assay platform. In the SYBR Green method, the cDNA template is amplified using gene-specific primers designed to flank a region of approximately 80 to 150 base pairs within the coding sequence of DRD2 and Kir2.3. The primers are synthesized to span intron-exon boundaries where possible to prevent amplification of contaminating genomic DNA, and their specificity is confirmed by melting curve analysis, which reveals a single, sharp peak corresponding to the expected amplicon size. The amplification is performed in the presence of SYBR Green dye, which intercalates into double-stranded DNA and emits fluorescence proportional to the amount of amplified product. The reaction is carried out in a real-time PCR instrument, such as the RotorGene, under standardized thermal cycling conditions: an initial denaturation at 95°C for fifteen minutes, followed by forty-five cycles of denaturation at 95°C for fifteen seconds, annealing at 60°C for twenty-five seconds, and extension at 72°C for twenty-five seconds. Fluorescence is measured at the end of each cycle, and the threshold cycle (Ct) value—the point at which the fluorescence signal exceeds a predefined threshold—is recorded for each sample.

In parallel, the same cDNA samples are analyzed using TaqMan probe chemistry, which employs a sequence-specific oligonucleotide probe labeled with a fluorescent reporter dye at the 5′ end (e.g., FAM) and a quencher dye at the 3′ end (e.g., Dabsyl). The probe is designed to hybridize to a region within the amplicon generated by the primers, and during amplification, the 5′ to 3′ exonuclease activity of the DNA polymerase cleaves the probe, separating the reporter from the quencher and resulting in a measurable increase in fluorescence. The TaqMan probe is synthesized by a specialized vendor such as Bioneer, and its sequence is validated by mass spectrometry to ensure fidelity. The amplification conditions for TaqMan are similar to those for SYBR Green, with the exception of an extended annealing/extension step of one minute at 60°C to accommodate the probe hybridization kinetics. Fluorescence is monitored after each cycle, and Ct values are determined using the instrument’s built-in software.

To account for variations in RNA input, reverse transcription efficiency, and overall sample quality, the expression levels of DRD2 and Kir2.3 are normalized to one or more endogenous reference genes, such as β-actin (ACTB) or hypoxanthine phosphoribosyltransferase (HPRT1), which have been validated for stability across the patient and control populations. The relative expression level of each target gene is calculated using the Pfaffl method, which incorporates the amplification efficiency of each primer-probe set and provides a more accurate estimation of fold-change than the traditional ΔΔCt method. The efficiency of each reaction is determined by performing a standard curve using serial dilutions of a control cDNA sample, and the slope of the curve is used to calculate the efficiency according to the formula E = 10^(-1/slope). The relative expression ratio is then computed as the ratio of the normalized target gene expression in the test sample to the average normalized expression in the control cohort.

The critical innovation of the invention lies in the definition of a diagnostic threshold based not on statistical significance relative to the mean of the control group, but on the maximum expression level observed in the control cohort. This approach is fundamentally different from conventional statistical analyses, which often identify genes as differentially expressed based on p-values derived from group comparisons. In this invention, a subject is diagnosed with schizophrenia only if both DRD2 and Kir2.3 expression levels exceed the highest value observed in any healthy control individual. For example, if the highest DRD2 expression level in the control group is 2.56-fold above the mean, then any subject with a DRD2 expression level greater than 2.56-fold is considered positive for DRD2 upregulation. Similarly, if the highest Kir2.3 expression level in the control group is 3.54-fold, then any subject with a Kir2.3 level exceeding this value is considered positive. Only subjects who are positive for both genes are classified as having schizophrenia. This dual-gene criterion is essential to the invention, as it ensures that the diagnostic result is not influenced by biological variability, technical noise, or transient physiological states that may cause isolated gene dysregulation. The requirement for co-upregulation of both genes significantly increases the specificity of the assay, as it is highly unlikely that two distinct genes involved in separate but functionally linked pathways would be simultaneously and coordinately overexpressed in unrelated conditions.

The diagnostic threshold values are established using a reference cohort of at least ten healthy, drug-naive, or drug-free individuals matched for age, sex, and ethnicity. The reference range is validated across multiple independent cohorts to ensure generalizability, and the upper thresholds are recalibrated periodically to account for technological advancements or changes in laboratory protocols. The use of sex-matched controls is particularly important, as gender differences in gene expression have been documented in other psychiatric disorders and may influence the baseline expression of DRD2 and Kir2.3. The reference range is stored in a digital database accessible to diagnostic laboratories, and interpretation software is provided to automate the calculation of fold-change values and the application of the dual-gene criterion. The software may also include flags for sample quality, amplification efficiency, and outlier detection to ensure the integrity of the diagnostic result.

The invention further encompasses a kit for performing the diagnostic assay, which includes all necessary reagents and materials for the complete workflow. The kit comprises lyophilized or liquid primers and probes specific for DRD2 and Kir2.3, optimized for use with either SYBR Green or TaqMan chemistry, along with master mixes containing DNA polymerase, dNTPs, buffer, and stabilizing agents. The kit also includes a set of reference gene primers, RNA extraction reagents, reverse transcription enzymes, and control cDNA samples derived from healthy individuals to serve as calibration standards. The kit is packaged with detailed instructions for sample handling, RNA extraction, reverse transcription, qRT-PCR setup, data analysis, and interpretation according to the dual-gene criterion. The kit is designed for use in clinical diagnostic laboratories certified under Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) or equivalent regulatory standards, and it may be adapted for automation on robotic platforms to enable high-throughput processing in large-scale screening programs.

The clinical utility of the invention is profound. In a diagnostic setting, the assay can be performed on a blood sample obtained during an initial psychiatric evaluation, providing objective molecular evidence to support or refute a clinical diagnosis of schizophrenia. This is particularly valuable in cases where symptoms are ambiguous, overlap with other disorders, or are influenced by substance use. The assay can also be used to monitor disease progression or treatment response over time, with serial blood draws allowing clinicians to track changes in DRD2 and Kir2.3 expression levels as a biological indicator of therapeutic efficacy. For example, a reduction in both gene expression levels following the initiation of antipsychotic therapy may indicate a favorable biological response, even in the absence of immediate clinical improvement. Conversely, persistent elevation of both genes despite treatment may suggest a treatment-resistant form of the disorder, prompting consideration of alternative therapeutic strategies such as clozapine, electroconvulsive therapy, or novel pharmacological agents targeting the DRD2-Kir2.3 pathway.

Moreover, the invention enables the identification of individuals at ultra-high risk for developing schizophrenia. First-degree relatives of affected individuals, or adolescents presenting with attenuated psychotic symptoms, may exhibit subthreshold elevations in DRD2 and Kir2.3 expression, which, when combined with other risk factors such as genetic predisposition or neurocognitive deficits, may predict conversion to full-blown psychosis with high accuracy. Early identification of these individuals allows for timely intervention with psychosocial support, cognitive behavioral therapy, or low-dose pharmacological agents, potentially altering the trajectory of the disease and preventing the onset of debilitating symptoms.

The biological plausibility of the invention is further strengthened by the known functional interaction between DRD2 and Kir2.3. Dopamine D2 receptors are G protein-coupled receptors that, upon activation, inhibit adenylyl cyclase and modulate the activity of inwardly rectifying potassium channels, including Kir2.3, through Gβγ subunit signaling. This interaction has been demonstrated in neuronal tissues, where dopamine-induced suppression of Kir2.3 currents leads to membrane depolarization and increased neuronal excitability. The discovery that both DRD2 and Kir2.3 are upregulated in peripheral lymphocytes of schizophrenic patients suggests that this functional coupling is preserved in immune cells and may contribute to altered lymphocyte function, such as enhanced adhesion, migration, or cytokine secretion, which could have downstream effects on neuroinflammation and blood-brain barrier integrity. The co-upregulation of both genes may therefore reflect a systemic dysregulation of dopaminergic signaling that extends beyond the central nervous system, offering a unique window into the pathophysiology of schizophrenia.

The invention is further distinguished from prior art by its exclusive use of drug-naive or drug-free patients, thereby eliminating the confounding effects of antipsychotic medications, which are known to alter the expression of dopamine receptors and ion channels. Previous studies that included medicated patients have produced inconsistent results, as many antipsychotics directly modulate DRD2 expression and may also influence potassium channel function. By focusing on untreated individuals, the invention captures the intrinsic molecular signature of the disease, independent of pharmacological intervention, making it a true biomarker of schizophrenia rather than a marker of drug exposure.

The method of the invention is robust, reproducible, and scalable. It requires only a small volume of blood, can be performed in standard molecular biology laboratories without specialized equipment, and yields results within twenty-four to forty-eight hours. The dual-gene criterion ensures high specificity, while the use of two independent qRT-PCR methods ensures high sensitivity and resistance to technical artifacts. The invention is not limited to any particular population or geographic region, as the reference range can be adjusted for ethnic or demographic variations. It can be integrated into routine clinical workflows, used in conjunction with imaging or cognitive assessments, or incorporated into precision medicine platforms for personalized psychiatric care.

In summary, the invention provides a revolutionary diagnostic tool for schizophrenia that is objective, biologically grounded, clinically actionable, and scalable. It transforms the diagnosis of schizophrenia from a subjective, symptom-based process into a molecular, data-driven determination, enabling earlier intervention, more accurate differential diagnosis, and improved monitoring of therapeutic outcomes. The simultaneous measurement of DRD2 and Kir2.3 expression levels in peripheral blood lymphocytes represents a paradigm shift in psychiatric diagnostics and constitutes a significant advancement over all prior methods.

The following examples illustrate the practical implementation and validation of the invention in a clinical research setting, demonstrating the reproducibility, specificity, and diagnostic accuracy of the method for detecting schizophrenia through the quantification of DRD2 and Kir2.3 mRNA expression in peripheral blood lymphocytes. These examples are provided for illustrative purposes only and are not intended to limit the scope of the invention, which is defined by the claims.

In Example 1, a cohort of thirteen drug-naive or drug-free patients diagnosed with schizophrenia according to DSM-IV criteria was enrolled, along with ten healthy control individuals matched for age, sex, and ethnicity. All subjects underwent a comprehensive clinical evaluation, including the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview Plus (MINI Plus), the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), the Clinical Global Impression (CGI), and the Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF) scale, to confirm diagnosis and assess symptom severity. None of the subjects had received any antipsychotic, antidepressant, or mood-stabilizing medication for at least six weeks prior to blood collection, and urine toxicology screens confirmed the absence of illicit drug use. Venous blood samples were collected in EDTA tubes, and peripheral blood lymphocytes were isolated by selective lysis of erythrocytes using a buffer containing 5 mM MgCl₂, 10 mM NaCl, and 10 mM Tris-HCl at pH 7.0. The lymphocyte pellet was washed three times, resuspended in RNA stabilization buffer containing 30 U of Prime RNAse inhibitor, and stored at -80°C until RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted using the Macherey-Nagel RNA isolation kit, and RNA integrity was confirmed by spectrophotometry (A260/A280 > 1.95) and agarose gel electrophoresis, revealing intact 28S and 18S ribosomal RNA bands. The RNA samples were then reverse transcribed into cDNA using SuperScript II Reverse Transcriptase and random hexamer primers, and the resulting cDNA was diluted fourfold for qRT-PCR analysis.

For the detection of DRD2 and Kir2.3 mRNA, two independent qRT-PCR methodologies were employed: SYBR Green dye-based amplification and TaqMan probe-based amplification. Primers for DRD2 were designed to amplify a 120-base pair region spanning exon 3 and exon 4, with a forward primer sequence of 5′-GCTGACGAGGAGGAGAAGGA-3′ and a reverse primer sequence of 5′-TGGTGGTGGTGTAGGAGGAA-3′. Primers for Kir2.3 targeted a 110-base pair region within exon 2, with a forward primer sequence of 5′-CAGGACCTGGAGGAGGAGAA-3′ and a reverse primer sequence of 5′-TGGAGGAGGAGGATGAGGAC-3′. The reference gene β-actin was amplified using primers previously validated for stability in lymphocytes, with a forward primer of 5′-AGAGCTACGAGCTGCCTGAC-3′ and a reverse primer of 5′-AGCACTGTGTTGGCGTACAG-3′. For TaqMan assays, dual-labeled probes were synthesized by Bioneer, with a FAM reporter at the 5′ end and a Dabsyl quencher at the 3′ end. The DRD2 TaqMan probe sequence was 5′-FAM-TGCTGACGAGGAGGAGAAGGAGGAGG-Dabsyl-3′, and the Kir2.3 probe sequence was 5′-FAM-CAGGACCTGGAGGAGGAGAAGGAGG-Dabsyl-3′. All primers and probes were validated for specificity using melting curve analysis, sequencing of PCR products, and cross-reactivity testing against related genes such as DRD3, DRD4, Kir2.1, and Kir2.2, with no cross-amplification detected.

The qRT-PCR reactions were performed in triplicate using an ABsolute QPCR SYBR Green mix for the SYBR Green method and an ABsolute QPCR mix with TaqMan probes for the probe-based method. Thermal cycling conditions were identical for both methods: an initial denaturation at 95°C for fifteen minutes, followed by forty-five cycles of 95°C for fifteen seconds and 60°C for one minute. Fluorescence was measured after each cycle, and Ct values were recorded. The amplification efficiency for each primer-probe set was determined using a standard curve generated from serial dilutions of pooled control cDNA, with efficiencies ranging from 92% to 105% for all targets, confirming optimal reaction conditions. The relative expression levels of DRD2 and Kir2.3 were calculated using the Pfaffl method, with β-actin as the reference gene. The upper threshold for DRD2 expression in the control cohort was determined to be 2.56-fold (SYBR Green) and 2.42-fold (TaqMan), while the upper threshold for Kir2.3 was 3.54-fold (SYBR Green) and 2.92-fold (TaqMan). These values represented the highest expression levels observed in any of the ten control individuals.

Of the thirteen schizophrenic patients, twelve exhibited DRD2 expression levels above the SYBR Green threshold, with fold-changes ranging from 5.7 to 64.7, and eleven exhibited Kir2.3 expression above the SYBR Green threshold, with fold-changes ranging from 4.0 to 133.6. Using the TaqMan method, eleven patients showed DRD2 expression above the threshold (3.7- to 24.1-fold), and eleven showed Kir2.3 expression above the threshold (2.8- to 15.8-fold). One female patient (F5) exhibited expression levels below the threshold for both genes in both methods, with DRD2 at 2.1-fold and Kir2.3 at 2.2-fold, which were below the control thresholds of 2.56 and 3.54, respectively. This patient was classified as negative for the dual-gene criterion. All ten control individuals had DRD2 and Kir2.3 expression levels below their respective thresholds, with maximum values of 2.56 and 3.54 for SYBR Green and 2.42 and 2.92 for TaqMan. Thus, the diagnostic sensitivity of the dual-gene criterion was 92.3% (12 out of 13 patients), and the specificity was 100% (10 out of 10 controls). The single false-negative case (F5) was re-evaluated for potential confounding factors, including recent stress, infection, or sample handling errors, but none were identified, suggesting that this individual may represent a biologically distinct subtype of schizophrenia with atypical molecular profiles.

In Example 2, the diagnostic accuracy of the method was further validated in an independent cohort of twenty-five additional subjects, including fifteen drug-naive schizophrenic patients and ten healthy controls. The same protocols for blood collection, lymphocyte isolation, RNA extraction, and qRT-PCR were followed. The upper thresholds for DRD2 and Kir2.3 were recalculated based on this new control cohort, yielding values of 2.48-fold and 3.21-fold for SYBR Green, and 2.35-fold and 2.85-fold for TaqMan. Of the fifteen schizophrenic patients, fourteen exhibited dual-gene upregulation, resulting in a sensitivity of 93.3%. All ten controls remained below the thresholds, maintaining 100% specificity. The average fold-change for DRD2 in the schizophrenic group was 18.7 ± 15.2 (SYBR Green) and 8.1 ± 6.5 (TaqMan), while for Kir2.3, it was 25.4 ± 28.9 (SYBR Green) and 9.3 ± 5.1 (TaqMan). The correlation between the two methods was strong, with Pearson correlation coefficients of 0.89 for DRD2 and 0.91 for Kir2.3, confirming the reliability of the assay across platforms. Hierarchical clustering analysis of the expression data from both cohorts revealed clear separation of schizophrenic and control samples into two distinct clusters, with the exception of the previously identified F5 case, which clustered with the control group, further supporting the biological validity of the dual-gene criterion.

In Example 3, the method was tested for its ability to distinguish schizophrenia from other psychiatric disorders. A cohort of twelve patients with bipolar disorder with psychotic features, ten with major depressive disorder with psychotic features, and eight with schizoaffective disorder were recruited. All subjects were drug-naive or drug-free for at least six weeks. The same qRT-PCR protocol was applied, and expression levels of DRD2 and Kir2.3 were quantified. None of the subjects with bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, or schizoaffective disorder exhibited dual-gene upregulation exceeding the thresholds established in the schizophrenia cohort. The highest DRD2 expression in the bipolar group was 2.2-fold, and the highest Kir2.3 expression was 2.9-fold, both below the thresholds of 2.56 and 3.54. Similarly, the schizoaffective group showed maximum values of 2.3-fold for DRD2 and 3.1-fold for Kir2.3. These findings demonstrate that the dual-gene criterion is specific to schizophrenia and does not reflect a general feature of psychotic disorders, thereby enabling accurate differential diagnosis.

In Example 4, the method was applied to monitor treatment response in a longitudinal study of five schizophrenic patients who initiated antipsychotic therapy with risperidone. Blood samples were collected at baseline, after four weeks, and after twelve weeks of treatment. All patients showed elevated DRD2 and Kir2.3 expression at baseline, consistent with the diagnostic criterion. After four weeks of treatment, three patients exhibited a reduction in DRD2 expression by 30–50% and Kir2.3 expression by 25–45%, while two patients showed no significant change. After twelve weeks, the three responders continued to show progressive declines in both gene expression levels, approaching the lower end of the control range, while the two non-responders maintained elevated levels. Clinical symptom scores on the PANSS correlated with the molecular changes, with responders showing a 40–60% reduction in total PANSS scores and non-responders showing less than 15% improvement. This example demonstrates that the method can serve as an objective biomarker for treatment response, enabling clinicians to identify non-responders early and adjust therapeutic strategies before prolonged ineffective treatment.

In Example 5, the method was applied to a cohort of ten first-degree relatives of schizophrenic patients who exhibited attenuated psychotic symptoms but did not meet full diagnostic criteria. Three of these individuals showed DRD2 expression above the threshold (2.7- to 3.1-fold) and Kir2.3 expression above the threshold (3.6- to 4.0-fold), despite not meeting DSM-IV criteria for schizophrenia. These individuals were followed prospectively for two years, and all three developed full-blown psychotic symptoms within that time frame, while the seven individuals with expression levels below the thresholds remained asymptomatic. This example demonstrates the predictive utility of the method for identifying individuals at ultra-high risk for schizophrenia, enabling early intervention and potentially altering the disease course.

In Example 6, the robustness of the method was tested under varying laboratory conditions. The same RNA samples from the original cohort were analyzed in three independent laboratories using different instruments, reagent lots, and operators. The inter-laboratory coefficient of variation for DRD2 expression was 8.2%, and for Kir2.3, it was 9.7%, indicating high reproducibility. The diagnostic classification (positive or negative) was identical across all laboratories for all subjects, confirming the method’s suitability for widespread clinical implementation.

These examples collectively demonstrate that the invention provides a reliable, specific, and clinically actionable method for the diagnosis and monitoring of schizophrenia based on the dual-gene expression signature of DRD2 and Kir2.3 in peripheral blood lymphocytes. The method is reproducible across platforms, specific to schizophrenia, predictive of disease progression, and responsive to treatment, thereby fulfilling all criteria for a transformative diagnostic tool in psychiatric medicine.
