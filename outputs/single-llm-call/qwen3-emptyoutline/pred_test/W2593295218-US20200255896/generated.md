# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of noninvasive prenatal testing, specifically to a highly refined method for detecting fetal chromosomal aneuploidies using cell-free DNA extracted from maternal plasma. The invention encompasses a comprehensive, automated, and bioinformatically optimized laboratory-developed test that significantly enhances the accuracy, specificity, and clinical reliability of prenatal screening by minimizing false-positive results arising from both technical artifacts and biological confounders. The method integrates precise sample processing, high-throughput next-generation sequencing, advanced GC-content bias correction, proprietary statistical normalization, and genome-wide anomaly detection to distinguish true fetal aneuploidies from maternal genomic abnormalities such as microduplications, global copy number variations, and mosaicism. This invention is particularly suited for clinical diagnostic laboratories seeking to implement a second-generation noninvasive prenatal screening platform with substantially improved positive predictive value for trisomies 21, 18, and 13, as well as for sex chromosome aneuploidies and select microdeletion syndromes. The system is designed to operate within existing clinical workflows, utilizing standard blood collection tubes, automated liquid handling systems, and commercially available sequencing platforms, while introducing novel computational algorithms that enable unambiguous classification of fetal chromosomal status without reliance on external reference databases or population-based normalization models. The invention further includes procedural safeguards to identify and flag maternal genomic anomalies that could otherwise be misinterpreted as fetal aneuploidy, thereby reducing unnecessary invasive procedures and associated clinical risks.

## BACKGROUND

Noninvasive prenatal screening based on the analysis of cell-free fetal DNA in maternal plasma has revolutionized the field of prenatal diagnostics by offering a highly sensitive and specific alternative to traditional serum-based screening and invasive diagnostic procedures such as amniocentesis and chorionic villus sampling. Prior methodologies, including maternal serum analyte profiling and nuchal translucency measurements, suffered from relatively high false-positive rates, resulting in significant psychological distress for expectant parents and an unnecessary burden of invasive diagnostic interventions, each carrying a nontrivial risk of pregnancy loss. The advent of massively parallel sequencing technologies enabled the quantification of fetal DNA fragments derived from all chromosomes, allowing for the detection of chromosomal imbalances through relative dosage analysis. Early implementations of this approach, commonly referred to as massive parallel shotgun sequencing, demonstrated improved performance over conventional screening but were still limited by inherent technical biases, particularly in GC-rich chromosomal regions such as chromosomes 13 and 18, where sequencing efficiency and read distribution were disproportionately affected. These biases introduced variability in the measurement of chromosome representation, leading to overlapping Z-score distributions between affected and unaffected pregnancies and resulting in indeterminate or false-positive outcomes. Furthermore, biological factors such as confined placental mosaicism, vanishing twin syndrome, maternal chromosomal duplications, and circulating tumor DNA were recognized as potential sources of discordant results, yet were not systematically evaluated or accounted for in most commercial assays. As a consequence, the positive predictive value of these first-generation tests remained suboptimal, particularly for trisomy 13 and trisomy 18, where prevalence is low and even minor false-positive rates substantially erode diagnostic confidence. For example, in populations with a trisomy 13 prevalence of approximately 1 in 1,500, a test with 99.9% specificity still yields a positive predictive value below 55%, meaning that nearly half of all positive results are false alarms. This limitation has led to persistent clinical uncertainty, inconsistent patient counseling, and variable adoption of noninvasive screening across healthcare systems. Moreover, the detection of sex chromosome aneuploidies and microdeletion syndromes remained particularly challenging due to lower fetal DNA fractions, reduced sequence coverage, and the absence of robust normalization strategies for sex chromosomes. Existing methods often relied on absolute read counts or simplistic thresholds that failed to account for biological variability in fetal fraction, maternal background noise, or technical noise introduced during library preparation and sequencing. Additionally, no prior system provided a standardized, automated, and validated protocol for the simultaneous detection of maternal genomic abnormalities that mimic fetal aneuploidy, such as large-scale maternal duplications or global copy number changes arising from benign conditions like uterine fibroids. As a result, many laboratories were forced to interpret ambiguous results on a case-by-case basis, often requiring additional maternal testing or follow-up sequencing, which delayed clinical decision-making and increased costs. There remains a critical unmet need in the field for a noninvasive prenatal screening method that not only achieves near-perfect separation between affected and unaffected pregnancies but also actively identifies and excludes confounding biological and technical artifacts that compromise diagnostic accuracy. The present invention fulfills this need by integrating end-to-end automation, proprietary bioinformatic normalization, and genome-wide anomaly detection into a single, clinically validated platform that delivers unprecedented specificity and interpretive clarity.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel, automated, and highly accurate method for noninvasive prenatal screening of fetal chromosomal aneuploidies using cell-free DNA isolated from maternal plasma. The method comprises a series of integrated steps designed to maximize analytical sensitivity and specificity while systematically eliminating sources of false-positive results that have historically undermined the clinical utility of prior noninvasive screening technologies. The invention begins with the collection of maternal whole blood in specialized stabilization tubes, followed by automated plasma isolation using a robotic liquid handling system that performs sequential centrifugation and transfer steps to minimize cellular contamination and ensure consistent cfDNA yield. Cell-free DNA is then extracted from a standardized volume of plasma using a proprietary high-yield purification chemistry, which enhances recovery of low-molecular-weight fetal fragments while minimizing co-isolation of maternal genomic DNA. The extracted DNA is converted into sequencing-ready libraries using a standardized PCR-based protocol that incorporates a unique 10-base pair molecular barcode for sample multiplexing, enabling high-throughput processing with minimal cross-contamination. Libraries are quantified using a fluorescent dye-based assay, normalized to a precise concentration, and pooled in predefined batches for sequencing on a next-generation sequencing platform. Sequencing is performed using single-read 36-base pair reads with a minimum depth of 9 million reads per sample, ensuring sufficient statistical power for chromosome-wide analysis. The raw sequencing data is processed through a proprietary bioinformatics pipeline that performs binning of reads into non-overlapping 50-kilobase genomic segments, followed by autosomal normalization to correct for sample-to-sample variation in total read count. A critical innovation of the invention is the application of a locally weighted polynomial regression (LOESS) algorithm, calibrated using reference data from the human genome assembly hg19, to correct for GC-content bias across all chromosomes, with particular emphasis on GC-rich regions such as chromosomes 13 and 18. Following GC correction, the normalized read counts are subjected to a principal component analysis to remove high-order technical artifacts unrelated to chromosomal dosage. Chromosome representation is then calculated as the proportion of reads mapped to each autosome relative to the total autosomal read count, and a Z-score is computed for each chromosome using a median absolute deviation-based normalization derived from a training set of over five thousand previously analyzed samples. This statistical approach ensures that the Z-score distribution for unaffected pregnancies remains tightly clustered around zero, while affected pregnancies exhibit clear, statistically significant elevations. A key feature of the invention is the implementation of a dual-threshold reporting system: samples with Z-scores greater than eight are classified as positive for aneuploidy, samples with Z-scores less than or equal to four are classified as negative, and samples with Z-scores between four and eight are flagged for manual review. This intermediate zone is not reported as positive or negative but is instead subjected to a genome-wide ideogram analysis to detect localized chromosomal duplications that may reflect maternal microduplications rather than fetal aneuploidy. The invention further includes a proprietary algorithm for fetal fraction estimation in both male and female fetuses: for male pregnancies, fetal fraction is calculated based on the overrepresentation of Y-chromosome sequences relative to a background established from female samples; for female pregnancies, a novel, internally validated model is employed that leverages underrepresentation of the X chromosome relative to a run-specific median derived from female reference samples. The fetal fraction estimate is not used to adjust the Z-score calculation but serves as a quality control metric to exclude samples with insufficient fetal DNA content. The invention further incorporates a genome-wide scan for global copy number abnormalities, wherein elevated or depressed Z-scores across multiple chromosomes are automatically flagged as potential indicators of maternal conditions such as uterine fibroids or malignancies that shed DNA into the circulation. In cases where a positive result for trisomy 21, 18, or 13 is detected, the entire genome is reviewed to ensure that the signal is confined to the target chromosome and not part of a broader pattern of chromosomal imbalance. The method further includes a validated protocol for the detection of sex chromosome aneuploidies, wherein fetal fraction estimates derived from both X and Y chromosomes are plotted against each other to establish a decision boundary that distinguishes true fetal aneuploidies from maternal mosaicism or technical noise. Microdeletion syndromes, such as DiGeorge syndrome, are detected by aggregating read counts within predefined genomic intervals and applying the same Z-score methodology used for whole-chromosome analysis. The invention is further characterized by the implementation of a clinical reporting protocol that mandates review of chromosome ideograms for all intermediate and positive results, thereby preventing the reporting of false-positive results caused by maternal microduplications or global copy number variants. The method has been validated across more than ten thousand clinical samples and demonstrates 100% discrimination between affected and unaffected pregnancies for trisomies 21, 18, and 13 when the dual-threshold system and genome-wide review are employed. The positive predictive value for trisomy 21 exceeds 98%, for trisomy 18 exceeds 92%, and for trisomy 13 exceeds 69%, with false-positive rates reduced to less than 0.2% in clinical practice. The invention thus represents a substantial advancement over prior noninvasive prenatal screening methods by combining technical precision, biological insight, and clinical safeguards into a single, automated, and clinically validated system that significantly improves diagnostic confidence and reduces unnecessary invasive procedures.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention describes a comprehensive, end-to-end method for noninvasive prenatal screening of fetal chromosomal aneuploidies that integrates automated sample processing, high-throughput sequencing, and proprietary bioinformatic analysis to achieve unprecedented levels of diagnostic accuracy. The method is designed to be implemented in a clinical diagnostic laboratory setting and is fully compatible with existing laboratory infrastructure, including automated liquid handlers, next-generation sequencing platforms, and standard molecular biology reagents. The process begins with the collection of maternal whole blood in two 10 mL Cell-Free DNA BCT blood collection tubes, which are stored and transported at ambient temperature to preserve the integrity of cell-free DNA. Upon receipt at the laboratory, samples are processed within four days of collection to minimize degradation and ensure optimal cfDNA yield. Plasma isolation is performed using a Tecan EVO 200 liquid handler, which automates a two-step centrifugation protocol: an initial centrifugation at 2,500 × g for 10 minutes at 22°C to pellet cellular components, followed by a second centrifugation at 3,200 × g for 20 minutes at 22°C to remove residual cellular debris. The supernatant is then transferred to a final 15 mL conical tube, ensuring that the plasma fraction is free of contaminating cells that could contribute genomic DNA to the analysis. From each sample, four milliliters of plasma are used for cell-free DNA extraction, which is performed using a proprietary high-yield chemistry based on DynaMax magnetic bead technology. This extraction method is optimized to recover the full spectrum of cell-free DNA fragments, including those of fetal origin, which are typically shorter than maternal fragments. The extraction process is automated using a Kingfisher Flex Purification System, which ensures consistent binding, washing, and elution conditions across all samples, thereby minimizing batch-to-batch variability. The extracted DNA is quantified using the Quant-It PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit, which provides a highly sensitive and accurate measurement of double-stranded DNA concentration. Following quantification, sequencing libraries are prepared using the NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina, which includes end-repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation steps, followed by limited-cycle PCR amplification. A critical innovation in library preparation is the use of a universal forward primer and a reverse primer containing a 10-base pair molecular barcode, which allows for the multiplexing of up to 12 samples per sequencing lane while enabling accurate sample tracking and de-multiplexing post-sequencing. The PCR amplification is performed under standardized conditions: an initial denaturation at 98°C for 30 seconds, followed by 10 cycles of denaturation at 98°C for 10 seconds, annealing at 65°C for 30 seconds, and extension at 72°C for 30 seconds, with a final extension at 72°C for 5 minutes. The use of a low cycle number minimizes PCR bias and ensures that the relative representation of genomic regions is preserved. After amplification, the libraries are purified using Agencourt AMPure XP beads at a 1:1 bead-to-sample ratio, which effectively removes unincorporated primers, nucleotides, and short fragments that could interfere with sequencing. The purified libraries are quantified again using the PicoGreen assay and normalized to a concentration of 2 nanomolar prior to pooling. Each pool contains 12 samples, and a 5% PhiX Control library is added to each pool to provide a reference for base calling and to improve sequencing quality metrics. The pooled libraries are denatured and diluted to a concentration of 15 picomolar and loaded onto high-output flow cells using the Illumina cBot system, which facilitates clonal amplification of DNA fragments on the surface of the flow cell. Sequencing is performed on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform using single-read 36-base pair sequencing, followed by 10 cycles of index sequencing to read the molecular barcodes. A minimum of nine million reads per sample is required to ensure sufficient statistical power for chromosome-wide analysis, and all reads are required to have a quality score greater than 30 to ensure high base-calling accuracy. The raw sequencing data is streamed to a high-capacity Isilon server, where it is automatically processed by a proprietary bioinformatics pipeline. The pipeline begins with alignment of reads to the human reference genome (hg19), followed by binning of aligned reads into 50-kilobase non-overlapping windows across all autosomes and sex chromosomes. Each bin is assigned a read count, which is then scaled by the total number of autosomal reads in the sample to correct for differences in sequencing depth. This normalization step ensures that variations in total read yield do not artificially influence chromosome representation. The next critical step is GC-content correction, which is performed using a locally weighted polynomial regression (LOESS) algorithm calibrated on a reference set of genomic regions with known GC content derived from the hg19 assembly. This correction is applied independently to each sample and accounts for the fact that GC-rich regions, such as chromosomes 13 and 18, are inherently underrepresented in sequencing data due to lower efficiency of library preparation and sequencing. The LOESS algorithm fits a smooth curve to the relationship between GC content and read count across the genome, allowing for the prediction and correction of expected biases. Following GC correction, a principal component analysis is applied to the normalized data to identify and remove high-order technical artifacts, such as batch effects, library preparation inconsistencies, or sequencing platform drift. The corrected read counts are then used to calculate chromosome representation for each autosome as the ratio of reads mapped to that chromosome divided by the sum of reads mapped to all autosomes. This representation value is then converted into a Z-score using a robust statistical model based on the median absolute deviation (MAD) of a training set consisting of 5,406 previously analyzed samples from unaffected pregnancies. The use of MAD instead of standard deviation ensures that outliers do not skew the baseline distribution, resulting in a more stable and reproducible reference. The Z-score for each chromosome is calculated as the difference between the sample’s chromosome representation and the median representation of the training set, divided by the median absolute deviation of the training set. This approach ensures that the distribution of Z-scores for unaffected pregnancies is tightly centered around zero, with minimal variance. A key innovation of the invention is the implementation of a dual-threshold reporting system: samples with Z-scores less than or equal to four are classified as negative for aneuploidy, samples with Z-scores greater than eight are classified as positive, and samples with Z-scores between four and eight are designated as intermediate and subject to manual review. This threshold system is not arbitrary but is derived from empirical data demonstrating that no unaffected pregnancy in the training or validation sets exhibited a Z-score above four, and no affected pregnancy exhibited a Z-score below eight, following GC correction and statistical smoothing. This complete separation between affected and unaffected populations is a defining feature of the invention and represents a substantial improvement over prior methods, which often exhibited overlapping distributions and required probabilistic interpretation. For samples classified as positive or intermediate, a genome-wide ideogram is generated, which displays the normalized read count for each 50-kilobase bin across all chromosomes as a continuous plot resembling a microarray profile. This ideogram allows for the visual inspection of whether the elevated Z-score is confined to an entire chromosome, as would be expected in a true fetal trisomy, or whether it is restricted to a small region, which may indicate a maternal microduplication. In cases where a localized elevation is observed, maternal blood samples are requested for microarray analysis to confirm the presence of a maternal genomic duplication. The invention further includes a proprietary algorithm for fetal fraction estimation, which is critical for assessing sample quality and for the detection of sex chromosome aneuploidies. For male fetuses, fetal fraction is calculated based on the overrepresentation of Y-chromosome reads relative to a background level established from a training set of over 3,500 female samples, including non-pregnant women and women carrying female fetuses. The background level is defined as the mean Y-chromosome read count in these samples, and the slope of the relationship between Y-chromosome read count and fetal fraction is determined using linear regression. For female fetuses, where Y-chromosome reads are absent, a novel model is employed that calculates fetal fraction based on the underrepresentation of the X chromosome relative to the median X-chromosome representation observed in female samples on the same sequencing run. The formula used is FF = 2 × (1 − Xi/Xf), where Xi is the median X-chromosome representation for the sample and Xf is the median X-chromosome representation across all female samples on the run. This model is validated against known fetal sex and fetal fraction estimates derived from other methods and demonstrates high concordance. The fetal fraction estimate is not used to adjust the Z-score calculation but serves as a quality control metric: samples with fetal fraction below 4% are flagged as low quality and are not reported. For sex chromosome aneuploidy detection, the fetal fraction estimates derived from the X and Y chromosomes are plotted against each other, and a decision boundary is established using a training set of 649 samples with known karyotypes. This boundary distinguishes true fetal aneuploidies such as 45,X, 47,XXX, 47,XXY, and 47,XYY from maternal mosaicism or technical noise. The invention further enables the detection of microdeletion syndromes, such as DiGeorge syndrome (22q11.2 deletion), by aggregating read counts within predefined genomic intervals corresponding to the region of interest and applying the same Z-score methodology used for whole-chromosome analysis. The boundaries of these regions are defined based on published cytogenetic maps and validated against known positive and negative samples. In addition to detecting fetal aneuploidies, the invention includes a genome-wide scan for global copy number abnormalities. If a positive result for trisomy 21, 18, or 13 is detected, the entire genome is automatically reviewed for concordant elevations or depressions in Z-scores across multiple chromosomes. If multiple chromosomes exhibit Z-scores greater than +4 or less than −8, the result is flagged as indicative of a maternal global copy number abnormality, such as a large uterine fibroid or malignancy, and is not reported as a fetal aneuploidy. This feature is critical for preventing false-positive results caused by maternal conditions that shed DNA into the circulation. The method further includes a clinical reporting protocol that requires manual review of all intermediate and positive results by a certified laboratory director or genetic counselor, who examines the chromosome ideograms, fetal fraction estimates, and genome-wide Z-score patterns before issuing a final report. This multi-layered approach ensures that false-positive results arising from maternal microduplications, global copy number changes, or technical artifacts are systematically identified and excluded from clinical reporting. The invention has been validated across more than ten thousand clinical samples and demonstrates 100% sensitivity and specificity for trisomies 21, 18, and 13 when the full protocol is followed. The positive predictive value for trisomy 21 exceeds 98%, for trisomy 18 exceeds 92%, and for trisomy 13 exceeds 69%, with false-positive rates reduced to less than 0.2%. The method is fully automated from sample receipt to result generation, with minimal manual intervention required, making it suitable for high-throughput clinical laboratories. The invention thus represents a transformative advancement in noninvasive prenatal screening by combining technical precision, biological insight, and clinical safeguards into a single, validated, and scalable platform that significantly improves diagnostic accuracy and reduces unnecessary invasive procedures.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1: Assay Development

The development of the noninvasive prenatal screening method described herein was initiated with the goal of achieving complete separation between affected and unaffected pregnancies through a combination of technical optimization and bioinformatic innovation. The process began with the acquisition of a diverse set of maternal plasma samples, including 3,750 samples from Sequenom, 165 from Precision Medicine, and 10 from consented volunteers, representing a broad spectrum of gestational ages, maternal ages, and fetal chromosomal statuses. These samples were processed using a standardized protocol that included automated plasma isolation, cfDNA extraction using DynaMax chemistry, and library preparation with the NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit. Initial sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform using single-read 36-base pair sequencing, with a target of at least 9 million reads per sample. The raw data was analyzed using a preliminary bioinformatics pipeline that performed binning of reads into 50-kilobase windows and calculated chromosome representation as the proportion of reads mapped to each chromosome relative to the total autosomal read count. Early analysis revealed that chromosome 13 and chromosome 18 exhibited significant underrepresentation in sequencing data due to their high GC content, resulting in Z-scores that overlapped between affected and unaffected pregnancies. For example, in a subset of 150 samples with confirmed trisomy 13, over 60% exhibited Z-scores below 4 when using raw, uncorrected data, leading to a high rate of false-negative results. To address this, a GC-content correction algorithm was developed using a locally weighted polynomial regression (LOESS) model calibrated on the hg19 reference genome. The model was trained on a set of 5,406 samples from unaffected pregnancies to establish the expected relationship between GC content and read count across the genome. After applying the correction, the Z-scores for trisomy 13 samples increased dramatically, with all 150 samples now exhibiting Z-scores above 8, while no unaffected sample exceeded a Z-score of 4. Similar improvements were observed for trisomy 18, where the proportion of samples with Z-scores below 4 decreased from 45% to 0% following GC correction. The use of median absolute deviation (MAD) instead of standard deviation for Z-score calculation further enhanced the robustness of the system by minimizing the influence of outliers. The next phase of development focused on the identification of optimal thresholds for clinical reporting. A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed on a validation set of 552 samples, including 32 with confirmed trisomies and 520 unaffected pregnancies. The analysis demonstrated that a Z-score threshold of 4 for negative results and 8 for positive results provided complete separation between the two groups, with no overlap. This dual-threshold system was adopted as the foundation for clinical reporting. To further refine the method, a genome-wide ideogram visualization tool was developed to display normalized read counts for each 50-kilobase bin across all chromosomes. This tool enabled the identification of localized chromosomal duplications that were not apparent from chromosome-level Z-scores alone. During routine testing, two cases were identified with intermediate Z-scores of 5.11 and 6.93 for chromosomes 21 and 18, respectively. Upon review of the ideograms, these signals were found to be confined to small regions of the respective chromosomes, suggesting a maternal origin. Maternal blood samples were obtained and analyzed using microarray technology, which confirmed the presence of microduplications on chromosome 21 and chromosome 18. These findings led to the implementation of a mandatory review protocol for all intermediate and positive results, requiring manual inspection of the ideogram to distinguish fetal from maternal abnormalities. The fetal fraction estimation algorithm was developed using a training set of 3,589 samples with known fetal sex. For male fetuses, the relationship between Y-chromosome read count and fetal fraction was modeled using linear regression, and a background threshold was established based on the mean Y-chromosome read count in female samples. For female fetuses, a novel model was developed based on X-chromosome underrepresentation, which was validated against known fetal sex and fetal fraction estimates derived from other methods. The model demonstrated high concordance and was incorporated into the reporting system as a quality control metric. The final version of the assay was validated across 1,288 frozen plasma samples from unaffected pregnancies, which confirmed that the Z-score distribution remained tightly clustered around zero, with no sample exceeding a Z-score of 4. The entire process was automated using robotic liquid handlers and standardized reagent protocols, ensuring reproducibility and scalability. The final assay was capable of processing over 1,000 samples per week with minimal manual intervention, and the results were consistently accurate across multiple sequencing runs and laboratory personnel. The development phase concluded with the establishment of a comprehensive quality control framework, including daily control samples, batch-to-batch consistency checks, and real-time monitoring of sequencing quality metrics. This rigorous development process laid the foundation for the clinical implementation of the method and ensured that the assay met the highest standards of analytical validity.

### Example 2: Assay Verification and Validation

The verification and validation of the noninvasive prenatal screening method were conducted in a phased, multi-center approach to ensure robustness, reproducibility, and clinical relevance. Verification was performed using a set of 2,085 samples that included known aneuploid pregnancies and unaffected controls. This set contained 69 samples with trisomy 21, 20 with trisomy 18, 17 with trisomy 13, and 1,979 unaffected pregnancies. All samples were processed using the fully automated protocol described in Example 1, including plasma isolation, cfDNA extraction, library preparation, sequencing, and bioinformatic analysis. The results demonstrated complete separation between affected and unaffected pregnancies: no unaffected sample exhibited a Z-score greater than 4, and no affected sample exhibited a Z-score less than 8 following GC correction and statistical smoothing. This 100% discrimination was consistent across all chromosomes and was not influenced by gestational age, maternal weight, or fetal sex. The sensitivity for trisomy 21 was 100% (69/69), for trisomy 18 was 100% (20/20), and for trisomy 13 was 100% (17/17). The specificity was 100% (1,979/1,979), with no false-positive results observed. The positive predictive value for trisomy 21 was 100%, as all positive results were confirmed by invasive testing. The validation phase expanded the sample set to include 552 additional samples, including 21 with trisomy 21, 10 with trisomy 18, 1 with trisomy 13, and 1 with Turner syndrome (45,X). Again, all affected samples exhibited Z-scores greater than 8, and all unaffected samples exhibited Z-scores less than or equal to 4. The inclusion of the Turner syndrome case demonstrated the ability of the method to detect sex chromosome aneuploidies with high accuracy. A separate validation cohort of 115 twin gestations was analyzed, including 10 with trisomy 21, 4 with trisomy 18, and 13 with trisomy 13. All affected twin pregnancies exhibited Z-scores greater than 11, while all unaffected twins exhibited Z-scores below 4, indicating that the method maintained high performance even in the context of multiple gestations. To assess inter-laboratory concordance, 100 consented volunteer samples were split between the developing laboratory and Sequenom, a leading commercial provider of noninvasive prenatal screening. All 100 results were concordant, including the single case of trisomy 21, which was correctly identified by both laboratories. Fetal sex determination was evaluated in 372 samples, with results compared to those obtained using the Sequenom Maternity21 Plus assay. Concordance was observed in 371 of 372 samples (99.7%), with the single discordant case having a fetal fraction of 2.75%, below the reporting threshold of 4%. This case was correctly flagged as low quality and would not have been reported in clinical practice. The detection of microdeletions was validated using a single case of DiGeorge syndrome (22q11.2 deletion), which exhibited a Z-score of −7 for the targeted region, consistent with a deletion. The result was confirmed by amniocentesis, demonstrating the method’s ability to detect sub-chromosomal abnormalities. The genome-wide scan for global copy number abnormalities was validated using a case of trisomy 21 with a Z-score of 21, which upon review revealed elevated Z-scores for chromosomes 3 and 9 and depressed Z-scores for chromosomes 4, 6, and 11. Maternal imaging confirmed the presence of large uterine fibroids, and the result was correctly classified as a maternal artifact rather than a fetal aneuploidy. The method was also tested for its ability to detect mosaic trisomy 21, with three cases identified in the validation set. One case had a Z-score of 3.57, which was below the reporting threshold, but a second sample submitted after clinical consultation had a Z-score of 4.22, and a third had a Z-score of 5.57. Amniocentesis confirmed mosaic trisomy 21 with ratios of 7:29, 15:5, and an unquantified ratio in the third case, demonstrating that the method can detect mosaicism even when the fetal fraction is low. The validation studies also included analysis of sex chromosome aneuploidies, with seven cases of Turner syndrome, five cases of 47,XXX, two cases of 47,XXY, and one case of 47,XYY. All cases were correctly identified, with the exception of one case of maternal mosaicism for 45,X, which was flagged as a suspected maternal abnormality due to a fetal fraction estimate exceeding 50%. This case was not reported as a fetal aneuploidy, demonstrating the method’s ability to distinguish maternal from fetal origins. The validation data confirmed that the dual-threshold system, GC correction, genome-wide ideogram review, and fetal fraction estimation collectively achieved 100% discrimination between affected and unaffected pregnancies for trisomies 21, 18, and 13, with no false-positive results attributable to technical artifacts or biological confounders. The method was shown to be highly reproducible across multiple sequencing runs, operators, and laboratory locations. The validation phase established the analytical validity of the assay and provided the foundation for its clinical implementation.

### Example 3: Clinical Implementations

The clinical implementation of the noninvasive prenatal screening method described herein was initiated following the successful completion of verification and validation studies, with the method being deployed in a high-volume clinical reference laboratory serving patients across multiple geographic regions. The assay was offered to pregnant women beginning at 10 weeks’ gestation, with the majority of samples received between 10 and 15 weeks. Over the first 10,000 clinical samples, 180 (1.8%) yielded abnormal results, including 103 positive for trisomy 21, 36 for trisomy 18, 21 for trisomy 13, 9 for Turner syndrome, 5 for 47,XXX, 2 for 47,XXY, 1 for 47,XYY, 1 for DiGeorge microdeletion, and 2 with dual abnormalities. Of the 103 trisomy 21-positive results, 87 had complete follow-up data, with 86 confirmed by invasive testing or physical examination at delivery, yielding a positive predictive value of 98.8%. The single false-positive result was associated with a maternal global copy number abnormality involving multiple chromosomes and was correctly identified and excluded from reporting due to the genome-wide review protocol. Of the 36 trisomy 18-positive results, 30 had complete follow-up, with 28 confirmed as true fetal aneuploidies, yielding a positive predictive value of 93.3%. One false-positive result was associated with a twin gestation in which one fetus had a coccygeal mass, a known source of chromosomal instability, and was appropriately excluded. Of the 21 trisomy 13-positive results, 17 had complete follow-up, with 9 confirmed as true aneuploidies and 4 classified as false-positive, yielding a positive predictive value of 69%. Two of the false-positive cases were linked to maternal microduplications, one to a suspected vanishing twin, and one to an unexplained source. The detection of maternal microduplications was a critical clinical innovation: of the 10,000 samples, 25 cases (0.25%) were identified with intermediate Z-scores suggestive of localized duplications, and 22 of these were confirmed on maternal microarray analysis to represent maternal genomic abnormalities. These cases would have been reported as true fetal aneuploidies under prior methodologies, leading to unnecessary invasive procedures and potential pregnancy terminations. The genome-wide scan for global copy number abnormalities identified six additional cases with elevated Z-scores for chromosomes 13, 18, or 21 accompanied by concordant abnormalities on other chromosomes, all of which were later confirmed to be associated with maternal uterine fibroids or other benign conditions. These cases were correctly classified as non-fetal and were not reported as positive. The method also demonstrated high accuracy in sex chromosome aneuploidy detection: of the seven Turner syndrome cases with follow-up, six were confirmed as true fetal aneuploidies, and one was identified as maternal mosaicism and excluded from reporting, yielding a positive predictive value of 85.7%. The two cases of 47,XXX were both confirmed, and the single case of 47,XXY was confirmed by amniocentesis. The single case of 47,XYY lacked follow-up but was consistent with the expected pattern. The detection of the DiGeorge microdeletion was confirmed by amniocentesis, demonstrating the method’s utility for sub-chromosomal analysis. The fetal fraction estimation system successfully flagged 63 samples (0.59%) as low quality due to fetal fraction below 4%, preventing the reporting of unreliable results. An additional 31 samples (0.29%) were excluded due to failure to meet quality metrics, including insufficient read depth, poor sequencing quality, or library preparation failure. The overall failure rate of 0.88% was comparable to or lower than that of commercial assays. The clinical implementation also revealed that 10% of women with positive trisomy 21 results elected to terminate their pregnancies without invasive confirmation, while 30% continued their pregnancies without any further testing, underscoring the importance of pre-test counseling and the need for clinicians to recognize that this method remains a screening test. The method was found to be highly reproducible across multiple operators, sequencing runs, and laboratory locations, with no significant variation in performance. The automated nature of the system allowed for high throughput, with over 1,000 samples processed per week, and the turnaround time from sample receipt to result delivery was consistently less than seven days. The integration of the genome-wide ideogram review and maternal anomaly detection protocols significantly reduced the number of false-positive results, thereby improving diagnostic confidence and reducing unnecessary invasive procedures. The method has since been adopted by multiple clinical laboratories and has demonstrated sustained performance over more than 50,000 clinical samples, with no recurrence of false-positive results attributable to maternal microduplications or global copy number abnormalities. The clinical implementation of this invention has transformed the standard of care for noninvasive prenatal screening, providing clinicians and patients with a reliable, accurate, and interpretable test that minimizes the risk of misdiagnosis and supports informed decision-making.

## EQUIVALENTS

While the present invention has been described in detail with reference to specific embodiments, it is understood that numerous modifications and equivalents may be made without departing from the scope of the invention. For example, the use of alternative sequencing platforms, such as the Illumina NovaSeq or NextSeq systems, may be substituted for the HiSeq 2500 without altering the fundamental principles of the method, provided that the read length, depth, and quality metrics are maintained at equivalent or superior levels.
