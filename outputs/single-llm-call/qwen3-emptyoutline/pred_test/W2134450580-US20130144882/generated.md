# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The field of molecular diagnostics and computational biology has long sought methods to enhance the accuracy and reliability of disease classification using gene expression data derived from high-throughput technologies such as microarrays and RNA sequencing. Traditional approaches to analyzing such data have primarily relied on identifying individual genes that exhibit statistically significant differential expression between disease and control states. These methods, while foundational, suffer from inherent limitations: they operate under the assumption that disease-relevant biological signals are concentrated in a small number of highly differentially expressed genes, and they largely disregard the systemic, cooperative nature of gene regulation and protein interaction networks. In reality, many disease-associated biological processes are orchestrated not by single genes with large expression changes, but by coordinated, subtle shifts in the expression of functionally related gene groups—genes that individually may show only marginal or statistically insignificant alterations, yet collectively contribute to a robust phenotypic signature. These subtle, coordinated changes are often dismissed as noise in conventional analyses, leading to the loss of valuable diagnostic information and limiting the predictive power of classification models.

The integration of biological networks into gene expression analysis has emerged as a promising strategy to overcome these limitations. By incorporating prior knowledge of protein-protein interactions, metabolic pathways, or regulatory relationships, network-based methods aim to contextualize gene expression changes within the functional architecture of the cell. Early efforts in this direction focused on identifying enriched pathways or gene ontology terms among differentially expressed genes, but these approaches remained largely agnostic to the spatial organization of genes within the network. Subsequent developments introduced network-based ranking algorithms, such as PageRank or random walk variants, which assign importance scores to genes based on their topological position within the network. While these methods improved the prioritization of candidate biomarkers, they still treated each gene as an independent entity, failing to capture the collective behavior of neighboring genes that are physically or functionally connected. Other approaches employed graph clustering techniques to partition the network into modules or communities, assuming that genes within a cluster share similar expression patterns. However, clustering methods often produce disjointed, non-ordered groupings that lack the sequential structure required for continuous feature transformation in machine learning applications. Moreover, the inherent ambiguity in defining cluster boundaries in complex, overlapping networks frequently results in arbitrary or unstable groupings that do not reliably generalize across datasets or platforms.

A critical gap in existing methodologies lies in the inability to simultaneously preserve both the functional coherence of gene groups and their relative positional order within a network. Most current techniques either rank genes individually without regard to local neighborhood structure, or cluster them into discrete groups without establishing a meaningful sequence that reflects biological hierarchy or interaction flow. This dichotomy prevents the creation of a continuous, ordered representation of gene expression that can be meaningfully integrated into classification algorithms. Furthermore, many network-based methods are sensitive to the choice of seed genes, the quality of the underlying interaction database, and the algorithmic parameters used for ranking or clustering, leading to inconsistent results across studies. The absence of a robust, reproducible method for transforming raw gene expression profiles into structured, network-informed features has hindered the translation of network biology into clinically applicable diagnostic tools.

The present invention addresses these longstanding challenges by introducing a novel framework for gene expression analysis that leverages a biologically informed, algorithmically optimized ordering of genes within a disease-specific interaction network to generate integrated expression profiles. Unlike prior approaches that rely on static rankings or fragmented clusters, this invention employs an ant colony optimization reordering (ACOR) algorithm to systematically rearrange the adjacency matrix of a protein-protein interaction network such that functionally related genes are not only grouped together but are also arranged in a continuous, meaningful sequence that reflects their topological and functional relationships. The ACOR algorithm, inspired by the foraging behavior of real ant colonies, iteratively explores the space of possible gene orderings by simulating the movement of artificial ants across the network, depositing pheromone trails that reinforce paths leading to high-density, coherent groupings. Over successive iterations, this process converges on an optimal ordering that maximizes local connectivity and minimizes topological fragmentation, resulting in a reordered network where adjacent genes are more likely to participate in shared biological processes, physical interactions, or regulatory cascades.

The core innovation of this invention lies in the transformation of gene expression data into a new feature space defined by this reordered gene sequence. Rather than treating each gene’s expression value in isolation, the method applies a Gaussian influence function to integrate the expression of each gene with those of its immediate neighbors along the ordered list. This integration amplifies the collective signal of functionally related genes whose individual expression changes are too weak to be detected by standard statistical thresholds, while simultaneously suppressing random fluctuations and technical noise. The resulting integrated expression profile is a continuous, smoothed representation of the underlying biological state, where peaks correspond to clusters of co-regulated genes rather than single outliers. This transformed feature vector, which preserves both local gene-gene interactions and global network topology, serves as the input to standard machine learning classifiers such as support vector machines, random forests, or neural networks, enabling significantly improved classification accuracy even when trained on small, heterogeneous datasets and tested on large, independent cohorts.

This approach has been validated in the context of Alzheimer’s disease, where it was applied to microarray datasets from distinct platforms and sample populations. The method demonstrated a marked improvement in classification accuracy—rising from 74.83% using conventional expression profiling to 82.78% using the integrated profiles generated by the ACOR-based method—while outperforming alternative network-based approaches based on ranking, clustering, or random ordering. Notably, the method achieved superior performance even when compared to a previously published 1,372-probe signature, which yielded only 61.59% accuracy under identical conditions. These results underscore the capacity of the invention to extract diagnostic information from genes that are otherwise overlooked, thereby transforming what was previously considered noise into a powerful, interpretable signal. Importantly, the method does not require large training sets, making it particularly suitable for rare diseases or conditions where sample availability is limited. It is also platform-independent, as it operates on gene-level features rather than probe-specific signals, allowing for seamless application across different expression profiling technologies.

The invention further provides a generalizable framework that extends beyond Alzheimer’s disease to any condition for which a disease-specific protein-protein interaction network can be constructed. The methodology is not dependent on a particular set of seed genes, nor is it restricted to a specific type of interaction data, making it broadly applicable to cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, autoimmune conditions, and other complex diseases. The use of the ACOR algorithm ensures that the resulting gene ordering is not arbitrary but emerges from a principled, optimization-driven process that captures the intrinsic structure of biological networks. This structural fidelity, combined with the mathematical elegance of the Gaussian integration step, results in a feature transformation that is both biologically meaningful and computationally robust. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the analysis of gene expression data: moving from a reductionist focus on individual genes to a systems-level understanding of coordinated gene activity, enabling the discovery of previously hidden biomarker signatures and significantly enhancing the diagnostic potential of molecular profiling.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method for improving the accuracy of disease classification through the transformation of gene expression data into integrated expression profiles derived from a disease-specific protein-protein interaction network that has been reordered using an ant colony optimization algorithm. The method comprises the steps of selecting a set of seed genes associated with a disease of interest, constructing a disease-specific protein-protein interaction network by expanding the seed genes through nearest neighbor expansion within a comprehensive human protein interaction database, applying an ant colony optimization reordering algorithm to rearrange the adjacency matrix of the network such that functionally related genes are grouped together in a continuous, ordered sequence, mapping the gene expression values from a sample dataset onto this reordered gene list, and integrating the expression values of each gene with those of its immediate neighbors using a Gaussian influence function to generate a smoothed, integrated expression profile for each sample. These integrated profiles serve as feature vectors that are then input into a machine learning classifier to distinguish between disease and control states with significantly enhanced accuracy compared to traditional methods.

The ant colony optimization reordering algorithm operates by simulating multiple artificial ants that traverse the network, depositing and following pheromone trails that reinforce paths leading to high-density, coherent groupings of interacting proteins. Through iterative refinement, the algorithm converges upon an optimal ordering of genes that maximizes local connectivity and minimizes topological fragmentation, resulting in a reordered adjacency matrix that exhibits a fractal-like pattern indicative of hierarchical organization and functional modularity. This ordering is not merely a clustering of genes but a sequential arrangement that preserves the relative positional relationships among genes, enabling the subsequent integration of expression values along a biologically meaningful continuum. The Gaussian influence function, applied to each gene in the ordered list, assigns a weight to neighboring genes based on their distance along the sequence, such that genes in close proximity exert a stronger influence on each other’s integrated expression value than those farther apart. This integration effectively amplifies the collective signal of functionally related genes whose individual expression changes are subtle or statistically insignificant, while attenuating noise and random variation.

The invention is particularly advantageous in scenarios where training data is limited in size or derived from heterogeneous platforms, as it enables the extraction of robust diagnostic signals from small datasets and generalizes effectively to larger, independent testing cohorts. The method does not require prior knowledge of gene co-expression patterns or predefined gene sets, relying instead on the topological structure of the protein interaction network to guide the ordering process. It is also independent of the specific classification algorithm employed, as the integrated expression profiles are compatible with any supervised learning model, including support vector machines, random forests, logistic regression, or deep neural networks. The invention further enables the identification of previously undetected gene groups that contribute to disease pathogenesis but are overlooked by conventional differential expression analysis, thereby uncovering novel biomarker candidates and providing mechanistic insights into disease biology.

The integrated expression profiles generated by this method are not only predictive but also interpretable, as the peaks and troughs in the profile correspond to clusters of functionally related genes whose coordinated expression reflects underlying biological processes. This allows for the direct mapping of classification outcomes to biological modules, facilitating hypothesis generation and experimental validation. The method has been demonstrated to outperform existing network-based approaches, including those based on gene ranking, hierarchical clustering, and random ordering, in terms of classification accuracy, robustness, and generalizability. When applied to Alzheimer’s disease using publicly available microarray datasets, the invention achieved a classification accuracy of 82.78% on an independent testing set, substantially surpassing the performance of a previously published 1,372-probe signature (61.59%) and conventional expression profiling (74.83%). The improvement was consistent across multiple values of the Gaussian influence coefficient, demonstrating the stability and reliability of the method.

The invention further provides a computational framework that is scalable, reproducible, and adaptable to other diseases and biological systems. The construction of the protein-protein interaction network can be performed using any comprehensive interaction database, and the selection of seed genes can be derived from multiple sources, including genetic association studies, literature curation, or functional annotations. The ant colony optimization algorithm is implemented in a manner that ensures deterministic convergence and avoids local optima, guaranteeing consistent results across repeated runs. The entire process—from network construction to profile integration—is fully automated and requires no manual intervention, making it suitable for high-throughput clinical applications. The resulting integrated expression profiles can be stored, compared, and analyzed as standardized feature vectors, enabling the development of diagnostic assays, predictive models, and personalized medicine tools. The invention thus represents a transformative advancement in the field of molecular diagnostics, bridging the gap between systems biology and clinical application by converting complex, noisy gene expression data into clear, interpretable, and highly predictive signatures of disease state.

## DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS OF THE PRESENT INVENTION

The present invention is embodied in a systematic, computationally rigorous method for transforming gene expression data into integrated expression profiles that enhance the accuracy of disease classification through the incorporation of biological network topology. The method is implemented as a multi-stage computational pipeline, each stage of which is designed to preserve and amplify the biological signal inherent in coordinated gene expression while minimizing the influence of technical noise and stochastic variation. The first stage involves the selection of seed genes that are known or strongly implicated in the disease of interest. These seed genes may be derived from a variety of authoritative biological databases, including but not limited to AlzGene for Alzheimer’s disease, OMIM for monogenic disorders, GWAS catalogs for complex traits, or curated literature reviews that compile genetic and molecular evidence of disease association. The selection criteria for seed genes are not restricted to genes with large differential expression in prior studies; rather, they include genes with strong genetic evidence, functional annotations, or pathway membership, even if their expression changes are modest. This inclusive approach ensures that the network construction process is grounded in established biological knowledge without being biased toward genes that happen to show strong expression signals in a particular dataset.

Once the seed genes are identified, the second stage of the method constructs a disease-specific protein-protein interaction network through a nearest neighbor expansion algorithm applied to a comprehensive, high-confidence human protein interaction database. This database may include interactions derived from high-throughput experimental methods such as yeast two-hybrid screens, affinity purification-mass spectrometry, or co-expression studies, as well as computationally predicted interactions validated by multiple lines of evidence. The expansion process begins with the seed genes as initial nodes and iteratively adds their direct interaction partners—proteins that physically bind to or regulate the seed genes—until a network of sufficient size and connectivity is formed. The expansion may be constrained by interaction confidence scores, tissue specificity, or subcellular localization to ensure biological relevance, but in the preferred embodiment, the full complement of interactions is retained to maximize network coverage. The resulting network is represented as an adjacency matrix, where each row and column corresponds to a protein, and each entry indicates the presence or strength of an interaction between the corresponding pair. This matrix is initially unordered, with genes arranged in arbitrary alphabetical or accession-based order, and lacks any topological structure that reflects functional relationships.

The third and most critical stage of the method applies the ant colony optimization reordering algorithm to this adjacency matrix. The ACOR algorithm operates by simulating a population of artificial ants that traverse the network, moving from one node to another along existing interaction edges. Each ant carries a pheromone trail that it deposits on the edges it traverses, and the probability of an ant selecting a particular edge for its next step is proportional to the amount of pheromone on that edge and the attractiveness of the destination node, which is determined by its degree of connectivity and its functional similarity to the current node. Over multiple iterations, ants explore a vast number of possible orderings of the network nodes, with those orderings that result in dense, contiguous clusters of interacting proteins receiving stronger pheromone reinforcement. The algorithm incorporates mechanisms to prevent premature convergence, including pheromone evaporation and stochastic exploration, ensuring that the search space is thoroughly sampled and that the global optimum is reached. The result of this optimization process is a reordered adjacency matrix in which genes are arranged in a sequence that maximizes local connectivity and minimizes the number of interactions that span distant positions in the ordering. This reordered matrix exhibits a distinctive fractal-like pattern, with self-similar clusters nested within larger clusters, reflecting the hierarchical organization of biological systems. The ordering is not a simple clustering but a linear sequence that preserves the relative positions of functionally related genes, enabling the subsequent integration of expression values along a biologically coherent continuum.

The fourth stage of the method maps the gene expression values from a sample dataset onto this reordered gene list. Each sample in the dataset—whether from a training set or a testing set—is represented as a vector of expression values corresponding to each gene in the reordered list. These expression values may be derived from microarray hybridization intensities, RNA sequencing read counts, or any other quantitative measure of gene expression. The values are normalized to account for technical variability across samples and platforms, typically using quantile normalization, log transformation, or Z-score standardization. The key innovation of this stage lies in the integration of these expression values using a Gaussian influence function. For each gene in the ordered list, the integrated expression value is computed as a weighted sum of the expression values of all genes within a defined influence radius, where the weight assigned to each neighboring gene decreases exponentially with its distance along the sequence according to a Gaussian kernel. The width of the Gaussian kernel, controlled by a parameter r, determines the spatial extent of integration: a small r value results in localized smoothing, where only immediate neighbors influence each other, while a larger r value allows for broader integration across multiple gene clusters. The optimal value of r is determined empirically through cross-validation on the training set, ensuring that the integration captures the most informative biological signal without over-smoothing or losing discriminative power.

The resulting integrated expression profile for each sample is a single, continuous vector of values, where each position corresponds to a gene in the reordered list, and each value represents the collective expression signal of that gene and its functionally related neighbors. This profile is not merely a smoothed version of the original expression data; it is a transformed feature representation that encodes the topological organization of the underlying biological network. Peaks in the integrated profile correspond to clusters of co-regulated genes whose individual expression changes are too subtle to be detected in isolation but whose coordinated activity collectively defines a disease state. Troughs represent regions of low or inconsistent expression, often corresponding to genes with no known association to the disease or genes that are not part of the core disease module. The integrated profile is then used as input to a machine learning classifier, such as a support vector machine with a radial basis function kernel, a random forest, or a logistic regression model, to distinguish between disease and control samples. The classifier is trained on the integrated profiles from the training set and evaluated on an independent testing set, with performance metrics such as accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve used to assess its predictive power.

In a preferred embodiment, the method is applied to Alzheimer’s disease using two publicly available microarray datasets: a small training set (GSE1297, 31 samples) and a larger, independent testing set (GSE5281, 151 samples). The seed genes are selected from AlzGene and OMIM, yielding a set of 1074 genes that form the basis of the protein-protein interaction network. The ACOR algorithm is applied under populated mode, with 500 ants, 1000 iterations, and a pheromone evaporation rate of 0.1, resulting in a reordered gene list that exhibits a clear fractal structure with distinct clusters of seed genes positioned at the boundaries of major network modules. The Gaussian influence coefficient r is optimized to 0.9, maximizing classification accuracy on the training set. The integrated expression profiles are then generated for all samples, and a support vector machine classifier is trained on the training set and tested on the independent cohort. The method achieves a classification accuracy of 82.78%, significantly outperforming conventional differential expression analysis (74.83%) and a previously published 1,372-probe signature (61.59%). The same method, when applied using alternative orderings—such as gene ranking based on PageRank, hierarchical clustering, or random permutation—yields lower accuracy, demonstrating that the specific ordering generated by the ACOR algorithm is essential to the method’s success.

In another embodiment, the method is extended to other neurodegenerative diseases, such as Parkinson’s disease or Huntington’s disease, by substituting the seed genes with those associated with the respective condition. The same computational pipeline is applied without modification, and the resulting integrated profiles are used to classify disease status with comparable accuracy. In yet another embodiment, the method is adapted for use with RNA sequencing data by converting read counts to normalized expression values and applying the same integration procedure. The invention is also applicable to cancer classification, where seed genes may be derived from oncogenic pathways or tumor suppressor networks, and the integrated profiles are used to distinguish between tumor subtypes, stages, or treatment responses. The method is further generalized to multi-class classification problems, such as distinguishing between mild, moderate, and severe disease states, by generating integrated profiles for each class and training a multi-class classifier to predict the disease severity level.

The invention further encompasses a computer-readable medium storing a program that, when executed by a processor, performs the steps of the method. The program includes modules for network construction, ACOR-based reordering, expression profile integration, and classification, and may be implemented as a software suite, web-based application, or cloud-based service accessible to researchers and clinicians. The output of the system includes not only the classification result but also the integrated expression profile, the reordered gene list, and the positions of the most influential gene clusters, enabling downstream biological interpretation. The method is designed to be reproducible, with all parameters, algorithms, and data sources documented and version-controlled, ensuring that results can be independently verified and validated across laboratories. The invention thus provides a complete, end-to-end solution for transforming noisy, high-dimensional gene expression data into interpretable, predictive biomarker signatures that reflect the underlying architecture of biological systems, enabling more accurate, reliable, and biologically grounded disease classification.
