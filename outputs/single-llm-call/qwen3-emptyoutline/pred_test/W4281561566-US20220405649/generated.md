# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of machine learning has long been dominated by classical algorithms that rely on statistical patterns derived from historical data to make predictions across a wide array of industrial applications. From financial risk modeling and customer churn prediction to medical diagnostics and fraud detection, the ability to accurately classify or regress outcomes based on multivariate input features remains a cornerstone of automated decision-making systems. Over the past two decades, significant advancements have been made in the design of predictive models, particularly through the use of ensemble methods such as boosting, bagging, and stacking, which combine the outputs of multiple base learners to improve generalization, reduce variance, and enhance robustness against overfitting. Among these, AdaBoost and its variants have proven particularly effective by iteratively reweighting misclassified samples and sequentially training weak learners—typically shallow decision trees—to focus on previously difficult-to-classify instances. The cumulative decision of these learners, weighted by their individual performance, often yields superior accuracy compared to any single model.

Despite these successes, classical machine learning models face inherent limitations when confronted with high-dimensional, non-linear, or highly entangled data distributions. The curse of dimensionality, the computational cost of kernel evaluations in large feature spaces, and the difficulty of capturing complex interactions between features using linear or even kernelized models have motivated researchers to explore alternative computational paradigms. Quantum computing, with its ability to represent and manipulate information in high-dimensional Hilbert spaces through superposition, entanglement, and interference, has emerged as a promising candidate for overcoming these bottlenecks. In particular, quantum machine learning (QML) seeks to leverage quantum hardware to accelerate or enhance classical machine learning tasks by encoding data into quantum states and applying parameterized quantum circuits to induce non-linear transformations that are intractable for classical computers.

One of the earliest and most influential proposals in this domain is the quantum support vector machine (QSVM), introduced as a quantum analog to the classical support vector machine (SVM). In QSVM, the kernel function is computed via the inner product of quantum states prepared by a feature map—a unitary transformation that encodes classical input data into a quantum register. The resulting quantum kernel, defined as the squared modulus of the overlap between two encoded states, implicitly maps the input data into an exponentially large feature space without explicitly constructing the high-dimensional vectors. This implicit mapping, enabled by quantum interference and entanglement, theoretically allows QSVMs to discover decision boundaries that are inaccessible to classical kernels such as the radial basis function (RBF) or polynomial kernels. Early experimental implementations of QSVM on superconducting quantum processors, such as those provided by IBM Quantum, demonstrated feasibility but did not conclusively establish a performance advantage over classical counterparts on real-world datasets.

Subsequent research has sought to refine QSVM architectures by introducing parameterized feature maps, regularization techniques, and hybrid classical-quantum optimization loops. Notably, studies have explored the use of shallow quantum circuits composed of single-qubit rotations and entangling gates to construct feature maps that are both hardware-efficient and expressive enough to capture non-linear relationships. However, despite these advances, most published works have focused on single QSVM models trained in isolation, with little attention paid to the potential for combining multiple QSVMs into an ensemble structure. Moreover, the selection of feature maps has largely been guided by heuristic or physics-inspired designs—such as Ising-model-derived Hamiltonians or Pauli-Z rotations—without systematic exploration of the vast space of possible quantum circuit topologies. This reliance on fixed or manually selected architectures limits the adaptability of QSVMs to diverse data distributions and introduces a significant barrier to adoption by practitioners without expertise in quantum information theory.

Furthermore, while some efforts have been made to apply boosting principles to quantum learning systems, these have been largely confined to adiabatic quantum computing platforms such as D-Wave annealers, where weak classifiers are implemented as single-level decision trees. Other proposals have considered quantum ensembles primarily from the perspective of parallelization and theoretical speedup, rather than performance enhancement through diversity and sequential refinement. Crucially, none of these prior approaches have integrated an automated, iterative model selection process that dynamically chooses among a broad set of quantum feature map configurations at each boosting step, nor have they systematically enforced exploration of distinct quantum kernels to avoid redundancy in the ensemble. As a result, existing quantum machine learning frameworks remain largely static in their architecture, constrained by pre-defined circuit designs, and lack the adaptive, data-driven optimization mechanisms that have proven indispensable in classical machine learning.

There is also a critical gap in the literature regarding the applicability of quantum ensemble methods beyond binary classification. While many studies focus narrowly on classification tasks, real-world industrial problems frequently require regression, multi-class prediction, or probabilistic calibration—capabilities that are rarely addressed in the context of quantum learning. Additionally, the evaluation of quantum models has often relied on simulated datasets with known structure, such as moons, circles, or XOR patterns, which, while useful for benchmarking, do not reflect the noise, imbalance, and high dimensionality of practical data. Consequently, there is a pressing need for a robust, scalable, and automated quantum ensemble framework that can be applied to diverse data types, rigorously validated across multiple benchmarks, and designed for seamless integration into existing machine learning pipelines.

This invention addresses these shortcomings by introducing a novel, fully automated boosting framework for quantum support vector machines that dynamically selects and combines multiple quantum kernel classifiers through an iterative, adaptive process. Unlike prior approaches, this method does not rely on fixed or physics-inspired feature maps, nor does it assume the weakness of individual learners. Instead, it treats each QSVM as a potentially strong classifier and leverages the diversity of quantum feature map topologies to construct an ensemble that systematically explores different regions of the quantum Hilbert space. At each iteration, the algorithm performs a grid search over a comprehensive set of Pauli-based feature maps, rotation parameters, and regularization strengths, selecting the model that minimizes validation error. Crucially, once a feature map configuration is selected, it is excluded from future iterations to ensure that subsequent learners explore novel decision boundaries, thereby preventing redundancy and encouraging complementary learning. This process continues until a stopping criterion is met—such as perfect classification, performance no better than random guessing, or a maximum ensemble size—after which the final model is formed by a weighted majority vote of all selected classifiers.

The invention further extends beyond classification by demonstrating compatibility with regression tasks, thereby broadening its applicability to continuous outcome prediction problems. It is implemented on universal gate-based quantum processors, making it compatible with current and near-term quantum hardware such as IBM Quantum System One, and is designed to operate within a classical optimization loop that handles parameter tuning, early stopping, and ensemble weighting. The method is entirely agnostic to the underlying data distribution, requiring no prior assumptions about the nature of the feature interactions, and is capable of discovering optimal quantum kernels without domain-specific knowledge. Through extensive numerical simulations on synthetic datasets—including XOR, moons, and circles—the invention demonstrates consistent performance gains over single QSVMs, outperforming classical SVMs and gradient-boosted decision trees in specific regimes, and achieving near-perfect accuracy on highly non-linear data structures. Moreover, the automated nature of the approach eliminates the need for manual feature map selection, making it accessible to data scientists without quantum expertise and paving the way for widespread adoption in industrial settings.

In summary, the background of this invention is rooted in the convergence of two powerful paradigms: the proven effectiveness of ensemble learning in classical machine learning and the theoretical promise of quantum-enhanced feature spaces. While prior work has treated these domains as separate, this invention unifies them into a single, automated, and adaptive framework that not only enhances predictive performance but also democratizes access to quantum machine learning by removing the need for specialized physical intuition. The result is a novel, scalable, and industrially viable method for constructing high-performance quantum ensembles that surpass the capabilities of both classical and single-learner quantum models, thereby establishing a new standard for quantum-assisted decision systems.

## SUMMARY

This invention discloses a novel, automated ensemble method for enhancing the predictive performance of quantum support vector machines through iterative, adaptive selection of quantum kernel classifiers. The method operates by constructing a sequence of quantum classifiers, each based on a distinct feature map encoded into a quantum register, and combining their predictions through a weighted voting mechanism. Unlike conventional boosting algorithms that rely on weak learners such as decision stumps, this invention employs quantum support vector machines as the base learners, each of which is capable of capturing complex, non-linear decision boundaries due to the high-dimensional nature of the quantum feature space. The key innovation lies in the dynamic and automated selection of feature map configurations at each boosting iteration, where a grid search is performed over a predefined set of Pauli-based quantum circuits, rotation scaling factors, and regularization parameters. The best-performing model, as determined by validation error, is selected and added to the ensemble, after which its specific feature map configuration is permanently excluded from subsequent iterations to ensure diversity in the quantum kernel space. This enforced exploration of distinct quantum transformations prevents redundancy and compels the ensemble to discover complementary decision boundaries across the Hilbert space, thereby enhancing generalization and robustness.

The method begins with the initialization of training, validation, and test datasets, along with a comprehensive parameter grid that includes multiple quantum feature maps, each constructed from combinations of single-qubit Pauli rotations (X, Y, Z) and Hadamard gates, as well as a continuous range of rotation scaling factors and regularization coefficients. Each data sample is initially assigned equal weight, and the algorithm proceeds iteratively. At each step, a grid search is executed over all permissible combinations of feature maps, rotation multipliers, and regularization strengths, with the performance of each candidate model evaluated on the validation set. The model yielding the lowest classification or regression error is selected, and its associated feature map is removed from the parameter space for all future iterations. The weight of each training sample is then updated based on the error rate of the newly selected classifier, with misclassified samples receiving increased weight to emphasize their importance in subsequent iterations. The process continues until one of three early stopping conditions is satisfied: (1) the selected classifier achieves perfect accuracy on the training set, (2) the classifier performs no better than random guessing, or (3) a predetermined maximum number of estimators has been reached. Upon termination, the final model is formed by aggregating the predictions of all selected quantum classifiers, with each classifier’s contribution weighted by its individual performance, as determined by a logarithmic function of its error rate.

The invention is not limited to binary classification tasks and is equally applicable to multi-class classification and regression problems, with appropriate modifications to the loss function and error metric. The entire process is implemented within a hybrid classical-quantum computational framework, wherein quantum circuits are executed on universal gate-based quantum processors, and classical optimization routines handle parameter selection, weight updates, and ensemble aggregation. The method is designed for compatibility with current superconducting qubit architectures, such as those employed in IBM Quantum System One, and requires no assumptions regarding the underlying data distribution or feature interactions. By systematically exploring a broad space of quantum kernel configurations and enforcing diversity through exclusion of previously selected feature maps, the invention achieves superior performance compared to single quantum support vector machines, classical SVMs with RBF or linear kernels, and gradient-boosted decision trees on a variety of synthetic datasets, including XOR, moons, and circles. Numerical simulations demonstrate that the ensemble method consistently improves classification accuracy by up to 7.5 percent over single QSVMs, particularly on datasets with complex, non-linear structures where individual learners struggle. Moreover, the automation of model selection eliminates the need for manual tuning of quantum circuit architectures, making the method accessible to practitioners without expertise in quantum physics or quantum circuit design. The invention thus represents a significant advancement in the practical deployment of quantum machine learning by transforming it from a theoretical construct into a robust, automated, and industrially deployable decision support system.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The detailed description of this invention encompasses the complete architecture, operational workflow, parameterization, and implementation framework of the automated quantum ensemble method for support vector machines. The system is designed to operate within a hybrid classical-quantum computational environment, wherein classical computing resources manage data preprocessing, parameter optimization, and ensemble aggregation, while quantum processors execute the core kernel computations required for evaluating quantum support vector machine classifiers. The method is structured as a sequential, iterative procedure that constructs an ensemble of quantum classifiers by dynamically selecting the most effective feature map configuration at each step, ensuring that each subsequent learner contributes unique information to the final decision boundary. This approach fundamentally departs from traditional boosting algorithms, which rely on weak, shallow learners such as decision stumps, and instead leverages the inherent expressiveness of quantum kernel functions to construct strong, non-linear classifiers that are individually capable of capturing complex data relationships.

The process begins with the preparation of input data, which is assumed to be in the form of a classical dataset consisting of N observations, each described by a set of D real-valued features and a corresponding label. For classification tasks, the label is binary, taking values in {−1, +1}, while for regression tasks, the label is a continuous scalar. The data is partitioned into three disjoint subsets: a training set, a validation set, and a test set, with each subset containing an equal number of samples. This partitioning ensures that the model selection process is performed on an independent validation set, thereby preventing overfitting to the training data and providing an unbiased estimate of generalization performance. The test set remains completely withheld throughout the entire training and selection process and is used exclusively for final evaluation of the ensemble’s predictive capability.

Upon initialization, the algorithm defines a comprehensive parameter grid that governs the space of possible quantum feature maps and associated hyperparameters. The feature maps are constructed using a set of unitary transformations applied to a register of n qubits, where n is determined by the dimensionality of the input feature space. For datasets with two features, as used in the experimental validation, n is set to two, allowing each feature to be encoded into a separate qubit. The feature map is defined as a sequence of quantum gates that transform the initial state |0⟩⊗n into a quantum state that encodes the input data x ∈ ℝ^D. The specific form of the feature map is given by a product of single-qubit rotations and Hadamard gates, where each qubit undergoes a rotation around one of the Pauli axes (X, Y, or Z), scaled by a factor α that modulates the sensitivity of the encoding to the input features. The set of possible feature maps includes all combinations of Pauli operators applied to each qubit, excluding the identity operator, such that for a two-qubit system, the possible feature maps include configurations such as H ⊗ H, H ⊗ X, H ⊗ Y, H ⊗ Z, X ⊗ H, Y ⊗ H, Z ⊗ H, X ⊗ X, X ⊗ Y, and so on, up to all 27 non-trivial combinations of Pauli operators on two qubits. Each feature map is parameterized by a rotation angle α, which is drawn from a continuous interval (0, 2], allowing fine-tuning of the encoding’s non-linearity. Additionally, a regularization parameter C, analogous to the penalty parameter in classical SVMs, is varied over the range [1, 100] to control the trade-off between maximizing the margin and minimizing classification error.

At the first iteration of the algorithm, all training samples are assigned equal weight, w_i = 1/N for i = 1, ..., N. The algorithm then performs a grid search over all permissible combinations of feature maps, rotation factors α, and regularization parameters C. For each combination, a quantum circuit is synthesized, consisting of the specified feature map followed by a quantum kernel evaluation. The kernel function is computed as the squared modulus of the inner product between the quantum states corresponding to two input samples, i.e., k(x_i, x_j) = |⟨ψ(x_i)|ψ(x_j)⟩|^2, where |ψ(x)⟩ is the quantum state produced by applying the feature map to the input x. This kernel matrix is then passed to a classical support vector classifier, implemented using the scikit-learn SVC module, which trains a binary classifier using the quantum kernel as its similarity measure. The classifier is trained on the training set and evaluated on the validation set to compute its classification error rate, err_m, defined as the proportion of misclassified samples in the validation set.

The combination of feature map, α, and C that yields the lowest validation error is selected as the best model for the current iteration. Once selected, this specific configuration is permanently removed from the parameter grid for all subsequent iterations. This exclusion mechanism is a critical innovation of the invention, as it ensures that the ensemble does not repeatedly select similar or redundant quantum kernels. By forcing the algorithm to explore new regions of the Hilbert space at each step, the method encourages diversity in the decision boundaries learned by each classifier, thereby reducing correlation among ensemble members and improving overall generalization. This principle is analogous to diversity-promoting techniques in classical ensemble learning, such as subsampling features or data points, but is uniquely implemented here through the quantum circuit topology itself.

Following the selection of the best model, the weights of the training samples are updated according to the formula w_i ← w_i * exp(α_m * I(y_i ≠ ŷ_i)), where ŷ_i is the predicted label for sample i under the current classifier, y_i is the true label, and α_m = log((1 − err_m)/err_m) is the weight assigned to the classifier based on its performance. This update rule increases the weight of misclassified samples, thereby increasing their influence in subsequent iterations, while decreasing the weight of correctly classified samples. The classifier weight α_m is also stored and used later in the final aggregation of predictions. The algorithm then checks for early stopping conditions. The first condition is that the current classifier achieves perfect accuracy on the training set, i.e., err_m ≤ 0. In such a case, no further improvement is possible, and the algorithm terminates. The second condition is that the classifier performs no better than random guessing, i.e., err_m ≥ 0.5 for binary classification. This indicates that the selected model has no discriminative power and may even be detrimental to the ensemble, thus warranting termination. The third condition is that a predetermined maximum number of estimators, M_max, has been reached. This upper bound prevents the ensemble from growing indefinitely and mitigates the risk of overfitting. If none of the stopping conditions are met, the algorithm proceeds to the next iteration, repeating the grid search over the reduced parameter space.

This iterative process continues until one of the stopping conditions is triggered. Upon termination, the final ensemble model is constructed by aggregating the predictions of all selected classifiers. For a new input sample x, each classifier in the ensemble computes a predicted label ŷ_m(x) and a corresponding confidence score based on the decision function of the SVM. These predictions are then combined using a weighted majority vote, where the weight assigned to each classifier is its corresponding α_m value. The final prediction is given by sign(∑_{m=1}^M α_m * ŷ_m(x)), where M is the total number of classifiers in the ensemble. This weighted aggregation ensures that classifiers with higher performance contribute more significantly to the final decision, while those with lower accuracy have diminished influence.

The invention is not restricted to binary classification. For multi-class classification problems, the method is extended using a one-vs-rest (OvR) strategy, wherein a separate ensemble is trained for each class, with each ensemble distinguishing that class from all others. The final prediction is made by selecting the class with the highest aggregate confidence score across all ensembles. For regression tasks, the classification error is replaced with a continuous loss function, such as mean squared error or mean absolute error, and the final prediction is computed as a weighted average of the regression outputs from each classifier, rather than a majority vote. The same iterative selection and weight update mechanisms apply, with the only modification being the nature of the error metric and the aggregation rule.

The quantum circuits used in this method are implemented on universal gate-based quantum processors, such as those available on IBM Quantum System One, which support a native gate set including single-qubit rotations (R_x, R_y, R_z) and two-qubit entangling gates (CNOT, CZ). The feature maps are constructed using sequences of R_y gates, whose rotation angles are determined by the input features and the scaling factor α. For example, a feature map for a single qubit may be defined as R_y(α * x_1), where x_1 is the first feature of the input vector. For two qubits, a feature map may be defined as R_y(α * x_1) ⊗ R_y(α * x_2), followed by a CNOT gate to introduce entanglement. The choice of entangling gates is optional and may be omitted in shallow circuits, as the primary source of expressiveness comes from the non-linear encoding of features into the qubit states. The quantum kernel is computed by preparing the state |ψ(x_i)⟩ and |ψ(x_j)⟩, performing a swap test or direct state overlap estimation, and measuring the fidelity between the two states. This measurement is repeated multiple times to reduce statistical noise, and the resulting fidelity is squared to obtain the kernel value.

The classical component of the system is implemented using standard machine learning libraries, including scikit-learn for SVM training and evaluation, NumPy for numerical operations, and Qiskit or PennyLane for quantum circuit simulation and execution. The entire pipeline is executed on a classical computing environment with access to quantum simulators or real quantum hardware via cloud platforms. The system is designed to scale with the number of qubits and the size of the parameter grid, with computational complexity dominated by the grid search and the number of quantum circuit evaluations required per iteration. To mitigate computational overhead, the grid search can be parallelized across multiple classical processors, and quantum circuit evaluations can be batched to reduce latency on quantum hardware.

The method has been validated through extensive numerical simulations on synthetic datasets generated using scikit-learn’s make_moons, make_circles, and make_xor functions. Each dataset type was generated 50 times to ensure statistical robustness, with each instance containing 150 samples, equally divided among training, validation, and test sets. For each dataset, the proposed ensemble method was compared against three baseline models: a classical SVM with RBF and linear kernels, a classical SVM with optimized hyperparameters via grid search, and an XGBoost classifier with a comprehensive hyperparameter grid. The performance metric used was classification accuracy on the test set, averaged across all 50 instances of each dataset type.

Results demonstrated that the boosted QSVM ensemble achieved median accuracy of 100% on the circles dataset, outperforming both classical SVM and XGBoost, which achieved median accuracies of approximately 92% and 95%, respectively. On the XOR dataset, the ensemble achieved a median accuracy of 97.3%, compared to 96.8% for XGBoost and 95.1% for the classical SVM with RBF kernel. On the moons dataset, the ensemble achieved a median accuracy of 94.1%, compared to 91.7% for XGBoost and 89.3% for the classical SVM. The improvement was most pronounced on datasets with complex, non-linear structures, where the quantum kernel’s ability to implicitly map data into high-dimensional spaces provided a distinct advantage. Furthermore, analysis of ensemble composition revealed that only 31% of the boosted QSVM models contained more than one classifier, indicating that the method often converges rapidly to a single, highly effective quantum kernel. However, in cases where multiple classifiers were selected, the average improvement in accuracy over a single QSVM was 4.2% for XOR and 7.5% for moons, demonstrating the value of ensemble diversity in challenging data regimes.

The method also exhibited strong stability across iterations, with the exclusion mechanism effectively preventing the selection of redundant feature maps. In over 90% of runs, no feature map was selected more than once, confirming that the algorithm successfully explored a broad spectrum of quantum kernel configurations. The regularization parameter C was found to cluster around values between 10 and 50 in most successful models, indicating that moderate regularization is optimal for balancing margin maximization and error minimization. The rotation scaling factor α was typically selected in the range [0.8, 1.5], suggesting that moderate non-linearity in the encoding is sufficient to capture the underlying data structure without inducing overfitting.

Importantly, the method does not require any prior knowledge of the data distribution, feature interactions, or optimal circuit architecture. It is entirely data-driven, with all decisions regarding model selection, hyperparameter tuning, and ensemble composition made automatically through the iterative grid search and validation process. This automation removes the need for expert intervention in quantum circuit design, making the method accessible to data scientists, engineers, and analysts without training in quantum physics. The system can be integrated into existing machine learning pipelines as a drop-in replacement for classical boosting algorithms, with the only additional requirement being access to a quantum processor or simulator.

In industrial applications, this invention can be deployed in domains requiring high-accuracy classification or regression under complex data conditions. In finance, it can be used to detect fraudulent transactions by identifying subtle, non-linear patterns in transaction metadata. In healthcare, it can predict patient readmission risk by analyzing heterogeneous clinical records, laboratory results, and demographic factors. In manufacturing, it can forecast equipment failure by detecting anomalies in sensor time-series data. In each case, the method’s ability to discover optimal quantum kernels without manual tuning provides a significant advantage over classical models that require extensive feature engineering and hyperparameter optimization.

The invention further enables the discovery of novel quantum kernel architectures that may not be intuitively obvious to human designers. By systematically exploring the space of Pauli-based feature maps, the algorithm may uncover configurations that exploit quantum interference effects in ways that classical kernels cannot replicate. These discoveries could lead to the development of new quantum machine learning primitives that are subsequently incorporated into future hardware and software frameworks. The method is also extensible to larger feature spaces by increasing the number of qubits and expanding the parameter grid accordingly, making it scalable to real-world datasets with dozens or even hundreds of features.

In summary, the detailed description of this invention presents a complete, automated, and industrially viable framework for constructing high-performance quantum ensembles through iterative, diversity-enforcing selection of quantum kernel classifiers. The method combines the strengths of classical boosting with the representational power of quantum feature maps, resulting in a system that outperforms both classical and single-learner quantum models on a variety of benchmark datasets. Its automation, adaptability, and compatibility with existing quantum hardware make it a transformative advancement in the field of quantum machine learning, providing a practical pathway for deploying quantum-enhanced decision systems in real-world applications.
