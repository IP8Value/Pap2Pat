# DESCRIPTION

## INTRODUCTION

The extraction of a desired speaker’s voice from a noisy acoustic environment containing multiple concurrent speakers and stationary background noise remains one of the most challenging problems in modern speech signal processing. Despite decades of research and substantial advances in microphone array technologies, the practical deployment of speech enhancement systems in real-world settings—such as conference rooms, smart homes, or automotive cabins—continues to be hindered by the complex interplay of reverberation, spatial overlap of sound sources, and the dynamic nature of human speech activity. Traditional beamforming techniques, while effective in controlled environments, often fail to adequately suppress interfering speakers when they are spatially proximate to the target speaker or when their acoustic signatures are similar in spectral content. The minimum variance distortionless response (MVDR) beamformer, for instance, is designed to preserve the desired signal while minimizing the total power of noise and interference; however, when an interfering speaker is active and occupies a spatial location close to the desired speaker, the MVDR beamformer may inadvertently direct its beam toward the interferer, thereby amplifying rather than suppressing it. This limitation arises because the MVDR beamformer relies on a single constraint that enforces unity gain in the direction of the desired source, without explicitly accounting for the presence of additional sources of interest. As a result, the beamformer’s output becomes dominated by the strongest source within its main lobe, regardless of whether that source is the intended one.

To address this shortcoming, the linearly constrained minimum variance (LCMV) beamformer was introduced as a more flexible framework capable of simultaneously preserving multiple sources of interest while suppressing unwanted signals. Unlike the MVDR approach, which imposes a single constraint, the LCMV beamformer allows for the specification of multiple linear constraints, enabling the system to maintain gain for both the desired speaker and one or more interfering speakers, while still minimizing the overall output power of noise and unmodeled interference. This makes the LCMV beamformer particularly well-suited for scenarios involving multiple active speakers, such as round-table discussions, multi-party video conferencing, or collaborative workspaces. However, the practical implementation of the LCMV beamformer is contingent upon accurate knowledge of the acoustic transfer functions (ATFs) relating each source to each microphone in the array, commonly referred to as the relative transfer functions (RTFs), as well as a reliable estimate of the noise covariance matrix. In real-world applications, these parameters are rarely known a priori and must be estimated dynamically from the observed microphone signals. Traditional methods for estimating RTFs rely on assumptions such as the presence of silent intervals, single-speaker segments, or prior calibration under controlled conditions. These assumptions are often violated in natural, unstructured environments where speakers frequently overlap in time, making it difficult to isolate clean segments for parameter estimation.

Furthermore, the noise covariance matrix, which characterizes the statistical properties of background noise across the microphone array, is typically initialized using the initial frames of the recording, under the assumption that no speakers are active during this period. However, in many practical scenarios—especially in domestic or office environments—background noise may be non-stationary, or speech activity may begin immediately upon system activation, leaving no reliable noise-only reference. This leads to inaccurate noise modeling, which in turn degrades the performance of the beamformer by either under-suppressing noise or over-suppressing the desired speech signal. Similarly, the identification of which speaker is active at any given moment—whether it is the desired speaker or an interfering one—is critical for updating the RTF matrix in real time. Without such identification, the beamformer cannot adapt its constraints to reflect the changing acoustic landscape, resulting in persistent interference or signal distortion.

This invention overcomes these longstanding limitations by introducing a fully integrated, end-to-end speech enhancement system that dynamically and autonomously controls the parameters of the LCMV beamformer through two novel, mutually reinforcing mechanisms: a speaker position identifier (SPI) based on a pre-trained RTF library and a speech presence probability (SPP)-driven voice activity detector (VAD). The system operates without requiring manual calibration, pre-recorded training data, or user intervention during deployment. Instead, it leverages a pre-computed library of RTFs corresponding to fixed spatial positions in the environment, which are established during an initial, one-time calibration phase. During operation, the system continuously estimates the RTF of the currently active speaker using generalized eigenvalue decomposition of the signal correlation matrix and the noise covariance matrix. It then compares this estimated RTF against the entries in the pre-trained library using a cosine similarity metric to determine the spatial identity of the active speaker. This classification is used to selectively update the RTF matrix of the LCMV beamformer, ensuring that the constraints remain aligned with the true locations of the desired and interfering speakers, even in the presence of double-talk or rapid speaker movement.

Simultaneously, the system employs a deep neural network-based SPP estimator to generate a frame-level probability map indicating the likelihood of speech presence at each time-frequency bin. This SPP map is aggregated across frequency to produce a robust VAD decision that determines when noise-only segments occur. Unlike conventional VADs that rely on energy thresholds or spectral flatness measures, this SPP-based VAD is highly resistant to false positives caused by non-speech sounds such as keyboard typing, page turning, or ambient environmental noise. By using the SPP-based VAD to gate the recursive update of the noise covariance matrix, the system ensures that noise estimation is only performed during periods of true silence, thereby preserving the integrity of the noise model and preventing the contamination of the noise estimate by residual speech components. The combination of these two control mechanisms—SPI for RTF adaptation and SPP-VAD for noise covariance adaptation—enables the LCMV beamformer to operate with unprecedented accuracy and stability in dynamic, multi-speaker environments.

Following the beamforming stage, the system applies a post-processing filter based on the same NN-MM algorithm used for SPP estimation. This post-filter operates on the single-channel output of the LCMV beamformer, further suppressing residual noise and spectral distortions introduced during the beamforming process. The NN-MM algorithm, which combines a mixture-of-Gaussians model of phoneme spectra with a deep neural network classifier, generates a soft spectral attenuation mask that selectively enhances speech components while preserving the natural timbre of the voice. This two-stage processing—spatial filtering followed by spectral refinement—results in a significant improvement in both signal-to-interference ratio and signal-to-noise ratio, as demonstrated through extensive real-world testing in low-reverberation environments. The system is designed for seamless integration into existing audio capture devices, requiring only a multi-microphone array and a computational unit capable of running real-time signal processing algorithms. It does not require prior knowledge of speaker identities, does not rely on external audio cues such as wake words or user prompts, and functions effectively even when the desired speaker is not the loudest or closest to the microphone array. The invention thus represents a fundamental advancement in the field of multi-speaker speech enhancement, offering a robust, adaptive, and fully automated solution that bridges the gap between laboratory performance and real-world usability.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a novel system and method for extracting a desired speaker’s voice from a noisy mixture of multiple concurrent speakers and stationary background noise using a multi-microphone array. The system is designed to operate in real time without requiring user intervention, pre-recorded calibration for each specific environment, or prior knowledge of speaker identities. It achieves superior speech enhancement performance by integrating three core components: a linearly constrained minimum variance (LCMV) beamformer, a speaker position identifier (SPI) based on a pre-trained relative transfer function (RTF) library, and a speech presence probability (SPP)-driven voice activity detector (VAD) that governs the adaptive update of the noise covariance matrix. The system further employs a deep neural network-based post-filter to refine the beamformer’s output, resulting in a significant improvement in both signal-to-noise ratio and signal-to-interference ratio.

The invention begins with the deployment of a multi-microphone array in a fixed spatial configuration, such as a U-shaped arrangement of seven omnidirectional microphones, positioned in an environment where the locations of potential speakers are approximately constant, such as a conference room table or a vehicle cabin. During an initial, one-time calibration phase, the system records speech samples from each potential speaker position using different speakers and utterances unrelated to those used during operation. From these recordings, the system computes and stores a library of relative transfer functions (RTFs), each corresponding to a specific spatial location. These RTFs capture the unique acoustic signature of each position, including the effects of room geometry, furniture, and reverberation, but do not depend on the identity of the speaker producing the speech. This library is then permanently stored and used during all subsequent operational phases.

During operation, the system receives a multi-channel signal from the microphone array, which contains the desired speaker’s voice, one or more interfering speakers’ voices, and stationary background noise. The system first estimates the noise covariance matrix by initializing it using the initial frames of the recording, assumed to be noise-only. It then applies a deep neural network-based SPP estimator to the signal from a reference microphone to generate a time-frequency map of speech presence probabilities. This SPP map is aggregated across frequency to produce a binary VAD decision for each time frame. When the VAD indicates that no speech is present, the noise covariance matrix is recursively updated using a low-learning-rate adaptation mechanism, ensuring that the noise model remains accurate even in the presence of slowly varying environmental noise. When speech is detected, no adaptation is performed, preventing contamination of the noise estimate by speech components.

Simultaneously, the system estimates the current RTF of the dominant active speaker by applying generalized eigenvalue decomposition to the signal correlation matrix and the noise covariance matrix. The resulting generalized eigenvector associated with the largest eigenvalue is normalized to form an estimated RTF. This estimated RTF is then compared to each entry in the pre-trained RTF library using a cosine similarity metric. The position corresponding to the highest similarity score is identified as the location of the active speaker. If the identified position corresponds to the location of the desired speaker, the RTF associated with that position is used to update the desired source constraint in the LCMV beamformer. If the identified position corresponds to an interfering speaker, the RTF associated with that position is used to update the interference constraint. If the system determines that both speakers are active simultaneously—indicated by a lack of dominant similarity across the RTF library—the RTF matrix is held constant to prevent erroneous updates during double-talk.

The LCMV beamformer then applies its constraints to the multi-channel input signal, producing a single-channel output that preserves the desired speaker while suppressing the interfering speaker and background noise. This output is further refined by a post-filter based on the same NN-MM algorithm used for SPP estimation. The post-filter generates a soft spectral attenuation mask that selectively enhances speech components in the time-frequency domain while minimizing distortion to the speech signal’s natural characteristics. The final output is a clean, intelligible rendition of the desired speaker’s voice, significantly enhanced in both quality and intelligibility compared to the original noisy mixture.

The system is fully autonomous, requiring no manual tuning, user prompts, or external triggers. It adapts to changes in speaker location, background noise levels, and speech activity patterns in real time, making it suitable for deployment in unstructured environments. The use of a pre-trained RTF library eliminates the need for re-calibration for each new user or environment, while the SPP-based VAD ensures robust noise estimation even in noisy or reverberant conditions. The combination of spatial beamforming, position-based constraint adaptation, and spectral post-filtering results in a synergistic enhancement effect that neither component could achieve independently. Experimental results demonstrate that the system achieves an average improvement in signal-to-interference ratio of over 15 dB and an average improvement in signal-to-noise ratio of over 12 dB across a wide range of input conditions, outperforming conventional MVDR beamformers, static LCMV configurations, and other state-of-the-art speech enhancement methods. The invention is particularly suited for applications in smart meeting rooms, voice-controlled assistants, automotive infotainment systems, hearing aids, and teleconferencing platforms, where high-fidelity speech extraction from multi-speaker environments is critical to usability and user satisfaction.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention comprises a comprehensive, end-to-end system for the extraction of a desired speaker’s voice from a noisy mixture of multiple concurrent speakers and stationary background noise, utilizing a multi-microphone array and a suite of adaptive signal processing techniques that operate in real time without requiring user intervention or prior knowledge of speaker identities. The system is structured around three interdependent functional modules: a linearly constrained minimum variance (LCMV) beamformer that performs spatial filtering, a speaker position identifier (SPI) that dynamically updates the beamformer’s constraints based on spatial location, and a speech presence probability (SPP)-driven voice activity detector (VAD) that governs the adaptive estimation of the noise covariance matrix. These modules are further augmented by a deep neural network-based post-filter that refines the beamformer’s output in the spectral domain. The entire system is designed to operate seamlessly in low-reverberation environments where speaker positions are approximately fixed, such as conference rooms, vehicle interiors, or smart home environments, and is capable of functioning under a wide range of signal-to-noise and signal-to-interference ratios without degradation in performance.

The system begins with the deployment of a multi-microphone array, consisting of M microphones arranged in a predefined, fixed spatial configuration. In a preferred embodiment, the array comprises seven omnidirectional microphones arranged in a U-shape, with the reference microphone positioned at the center of the arc to ensure balanced spatial coverage. The microphones are calibrated for gain and phase alignment during manufacturing and remain fixed in position throughout the system’s operational lifetime. The array captures a mixture of acoustic signals, including the desired speaker’s voice, one or more interfering speakers’ voices, and stationary background noise such as HVAC hum, fan noise, or ambient room tone. Each of these signals is convolved with a distinct acoustic transfer function (ATF) that characterizes the propagation path from the speaker to each microphone. These ATFs are frequency-dependent and vary with the spatial location of the speaker relative to the microphone array. In the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) domain, the signal received at the m-th microphone at time-frame l and frequency bin k is represented as:

z_m(l,k) = h_{d,m}(l,k) s_d(l,k) + h_{i,m}(l,k) s_i(l,k) + v_m(l,k)

where z_m(l,k) denotes the complex-valued STFT coefficient of the signal at the m-th microphone, h_{d,m}(l,k) and h_{i,m}(l,k) are the ATFs corresponding to the desired and interfering speakers, respectively, s_d(l,k) and s_i(l,k) are the STFT coefficients of the desired and interfering speech signals, and v_m(l,k) represents the stationary background noise at the m-th microphone. The signals are assumed to be statistically uncorrelated, and the noise is modeled as zero-mean, wide-sense stationary, and spatially uncorrelated across microphones. The full multi-channel signal is compactly represented as a complex vector:

z(l,k) = [z_1(l,k), z_2(l,k), ..., z_M(l,k)]^T

The system operates under the assumption that the spatial locations of potential speakers are known and fixed, and that the number of possible speaker positions is limited and finite. This assumption is realistic in many practical applications, such as conference tables with designated seating, vehicle cabins with fixed passenger positions, or smart home environments where users typically occupy known locations. During an initial, one-time calibration phase, the system records speech samples from each of these predefined positions using a diverse set of speakers and utterances unrelated to those used during operation. These recordings are used to compute a library of relative transfer functions (RTFs), where each RTF corresponds to a specific spatial location. The RTF for a given position is defined as the ratio of the ATF between the source and each microphone to the ATF between the source and a designated reference microphone. For a given position s, the RTF is expressed as:

c_s(k) = [h_{s,1}(k)/h_{s,ref}(k), h_{s,2}(k)/h_{s,ref}(k), ..., h_{s,M}(k)/h_{s,ref}(k)]^T

where h_{s,m}(k) is the ATF between the source at position s and the m-th microphone, and h_{s,ref}(k) is the ATF between the source at position s and the reference microphone. The RTF library is constructed offline and stored in non-volatile memory. It contains N_s distinct RTFs, each corresponding to one of the N_s predefined speaker positions. In a preferred embodiment, N_s = 4, corresponding to four seating positions around a table. The RTFs are computed using generalized eigenvalue decomposition (GEVD) applied to the correlation matrix of the recorded speech signals and a noise-only reference matrix, ensuring that the resulting RTFs are robust to noise and reverberation. Importantly, the RTF library is independent of speaker identity, gender, speaking rate, or linguistic content; it encodes only the spatial acoustics of each position.

During operation, the system receives the multi-channel signal z(l,k) from the microphone array and initializes the noise covariance matrix Φ_vv(l,k) using the first 0.5 seconds of the recording, during which it is assumed that no speakers are active. This initialization is performed by averaging the outer product of the signal vectors over the initial noise-only frames:

Φ_vv(l,k) = (1/L) ∑_{l'=1}^{L} z(l',k) z^H(l',k)

where L is the number of frames in the initial noise segment, and (·)^H denotes the conjugate transpose. This initialization provides a baseline estimate of the noise statistics, which is subsequently refined using an adaptive mechanism governed by a speech presence probability (SPP)-based voice activity detector (VAD). The SPP estimator is implemented using a deep neural network (DNN) trained on a large corpus of speech and non-speech data, combined with a mixture-of-Gaussians (MoG) model of phoneme spectra. The DNN takes as input the magnitude spectrum of the reference microphone signal z_ref(l,k) and outputs a posterior probability for each phoneme class at each time-frequency bin. These phoneme posteriors are combined with the MoG model to generate a time-frequency SPP map ρ(l,k), where each element ρ(l,k) represents the probability that speech is present at time-frame l and frequency bin k. The SPP map is aggregated across frequency to produce a frame-level VAD decision:

VAD(l) = 1 if ∑_k ρ(l,k) > T_r, else 0

where T_r is a threshold determined empirically as one-quarter of the total number of frequency bins (T_r = N_DFT / 4). This threshold is chosen to balance sensitivity to speech activity with robustness to false positives caused by non-speech transients. When VAD(l) = 0, indicating a noise-only frame, the noise covariance matrix is recursively updated using a low learning rate α:

Φ_vv(l,k) = α Φ_vv(l-1,k) + (1 - α) z(l,k) z^H(l,k)

When VAD(l) = 1, indicating the presence of speech, no update is performed, thereby preventing the contamination of the noise estimate by speech components. This mechanism ensures that the noise covariance matrix remains accurate even in environments where background noise is non-stationary or slowly varying, such as in the presence of fluctuating HVAC systems or intermittent ambient noise.

Simultaneously, the system estimates the RTF of the currently active speaker. This estimation is performed during frames where the VAD indicates speech activity and where the system determines that only one speaker is likely active. The estimation is based on generalized eigenvalue decomposition (GEVD) of the signal correlation matrix Φ_zz(l,k) and the noise covariance matrix Φ_vv(l,k):

Φ_zz(l,k) f(k) = λ(k) Φ_vv(l,k) f(k)

where f(k) is the generalized eigenvector and λ(k) is the corresponding generalized eigenvalue. Under the assumption that only one speaker is active during the segment, the eigenvector associated with the largest eigenvalue is proportional to the RTF of the active speaker. This eigenvector is normalized to form an estimated RTF:

ĉ(l,k) = f_max(k) / ||f_max(k)||

where f_max(k) is the generalized eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue. The estimated RTF ĉ(l,k) is then compared to each entry in the pre-trained RTF library using a cosine similarity metric:

D_s(l,k) = |ĉ^H(l,k) c_s(k)| / (||ĉ(l,k)|| ||c_s(k)||)

where c_s(k) is the s-th RTF in the library. The cosine similarity is computed for each frequency bin k and for each of the N_s positions. The aggregated similarity for each position is obtained by averaging across frequency:

D_s(l) = (1/K) ∑_k D_s(l,k)

The position with the highest aggregated similarity is identified as the location of the active speaker:

I(l) = argmax_s D_s(l)

If I(l) corresponds to the known location of the desired speaker, then the RTF associated with that position is used to update the desired constraint in the LCMV beamformer. If I(l) corresponds to a known interfering speaker position, then the RTF associated with that position is used to update the interference constraint. If the system determines that both speakers are active simultaneously—indicated by a lack of dominant similarity across the RTF library, such as when the top two positions have similar D_s(l) values within a predefined tolerance—the RTF matrix is held constant to prevent erroneous updates during double-talk. This mechanism ensures that the beamformer’s constraints remain aligned with the true spatial locations of the speakers, even when their identities are unknown and their positions change over time.

The LCMV beamformer is then applied to the multi-channel signal z(l,k) to produce a single-channel output ŝ_d(l,k). The beamformer is designed to satisfy the following linear constraints:

C^H(l,k) w(l,k) = g(l,k)

where C(l,k) is the RTF matrix composed of the desired and interfering RTFs, w(l,k) is the beamformer weight vector, and g(l,k) is the desired response vector, set to [1, 0]^T to preserve the desired speaker while suppressing the interfering speaker. The optimal solution to this constrained optimization problem is given by:

w(l,k) = Φ_vv^{-1}(l,k) C(l,k) [C^H(l,k) Φ_vv^{-1}(l,k) C(l,k)]^{-1} g(l,k)

The resulting beamformer output is:

ŝ_d(l,k) = w^H(l,k) z(l,k)

This output represents a spatially filtered version of the desired speaker’s voice, with the interfering speaker and background noise significantly attenuated. However, due to residual leakage, spectral distortion, and imperfect RTF estimation, the output still contains some noise and artifacts. To further enhance the quality of the output, the system applies a post-filter based on the same NN-MM algorithm used for SPP estimation. The post-filter operates on the single-channel beamformer output ŝ_d(l,k) and generates a soft spectral attenuation mask β(l,k) that is applied in the log-spectral domain:

ŝ_d'(l,k) = exp(β(l,k) log|ŝ_d(l,k)|) · sign(ŝ_d(l,k))

where β(l,k) is a value between 0 and 1, determined by the NN-MM algorithm, and represents the degree of attenuation to be applied at each time-frequency bin. The NN-MM algorithm uses the DNN to classify each time-frequency bin into a phoneme class, and then applies the corresponding Gaussian component from the MoG model to estimate the ideal spectral magnitude of the speech signal. The attenuation mask is computed as the ratio of the estimated clean speech magnitude to the observed noisy magnitude, clipped to prevent over-attenuation. This post-filtering stage selectively enhances speech components while preserving the natural timbre and prosody of the voice, resulting in a final output that is significantly more intelligible and natural-sounding than the beamformer output alone.

The system is fully autonomous and requires no user input during operation. It does not rely on wake words, speaker identification, or external triggers. The calibration phase, which establishes the RTF library, is performed once during system setup and does not need to be repeated. The system adapts to changes in speaker location, background noise levels, and speech activity patterns in real time, making it suitable for deployment in dynamic environments. Experimental evaluations conducted in a low-reverberation conference room environment with seven microphones and six speakers demonstrate that the system achieves an average improvement in signal-to-interference ratio (SIR) of 15.3 dB and an average improvement in signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of 12.7 dB across input SNR levels ranging from -5 dB to 15 dB and input SIR levels from -5 dB to 15 dB. The performance is consistent across male and female speakers, different speaking rates, and varying levels of background noise. The system outperforms conventional MVDR beamformers, static LCMV configurations, and other state-of-the-art speech enhancement methods in both objective metrics and subjective listening tests. The combination of spatial beamforming, position-based constraint adaptation, and spectral post-filtering results in a synergistic enhancement effect that neither component could achieve independently. The invention thus represents a fundamental advancement in the field of multi-speaker speech enhancement, offering a robust, adaptive, and fully automated solution that bridges the gap between laboratory performance and real-world usability.
