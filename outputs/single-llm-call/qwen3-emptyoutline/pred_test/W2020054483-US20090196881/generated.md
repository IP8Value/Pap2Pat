# DESCRIPTION

## INTRODUCTION

The emergence of spring viremia of carp virus (SVCV) as a globally significant aquatic pathogen has necessitated the development of effective, scalable, and environmentally sustainable prophylactic interventions. Historically, SVCV has been responsible for catastrophic mortality events in cyprinid populations across Europe and, more recently, North America, where its introduction has triggered urgent regulatory responses and biosecurity protocols. The virus, a member of the Rhabdoviridae family, exhibits a narrow but devastating host range, primarily targeting common carp (Cyprinus carpio carpio) and its ornamental variant, koi (Cyprinus carpio koi), though susceptibility has been documented across multiple cyprinid species including goldfish (Carassius auratus), crucian carp, and tench. Outbreaks are strongly correlated with seasonal temperature transitions, particularly during the spring when water temperatures rise from winter lows into the optimal pathogenic range of 11 to 17 degrees Celsius. Under these conditions, the virus induces systemic infection characterized by hemorrhagic necrosis of the spleen, kidney, intestine, and air bladder, culminating in rapid and often near-total mortality within affected populations. The absence of any commercially available therapeutic or prophylactic agent has left aquaculture operations and wild fish populations vulnerable to repeated incursions, particularly in regions where SVCV remains an exotic pathogen subject to strict international reporting requirements under the Office International des Epizooties (OIE).

Prior attempts to mitigate SVCV through traditional vaccine platforms—such as inactivated whole-virus preparations—have yielded inconsistent and largely inadequate levels of protection. These formulations are burdened by technical challenges including incomplete viral inactivation, risks of residual pathogenicity, high production costs, and regulatory constraints that impede widespread deployment. Furthermore, the immunological response elicited by these vaccines has not been quantitatively validated in controlled challenge settings, limiting their utility as reliable tools for disease prevention. In response to these limitations, DNA vaccine technology has emerged as a promising alternative, offering advantages such as rapid design and manufacturing, thermal stability, absence of chemical adjuvants, and the potential for long-term immunity without risk of reversion to virulence. While DNA vaccines have demonstrated remarkable success against other rhabdoviral pathogens in salmonids, including infectious hematopoietic necrosis virus (IHNV) and viral hemorrhagic septicemia virus (VHSV), analogous efforts targeting SVCV have historically underperformed, particularly when based on European isolates. This discrepancy is likely attributable to genetic divergence between SVCV genogroups: European strains belong to genogroups Ib, Ic, and Id, whereas all North American isolates cluster within the Ia genogroup, exhibiting significant sequence variation in the glycoprotein gene—the primary target of protective immune responses. Consequently, vaccines derived from heterologous strains may fail to elicit sufficient cross-reactive immunity, rendering them ineffective in the context of North American outbreaks.

This invention addresses these critical gaps by introducing a novel DNA vaccine construct engineered to express the complete glycoprotein gene of the North Carolina isolate of SVCV (SVCVnc), a representative strain of the Ia genogroup responsible for all known introductions and outbreaks in North America. The vaccine construct, designated pSGnc, was developed through precise molecular cloning techniques that ensured fidelity of the native G-gene sequence, including all structural and antigenic determinants critical for immune recognition. Unlike prior attempts that utilized fragmented or mixed plasmid cocktails, this invention employs a single, fully functional plasmid encoding the entire glycoprotein under the control of a constitutive eukaryotic promoter, enabling robust and sustained in vivo expression. The efficacy of this construct was rigorously evaluated through a series of four independent vaccination-challenge trials conducted in koi, a species identified through systematic susceptibility testing as the most reliable and reproducible model for SVCVnc infection. These trials employed a standardized challenge protocol incorporating a rapid cold-water stressor to synchronize immune vulnerability, followed by homologous intraperitoneal exposure to a lethal dose of SVCVnc. The results demonstrated consistent and statistically significant protection across all trials, with relative percent survival values ranging from 50% to 88%, and in two trials, complete absence of detectable virus in all surviving vaccinated fish. Importantly, no protection was conferred by either a non-vaccinated control group or a mock vaccine expressing luciferase, confirming that the observed immunity was specifically attributable to the SVCV glycoprotein expressed by pSGnc. This invention represents the first documented instance of a DNA vaccine conferring robust, reproducible, and quantifiable protection against a North American SVCV strain in koi, and establishes a new benchmark for efficacy in SVCV vaccine development. The implications extend beyond aquaculture, offering a scalable, cost-effective, and ecologically responsible solution to prevent the establishment and spread of SVCV in native and introduced cyprinid populations across North America and other regions at risk of incursion.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a DNA vaccine for the prevention of spring viremia of carp virus (SVCV) infection in cyprinid fish, particularly koi and common carp, comprising a plasmid vector encoding the complete glycoprotein gene of a North American isolate of SVCV, specifically the North Carolina strain (SVCVnc), under the control of a eukaryotic promoter. The plasmid construct, designated pSGnc, is designed to be administered via intramuscular injection into the epaxial musculature of live fish, where it is taken up by host cells, resulting in the endogenous expression of the SVCV glycoprotein and subsequent induction of a protective adaptive immune response. The glycoprotein gene sequence utilized in this invention is identical to the full-length nucleotide sequence of the SVCVnc isolate deposited under GenBank accession number AY527273, and contains no mutations, deletions, or insertions introduced during cloning or amplification. The plasmid backbone is derived from the pcDNA3.1 vector, which provides a strong cytomegalovirus immediate-early promoter, a polyadenylation signal, and bacterial origin of replication for scalable production in Escherichia coli. The vaccine is formulated in sterile phosphate-buffered saline and delivered in a volume of fifty microliters per fish, containing a dose of ten micrograms of purified plasmid DNA, a dosage determined through empirical optimization to balance immunogenicity with practicality in aquaculture settings. The invention further encompasses a method of immunizing cyprinid fish against SVCV by administering the pSGnc vaccine at least twenty-eight days prior to anticipated exposure to the virus, with the optimal immune response observed following exposure to a rapid thermal stressor from ambient water temperatures of 19–20 degrees Celsius to a challenge temperature of 10–12 degrees Celsius, mimicking natural seasonal transitions that predispose fish to SVCV infection. The vaccine confers significant protection against homologous SVCVnc challenge, as evidenced by cumulative percent mortality reductions of 50% to 88% compared to unvaccinated controls, and in some trials, complete absence of detectable virus in all surviving fish thirty days post-challenge. The invention further includes the use of koi as the primary target species for vaccination, based on their consistent and reproducible susceptibility to SVCVnc under controlled laboratory conditions, which distinguishes them from other cyprinids such as goldfish, which demonstrated low susceptibility in preliminary challenge trials. The vaccine is stable under standard storage conditions, does not require refrigeration for short-term transport, and can be manufactured at scale using standard plasmid purification techniques, making it suitable for deployment in commercial aquaculture facilities, ornamental fish farms, and conservation programs. The invention further provides a method for evaluating vaccine efficacy through the use of a standardized challenge model involving intraperitoneal inoculation with a lethal dose of SVCVnc following a rapid cold-water stressor, thereby enabling consistent and reproducible assessment of protective immunity across multiple trials and laboratories. The vaccine is non-infectious, non-replicating, and contains no live viral components, eliminating the risk of environmental contamination or reversion to pathogenicity. The invention further encompasses the use of the pSGnc vaccine in combination with biosecurity protocols, including quarantine procedures, water treatment, and pathogen monitoring, to form a comprehensive strategy for the prevention and control of SVCV in susceptible fish populations. The invention represents a substantial advancement over prior art by providing the first DNA vaccine with proven efficacy against a North American SVCV isolate, demonstrating consistent protection across varying fish ages and challenge doses, and establishing a validated challenge model that enables reliable evaluation of vaccine performance in a species of high economic and ecological importance.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Susceptibility Testing of Goldfish and Koi

The development of a reliable and reproducible experimental challenge model was an essential prerequisite to the evaluation of any potential vaccine candidate against spring viremia of carp virus (SVCV). Prior to the initiation of vaccination trials, a comprehensive series of susceptibility experiments were conducted to identify the most appropriate host species and challenge protocol for subsequent vaccine efficacy assessments. Goldfish (Carassius auratus) and koi (Cyprinus carpio koi) were selected as candidate species due to their close phylogenetic relationship to the primary natural host, common carp, and their documented susceptibility to SVCV in prior reports. Goldfish were obtained from a facility certified as free of SVCV by international standards and maintained under controlled conditions at a water temperature of 16 to 18 degrees Celsius, with daily monitoring for health indicators and feeding every other day with a standardized commercial pellet diet. Koi were sourced from a domestic, specific pathogen-free breeding program that participates in the United States Department of Agriculture’s Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service screening protocol, ensuring the absence of known pathogens including SVCV. Both species were acclimated to the laboratory environment for a minimum of two weeks prior to experimentation to minimize stress-induced confounding variables. All procedures were conducted within an aquatic biosafety level 3 containment facility, adhering strictly to international regulations governing the handling of notifiable aquatic pathogens.

Initial susceptibility trials involved both immersion and intraperitoneal challenge routes. Goldfish, averaging 14.6 grams in weight, were exposed to three different concentrations of SVCVnc via immersion: 1 × 10³, 1 × 10⁴, and 1 × 10⁵ plaque-forming units per milliliter. Each concentration was tested in duplicate tanks containing ten fish per tank, with mock challenges conducted using culture medium alone as a negative control. In parallel, a single intraperitoneal challenge was administered to goldfish using a dose of 1 × 10⁶ plaque-forming units per fish. Over a thirty-day observation period, the cumulative percent mortality among goldfish exposed to any of the immersion or injection challenges remained below 4%, with no clinical signs of disease observed in any surviving individuals. These results indicated that, under the conditions tested, goldfish exhibited a high degree of resistance to the North Carolina isolate of SVCV, despite prior literature suggesting susceptibility to European strains. This finding was consistent across two independent experimental replicates, suggesting that resistance may be strain-specific, age-dependent, or influenced by genetic factors within the specific goldfish stock utilized.

In contrast, koi demonstrated a marked and consistent susceptibility to SVCVnc. Preliminary trials involving one-month-old koi weighing approximately 0.94 grams revealed that all individuals challenged via intraperitoneal injection with 1 × 10⁶ plaque-forming units per fish succumbed to infection within fourteen days, while 75% of those exposed via immersion at 1 × 10⁵ plaque-forming units per milliliter died within the same timeframe. These results were confirmed in a second, larger-scale experiment involving duplicate groups of ten koi per treatment, which yielded identical mortality outcomes. Virus quantification of whole-body homogenates from dead and surviving koi confirmed the presence of high viral titers exceeding 1 × 10⁶ plaque-forming units per gram in all mortalities, while survivors also tested positive for virus, indicating systemic infection even in animals that did not immediately succumb. These findings established koi as the most suitable model species for subsequent vaccine trials, as they consistently developed lethal infection following exposure to SVCVnc under controlled conditions, whereas goldfish did not.

To further refine the challenge protocol, two distinct cold-water stress regimens were evaluated for their ability to enhance susceptibility. The first regimen, previously described in the literature, involved a gradual acclimation process in which water temperature was reduced by one degree Celsius per day over a fourteen-day period until reaching 10–12 degrees Celsius, followed by a five-day holding period prior to challenge. The second regimen, a rapid stressor, involved direct transfer of fish from stock water temperatures of 16–18 degrees Celsius to challenge temperatures of 10–12 degrees Celsius on the same day as virus exposure. Both regimens were tested in duplicate groups of ten koi at three and a half months of age, with identical challenge doses administered via immersion and intraperitoneal injection. The cumulative percent mortality was identical between the two stress protocols for both challenge routes: 75% for immersion and 100% for injection. The mean day to death, calculated as the sum of days post-challenge at which each fish died divided by the total number of mortalities, showed no statistically significant difference between the two protocols. Furthermore, virus titers in dead and surviving fish were comparable across both stress regimens, with all sampled fish testing positive for SVCVnc at concentrations exceeding 1 × 10⁶ plaque-forming units per gram. These results demonstrated that the rapid cold stressor was equally effective in inducing susceptibility to SVCVnc while significantly reducing the time required to prepare fish for challenge—from fourteen days to a single day. Given the logistical and economic advantages of the rapid stressor, it was adopted as the standard protocol for all subsequent vaccination and challenge experiments. The combination of koi as the host species and the rapid cold-water stressor as the immune-sensitizing trigger established a robust, reproducible, and efficient challenge model that enabled the reliable assessment of vaccine efficacy under controlled conditions.

### Example 2

The development of the pSGnc DNA vaccine construct was initiated following the molecular characterization of the glycoprotein gene from the North Carolina isolate of SVCV (SVCVnc), which had been previously sequenced and deposited under GenBank accession number AY527273. The glycoprotein gene was selected as the target for vaccine design because it encodes the sole surface-exposed antigen of the virus, responsible for receptor binding, membrane fusion, and the primary target of neutralizing antibodies and cytotoxic T-cell responses in fish. Unlike other rhabdoviruses such as IHNV and VHSV, which possess a nonvirion (NV) gene of uncertain function, SVCV lacks this gene, making the glycoprotein the dominant and most critical antigenic determinant for protective immunity. The full-length glycoprotein gene was amplified from total RNA extracted from SVCVnc viral stock using reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) with gene-specific primers designed to flank the entire open reading frame. The forward primer, 5′-CACCATGTCTATCATCAGCTACATC-3′, incorporated an ATG start codon and a Kozak sequence to enhance translation initiation, while the reverse primer, 5′-CTAAACGAAGGACCGCATTTCGTG-3′, included a stop codon and complementary sequence for directional cloning. Amplification was performed under optimized thermal cycling conditions to ensure fidelity, with verification of product size and specificity through agarose gel electrophoresis and sequencing of the initial amplicon.

The resulting PCR product was cloned directionally into the pcDNA3.1(+) expression vector using the TOPO TA cloning system, which facilitates efficient ligation of PCR products with 3′-A overhangs generated by Taq polymerase. Transformants were selected using ampicillin resistance and screened via rapid colony PCR using vector-specific primers (T7 and BGH) and internal SVCV glycoprotein primers (379+ and 978-/F1) to confirm the presence and orientation of the insert. Positive clones were subjected to restriction enzyme digestion with EcoRI and XhoI to verify the expected insert size of approximately 1,500 base pairs. A single clone, designated pSGnc, was selected for further characterization based on the integrity of the insert and the absence of secondary mutations. The entire nucleotide sequence of the glycoprotein gene within pSGnc was determined by Sanger sequencing using a panel of overlapping primers spanning the full length of the insert. Comparison with the original SVCVnc sequence (AY527273) confirmed 100% identity, with no nucleotide substitutions, insertions, or deletions introduced during RT-PCR amplification or cloning. This fidelity was critical, as even minor mutations in the glycoprotein gene could alter antigenic epitopes and compromise vaccine efficacy. The plasmid was then propagated in Escherichia coli DH5α cells using standard overnight culture techniques, followed by purification via alkaline lysis and cesium chloride gradient centrifugation to achieve endotoxin-free, high-purity plasmid DNA suitable for in vivo administration. The final plasmid preparation was quantified spectrophotometrically and confirmed for purity by agarose gel electrophoresis, with no detectable RNA or genomic DNA contamination.

The vaccine formulation was prepared by resuspending the purified pSGnc plasmid in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at a concentration of 200 micrograms per milliliter, resulting in a delivery volume of fifty microliters per fish containing ten micrograms of plasmid DNA. This dosage was selected based on prior studies with IHNV and VHSV DNA vaccines in juvenile salmonids, which demonstrated that doses between one and ten micrograms were sufficient to elicit protective immunity, while higher doses did not consistently improve outcomes and increased the risk of non-specific immune activation. The vaccine was administered via intramuscular injection into the epaxial musculature, located midway between the posterior edge of the dorsal fin and the lateral line, a site previously validated for efficient DNA uptake and antigen presentation in cyprinids. Injection was performed under mild anesthesia induced by immersion in tricaine methane sulfonate buffered to pH 7.5, ensuring minimal stress and precise delivery. Following vaccination, fish were transferred to separate holding tanks maintained at 19–20 degrees Celsius, a temperature slightly elevated from the standard rearing temperature to enhance immune cell activity and promote antigen expression. Fish were monitored daily for signs of distress, and feeding was resumed every other day to maintain physiological health without inducing excessive metabolic stress. The mock vaccine control, designated pLuc, consisted of the pcDNA3.1 vector containing the firefly luciferase gene, which encodes a non-fish pathogen-derived protein with no known immunological cross-reactivity to SVCV. This construct was produced using identical purification methods and administered at the same dose and route to ensure that any observed differences in survival were attributable to the SVCV glycoprotein antigen and not to non-specific effects of plasmid DNA or injection trauma.

### Example 3

Four independent vaccination-challenge trials were conducted to evaluate the efficacy of the pSGnc DNA vaccine under varying conditions of fish age, challenge dose, and season. Each trial followed a standardized protocol in which three treatment groups were established: (1) fish vaccinated with pSGnc, (2) fish vaccinated with the pLuc mock vaccine, and (3) non-vaccinated controls that received no injection and were housed in separate tanks receiving the same water source. The average weight and age of koi at the time of vaccination varied across trials to assess whether vaccine efficacy was influenced by developmental stage. In Trial A, koi averaged 4.2 months of age and weighed 3.8 grams; in Trial B, fish were 4.5 months old and weighed 4.1 grams; in Trial C, fish were 6.1 months old and weighed 8.9 grams; and in Trial D, fish were 6.3 months old and weighed 9.2 grams. Despite these differences, all fish were vaccinated using the same protocol: a single intramuscular injection of ten micrograms of pSGnc or pLuc in fifty microliters of PBS. Twenty-eight days following vaccination, corresponding to approximately 546 degree-days based on a base temperature of 10 degrees Celsius, fish were transferred to the aquatic biosafety level 3 facility and subjected to the rapid cold-water stressor, with water temperature reduced from 19–20 degrees Celsius to 10–12 degrees Celsius on the same day. Twenty-four hours after the temperature shift, fish were challenged via intraperitoneal injection with a lethal dose of SVCVnc. Challenge doses were adjusted across trials to evaluate dose-dependent protection: Trial A used 1 × 10⁶ plaque-forming units per fish, Trial B used the same dose, Trial C used 5 × 10⁵ plaque-forming units per fish, and Trial D used 1 × 10⁵ plaque-forming units per fish. Mock-challenged fish received an equivalent volume of culture medium without virus. Each treatment group was divided into duplicate tanks of ten fish per tank to ensure statistical validity and reproducibility.

Fish were monitored daily for mortality, and dead individuals were immediately removed, stored at −80 degrees Celsius, and processed for virus quantification via plaque assay on epithelioma papulosum cyprini (EPC) cell monolayers. Survivors were sampled at thirty days post-challenge for virus detection to determine whether infection had been cleared. Cumulative percent mortality (CPM) was calculated as the number of dead fish divided by the total number of fish per treatment group, multiplied by 100. Relative percent survival (RPS) was calculated using the formula: [1 − (CPM of vaccinated group / CPM of negative control)] × 100. In all four trials, the non-vaccinated and pLuc-vaccinated control groups exhibited high mortality rates ranging from 70% to 100%, with no statistically significant difference between the two control groups, confirming that the plasmid backbone and injection procedure did not confer protection. In contrast, the pSGnc-vaccinated groups demonstrated significantly reduced mortality, with CPM values of 50%, 30%, 10%, and 10% in Trials A, B, C, and D, respectively. Corresponding RPS values were 50%, 70%, 88%, and 88%, demonstrating consistent and statistically significant protection (p ≤ 0.05) in all trials, as determined by Fisher’s exact test and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with Mantel-Cox log-rank testing. The survival curves for pSGnc-vaccinated fish diverged significantly from those of control groups, with a marked delay in time to death and a higher proportion of long-term survivors.

Virus titers in dead fish from all treatment groups ranged from 7.9 × 10⁵ to 1.8 × 10⁷ plaque-forming units per gram, confirming that mortality was directly attributable to SVCVnc infection. In contrast, survivors from the non-vaccinated and pLuc groups consistently tested positive for virus, with titers ranging from 4.1 × 10³ to 4.5 × 10⁵ plaque-forming units per gram, indicating persistent infection. Among pSGnc-vaccinated survivors, virus was undetectable in all 23 fish tested in Trials C and D, and only three of twelve survivors in Trials A and B tested positive, with titers below 1 × 10⁴ plaque-forming units per gram. The absence of detectable virus in the majority of vaccinated survivors strongly suggests that the vaccine not only reduced mortality but also facilitated complete viral clearance, indicating the induction of a functional and protective adaptive immune response. The variation in RPS across trials did not correlate with fish age, weight, or challenge dose, as higher protection was observed in both younger fish challenged with higher doses (Trial B) and older fish challenged with lower doses (Trial D). This suggests that the vaccine’s efficacy is robust across a range of conditions and is not dependent on a single variable. The consistency of protection across four independent trials, conducted at different times of the year and with varying fish cohorts, confirms that the pSGnc vaccine confers reliable and reproducible immunity against homologous SVCVnc challenge. The invention thus provides a prophylactic tool capable of preventing disease in koi populations under realistic aquaculture conditions, where environmental variability and pathogen exposure are inevitable. The vaccine’s performance exceeds that of all previously reported SVCV DNA constructs, which have demonstrated RPS values below 50% and inconsistent protection, particularly when derived from European genogroups. This invention represents the first DNA vaccine to achieve RPS values exceeding 85% in koi and to demonstrate complete viral clearance in a substantial proportion of vaccinated survivors, establishing a new standard for SVCV vaccine efficacy.
