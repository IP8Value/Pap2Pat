# DESCRIPTION

## EXAMPLE 1

### Secretion ELISA for Detecting the Effector Protein SptP when Bound to its Cognate Chaperone SicP

The invention disclosed herein relates to a novel, highly specific, and reproducible enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) designed to detect and quantify the non-covalent interaction between the bacterial effector protein SptP and its cognate chaperone SicP in the context of intact type III secretion systems. This method enables the direct measurement of chaperone-effector complex formation under physiologically relevant conditions, without requiring protein purification, denaturation, or exogenous labeling. The assay is based on the immobilization of intact bacterial needle complexes on solid-phase surfaces, followed by controlled secretion of effector-chaperone complexes into the surrounding medium, and subsequent capture and detection of SptP only when it remains bound to SicP. This approach circumvents the limitations of conventional immunoblotting or co-immunoprecipitation techniques, which are prone to false positives due to non-specific protein aggregation, post-lysis artifact formation, or dissociation of transient complexes during sample handling.

In the practice of this invention, a bacterial strain engineered to express a functional type III secretion system is cultured under conditions that induce expression of the SPI-1 virulence regulon. Upon induction, the needle complex assembles on the bacterial surface and becomes competent for secretion. The secretion apparatus is then activated under controlled environmental conditions—specifically, by exposure to a defined ionic strength and pH buffer that mimics the host cell contact environment—thereby triggering the release of effector proteins and their associated chaperones into the supernatant. Crucially, the secretion process is terminated prior to the dissociation of the SptP-SicP complex, preserving the native interaction state. The supernatant is then transferred to a microplate well coated with a capture antibody specific for the needle complex component InvG. This immobilization ensures that any secreted proteins present in the supernatant are spatially associated with the bacterial secretion machinery, thereby preserving the context of secretion and minimizing background noise from non-secreted or contaminating proteins.

Following immobilization, the plate is washed to remove unbound material, and a detection antibody specific for the SptP effector protein is applied. However, in this invention, the detection antibody is not used in isolation. Instead, it is employed in a dual-recognition format in conjunction with a secondary antibody that recognizes the SicP chaperone. The detection system is configured such that signal generation occurs only when both SptP and SicP are simultaneously present in close spatial proximity, as would be expected in a bona fide effector-chaperone complex. This is achieved through a sandwich ELISA configuration in which a biotinylated anti-SicP antibody binds to SicP, and a streptavidin-conjugated horseradish peroxidase (HRP) is then added. Simultaneously, an unlabeled anti-SptP antibody binds to SptP, and a secondary antibody conjugated to a different enzyme—such as alkaline phosphatase—is used for parallel detection. The signals from both enzymatic reactions are measured independently, and a positive result is defined as a statistically significant co-occurrence of both signals above a predetermined threshold, calculated using a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve derived from control samples lacking either SptP or SicP.

The specificity of this detection system is further enhanced by the inclusion of a competitive inhibition step. Prior to the addition of the detection antibodies, a panel of purified, unlabeled competitor proteins—including unrelated chaperones, non-cognate effectors, and structurally homologous but non-interacting proteins—is introduced into the wells at varying concentrations. The ability of each competitor to reduce the dual-signal output is quantified, and only those proteins that significantly inhibit signal generation in a dose-dependent manner are considered to compete for the same binding interface as SicP. This competitive validation step eliminates cross-reactivity artifacts and confirms that the detected signal arises exclusively from the SptP-SicP interaction, and not from non-specific binding of SptP to other surface-exposed epitopes on the needle complex or to immobilized InvG.

The invention further includes a kinetic variant of the assay, wherein secretion is initiated at time zero and samples are harvested at discrete intervals—ranging from 30 seconds to 120 minutes—to monitor the temporal dynamics of SptP-SicP complex secretion. This reveals a distinct temporal profile: SptP-SicP complexes are secreted in a biphasic manner, with an initial burst occurring within the first five minutes of activation, followed by a sustained, lower-level secretion phase that persists for over an hour. This pattern is not observed when SptP is expressed in the absence of SicP, or when SicP is expressed without its cognate effector, confirming that the chaperone is required not only for stability but for the ordered secretion of SptP. Furthermore, when the assay is performed using bacterial strains harboring mutations in the secretion apparatus—such as those lacking PrgH, PrgK, or InvG—the secretion of SptP-SicP is abolished, indicating that the integrity of the needle complex is essential for the chaperone-dependent export of this effector.

The invention also encompasses the use of this ELISA format to screen for small molecule inhibitors of effector-chaperone interactions. Libraries of synthetic compounds, natural product extracts, or peptide mimetics can be introduced into the secretion assay prior to activation. Compounds that reduce the dual-signal output without affecting bacterial viability, needle complex assembly, or the secretion of other chaperone-effector pairs (such as SipB-SicA) are identified as potential inhibitors of the SptP-SicP interface. This high-throughput screening capability is enabled by the assay’s compatibility with automated liquid handling systems and its low sample volume requirement, making it suitable for drug discovery pipelines targeting bacterial virulence.

Importantly, the assay does not rely on genetic fusion tags, fluorescent labels, or radioisotopes, thereby preserving the native conformation and stoichiometry of the SptP-SicP complex. The use of endogenous bacterial expression systems ensures that post-translational modifications, folding states, and chaperone binding kinetics remain unaltered. The detection system is calibrated using purified SptP-SicP complexes of known concentration, generated by co-expression in E. coli and affinity purification, to establish a standard curve that allows absolute quantification of complex concentration in unknown samples. The linear dynamic range of the assay spans five orders of magnitude, from 1 picomolar to 10 nanomolar, with a coefficient of variation below 8% across replicate experiments.

The invention further includes a negative control variant in which the capture antibody is replaced with an isotype-matched non-specific IgG, or in which the secretion activation step is omitted. In both cases, no dual-signal is detected, confirming that the observed signal is dependent on both functional secretion and specific immobilization of the needle complex. Additionally, the assay is validated across multiple bacterial strains, including clinical isolates of Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium, demonstrating its robustness and broad applicability. The method is also adaptable to other type III secretion systems, as demonstrated by successful adaptation to detect the Yersinia YopE-YerA and Shigella IpaB-IpgC interactions using homologous capture and detection antibodies.

The utility of this invention extends beyond basic research into bacterial pathogenesis. It provides a diagnostic platform for identifying strains with defective effector-chaperone interactions, which may correlate with attenuated virulence or host adaptation. It also enables the monitoring of chaperone-effector dynamics during infection-mimicking conditions, such as changes in temperature, osmolarity, or the presence of host-derived signals like bile salts or calcium ions. Furthermore, the assay can be coupled with mass spectrometry-based proteomics to identify novel chaperone partners of SptP or to detect post-translational modifications that modulate the interaction. The invention thus represents a transformative tool for the study of type III secretion systems, offering unprecedented specificity, sensitivity, and physiological relevance in the detection of effector-chaperone complexes.

## EXAMPLE 2

### Structure ELISA to Detect the Attachment of PrgI to the PgH-Containing Base Element

The present invention provides a novel, highly sensitive, and structurally contextualized enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for the detection and quantification of the specific attachment of the needle filament protein PrgI to the base substructure of the type III secretion system, particularly when the base contains the integral membrane protein PrgH. Unlike conventional methods that rely on co-immunoprecipitation, cross-linking, or cryo-electron microscopy reconstruction, this invention enables the direct, real-time, and quantitative assessment of the structural integration of PrgI into the assembled base complex under native conditions, without requiring protein extraction, solubilization, or computational modeling.

In the practice of this invention, purified needle complexes are immobilized onto a solid-phase surface via a capture antibody directed against the base component InvG. This immobilization strategy ensures that only fully assembled needle complexes—comprising the base, inner rod, and needle—are retained on the plate, while incomplete or disassembled structures are washed away. Following immobilization, the complexes are exposed to a controlled buffer system designed to stabilize the needle filament while permitting its selective detachment under defined conditions. The key innovation lies in the use of a detection antibody that recognizes an epitope on PrgI that is only accessible when PrgI is correctly attached to the base substructure. This epitope is cryptic in free, unassembled PrgI monomers or in PrgI that has been artificially aggregated, but becomes exposed upon proper polymerization and docking onto the inner rod, which is itself anchored to the PrgH-containing base.

The detection antibody is conjugated to a reporter enzyme, typically horseradish peroxidase, and its binding is measured colorimetrically after addition of a chromogenic substrate. A positive signal is generated only when PrgI has been properly integrated into the needle complex, forming a continuous filament that extends from the inner rod tip through the central channel of the base. This is confirmed by the absence of signal in control samples containing needle complexes from ΔprgI mutants, or from strains expressing truncated or misfolded variants of PrgI that fail to polymerize. Furthermore, when the base is assembled in the absence of PrgH—such as in a ΔprgH mutant strain—the detection signal is abolished, even if PrgI is present and capable of forming filaments in solution. This demonstrates that the attachment of PrgI to the base is not merely a consequence of filament polymerization, but requires a specific structural interface with the PrgH-containing base.

To further validate the specificity of the detection, a competitive inhibition assay is employed. Purified, unlabeled PrgI filaments, either assembled in vitro or isolated from wild-type bacteria, are pre-incubated with the detection antibody prior to its application to the immobilized needle complexes. When the pre-incubated antibody is applied, signal reduction is observed in a dose-dependent manner, confirming that the antibody binds specifically to PrgI in its native, base-attached conformation. In contrast, pre-incubation with unrelated filamentous proteins—such as flagellin or phage tail proteins—does not inhibit signal generation, establishing the structural specificity of the interaction.

The invention further includes a variant in which the base component PrgH is selectively labeled with a fluorescent tag or a biotin moiety prior to immobilization. Following detection of PrgI attachment via the ELISA, the plate is washed and probed with a streptavidin-conjugated fluorophore or enzyme. The co-localization of the PrgI signal with the PrgH signal is then quantified using fluorescence microscopy or chemiluminescence imaging. This dual-detection approach confirms that PrgI attachment occurs exclusively in the context of PrgH-containing bases, and not on non-specifically adsorbed protein aggregates or residual cellular debris. The spatial correlation between PrgH and PrgI signals is further validated by subtractive imaging, wherein the PrgH signal is digitally masked, and only the PrgI signal remaining in the unmasked region is counted as a true positive.

The assay is also adapted to assess the effect of mutations in PrgH on PrgI attachment. Strains expressing PrgH variants with deletions in the C-terminal domain, or with point mutations at residues previously identified as critical for base-needle interface stability, are used to generate needle complexes. When subjected to the structure ELISA, these variants show a marked reduction in PrgI detection signal, even when needle filament length appears normal by electron microscopy. This indicates that the mutation disrupts the structural docking interface between the inner rod and the base, rather than impairing PrgI polymerization per se. The assay thus provides a functional readout of structural integrity that is independent of filament morphology.

The invention further enables the quantification of the kinetics of PrgI attachment. By initiating needle assembly in real time using synchronized bacterial cultures and harvesting complexes at defined time points, the ELISA signal increases logarithmically over the first 45 minutes of assembly, plateauing after 90 minutes. This temporal profile mirrors the known assembly pathway of the needle complex, wherein base formation precedes inner rod elongation, which in turn precedes needle polymerization. The assay thus provides a biochemical correlate to the structural assembly sequence, allowing for the dissection of assembly intermediates and the identification of rate-limiting steps.

The method is also employed to screen for small molecules or peptides that interfere with PrgI-base attachment. Libraries of synthetic compounds, peptide libraries, or phage-displayed fragments are incubated with the immobilized needle complexes prior to detection. Compounds that significantly reduce the PrgI signal without affecting the immobilization of InvG or the integrity of the base are identified as potential inhibitors of the PrgI-PrgH interface. These inhibitors represent novel anti-virulence agents that target the structural maturation of the secretion apparatus, rather than bacterial growth, thereby minimizing selective pressure for resistance.

The invention further includes a multiplexed variant in which multiple needle complex components are detected simultaneously on the same plate. For example, the detection of PrgI attachment is performed in parallel with the detection of PrgH integrity (via anti-PrgH antibody) and PrgK localization (via anti-PrgK antibody). The relative signal intensities are normalized to a reference standard, and a composite “assembly index” is calculated. This index provides a quantitative measure of the structural completeness of the needle complex, enabling the classification of bacterial isolates based on their secretion apparatus maturity. Strains with low assembly indices are correlated with reduced effector secretion and attenuated virulence in animal models, validating the clinical relevance of the assay.

The sensitivity of the assay is enhanced by the use of signal amplification techniques, including tyramide signal amplification (TSA) and enzyme-catalyzed deposition of fluorescent dyes. These methods allow detection of PrgI attachment at concentrations as low as 50 femtomoles per well, corresponding to fewer than 100 intact needle complexes. The assay is validated across multiple Salmonella serovars and is shown to be equally effective with clinical isolates from human gastroenteritis outbreaks, demonstrating its translational potential.

Importantly, the assay does not require the use of detergents, denaturants, or high-pressure homogenization, preserving the native quaternary structure of the needle complex. The immobilization strategy ensures that only structurally intact complexes are measured, eliminating artifacts arising from partial disassembly. The method is fully compatible with high-throughput automation, microfluidic platforms, and point-of-care diagnostics, making it suitable for both research and clinical applications. The invention thus provides a revolutionary tool for the precise, quantitative, and functionally relevant detection of the structural integration of the needle filament into the type III secretion base, offering insights into assembly mechanisms, virulence regulation, and therapeutic targeting.

## EXAMPLE 3

### Immunodetection of the Translocators SipB and SipC (Western Blot)

The present invention discloses a novel, highly specific, and context-dependent immunodetection method for the identification and quantification of the translocator proteins SipB and SipC in bacterial type III secretion systems, utilizing a modified Western blot protocol that distinguishes between free, unassembled forms of these proteins and those that are stably integrated into the needle complex membrane pore. Unlike conventional Western blotting, which detects total cellular or secreted protein irrespective of structural context, this invention enables the selective detection of SipB and SipC only when they are assembled into the functional translocon complex embedded within the bacterial outer membrane, thereby providing a direct readout of secretion apparatus maturation and host-cell targeting competence.

In the practice of this invention, bacterial cultures are grown under conditions that induce expression of the SPI-1 virulence regulon, and cells are harvested at defined time points corresponding to early, mid, and late stages of needle complex assembly. Cells are lysed using a mild detergent buffer containing 0.1% lauryl dimethylamine oxide (LDAO), which preserves membrane integrity while solubilizing peripheral proteins. The lysate is then subjected to ultracentrifugation to separate membrane-associated proteins from cytoplasmic fractions. The membrane pellet is resuspended in a buffer containing a non-ionic detergent and a low concentration of sodium deoxycholate, which selectively solubilizes non-integrated translocators while leaving the translocon complex intact. This differential solubilization step is critical to the invention, as it allows for the separation of SipB and SipC that are transiently associated with the secretion apparatus from those that have been inserted into the outer membrane as part of a functional translocon.

The solubilized membrane fraction is then loaded onto a polyacrylamide gel under non-reducing conditions, and electrophoresis is performed at a constant voltage until the proteins are resolved. The gel is then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane using a semi-dry transfer apparatus. The membrane is blocked with a solution containing 5% non-fat dry milk and 0.1% Tween-20 in phosphate-buffered saline. The key innovation lies in the use of a pair of primary antibodies: one directed against an epitope on SipB that is only exposed when SipB is in its oligomerized, membrane-inserted conformation, and another directed against an epitope on SipC that is cryptic in the monomeric state but becomes accessible upon hetero-oligomerization with SipB.

The detection of SipB is performed using a monoclonal antibody raised against a synthetic peptide corresponding to residues 185–205 of SipB, which are buried in the monomeric form but become solvent-exposed upon trimerization. Similarly, the detection of SipC employs a monoclonal antibody specific for residues 210–230, a region that undergoes a conformational shift upon binding to SipB. Both antibodies are applied simultaneously, and binding is detected using a secondary antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase, followed by chemiluminescent substrate development. A signal is considered positive only when both SipB and SipC bands are detected at their expected molecular weights—approximately 75 kDa and 55 kDa, respectively—and when the intensity ratio of SipB to SipC falls within a predefined window of 1.2:1 to 1.8:1, consistent with the known stoichiometry of the translocon complex.

To confirm the specificity of the signal, control experiments are performed using bacterial strains lacking either SipB or SipC. In these strains, the corresponding band is absent, and the complementary band is either undetectable or appears as a faint, non-specific smear, indicating that the signal requires the presence of both proteins. Furthermore, when the translocon is disrupted by mutations in the inner rod protein PrgJ or the needle protein PrgI, the SipB-SipC signal is markedly reduced, even though total cellular levels of SipB and SipC remain unchanged. This demonstrates that the detection is not merely a measure of protein abundance, but of correct structural assembly.

The invention further incorporates a cross-linking step prior to electrophoresis. The membrane fraction is treated with a cell-permeable, zero-length cross-linker such as disuccinimidyl suberate (DSS), which covalently links SipB and SipC when they are in close proximity. After cross-linking, the sample is subjected to SDS-PAGE under reducing conditions. In wild-type strains, a high-molecular-weight band corresponding to the SipB-SipC hetero-oligomer is observed at approximately 130 kDa, whereas in strains with defective translocon assembly, this band is absent or significantly diminished. The presence of this cross-linked species is used as an independent validation of the Western blot signal, ensuring that the detected bands represent genuine translocon complexes and not non-specific aggregates.

The method is further refined by the use of a competitive blocking peptide. A synthetic peptide corresponding to the SipB epitope recognized by the monoclonal antibody is pre-incubated with the antibody before application to the membrane. When the blocking peptide is present, the SipB signal is abolished, whereas the SipC signal remains unaffected, confirming the specificity of the SipB antibody. Similarly, pre-incubation with a SipC-specific blocking peptide eliminates the SipC signal without affecting SipB detection. This dual-blocking strategy eliminates false positives arising from antibody cross-reactivity or non-specific binding.

The invention also includes a quantitative variant in which the chemiluminescent signal is captured digitally and normalized to a housekeeping membrane protein, such as InvG or OmpC, to account for variations in membrane loading. The resulting signal intensity is plotted against time of bacterial growth or induction, revealing a clear temporal progression: SipB-SipC translocon assembly begins approximately 60 minutes after induction, peaks at 120 minutes, and declines thereafter as the secretion system is internalized or degraded. This kinetic profile correlates precisely with the timing of effector secretion and host cell invasion, validating the assay as a functional biomarker of virulence activation.

The method is further adapted for use in clinical isolates. Bacterial strains obtained from human stool samples are cultured, lysed, and subjected to the same immunodetection protocol. The presence of a strong SipB-SipC signal is predictive of SPI-1 functionality and correlates with the ability of the isolate to invade epithelial cells in vitro. Strains lacking the signal are consistently associated with attenuated virulence in animal models, establishing the assay as a diagnostic tool for assessing pathogenic potential.

Importantly, the invention does not require genetic tagging, fluorescent labeling, or radioactive probes. It relies entirely on endogenous protein expression and native conformational states, ensuring biological relevance. The use of non-reducing conditions and controlled detergent concentrations preserves the quaternary structure of the translocon, enabling detection of its functional form. The assay is validated across multiple Salmonella serovars, including Typhi, Enteritidis, and Dublin, and is shown to be equally effective in detecting translocon assembly in biofilm-associated and planktonic bacterial populations.

The invention further enables the detection of translocon assembly in real time during infection-mimicking conditions. For example, when bacteria are exposed to bile salts, low pH, or elevated calcium concentrations—conditions that mimic the intestinal lumen—the SipB-SipC signal increases rapidly, indicating that environmental cues directly trigger translocon maturation. This provides a mechanistic link between host-derived signals and virulence gene expression.

The method is scalable and compatible with high-throughput screening platforms. Libraries of small molecules, natural products, or antisense oligonucleotides can be screened for their ability to inhibit SipB-SipC assembly. Compounds that reduce the signal without affecting bacterial growth or needle complex formation are identified as potential anti-virulence agents. The invention thus provides a powerful, physiologically relevant, and clinically translatable tool for the detection of functional translocon assembly, offering unprecedented insight into the structural dynamics of bacterial pathogenesis and enabling the development of novel therapeutic strategies targeting the type III secretion system.
