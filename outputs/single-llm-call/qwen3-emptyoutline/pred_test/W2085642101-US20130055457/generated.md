## FIELD OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present disclosure relates to the field of agricultural biotechnology, specifically to the genetic engineering of crop plants for the improvement of complex agronomic traits such as grain yield, drought-stress tolerance, and environmental adaptability. More particularly, the invention pertains to the use of recombinant nucleic acid constructs containing native regulatory and coding sequences of the ZAR1 gene from Zea mays, wherein the ZAR1 gene is derived from distinct heterotic groups of maize breeding germplasm, and wherein the expression of these sequences in transgenic maize plants results in enhanced plant performance under variable environmental conditions. The invention further encompasses methods for producing transgenic maize plants that express heterozygous combinations of ZAR1 alleles, either through the stacking of multiple transgene insertions or through the use of a single construct containing both allelic variants, thereby mimicking the genetic architecture of hybrid vigor observed in conventional breeding. The disclosed compositions and methods are particularly useful for improving yield stability and productivity in maize grown under diverse environmental regimes, including hot and dry, temperate, humid, and high-latitude conditions, where traditional breeding approaches have demonstrated limitations in achieving consistent performance across environments. The invention also provides tools for identifying and selecting genetic variants of ZAR1 that are associated with favorable agronomic outcomes, enabling the integration of transgenic technology with conventional breeding programs to accelerate the development of superior hybrid varieties. The field of application extends to commercial agriculture, seed production, and precision crop management systems that rely on genetically defined traits to optimize resource use efficiency, reduce yield variability, and enhance resilience to abiotic stresses such as water deficit and high planting density.

## BACKGROUND OF THE DISCLOSURE

The global demand for increased agricultural productivity, particularly in staple cereal crops such as maize, has intensified the need for innovative approaches to enhance yield and stress tolerance beyond the limits of conventional breeding. While decades of selective breeding have steadily improved grain yield in maize through the optimization of heterotic group interactions and the accumulation of favorable alleles, the rate of genetic gain has begun to plateau in many regions, necessitating the integration of molecular tools to overcome physiological and environmental constraints. Transgenic technologies have been successfully applied to introduce traits such as insect resistance and herbicide tolerance, which are governed by single genes with high penetrance and minimal environmental interaction. However, efforts to engineer complex agronomic traits—such as grain yield, drought tolerance, nitrogen use efficiency, and plant architecture—have met with limited success due to the polygenic nature of these traits, their sensitivity to environmental fluctuations, and the difficulty in predicting transgene performance across diverse growing conditions. Previous attempts to overexpress genes associated with organ size control, such as the Arabidopsis ARGOS gene, resulted in increased plant biomass but often at the cost of delayed development or reduced reproductive output, limiting their practical utility in field crops. In maize, the identification of homologous genes such as the ZAR family, which includes ZAR1 as the closest orthologue to ARGOS, offered a promising avenue for targeted manipulation of growth and yield. However, prior studies had not investigated whether natural allelic variation within elite breeding germplasm could be leveraged to enhance transgene efficacy or whether the heterotic structure of maize, which underpins the success of hybrid varieties, could be exploited in transgenic systems. Furthermore, no prior work had established a systematic framework for evaluating transgene-by-environment interactions in maize using environmentally stratified field trials, nor had any study demonstrated that stacking two naturally occurring allelic variants of a single gene in a heterozygous configuration could produce a synergistic yield advantage exceeding that of either allele alone. The absence of such knowledge has created a significant gap in the ability to translate molecular discoveries into commercially viable traits for complex agronomic improvement. Conventional breeding relies on the selection of favorable allele combinations within heterotic groups and the exploitation of heterosis in hybrid crosses, but these principles have not been systematically applied to transgenic approaches. The discovery that the ZAR1 locus exhibits functional allelic divergence between Stiff Stalk and Non Stiff Stalk heterotic groups, that these alleles are differentially expressed in response to environmental stress, and that their co-expression in transgenic plants leads to enhanced yield stability across environments represents a fundamental departure from prior paradigms in plant biotechnology. The lack of understanding of how transgene efficacy is modulated by environmental variables has hindered the deployment of transgenic solutions for complex traits, as inconsistent performance across locations has led to regulatory uncertainty and commercial risk. This invention addresses these longstanding challenges by providing a genetically defined, environmentally responsive, and heterosis-mimicking system for improving maize yield through the precise manipulation of naturally selected allelic variants of a key growth-regulatory gene.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present invention provides a novel and non-obvious method for enhancing grain yield and drought tolerance in maize through the transgenic expression of functionally distinct allelic variants of the ZAR1 gene, wherein the allelic variants are derived from the Stiff Stalk and Non Stiff Stalk heterotic groups of elite maize breeding germplasm and are expressed in a heterozygous configuration within transgenic plants. The invention is based on the unexpected discovery that the ZAR1 gene, which encodes a small regulatory protein involved in cell proliferation and organ size determination, exhibits natural allelic variation that has been preserved and partitioned between heterotic groups through decades of selective breeding. The two predominant alleles, designated ZAR1-SS1 and ZAR1-NS1, differ in both their promoter regions and coding sequences, resulting in differential expression patterns under environmental stress and distinct protein structures that influence functional activity. When these alleles are introduced into transgenic maize plants individually, each confers a moderate improvement in yield and drought tolerance, but only under specific environmental conditions: the ZAR1-SS1 allele performs best under hot and dry conditions, while the ZAR1-NS1 allele demonstrates greater stability under cooler and more humid environments. Crucially, when both alleles are co-expressed in a single transgenic plant through the use of a molecularly stacked expression cassette, the resulting plants exhibit a synergistic increase in grain yield that is significantly greater than that achieved by either allele alone and is more consistent across a broader range of environmental conditions. This synergistic effect is not attributable to increased total transcript abundance, as the stacked allele plants do not exhibit higher ZAR1 expression levels than plants expressing a single allele, indicating that the benefit arises from functional complementation between the allelic variants rather than dosage effects. The invention further includes the identification of environmental thresholds—such as daily maximum temperature, cumulative solar radiation, and rainfall during key developmental stages—that predict the direction and magnitude of transgene efficacy, enabling the targeted deployment of specific ZAR1 alleles or stacks in regions with defined climatic profiles. Additionally, the invention reveals that the ZAR1 locus resides within a highly conserved macro-haplotype block in elite germplasm, with minimal recombination in the flanking regions, suggesting that this locus has been a target of selection during the development of modern maize heterotic groups. The disclosed compositions and methods enable the integration of transgenic technology with conventional breeding strategies by providing genetically defined, environmentally responsive traits that mimic the heterotic performance of elite hybrids, thereby bridging the gap between molecular engineering and field-based selection. The invention thus represents a transformative approach to crop improvement, wherein natural allelic diversity, shaped by breeding history, is harnessed to create transgenic solutions that are not only more effective but also more predictable and broadly applicable across diverse agricultural environments.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE DISCLOSURE

### Nucleic Acids

The nucleic acids of the present invention comprise isolated and purified DNA molecules encoding the ZAR1 gene from Zea mays, wherein the gene is derived from either the Stiff Stalk (SS1) or Non Stiff Stalk (NS1) heterotic group of elite maize breeding germplasm, and wherein the nucleic acid sequence includes both the native promoter region and the full-length coding sequence of the respective allele. The SS1 allele of ZAR1 comprises a promoter region of approximately 1,376 base pairs upstream of the translation start site, while the NS1 allele contains a promoter region of approximately 1,724 base pairs, with multiple insertions and deletions distinguishing the two sequences in the region encompassing the putative transcriptional regulatory elements. These promoter variants are functionally distinct, as evidenced by allele-specific expression analyses in heterozygous hybrid plants, wherein the NS1 allele exhibits higher transcript abundance under drought stress conditions compared to the SS1 allele, despite identical genetic backgrounds. The coding sequences of the two alleles differ by a total of five non-synonymous substitutions, including a leucine-to-methionine substitution at a conserved motif critical for protein folding and a glycine-to-alanine substitution that alters the flexibility of a glycine-rich loop adjacent to the conserved domain. These amino acid changes are predicted to modify the secondary and tertiary structure of the ZAR1 protein, potentially affecting its interaction with downstream signaling components involved in cell proliferation and organ size regulation. The nucleic acids of the invention further include chimeric constructs wherein the native promoter of either the SS1 or NS1 allele is fused to the coding sequence of the corresponding allele, ensuring that the spatial, temporal, and stress-responsive expression patterns of the endogenous gene are preserved in the transgenic context. In a preferred embodiment, the nucleic acid construct contains both the SS1 and NS1 allelic variants, each under the control of their respective native promoters, arranged in tandem within a single T-DNA cassette to enable co-expression in the same cell. The nucleic acids may be synthesized de novo using chemical oligonucleotide assembly or amplified from genomic DNA of the respective inbred lines using sequence-specific primers designed to flank the promoter and coding regions, with the resulting fragments cloned into a binary vector suitable for Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. The vectors employed in the invention contain a selectable marker, such as a gene encoding resistance to hygromycin or phosphinothricin, and a visible marker, such as the Ds-Red fluorescent protein driven by an aleuron-specific promoter, to facilitate the identification of transgenic events. The nucleic acids are free of any heterologous enhancer elements, such as the cauliflower mosaic virus 35S enhancer, to avoid non-physiological expression patterns and to ensure that the transgene behaves in a manner consistent with the endogenous gene’s natural regulation. The nucleic acids of the invention are characterized by their ability to confer enhanced plant growth, increased cell number in vegetative tissues, improved drought tolerance, and elevated grain yield under specific environmental conditions, as demonstrated in transgenic maize plants grown under controlled and field conditions. The sequences of the ZAR1 alleles are distinct from those previously deposited in public databases, as they are derived from elite breeding lines and contain polymorphisms not found in reference genomes such as B73. The nucleic acids of the invention are not merely isolated variants of known genes but represent functionally differentiated alleles that have been selected and preserved in commercial germplasm, and their use in transgenic applications is novel and non-obvious in light of the prior art, which has not disclosed the functional consequences of combining these specific allelic variants in a single plant.

### Recombinant Expression Cassettes

The recombinant expression cassettes of the present invention are engineered DNA constructs designed for the stable integration and regulated expression of ZAR1 allelic variants in maize plants, wherein each cassette contains a native promoter from either the SS1 or NS1 allele of ZAR1 operably linked to the corresponding full-length coding sequence, followed by a transcriptional termination sequence derived from the nopaline synthase or proteinase inhibitor II gene. The cassettes are constructed using Gateway cloning technology to ensure high-fidelity recombination and to enable the rapid assembly of multiple allelic variants into a single construct. In a first embodiment, a single expression cassette contains the SS1 promoter fused to the SS1 coding sequence, and in a second embodiment, a separate cassette contains the NS1 promoter fused to the NS1 coding sequence. In a third, preferred embodiment, a single expression cassette contains both the SS1 and NS1 expression units arranged in tandem, with each unit comprising its own promoter, coding sequence, and terminator, thereby enabling the simultaneous expression of both allelic variants in the same plant cell. The cassettes are inserted into a binary vector backbone that includes a plant-selectable marker, such as the bar gene conferring resistance to glufosinate ammonium, and a fluorescent reporter gene, such as Ds-Red, driven by the aleuron-specific PromHvLTP2 promoter, to allow for visual identification of transgenic tissues during seed development. The cassettes are designed to avoid the inclusion of any heterologous enhancer sequences, such as the cauliflower mosaic virus 35S enhancer, to prevent aberrant overexpression and to ensure that the expression of ZAR1 remains under the control of its native regulatory elements, which are known to respond to developmental cues and environmental stress. The cassettes are flanked by T-DNA border sequences derived from Agrobacterium tumefaciens, enabling efficient transfer and integration into the maize genome following transformation. The orientation of the expression units within the tandem cassette is optimized to minimize transcriptional interference, with the SS1 unit positioned upstream of the NS1 unit, and both units separated by a minimal spacer sequence to prevent read-through transcription. The cassettes are verified by sequencing to confirm the integrity of the promoter, coding, and terminator regions, and by restriction enzyme digestion to ensure correct assembly. The expression cassettes are delivered into maize embryogenic callus via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation using strain LBA4404, and transgenic events are selected based on herbicide resistance and fluorescence. The resulting transgenic plants are backcrossed to non-transgenic inbred lines to segregate the transgene and obtain homozygous lines for phenotypic evaluation. The recombinant expression cassettes of the invention are distinguished from prior art constructs by their use of native, heterotic group-specific regulatory sequences, their ability to recapitulate the endogenous expression patterns of ZAR1 in response to environmental stimuli, and their capacity to produce synergistic phenotypic effects when both allelic variants are co-expressed. Unlike prior constructs that rely on constitutive or strong viral promoters to drive high-level expression, the cassettes of the invention are tailored to preserve the natural regulatory logic of the ZAR1 gene, resulting in a more physiologically appropriate and environmentally responsive phenotype. The cassettes are not merely tools for gene overexpression but are precision instruments for mimicking the genetic architecture of hybrid vigor through transgenic means, thereby enabling the direct translation of breeding principles into molecular solutions.

### Expression of Proteins in Host Cells

The expression of ZAR1 proteins in transgenic maize host cells is achieved through the stable integration of recombinant expression cassettes into the nuclear genome, resulting in the production of functional ZAR1 polypeptides that are localized to the cytoplasm and nucleus, where they modulate cellular processes associated with organ growth and stress response. The ZAR1 protein, encoded by the SS1 and NS1 alleles, is a small polypeptide of approximately 85 to 95 amino acids, lacking a transmembrane domain or signal peptide, and is predicted to function as a regulatory protein that interacts with components of the cell cycle or auxin signaling pathways. The protein encoded by the SS1 allele contains a methionine residue at position 42 and an alanine at position 58, while the NS1-encoded protein contains a leucine at position 42 and a glycine at position 58, resulting in structural differences that alter the conformational flexibility of a glycine-rich loop adjacent to a conserved hydrophobic motif. These structural variations are predicted to influence the protein’s interaction with binding partners, potentially altering its stability, subcellular localization, or transcriptional regulatory activity. The expression of ZAR1 proteins in transgenic plants is tightly regulated by the native promoters of the respective alleles, resulting in tissue-specific and environmentally responsive expression patterns. In immature ear tissue, both alleles are expressed at moderate levels, but under drought stress, the NS1 allele exhibits a more pronounced upregulation compared to the SS1 allele, suggesting a differential sensitivity to water deficit. In leaf tissue, ZAR1 expression is detectable during early vegetative stages and declines as the plant matures, consistent with its role in regulating cell proliferation during active growth phases. The transgenic plants expressing either the SS1 or NS1 allele alone show increased cell number in leaf epidermal tissues without a corresponding increase in cell size, indicating that the ZAR1 protein functions primarily to promote cell division rather than cell expansion. When both alleles are co-expressed in the same plant, the total ZAR1 protein abundance does not exceed that observed in plants expressing a single allele, yet the phenotypic effect on yield is significantly greater, indicating that the two protein variants act in a complementary manner, possibly through heterodimerization or by modulating distinct downstream pathways. The expression of ZAR1 proteins does not result in detectable pleiotropic effects on flowering time, plant height, or biomass allocation, suggesting that the protein’s activity is finely tuned to influence specific aspects of organ development without disrupting overall plant architecture. The proteins are stable under field conditions and are not subject to rapid degradation, as evidenced by consistent transcript and protein levels across multiple growing seasons. The expression of ZAR1 in transgenic plants does not interfere with the expression of endogenous ZAR1 alleles, as the transgenic constructs are designed to supplement, rather than replace, the native gene product. The functional activity of the ZAR1 proteins is confirmed through phenotypic analysis, wherein transgenic plants exhibit increased ear size, reduced kernel abortion, and improved yield under drought stress, all of which correlate with the presence of the transgene-encoded protein. The proteins are not secreted or localized to organelles, and their activity is confined to the cytoplasmic compartment, where they likely interact with regulatory factors involved in cell cycle progression. The expression of ZAR1 proteins in maize cells thus represents a novel mechanism for enhancing yield and stress tolerance through the modulation of cell proliferation without altering developmental timing or resource allocation, and the synergistic interaction between the SS1 and NS1 variants provides a unique and unexpected advantage that cannot be achieved by the expression of either variant alone.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The construction of recombinant expression cassettes containing the native promoter and coding sequences of the ZAR1 gene from the SS1 and NS1 alleles of Zea mays was performed using Gateway cloning technology. Genomic DNA was extracted from leaf tissue of inbred lines SS1 and NS1, and the promoter and coding regions of ZAR1 were amplified using sequence-specific primers designed to include attB recombination sites. The SS1 promoter region, spanning 1,376 base pairs upstream of the start codon, was amplified and cloned into the pENTR/D-TOPO vector, followed by recombination into the pMDC32 binary vector containing the Ds-Red fluorescent marker and the nopaline synthase terminator. Similarly, the NS1 promoter region, measuring 1,724 base pairs, was amplified and cloned into the same vector backbone. The coding sequences of both alleles, each approximately 255 base pairs in length, were amplified from cDNA and cloned into the pENTR vector, followed by recombination into the promoter-containing vectors to generate full-length expression cassettes. The resulting constructs, designated pMDC32-SS1 and pMDC32-NS1, were verified by restriction digestion and sequencing to confirm the integrity of the promoter-coding unit. A third construct, designated pMDC32-SS1+NS1, was generated by combining both the SS1 and NS1 expression units in tandem within a single T-DNA cassette, with each unit separated by a 150-base pair spacer sequence to prevent transcriptional interference. The spacer sequence was derived from an intron of the maize ubiquitin gene and was chosen for its neutral effect on gene expression. All constructs were introduced into Agrobacterium strain LBA4404, and embryogenic calli derived from the Hi-II maize line were transformed using standard protocols. Transgenic events were selected on media containing hygromycin and visually screened for Ds-Red fluorescence in the aleurone layer of developing kernels. Ten independent events for each construct were selected based on single-copy integration, confirmed by Southern blot analysis, and consistent transgene expression, as determined by quantitative RT-PCR. The transgenic plants were backcrossed to the non-transgenic Hi-II inbred line for three generations to obtain homozygous lines for phenotypic evaluation. The resulting transgenic plants exhibited no visible morphological abnormalities and showed normal growth patterns under greenhouse conditions, indicating that the expression of ZAR1 under its native regulatory elements did not disrupt essential developmental processes.

### Example 2

Field trials were conducted in 2007 at four locations across the U.S. Central Corn Belt to evaluate the effect of transgenic expression of the ZAR1-SS1 and ZAR1-NS1 alleles on grain yield. Each transgenic line was planted in replicated plots alongside non-transgenic null segregants, with environmental conditions at each location classified using the EnClass® system based on daily maximum temperature, cumulative solar radiation, and rainfall during the vegetative and reproductive stages. The four environments were categorized as Temperate Dry (TD), Temperate (T), Temperate Humid (TH), and High Latitude (HL). In the TD environment, the ZAR1-SS1 transgenic plants exhibited a 12.3% increase in grain yield compared to the non-transgenic control, while the ZAR1-NS1 plants showed a 5.7% increase. In the T environment, both transgenic lines showed a moderate yield advantage, with SS1 increasing yield by 8.9% and NS1 by 7.2%. In the TH environment, the ZAR1-SS1 plants exhibited a 6.1% yield reduction relative to the control, whereas the ZAR1-NS1 plants showed no significant difference. In the HL environment, the ZAR1-SS1 plants suffered a 14.2% yield reduction, while the ZAR1-NS1 plants showed a smaller, non-significant reduction of 3.8%. The differential performance of the two alleles across environments was statistically significant (P < 0.05) when analyzed using a mixed linear model that accounted for location, replication, and environmental class. The data demonstrated that the ZAR1-SS1 allele conferred a strong yield advantage under hot and dry conditions but was detrimental under cooler and wetter conditions, while the ZAR1-NS1 allele exhibited greater stability across environments, with less negative impact under stress conditions. These results indicated that the functional efficacy of the ZAR1 transgene was not uniform but was strongly modulated by environmental variables, establishing a clear transgene-by-environment interaction that had not been previously documented for any single gene in maize.

### Example 3

To assess the effect of ZAR1 expression on drought tolerance, a controlled drought-stress treatment was applied during the grain-filling stage in 2009 at two field locations classified as Temperate and High Latitude. Plants were subjected to water deprivation by withholding irrigation for 14 days beginning at silking, and physiological and yield parameters were measured. Under drought conditions, the ZAR1-SS1 transgenic plants exhibited a 22.4% reduction in tip kernel abortion compared to the non-transgenic control, a 19.1% reduction in anthesis-silking interval, and a 15.7% increase in kernel number per ear. The ZAR1-NS1 plants showed a 17.8% reduction in tip kernel abortion, a 16.3% reduction in anthesis-silking interval, and a 13.9% increase in kernel number. Both transgenic lines showed improved staygreen characteristics, with higher chlorophyll retention in the lower leaves during the post-flowering period. In the High Latitude environment, where ambient temperatures were lower and rainfall was higher, the ZAR1-SS1 plants exhibited a 9.3% yield reduction under drought, whereas the ZAR1-NS1 plants showed a 4.1% yield reduction, indicating that the NS1 allele conferred greater resilience under cooler, wetter drought conditions. The combined data from multiple years and locations confirmed that the ZAR1 transgene improved drought tolerance primarily by reducing kernel abortion and improving the synchrony of pollen shed and silk emergence, with the NS1 allele providing a broader spectrum of protection across environmental contexts. The physiological improvements were not due to altered water use efficiency, as stomatal conductance and transpiration rates were similar between transgenic and non-transgenic plants, suggesting that the mechanism of drought tolerance was mediated through enhanced cellular resilience rather than water conservation.

### Example 4

To determine whether the synergistic effect observed in heterozygous hybrids could be replicated in transgenic plants, a molecularly stacked expression cassette containing both the SS1 and NS1 ZAR1 alleles under their respective native promoters was constructed and introduced into maize. Ten independent transgenic events were generated and backcrossed to the non-transgenic parent to obtain homozygous lines. These plants were evaluated alongside single-allele transgenics and non-transgenic controls in five field locations across the Corn Belt during 2011, with environmental conditions again classified using the EnClass® system. The stacked allele plants exhibited a mean yield increase of 14.6% across all environments, compared to 8.1% for the SS1-only transgenics and 7.5% for the NS1-only transgenics. The yield advantage of the stacked plants was statistically significant (P < 0.01) in four out of five locations, whereas the single-allele transgenics showed significant increases in only two locations. In the Temperate Dry environment, the stacked plants showed a 17.2% yield increase, surpassing the 12.3% increase observed in the SS1-only plants. In the Temperate Humid environment, where the SS1-only plants suffered a 6.1% yield reduction, the stacked plants showed a 2.3% yield increase, demonstrating the ability of the NS1 allele to compensate for the negative effects of the SS1 allele under unfavorable conditions. In the High Latitude environment, the stacked plants showed a 1.8% yield increase, while the SS1-only plants showed a 14.2% reduction and the NS1-only plants showed a 3.8% reduction. The data indicated that the stacked allele plants exhibited a more consistent and robust yield response across environments than either single allele, suggesting that the two variants functioned in a complementary manner to buffer against environmental variability. Importantly, total ZAR1 transcript levels in the stacked plants were not significantly higher than in the single-allele plants, indicating that the enhanced performance was not due to increased gene dosage but rather to functional complementation between the allelic variants.

### Example 5

To investigate the molecular basis of the functional differences between the SS1 and NS1 alleles, allele-specific expression analysis was performed in the F1 hybrid of SS1 and NS1 under normal and drought-stressed conditions. RNA was extracted from immature ear tissue and seedlings, and RT-PCR was conducted using primers that amplified a region spanning a 61-base pair indel in the 5’ UTR, allowing for the physical separation of the two allelic transcripts on an agarose gel. Under control conditions, the NS1 allele was expressed at approximately 1.8-fold higher levels than the SS1 allele. Under drought stress, the NS1 transcript increased by 2.4-fold, while the SS1 transcript increased by only 1.3-fold, resulting in a 3.1-fold difference in expression between the two alleles. The differential expression was maintained across multiple tissues and developmental stages, indicating that the variation was due to cis-regulatory differences rather than trans-acting factors. The promoter region of the NS1 allele contained several putative stress-responsive elements, including ABRE and DRE motifs, that were absent or mutated in the SS1 promoter, suggesting that the NS1 promoter is more responsive to abiotic stress signals. Protein modeling of the two ZAR1 variants revealed that the leucine-to-methionine substitution at position 42 altered the hydrophobic core of the protein, potentially affecting its stability, while the glycine-to-alanine substitution at position 58 reduced the flexibility of a glycine-rich loop, which may influence protein-protein interactions. The structural differences were predicted to result in distinct binding affinities for downstream regulatory partners, providing a mechanistic basis for the observed functional complementation. The data collectively indicated that the two alleles differ in both their transcriptional regulation and protein structure, and that these differences contribute to their distinct phenotypic profiles in transgenic plants.

### Example 6

To determine whether the ZAR1 locus had been a target of selection in elite maize breeding germplasm, a macro-haplotype analysis was conducted on a panel of 245 inbred lines, including 174 Non Stiff Stalk and 71 Stiff Stalk lines, all of which carried the predominant ZAR1 haplotypes 1 and 2, respectively. Genome-wide marker data from 75 loci spanning an 8-centimorgan region flanking ZAR1 were analyzed to assess patterns of linkage disequilibrium and recombination. The analysis revealed that the entire 8-cM region surrounding the ZAR1 locus in the Stiff Stalk lines was completely conserved, with no evidence of recombination between any of the flanking markers. In contrast, the Non Stiff Stalk lines exhibited seven distinct macro-haplotypes, indicating that recombination had occurred frequently in this region among NS lines. The conservation of the SS1 haplotype block suggested that this region had been under strong selection, with minimal recombination preserving the functional combination of alleles. The presence of multiple NS haplotypes indicated that selection had acted on a broader set of genetic combinations in the NS group, possibly due to its role as the male parent in hybrid crosses. The ZAR1 locus itself was found to be in complete linkage disequilibrium with the flanking markers in the SS group, confirming that the functional allele had been maintained as a single, non-recombining unit. This pattern of haplotype conservation is consistent with the hypothesis that the ZAR1 locus has been a target of selection during the development of modern maize heterotic groups, and that the functional divergence between the SS1 and NS1 alleles has been preserved due to its contribution to hybrid performance.

### Example 7

Genetic association analysis was performed on a panel of 234 Non Stiff Stalk and 133 Stiff Stalk inbred lines phenotyped for drought-related traits over three years. The ZAR1 locus was found to be significantly associated with kernel weight, barrenness, plant height, and staygreen under drought-stressed conditions, with P-values ranging from 0.003 to 0.042. The effect sizes were small to moderate, consistent with the polygenic nature of these traits, but the direction of effect was consistently favorable when the SS1 and NS1 alleles were present in heterozygous combination. The analysis revealed that the highest yield performance occurred when the SS1 allele was inherited from the female parent and the NS1 allele from the male parent, mirroring the typical heterotic pattern in commercial hybrids. The association was independent of other known QTLs, as co-factors identified through genome-wide marker analysis did not account for the ZAR1 effect. The data indicated that the ZAR1 locus contributes to heterosis in a manner similar to other yield-related genes, and that its functional alleles have been selected and partitioned between heterotic groups to maximize hybrid performance. The transgenic results, wherein the stacked allele plants outperformed single-allele transgenics, directly mirrored the natural heterotic effect observed in conventional hybrids, providing a molecular explanation for the long-standing observation that hybrid vigor is often associated with the complementary action of divergent alleles.

### Example 8

To determine whether the transgene efficacy of ZAR1 could be predicted based on environmental variables, a partial least squares regression analysis was performed using yield data from three years of field trials and environmental data collected from weather stations at each location. Daily maximum temperature, cumulative solar radiation, and total rainfall during the vegetative and grain-filling stages were used as predictor variables, while grain yield relative to the non-transgenic control was the response variable. The analysis identified three key environmental factors that strongly correlated with transgene efficacy: daily maximum temperature above 32°C, cumulative solar radiation exceeding 1,200 MJ/m² during the grain-filling stage, and total rainfall below 150 mm during the same period. High values of these factors were associated with positive transgene effects, while low values were associated with negative effects. The model explained 78% of the variation in transgene efficacy, with temperature being the most significant predictor. The results demonstrated that the efficacy of the ZAR1 transgene could be accurately predicted based on climatic conditions, enabling the targeted deployment of specific alleles or stacks in regions with defined environmental profiles. The SS1 allele was predicted to perform best in regions with high temperature and low rainfall, while the NS1 allele was predicted to be more effective in regions with moderate temperature and higher rainfall. The stacked allele plants were predicted to perform well across a broader range of conditions, making them suitable for regions with variable or unpredictable climates. This predictive capability represents a significant advancement in transgenic crop development, as it allows for the rational design of trait deployment strategies based on environmental data rather than empirical testing.

### Example 9

To validate the commercial potential of the invention, a multi-location yield trial was conducted in 2012 across 12 sites in the U.S. Corn Belt, encompassing all four environmental classes identified in prior years. The trial included the stacked allele transgenic plants, the single-allele transgenics, the non-transgenic control, and two commercial hybrid checks. The stacked allele plants showed a consistent yield advantage over all other genotypes, with an average increase of 13.7% compared to the non-transgenic control and 8.2% compared to the commercial checks. The yield increase was statistically significant (P < 0.01) in 10 of the 12 locations, with the greatest gains observed in the Temperate Dry and Temperate environments. The stacked plants also exhibited improved standability, reduced ear rot incidence, and higher test weight, indicating that the yield advantage was not accompanied by negative trade-offs. The transgenic plants were agronomically indistinguishable from non-transgenic plants in terms of maturity, lodging resistance, and harvest index. The data confirmed that the molecularly stacked ZAR1 allele construct provides a commercially viable solution for improving yield stability and productivity in maize under diverse environmental conditions. The invention thus represents a breakthrough in the application of transgenic technology to complex agronomic traits, demonstrating that naturally selected allelic variation can be harnessed to create transgenic solutions that are more effective, more predictable, and more aligned with the principles of conventional breeding than any previously developed trait.
