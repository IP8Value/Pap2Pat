# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

### 1. Technical Field

The present invention relates to quantum computing systems and methods for simulating stochastic processes with enhanced efficiency, particularly in the context of financial modeling, statistical physics, and anomalous diffusion analysis. More specifically, the invention provides a novel quantum algorithmic framework for the analog simulation of fractional Brownian motion and related stochastic processes using spectral decomposition techniques, quantum Fourier transforms, and recursive amplitude encoding. This framework enables the coherent preparation of quantum states that encode entire trajectories of stochastic processes in exponentially compressed form, thereby facilitating quantum speedups in Monte Carlo estimation tasks that were previously intractable due to the prohibitive gate depth and qubit requirements of classical digital simulation methods. The invention is particularly applicable to high-dimensional, long-time-horizon problems where traditional Monte Carlo methods scale poorly, such as the pricing of path-dependent financial derivatives, the statistical characterization of sub- and super-diffusive particle motion in complex fluids, and the estimation of time-averaged functionals of correlated random processes.

### 2. Description of Related Art

Classical Monte Carlo methods have long served as the standard approach for estimating expectations over stochastic processes, including Brownian motion and its generalizations. These methods rely on sampling a large number of independent trajectories from the underlying probability distribution and computing empirical averages. For a process discretized over T time steps and requiring an estimation accuracy of ε, classical Monte Carlo typically demands on the order of O(T/ε²) samples to achieve a desired level of precision. This quadratic dependence on the inverse error parameter, combined with linear or superlinear scaling in the number of time steps, renders such methods computationally expensive for high-resolution simulations over extended time horizons. While variance reduction techniques and importance sampling have been developed to mitigate these costs, they do not fundamentally alter the asymptotic complexity and remain limited by the inherent statistical noise of sampling.

Quantum computing has offered theoretical promise for accelerating such tasks through quantum amplitude estimation, which can reduce the sample complexity from O(1/ε²) to O(1/ε), achieving a quadratic speedup in error scaling. However, this quantum advantage is contingent upon the ability to coherently prepare a quantum state that encodes the entire stochastic process in superposition. In prior approaches, this has been achieved by “quantizing” classical simulation algorithms—compiling the random number generation and trajectory construction steps into sequences of quantum gates, typically Toffoli and Hadamard gates, using uniformly random seeds encoded in ancillary qubits. While this digital simulation approach preserves the classical runtime asymptotically, it suffers from severe practical limitations: the quantum circuit depth grows proportionally with the number of time steps T, and the total number of gates scales as O(T · f(T)), where f(T) represents the cost of simulating a single step. For processes requiring high temporal resolution or long simulation horizons, such as those encountered in financial risk analysis or molecular dynamics, these gate counts become astronomically large, often exceeding the coherence limits of even future fault-tolerant quantum hardware. Recent estimates suggest that implementing such digital simulations for derivative pricing may require error rates many orders of magnitude below current technological capabilities.

Alternative quantum approaches have explored the use of quantum walks to simulate certain types of random walks with improved complexity, particularly when the underlying process exhibits Markovian structure. However, these methods are generally restricted to processes with independent or weakly correlated increments and fail to capture long-range temporal dependencies characteristic of fractional Brownian motion, which is essential for modeling phenomena such as volatility clustering in financial markets or anomalous diffusion in biological systems. Moreover, quantum walk methods do not inherently exploit the spectral structure of stochastic processes, nor do they provide a mechanism for efficiently encoding the entire trajectory in a manner that permits direct extraction of time-averaged observables without full state tomography.

Recent work has also investigated the efficient loading of specific probability distributions—such as Gaussian or log-normal—into quantum registers for use in financial algorithms. While these techniques are valuable for initializing single-variable distributions, they do not address the core challenge of encoding the full temporal correlation structure of a stochastic process across multiple time points. Furthermore, existing quantum state preparation methods typically assume fixed, known amplitudes, whereas stochastic processes require the preparation of superpositions over random trajectories, each with distinct amplitudes drawn from a distribution. This distinction renders prior state preparation techniques incompatible with the requirements of quantum Monte Carlo, which demands a unitary oracle that generates a coherent superposition of trajectories, not a mixture of independently prepared states.

Thus, despite theoretical advances in quantum algorithms for expectation estimation, the practical realization of a quantum speedup for stochastic process simulation has remained elusive due to the absence of a method that simultaneously achieves: (1) exponential compression of trajectory representation via amplitude encoding, (2) efficient preparation of correlated Gaussian increments without classical compilation, (3) compatibility with quantum amplitude estimation, and (4) scalability to long time horizons with logarithmic or polylogarithmic resource growth. The present invention overcomes these limitations by introducing a fundamentally new paradigm for quantum simulation of stochastic processes: analog simulation via spectral decomposition. By leveraging the Wiener series representation of Brownian motion and its generalization to fractional Brownian motion, the invention transforms the problem of trajectory generation into the problem of preparing a quantum state whose amplitudes correspond to the Fourier coefficients of the process, which are known to be independent Gaussian random variables with a power-law decay determined by the Hurst parameter. This approach decouples the simulation complexity from the number of time steps and instead ties it to the number of retained frequency components, which can be made independent of T and dependent only on the desired accuracy ε and the Hurst parameter H. The resulting quantum circuits exhibit gate counts and depths that scale polylogarithmically in T and polynomially in 1/ε, enabling a black-box quantum speedup over classical Monte Carlo that is both asymptotically superior and practically feasible within the constraints of near-term quantum hardware.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a quantum algorithm for the analog simulation of stochastic processes, particularly fractional Brownian motion, enabling efficient quantum Monte Carlo estimation of time-averaged functionals with a computational complexity that is exponentially superior to classical methods. The invention introduces a novel quantum representation of stochastic trajectories in which the value of the process at each time point is encoded in the amplitude of a quantum state, while the time index is stored in the computational basis, thereby achieving exponential compression of the trajectory representation using only O(log T) qubits for a T-step process. This analog encoding is prepared through a two-stage quantum spectral method: first, a quantum state is prepared whose amplitudes correspond to the Fourier coefficients of the stochastic process, which are independently distributed Gaussian random variables with variances decaying as a power law determined by the Hurst parameter H; second, the quantum Fourier transform is applied to convert this frequency-domain representation into the time-domain analog encoding of the trajectory. The preparation of the Gaussian amplitude state is accomplished via a recursive data loading technique that exploits the statistical independence of the angles in a binary tree state preparation circuit when applied to high-dimensional Gaussian vectors, allowing the entire distribution to be encoded without classical sampling or iterative amplitude amplification. This method is extended to fractional Brownian motion by adjusting the power-law decay of the Fourier coefficients according to the Hurst parameter, and further generalized to Lévy processes through the use of circulant matrix embeddings and the quantum Fourier transform. The resulting coherent analog state is compatible with quantum amplitude estimation, enabling the efficient estimation of time-averaged quantities such as realized variance or mean-squared displacement without requiring measurement of individual trajectory points. The invention achieves a black-box quantum speedup over classical Monte Carlo by reducing the overall complexity from O(T/ε²) to O(polylog(T) · ε⁻ᶜ), where c is a constant strictly less than 2 and dependent on the Hurst parameter H, with c approaching 3/2 for H > 1/2. This speedup is preserved even under post-selection over trajectory subsets, making the method applicable to path-dependent financial derivatives and statistical tests for anomalous diffusion where closed-form analytical solutions are unavailable. The invention further enables the direct estimation of inner products between the simulated process and arbitrary functions encoded in quantum states, opening new avenues for quantum-enhanced signal processing and statistical inference on stochastic data. The algorithm requires only polylogarithmic circuit depth and a number of qubits that scales linearly with the number of retained frequency components, making it feasible for implementation on future fault-tolerant quantum computers and providing a practical pathway to quantum advantage in fields ranging from quantitative finance to biophysics.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Example Method

The method of the present invention comprises a sequence of quantum operations designed to prepare a coherent analog encoding of a stochastic process, such as fractional Brownian motion, and subsequently estimate a time-averaged functional of the process using quantum amplitude estimation. The method begins by receiving as input a specification of the stochastic process, including its Hurst parameter H, the number of time steps T, the desired estimation accuracy ε, and a target function f(t) whose time average over the process trajectory is to be estimated. The method then proceeds through a series of quantum state preparation and transformation steps, each implemented as a unitary quantum circuit composed of elementary quantum gates.

The first step involves the preparation of a quantum state encoding the Fourier coefficients of the stochastic process. This is achieved by initializing a register of qubits representing the frequency domain and applying a recursive data loading procedure to generate a superposition of Gaussian amplitudes with variances decaying as k⁻⁽²ᴴ⁺¹⁾ for the k-th frequency component. The data loading is performed using a binary tree structure of controlled rotation gates, where each node in the tree corresponds to a beam splitter operation that splits the amplitude weight between two sub-regions of the state. Crucially, the rotation angles at each node are not fixed but are drawn from a known probability distribution derived from the properties of high-dimensional Gaussian vectors: specifically, the square of the sine of each rotation angle follows a beta distribution, and the angles themselves are statistically independent across nodes at different levels of the tree. This independence arises from the spherical symmetry of the Gaussian distribution and allows each angle to be prepared independently using a separate quantum circuit, avoiding the exponential complexity that would arise from correlated angle distributions. The resulting state is a superposition over all possible sets of Fourier coefficients, each corresponding to a possible realization of the stochastic process in the frequency domain.

In the second step, the quantum Fourier transform is applied to this frequency-domain state, converting the amplitudes into a time-domain representation. The quantum Fourier transform is implemented as a logarithmic-depth circuit composed of Hadamard and phase gates, and its application transforms the state into a superposition over time-indexed amplitudes, where the amplitude at each time index t corresponds to the value of the stochastic process at that time. This yields the analog encoding of the trajectory: a quantum state of the form ∑ₜ αₜ |t⟩, where |t⟩ is the binary encoding of the time index and αₜ is the amplitude proportional to the process value at time t. This state is normalized such that the probability of measuring time t is proportional to |αₜ|², but the actual value of the process at time t is encoded in the magnitude of αₜ, not in the basis state itself. This representation is fundamentally analog, as the continuous values of the process are stored in the continuous amplitudes of the quantum state, rather than being digitized into discrete registers.

The third step involves the preparation of an auxiliary quantum state encoding the target function f(t). This is achieved by initializing a second register and applying a unitary transformation that maps the time basis states |t⟩ to a superposition whose amplitudes are proportional to f(t). This step assumes that f(t) is efficiently preparable as a quantum state, which is true for a broad class of functions, including piecewise polynomials, exponential decays, and indicator functions over time intervals. The fourth step then performs a controlled operation that multiplies the amplitude of the process state by the amplitude of the function state, effectively computing the pointwise product f(t) · Bₕ(t) for each time t. This is implemented using a sequence of controlled-phase gates or controlled rotations conditioned on the time register, resulting in a joint state that encodes the product function in its amplitudes.

The fifth step involves the application of quantum amplitude estimation to extract the expected value of the time-averaged quantity. This is done by projecting the final state onto a subspace corresponding to the time average, typically by applying a uniform superposition over time and measuring the overlap with a target state. The amplitude estimation algorithm, which requires O(1/ε) applications of the unitary oracle constructed from the previous steps, then returns an estimate of the inner product ⟨f|Bₕ⟩ with additive error ε. In the case of variance swap pricing, f(t) is taken to be the constant function 1, and the inner product corresponds to the time average of the squared volatility, which directly determines the payoff of the derivative. In the case of anomalous diffusion analysis, f(t) is chosen to be a time-shifted indicator function, and the inner product computes the time-averaged mean-squared displacement, which is used to distinguish between different Hurst parameters. The entire procedure is repeated a number of times sufficient to reduce statistical uncertainty, but the number of repetitions is independent of T and scales only with 1/ε, yielding a total gate complexity that is polylogarithmic in T and polynomial in 1/ε.

The method further includes a post-selection variant, in which an additional register is used to encode the norm of the trajectory, and a conditional rotation is applied to retain only those trajectories whose norm falls within a specified range. This is implemented by computing the squared norm of the amplitude-encoded trajectory using a quantum circuit that sums the squares of the amplitudes, storing the result in an ancillary register, and then applying a controlled rotation that flips a flag qubit if the norm exceeds a threshold. The flag qubit is then used to conditionally erase or retain the trajectory state, effectively post-selecting on a subset of paths. This variant is crucial for pricing barrier options or other path-dependent derivatives for which no closed-form solution exists, as it removes the mathematical structure that would otherwise permit analytical evaluation. The post-selection step does not destroy the quantum speedup, as both the quantum and classical methods incur a similar penalty in sample complexity, preserving the relative advantage of the quantum approach.

The method is further extended to Lévy processes by replacing the Gaussian Fourier coefficients with those of a Lévy noise process, which are characterized by a flat power spectrum. In this case, the Fourier coefficients are not Gaussian but are instead drawn from a distribution determined by the Lévy measure. The simulation proceeds by embedding the Toeplitz integration operator into a circulant matrix, which is then diagonalized by the quantum Fourier transform. The resulting circuit applies the quantum Fourier transform to the Lévy noise state, multiplies by the eigenvalues corresponding to the kernel function, and applies the inverse quantum Fourier transform to recover the integrated trajectory. While this method does not yield a fully coherent analog encoding compatible with amplitude estimation due to non-Gaussian correlations in the Fourier domain, it still provides a significant reduction in gate count compared to classical digital simulation and enables efficient generation of Lévy trajectories for applications such as jump-diffusion modeling in finance.

### Description of a Computing System

The computing system of the present invention comprises a quantum processor configured to execute the analog simulation method for stochastic processes, a classical control unit for managing the quantum circuit parameters and post-processing results, and a memory subsystem for storing the quantum circuit definitions, process parameters, and intermediate quantum states. The quantum processor is implemented using a superconducting qubit architecture, trapped ion system, or photonic quantum computer capable of performing high-fidelity single-qubit rotations, two-qubit entangling gates, and mid-circuit measurement. The system includes a register of qubits dedicated to encoding the time index, with a number of qubits logarithmic in the maximum number of time steps T, and a second register for encoding the Fourier coefficients or Lévy noise amplitudes, with a number of qubits proportional to the number of retained frequency components L, which is determined by the desired accuracy ε and the Hurst parameter H. Additional ancilla qubits are allocated for the recursive data loading procedure, the quantum Fourier transform, and the amplitude estimation algorithm.

The classical control unit is implemented using a high-performance computing system running a software stack that includes a quantum circuit compiler, a parameter optimizer, and a statistical analysis module. The compiler translates the abstract description of the stochastic process—such as its Hurst parameter, drift, and volatility—into a sequence of quantum gates, taking into account the physical constraints of the quantum hardware, including gate fidelity, connectivity, and decoherence times. The optimizer determines the minimal number of frequency components L required to achieve the desired accuracy ε, leveraging the known decay rate of the Fourier coefficients to avoid over-sampling. For fractional Brownian motion, this is determined by the condition that the tail of the zeta function ζ(1 + 2H) truncated at L contributes less than ε² to the total variance, leading to L = O(ε⁻¹/²ᴴ). The statistical analysis module receives the output of the quantum amplitude estimation and computes confidence intervals, performs hypothesis testing, or computes option prices based on the estimated expectations.

The memory subsystem stores precomputed tables of rotation angles for the data loading circuit, which are derived analytically from the beta and gamma distributions and depend only on the height of each node in the binary tree structure. These tables are generated offline and loaded into the quantum control system prior to execution, eliminating the need for real-time classical computation during the quantum circuit run. The memory also stores the quantum circuit definitions for the quantum Fourier transform, the discrete sine transform, and the unary-to-binary conversion circuit, which are implemented as fixed subroutines. The system further includes a classical random number generator for initializing the quantum state with a seed, and a feedback loop that adjusts the circuit parameters based on the outcome of repeated executions, enabling adaptive error correction and variance reduction.

The system is designed to operate in a hybrid quantum-classical mode, where the quantum processor executes the core simulation and estimation steps, and the classical controller handles pre-processing, post-processing, and error mitigation. The quantum processor is shielded from environmental noise and operated at cryogenic temperatures to maintain coherence over the duration of the circuit, which, due to the logarithmic depth of the quantum Fourier transform and the recursive data loader, is on the order of O(polylog(T) + polylog(1/ε)) gate layers. The system supports parallel execution of multiple instances of the simulation circuit to accelerate statistical convergence, and includes a quantum memory module for storing intermediate states during long-running computations. The entire system is scalable, with the number of qubits and gate depth growing only slowly with T and ε, enabling the simulation of processes with T on the order of 10⁶ or more time steps using fewer than 1000 qubits, a feat that is intractable for classical digital simulation methods.

### Additional Considerations

The invention further encompasses a range of extensions and modifications that enhance its applicability and robustness across diverse domains. One such extension involves the integration of error mitigation techniques tailored to the analog nature of the simulation. Because the method encodes continuous values in quantum amplitudes, it is inherently susceptible to small phase and amplitude errors introduced by imperfect gate operations. To address this, the system employs a technique known as amplitude damping correction, in which the variance of the Gaussian amplitudes is dynamically adjusted during the data loading procedure to compensate for systematic errors in the rotation angles. This adjustment is computed using a classical calibration routine that measures the average norm of the output state over multiple runs and adjusts the scaling factor of the amplitudes accordingly. This ensures that the simulated process maintains the correct statistical properties, even in the presence of hardware imperfections.

Another consideration involves the generalization of the method to multi-dimensional stochastic processes. While the present invention focuses on one-dimensional processes such as Brownian motion, the same spectral approach can be extended to vector-valued processes by encoding each component of the process in a separate quantum register and applying the quantum Fourier transform independently to each. The resulting state is a tensor product of individual analog encodings, and the time-averaged functionals can be computed by measuring the inner product of each component with its corresponding function. This enables the simulation of correlated multi-asset financial models, such as those used in portfolio risk analysis, where the volatility of each asset evolves according to a fractional Brownian motion with different Hurst parameters and cross-correlations. The method can also be extended to spatially extended systems by discretizing a continuous spatial domain and encoding the field values at each spatial point in a separate register, allowing for the simulation of stochastic partial differential equations such as the fractional Langevin equation.

The invention also includes a method for verifying the correctness of the simulated process using quantum state tomography on a subset of the output state. While full tomography is infeasible due to the exponential size of the Hilbert space, partial tomography can be performed by measuring the marginal distribution of the process at a small number of time points and comparing it to the known theoretical distribution. This serves as a validation step before deploying the method for high-stakes applications such as financial pricing or scientific discovery. The verification procedure is implemented by applying a sequence of projective measurements to the time register and recording the empirical distribution of the amplitudes, which is then compared to the theoretical Gaussian distribution with the expected variance at each time point.

The invention further considers the implications of non-stationary processes and time-varying parameters. While the current method assumes constant drift and volatility, it can be extended to handle time-dependent parameters by encoding them as additional quantum registers that modulate the rotation angles during the data loading procedure. For example, if the Hurst parameter H varies over time, the system can use a quantum-controlled rotation that adjusts the decay rate of the Fourier coefficients based on the value of a time-dependent control qubit. This enables the simulation of regime-switching models in finance, where the market exhibits periods of high and low persistence, or in biology, where the diffusive behavior of a particle changes due to environmental conditions.

The method also supports the incorporation of external constraints, such as boundary conditions or absorbing barriers, through the use of quantum post-selection and conditional operations. For instance, in the pricing of barrier options, the system can be modified to erase trajectories that cross a specified price threshold by applying a conditional phase flip to the amplitude register when the process value exceeds the barrier. The resulting state is then renormalized, and the amplitude estimation is performed on the constrained subspace. This eliminates the need for rejection sampling and preserves the quantum speedup, as the conditional operation is implemented unitarily and does not require classical sampling.

Finally, the invention considers the possibility of de-quantization—whether classical algorithms can mimic the polylogarithmic scaling of the quantum method. While the analog encoding exploits the exponential dimensionality of quantum states, it is theoretically possible to construct a classical algorithm that samples from the same Fourier-domain representation using fast Fourier transforms and Gaussian sampling. However, such an algorithm would still require O(L) samples to estimate the time average, and since L scales as ε⁻¹/²ᴴ, the classical complexity would be O(ε⁻¹/²ᴴ), which is worse than the O(ε⁻ᶜ) quantum complexity for c < 1/2H. Moreover, the quantum method provides a direct path to estimating inner products and time averages without reconstructing the full trajectory, a capability that classical methods cannot replicate without full state reconstruction. Thus, while the quantum method may be de-quantized in principle, the practical efficiency and structural advantages of the analog encoding ensure a persistent and significant quantum advantage for the target applications.
