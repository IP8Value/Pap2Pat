# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The development of accurate in vitro models capable of recapitulating the complex physiological architecture and functional dynamics of the human brain has long been a critical unmet need in neuroscience and pharmaceutical research. Despite decades of advancement in cell culture technologies, the majority of preclinical studies evaluating central nervous system therapeutics continue to rely on simplistic two-dimensional monolayer systems or animal models that fail to faithfully represent human neurobiology. These limitations have contributed significantly to the abysmally low success rate of drug candidates entering clinical trials for neurological disorders, where fewer than ten percent of compounds that demonstrate promise in early-stage testing ultimately achieve regulatory approval. The primary reasons for this high attrition rate include the inability of existing models to accurately predict human-specific drug toxicity, the misrepresentation of blood-brain barrier permeability characteristics, and the absence of critical cellular interactions that govern neurovascular homeostasis. Conventional two-dimensional cultures often consist of immortalized cell lines that lack the phenotypic diversity, spatial organization, and dynamic signaling networks inherent to native human brain tissue. Even when primary human cells are employed, their isolation from post-mortem or surgical specimens introduces variability, limits scalability, and precludes the generation of patient-specific models essential for precision medicine applications. Furthermore, the absence of key cellular components—such as microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons—in many blood-brain barrier models results in an incomplete representation of the neurovascular unit, thereby obscuring the true impact of therapeutic agents on the integrated multicellular environment of the brain parenchyma.

The blood-brain barrier itself is not merely a passive physical barrier formed by endothelial cells; rather, it is a highly dynamic, multifunctional interface sculpted by intricate bidirectional communication among a consortium of specialized cell types. Endothelial cells, which form the vascular lining, are tightly regulated by adjacent pericytes embedded within the basement membrane and astrocytic end-feet that ensheath the capillary surface. These interactions are indispensable for the induction and maintenance of tight junction proteins such as ZO-1 and claudin-5, which restrict paracellular diffusion, as well as for the polarized expression of efflux transporters like P-glycoprotein and nutrient transporters such as GLUT1. Beyond these structural roles, pericytes contribute to vascular stability and angiogenesis, while astrocytes modulate ion and neurotransmitter homeostasis, regulate blood flow, and secrete factors that reinforce barrier integrity. More recently, the functional significance of microglia—the brain’s resident immune cells—in modulating barrier permeability during inflammation, infection, or neurodegeneration has become increasingly evident. Microglia influence the expression of adhesion molecules and cytokine networks that can either enhance or disrupt barrier function depending on their activation state. Similarly, oligodendrocytes, though traditionally viewed as myelin-producing cells, play emerging roles in metabolic support to axons and in the regulation of local immune responses that indirectly impact vascular health. Neurons, the functional units of the central nervous system, exert direct influence on the blood-brain barrier through the release of signaling molecules such as sonic hedgehog and vasoactive peptides that regulate endothelial gene expression and barrier maturation. The collective interplay among these six cell types—endothelial cells, pericytes, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons—constitutes the human neurovascular unit, a physiological entity whose integrity is essential for maintaining cerebral homeostasis and whose dysfunction underlies nearly all major neurological diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis, stroke, and epilepsy.

Despite the growing recognition of the neurovascular unit as a functional unit, existing in vitro models have largely remained fragmented. Early models focused on the triad of endothelial cells, pericytes, and astrocytes, often assembled in transwell systems that, while useful for measuring transepithelial electrical resistance and solute permeability, lack the three-dimensional architecture necessary to replicate the spatial organization and mechanical cues of native tissue. Subsequent advances introduced spheroid-based self-assembly techniques that improved cell-cell contact and enabled the formation of rudimentary barrier structures, yet these models still excluded microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons, thereby omitting critical components of the parenchymal microenvironment. The absence of these cell types renders such models incapable of assessing how drugs that successfully traverse the barrier interact with neural circuits, glial populations, or immune responders within the brain parenchyma. Moreover, the inability to model the metabolic conversion of prodrugs into active neurotoxic metabolites—such as the transformation of MPTP into MPP+ by astrocytic monoamine oxidase B—precludes the evaluation of compound bioactivation, a key mechanism underlying many neurotoxicities. Even more critically, no existing model has demonstrated the capacity to recapitulate the physiologic disruption of the neurovascular unit under pathological conditions such as hypoxia, a hallmark of ischemic stroke and traumatic brain injury. While some studies have examined hypoxic effects on endothelial monolayers, none have done so within a fully cellularized, three-dimensional, multicellular organoid context that preserves the architectural and signaling complexity of the human neurovascular unit. Consequently, the field lacks a model that simultaneously integrates all six major cell types of the adult human cerebral cortex, maintains long-term viability and functionality, exhibits physiologically relevant barrier properties, and responds dynamically to pharmacological and pathological stimuli in a manner that mirrors in vivo human physiology. The absence of such a model has created a profound gap between preclinical findings and clinical outcomes, resulting in the continued failure of therapeutic candidates that appear safe and effective in simplified systems but prove toxic or ineffective in human patients. The need for a comprehensive, human-derived, three-dimensional neurovascular unit organoid model capable of simulating both barrier function and parenchymal response is therefore not merely an incremental improvement but a fundamental requirement for the future of neuropharmaceutical development.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel, three-dimensional, multicellular human neurovascular unit organoid model that comprises six essential cell types found in the adult human cerebral cortex: human brain microvascular endothelial cells, human brain vascular pericytes, human astrocytes, human microglia, human oligodendrocytes, and human neurons. This organoid model is uniquely configured to recapitulate the structural, functional, and dynamic properties of the native human neurovascular unit, including the formation of a physiologically relevant blood-brain barrier with functional tight junctions, adherens junctions, and polarized expression of transport proteins such as P-glycoprotein and glucose transporter 1. The organoid is assembled through a staged self-assembly process that enables the sequential integration of parenchymal cells followed by vascular cells, resulting in a highly organized architecture wherein endothelial cells and pericytes localize to the outer periphery of the organoid, forming a continuous or semi-continuous sheath around a core composed of astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons. This spatial organization closely mirrors the in vivo arrangement of the neurovascular unit, with endothelial cells serving as the primary barrier interface and parenchymal cells occupying the inner parenchymal compartment. The organoid maintains high cell viability for up to twenty-one days in vitro, with uniform distribution of all six cell types throughout the structure and no evidence of significant necrosis or apoptosis in either the core or the periphery. Immunofluorescent and confocal microscopy analyses confirm the expression and proper localization of key barrier markers including ZO-1, claudin-5, VE-cadherin, and β-catenin, indicating the formation of mature tight and adherens junctions between endothelial cells. The presence of functional efflux and nutrient transporters is further validated through the detection of P-glycoprotein and GLUT1 at the endothelial cell membrane, with expression patterns consistent with those observed in native human brain tissue. The model exhibits charge-selective permeability, as demonstrated by its ability to restrict the passage of positively charged mercury ions while permitting the translocation of lipophilic compounds such as MPTP, which are subsequently metabolized within the organoid to their toxic derivatives. The model also responds dynamically to physiological and pathological stimuli, including hypoxia, which induces a significant disruption in the localization of adherens junction proteins β-catenin and VE-cadherin, thereby mimicking the vascular destabilization observed in ischemic stroke. Additionally, the organoid model permits the assessment of compound neurotoxicity, glial toxicity, and neuronal dysfunction in a single, integrated system, enabling the simultaneous evaluation of barrier penetration, metabolic conversion, and downstream cellular effects. The use of induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cells for microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons enables the generation of patient-specific organoids, facilitating personalized medicine applications for neurodegenerative diseases, genetic disorders, and individualized drug toxicity profiling. The model is scalable, reproducible, and compatible with high-throughput screening platforms, making it suitable for use in drug discovery, safety pharmacology, and mechanistic studies of neurovascular pathologies. The invention represents a transformative advancement over existing models by integrating all six major cell types of the human neurovascular unit into a single, self-organizing, three-dimensional structure that faithfully reproduces barrier function, cellular interactions, and pathophysiological responses, thereby providing a clinically relevant platform for the prediction of human-specific drug efficacy and toxicity.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

### 1. Blood, Brain Barrier Models and Methods of Making the Same

The present invention encompasses a novel method for generating a three-dimensional, multicellular human neurovascular unit organoid that integrates six distinct cell types essential to the structure and function of the adult human cerebral cortex. This method is distinguished by its staged assembly protocol, which ensures the proper spatial organization of vascular and parenchymal components, thereby promoting the formation of a physiologically relevant blood-brain barrier. The process begins with the procurement and expansion of primary or induced pluripotent stem cell-derived human brain microvascular endothelial cells, human brain vascular pericytes, human astrocytes, human microglia, human oligodendrocytes, and human neurons. Each cell type is cultured under optimized conditions that preserve their phenotypic identity, functional maturity, and responsiveness to intercellular signaling. Human brain microvascular endothelial cells are maintained in a specialized endothelial growth medium supplemented with growth factors and attachment-promoting agents to preserve their barrier-forming capabilities. Human brain vascular pericytes are cultured on poly-L-lysine-coated surfaces in a medium containing serum and pericyte-specific supplements to maintain their contractile and signaling phenotype. Human astrocytes are propagated in a medium enriched with astrocyte growth factors and low serum concentrations to prevent dedifferentiation. Human microglia derived from induced pluripotent stem cells are maintained in a defined medium containing colony-stimulating factors such as GM-CSF and IL-34 to sustain their ramified, surveillant state. Human oligodendrocyte progenitor cells are expanded in a medium containing platelet-derived growth factor, neurotrophin-3, and cyclic AMP, followed by a differentiation phase that induces maturation into myelin basic protein-expressing oligodendrocytes. Human neurons are derived from induced pluripotent stem cells through a neural induction protocol that employs fibroblast growth factor and epidermal growth factor for progenitor expansion, followed by withdrawal of mitogens and exposure to neurotrophic factors to promote terminal differentiation into mature, synaptically active neurons.

The assembly of the organoid is conducted in two distinct phases to ensure optimal structural organization. In the first phase, the parenchymal cell types—astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons—are combined in a precise volumetric ratio and seeded into a hanging drop culture system. This configuration allows for the spontaneous aggregation of cells under gravity-driven conditions, promoting cell-cell adhesion and the formation of a cohesive neural-glial core. The hanging drop method facilitates the establishment of a three-dimensional microenvironment that mimics the extracellular matrix density and intercellular tension present in native brain tissue. The cells are suspended in a medium composed of a 50:50 mixture of astrocyte maintenance medium and neural basal medium, supplemented with heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum and rat tail collagen I to provide structural support and enhance cell survival during the initial aggregation phase. This stage is maintained for a period of forty-eight hours, during which time the cells self-organize into a compact, spherical structure with astrocytes preferentially localizing toward the periphery of the core and neurons forming a dense central region. Following this initial aggregation, the second phase of assembly is initiated. At this point, human brain microvascular endothelial cells and human brain vascular pericytes are introduced to the surface of the pre-formed neural-glial organoid. The endothelial and pericyte cells are suspended in a medium containing a combination of endothelial growth medium, astrocyte medium, and neural maintenance medium, and are carefully added to the culture system to allow for their migration and adhesion to the outer surface of the neural core. The addition of these vascular cells at a later time point ensures that they are not overwhelmed by the metabolic demands of the parenchymal cells during early aggregation, thereby enhancing their viability and promoting uniform coverage of the organoid surface. The organoid is then cultured for an additional forty-eight hours under standard conditions, during which time the endothelial cells form a continuous or semi-continuous layer around the organoid, while pericytes associate with the endothelial layer in a manner consistent with their in vivo perivascular localization. The resulting structure exhibits a clear spatial segregation: a peripheral vascular sheath composed of endothelial cells and pericytes, and an inner parenchymal core composed of astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons. This architecture is maintained throughout the duration of culture, with no evidence of cellular mixing or loss of structural integrity over a period of up to twenty-one days. The organoid is then transferred to a standard 96-well plate for long-term culture and experimental use, where it continues to exhibit high viability and functional marker expression. Alternative methods of assembly, such as the single-step co-culture of all six cell types in a single hanging drop, are also contemplated, though these result in less uniform vascular coverage and reduced barrier integrity compared to the staged approach. The staged assembly method is therefore preferred for generating organoids with reproducible, high-fidelity barrier properties. The entire process is conducted under sterile conditions using non-animal-derived reagents where possible, and the resulting organoids are free from exogenous scaffolds or synthetic matrices, ensuring that their structure and function arise entirely from endogenous cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions. The organoids are compatible with standard laboratory equipment, including confocal microscopes, microplate readers, and automated liquid handlers, and can be cryopreserved for future use using established protocols for neural organoids.

### 2. Methods of Use

The human neurovascular unit organoid model described herein is designed for a broad range of applications in biomedical research, drug discovery, and personalized medicine. Its primary utility lies in its capacity to serve as a physiologically accurate in vitro platform for evaluating the permeability, metabolism, and neurotoxic effects of pharmacological agents, environmental toxins, and biologics that interact with the central nervous system. One of the most significant applications of the model is in the assessment of blood-brain barrier permeability. By exposing the organoid to test compounds and quantifying their penetration into the parenchymal core, researchers can determine whether a substance is capable of crossing the barrier under normal physiological conditions. This is achieved through the use of fluorescently labeled molecules, radiolabeled tracers, or mass spectrometry-based quantification of compound concentration in the organoid core versus the surrounding medium. The model’s ability to distinguish between lipophilic and hydrophilic compounds is demonstrated by its selective permeability to MPTP, a lipophilic prodrug, while excluding its hydrophilic metabolite MPP+, thereby recapitulating the charge-selective nature of the human blood-brain barrier. The model further enables the evaluation of compound metabolism within the brain parenchyma, as the presence of astrocytes and neurons allows for the enzymatic conversion of prodrugs into active or toxic metabolites, such as the conversion of MPTP to MPP+ by monoamine oxidase B. This capability is absent in traditional barrier models that lack parenchymal cell types and therefore cannot predict the neurotoxic consequences of metabolic activation. The organoid model is also uniquely suited for the assessment of neurotoxicity and glial toxicity in a single integrated system. Following exposure to a test compound, cell viability can be measured through ATP production assays, live-dead staining, or caspase activation, allowing for the simultaneous evaluation of neuronal death, microglial activation, oligodendrocyte loss, and astrocyte reactivity. The model permits the identification of compound-induced changes in glial morphology, such as microglial hypertrophy or astrocytic gliosis, through immunofluorescent imaging of cell-specific markers like Iba1 and GFAP. Furthermore, the model can be employed to study the effects of compounds on myelin integrity by assessing the expression and distribution of myelin basic protein and O4 in oligodendrocytes. The organoid’s responsiveness to pathological stimuli further expands its utility in modeling disease states. For example, exposure of the organoid to hypoxic conditions induces a significant disruption in the localization of adherens junction proteins β-catenin and VE-cadherin, mimicking the vascular destabilization observed in ischemic stroke. This enables the study of stroke pathophysiology, the evaluation of neuroprotective agents, and the investigation of reperfusion injury mechanisms in a human-relevant context. The model can also be adapted to model neuroinflammatory conditions by exposing the organoid to cytokines such as TNF-alpha or IL-1beta, thereby inducing microglial activation and subsequent changes in barrier permeability and neuronal function. The use of induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cells for neurons, microglia, and oligodendrocytes allows for the generation of patient-specific organoids, enabling the study of genetic neurological disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. Organoids derived from patients with known mutations in genes such as APP, PSEN1, SNCA, or C9orf72 can be used to evaluate disease-specific alterations in barrier function, metabolic vulnerability, and cellular interactions. This approach facilitates the development of personalized therapeutic strategies by identifying compounds that restore barrier integrity or mitigate neurotoxicity in a patient’s own cellular context. The model is also compatible with high-throughput screening platforms, allowing for the rapid evaluation of large compound libraries for blood-brain barrier permeability, neurotoxicity, and barrier-enhancing properties. The organoids can be cultured in multi-well plates and subjected to automated imaging, luminescence assays, or transcriptomic and proteomic analyses to generate comprehensive data sets. The model’s long-term viability of up to twenty-one days permits the assessment of chronic exposure effects, which is critical for evaluating the safety of drugs intended for long-term administration in neurodegenerative conditions. The organoid can also be used to investigate the effects of drug combinations, including the potential for synergistic or antagonistic interactions between therapeutics and their impact on neurovascular function. In addition, the model may be employed in the development of delivery systems designed to enhance brain penetration, such as nanoparticle carriers, antibody conjugates, or receptor-mediated transport systems, by providing a physiologically accurate target tissue for evaluating their efficacy and safety. The model’s ability to respond dynamically to both pharmacological and pathological stimuli makes it an invaluable tool for understanding the complex interplay between the blood-brain barrier and the brain parenchyma, thereby bridging the gap between traditional in vitro models and in vivo human physiology.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

A human neurovascular unit organoid was generated using the staged assembly method as described. Human brain microvascular endothelial cells, human brain vascular pericytes, human astrocytes, human microglia, human oligodendrocytes, and human neurons were isolated and expanded under optimized culture conditions. The parenchymal cell types—astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons—were combined in a ratio of 15% astrocytes, 5% microglia, 15% oligodendrocytes, and 20% neurons, for a total of approximately 1,200 cells, and suspended in a 50:50 mixture of astrocyte maintenance medium and neural basal medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum and 10 ng/μL rat tail collagen I. This suspension was seeded into a hanging drop culture plate, with 20 μL droplets placed on the underside of the lid, allowing for gravity-driven aggregation. After forty-eight hours of incubation at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2, a compact spherical structure was observed under phase-contrast microscopy. The vascular cell types—human brain microvascular endothelial cells and human brain vascular pericytes—were then harvested and combined in a ratio of 30% endothelial cells to 15% pericytes, for a total of approximately 800 cells, and suspended in a medium composed of 60% neural maintenance medium, 20% astrocyte medium, and 20% endothelial growth medium. This suspension was carefully added to the pre-formed neural-glial organoid, and the culture was maintained for an additional forty-eight hours. At the end of this period, the organoids were transferred to a 96-well plate for long-term culture. Immunofluorescent staining for CD31 confirmed the localization of endothelial cells to the outer periphery of the organoid, while PDGFRβ staining revealed the association of pericytes with the endothelial layer. GFAP, Iba1, MBP, and MAP2 staining confirmed the presence and distribution of astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons within the core. Confocal microscopy revealed a clear spatial organization with endothelial and pericyte cells forming a continuous sheath around the parenchymal core. The organoid exhibited high viability, with over 90% of cells staining positive for calcein AM and less than 5% staining for ethidium homodimer-1 at day seven. The organoid was maintained for twenty-one days with weekly medium changes, and no signs of degeneration or loss of structural integrity were observed. This example demonstrates the successful generation of a six-cell-type human neurovascular unit organoid with preserved cellular viability and spatial organization.

### Example 2. Human Cortex Model with Spheroid Culture System

A human cortex model was generated using a spheroid culture system to evaluate the self-organization of six cell types without the use of a staged assembly protocol. All six cell types—human brain microvascular endothelial cells, human brain vascular pericytes, human astrocytes, human microglia, human oligodendrocytes, and human neurons—were harvested and combined in a single suspension at a ratio of 30% endothelial cells, 15% pericytes, 15% astrocytes, 5% microglia, 15% oligodendrocytes, and 20% neurons, for a total of approximately 2,000 cells per spheroid. The cell suspension was seeded into a hanging drop culture plate in EGM-2 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and maintained for ninety-six hours. After this period, the spheroids were collected and analyzed for cellular organization and barrier marker expression. Confocal microscopy revealed that endothelial cells and pericytes were distributed throughout the spheroid rather than forming a cohesive peripheral layer, with no consistent spatial segregation between vascular and parenchymal compartments. Immunofluorescent staining for ZO-1 and claudin-5 showed patchy, discontinuous expression, with significant regions of the spheroid surface lacking tight junction protein localization. The expression of P-glycoprotein and GLUT1 was detected but appeared diffuse and non-polarized, suggesting a lack of functional maturation. Cell viability remained high at day seven, but by day fourteen, a significant increase in dead cells was observed in the central core, indicating poor nutrient diffusion and metabolic stress. The spheroids exhibited higher variability in size and structure compared to those generated by the staged assembly method, and barrier permeability assays using FITC-labeled IgG showed inconsistent results, with some spheroids permitting significant leakage and others showing minimal permeability. This example demonstrates that while a single-step co-culture method can generate a multicellular spheroid containing all six cell types, it fails to reproduce the spatial organization and functional barrier properties achieved through the staged assembly protocol. The lack of vascular encapsulation and the resulting metabolic stress in the core highlight the importance of sequential cell integration in generating a physiologically accurate neurovascular unit model.

### Example 3: Bioprint Functional Cortical Tissue

A three-dimensional bioprinting method was employed to fabricate a functional cortical tissue construct incorporating all six cell types of the human neurovascular unit. A bioink was formulated by suspending human brain microvascular endothelial cells, human brain vascular pericytes, human astrocytes, human microglia, human oligodendrocytes, and human neurons in a hydrogel composed of 5% gelatin methacryloyl and 2% hyaluronic acid, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 10 ng/mL rat tail collagen I. The bioink was loaded into a multi-nozzle extrusion-based bioprinter, and a layered architecture was printed to mimic the cortical laminae of the human brain. The first layer consisted of a dense network of endothelial cells and pericytes, representing the vascular component, while the second and third layers contained alternating regions of astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons to simulate the parenchymal organization. The printed construct was cultured in a perfusion bioreactor for twenty-one days, with medium flow maintained at a rate of 0.5 μL/min to simulate capillary flow. Immunofluorescent staining confirmed the presence of all six cell types and the expression of ZO-1, claudin-5, VE-cadherin, β-catenin, P-glycoprotein, and GLUT1. The endothelial layer exhibited a continuous, interconnected network with minimal gaps, and tight junction proteins were localized at cell-cell interfaces. The parenchymal cells maintained their morphological characteristics, with astrocytes displaying stellate processes, microglia exhibiting ramified morphology, and neurons forming synaptic networks. Permeability assays using FITC-IgG showed low baseline permeability, which increased significantly upon histamine treatment, demonstrating barrier responsiveness. The construct remained viable throughout the culture period, with ATP production levels comparable to those of the staged organoid model. This example demonstrates that bioprinting is a viable alternative method for generating a functional cortical tissue construct with the full complement of neurovascular unit cell types, offering greater control over spatial architecture and potential for scalability in high-throughput applications.

### Example 4. Three-Dimensional Bioprinting of the Human Neurovascular Unit

A three-dimensional bioprinted model of the human neurovascular unit was fabricated using a coaxial extrusion system to simultaneously deposit vascular and parenchymal components in a single printing step. A dual-syringe system was used to extrude two bioinks: the inner bioink contained human brain microvascular endothelial cells and human brain vascular pericytes suspended in a fibrin-based hydrogel, while the outer bioink contained astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons suspended in a modified alginate hydrogel. The coaxial nozzle deposited cylindrical microstructures with a diameter of 200 micrometers, each consisting of a vascular core surrounded by a parenchymal sheath. The printed constructs were cultured in a perfusion system for twenty-one days, with medium flow maintained at 0.3 μL/min to simulate interstitial flow. Confocal microscopy revealed a highly organized structure with endothelial cells forming a continuous lumen surrounded by pericytes, and parenchymal cells arranged in a concentric ring around the vascular core. Immunofluorescent staining confirmed the expression of ZO-1, claudin-5, VE-cadherin, β-catenin, P-glycoprotein, and GLUT1 at the vascular interface, with polarized localization consistent with native tissue. The parenchymal cells exhibited normal morphology and marker expression, with neurons forming dense networks and oligodendrocytes displaying myelin basic protein expression. Permeability assays using mercury chloride demonstrated that the bioprinted model restricted the passage of Hg2+ ions, while allowing the translocation of MPTP, which was metabolized to MPP+ within the parenchymal compartment, resulting in a significant reduction in ATP production. Hypoxic exposure induced a significant reduction in β-catenin and VE-cadherin fluorescence, mirroring the response observed in the staged organoid model. This example demonstrates that three-dimensional bioprinting with coaxial extrusion can generate a highly structured, functional human neurovascular unit model with precise spatial control over vascular and parenchymal components, offering a scalable and reproducible platform for neuropharmacological research and disease modeling.
