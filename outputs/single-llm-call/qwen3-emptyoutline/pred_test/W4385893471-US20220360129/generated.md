# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to electric motors for high-power, high-reliability propulsion systems in electric vertical takeoff and landing (eVTOL) aircraft, and more specifically to an integrated magnetics, insulation, and cooling architecture that simultaneously enhances thermal management, electrical insulation, and electromagnetic performance in slotless permanent magnet synchronous machines. The invention addresses the critical challenge of managing extreme localized heating in the stator windings during high-torque flight phases such as takeoff and landing, where power demands can exceed ten times the cruise level, leading to rapid temperature rise in the conducting elements and increased risk of insulation degradation or failure. The architecture integrates thin copper sheets within the iron lamination stack to form a thermal shunt that efficiently conducts heat from the windings to an external heatsink, while simultaneously employing a ceramic coil holder that provides both enhanced lateral heat dissipation and superior dielectric isolation between conductive elements. This combination enables a significant reduction in thermal resistance from the active copper windings to the cooling medium without compromising electromagnetic efficiency, structural integrity, or electrical safety. The invention is particularly suited for applications where power density, reliability under transient thermal loads, and long-term insulation integrity are paramount, such as in aerospace propulsion systems, high-performance electric vehicles, and other mission-critical electromechanical systems operating under extreme duty cycles.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Electric propulsion systems for vertical takeoff and landing aircraft demand motors capable of delivering exceptionally high power densities while maintaining operational reliability under extreme thermal stress. Unlike conventional ground-based electric vehicles, eVTOL aircraft experience brief but intense power demands during takeoff and landing, during which the electrical current in the stator windings can increase by a factor of three to ten compared to cruise conditions. This surge in current leads to a proportional increase in resistive (copper) losses, which, due to the high current density and limited heat dissipation pathways in conventional motor designs, cause rapid temperature rise in the windings. In many existing slotless permanent magnet synchronous machines, the absence of iron teeth eliminates a natural thermal conduction path from the windings to the stator core, forcing heat to travel through low-conductivity insulation layers and air gaps before reaching the external cooling system. This results in elevated hotspot temperatures that threaten the integrity of the electrical insulation, particularly in high-voltage systems where partial discharge activity can accelerate insulation aging and lead to catastrophic failure.

Traditional cooling methods for electric motors—such as forced air, liquid cooling jackets, or heat pipes—are often inadequate for the spatial constraints and transient thermal profiles of eVTOL applications. External cooling systems struggle to mitigate internal temperature gradients because they act only at the periphery of the stator, leaving the inner regions of the windings thermally isolated. Furthermore, conventional insulation materials, such as polymer films or varnishes, provide electrical isolation but contribute negligibly to thermal conduction, thereby creating a thermal bottleneck that exacerbates temperature rise. Attempts to improve thermal performance by increasing the thickness of the stator core or incorporating metallic heat spreaders have been hindered by the conflicting requirements of magnetic flux path integrity and mechanical manufacturability. Iron laminations, while essential for guiding magnetic flux, possess inherently low thermal conductivity, and any attempt to replace or supplement them with higher-conductivity materials risks distorting the magnetic field distribution, reducing torque production, or inducing unwanted eddy current losses.

Recent innovations in motor design have explored the use of slotless architectures to maximize electrical loading by eliminating the iron teeth that traditionally occupy space between windings. While this increases the cross-sectional area available for copper conductors and improves torque density, it also concentrates copper losses within a more compact region, intensifying the thermal challenge. Moreover, the absence of iron teeth removes a natural mechanical support structure for the windings, necessitating alternative methods for coil retention and electrical isolation. Existing solutions have relied on epoxy potting or molded polymer housings, which, while providing mechanical stability, are thermally insulating and can trap heat, further limiting the motor’s ability to dissipate losses. Additionally, these materials often exhibit poor long-term dielectric stability under high-frequency switching and partial discharge conditions, which are common in high-power inverters used in eVTOL propulsion systems.

The need for a novel approach that simultaneously resolves the intertwined challenges of thermal management, electrical insulation, and electromagnetic efficiency has become increasingly urgent as the aerospace industry transitions toward electrified flight. Current state-of-the-art motors for eVTOL applications are constrained by their inability to sustain peak power for more than a few minutes without risking thermal runaway or insulation breakdown. The development of a motor architecture that can maintain safe operating temperatures during repeated high-torque cycles, while preserving electromagnetic performance and enhancing dielectric reliability, represents a critical technological gap. No prior art has successfully integrated a mechanically robust, thermally conductive, and electrically insulating structure directly into the stator assembly in a manner that simultaneously improves heat transfer from the windings, reduces thermal resistance along multiple parallel paths, and maintains the magnetic integrity of the core. The present invention fills this gap by introducing a novel integrated magnetics, insulation, and cooling architecture that combines interleaved copper and iron laminations with a precision-machined ceramic coil holder, creating a synergistic system that transforms the stator from a passive thermal barrier into an active heat management component.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel integrated magnetics, insulation, and cooling architecture for a slotless permanent magnet synchronous machine, designed to significantly enhance thermal performance while preserving electromagnetic efficiency and improving electrical insulation integrity. The architecture comprises two primary innovations: a laminated stator yoke composed of alternating layers of magnetic iron and high-conductivity copper, and a ceramic coil holder that mechanically supports and electrically isolates the form-wound stator windings while providing an additional parallel thermal pathway from the conductors to the outer surface of the yoke. The interleaved copper sheets function as thermal shunts, conducting heat radially outward from the windings through the iron laminations, which serve as the primary flux path, thereby reducing the thermal resistance between the copper conductors and the external cooling system. The ceramic coil holder, positioned between adjacent windings, provides a direct lateral conduction path from the sides of the conductors to the inner surface of the yoke, further reducing the temperature gradient across the winding assembly. The combination of these features results in a substantial reduction in the overall thermal resistance from the active copper windings to the heatsink, enabling the motor to sustain higher current densities without exceeding insulation temperature limits.

In a preferred embodiment, the stator yoke is constructed from thin laminations of electrical steel and copper foil, arranged in a periodic pattern such that the copper fill fraction is maintained at approximately 20 percent by volume. This ratio was determined through electromagnetic and thermal analysis to maximize thermal performance while minimizing the adverse impact on magnetic flux density and torque production. At this fill level, the peak flux density in the iron laminations increases only moderately, and the resulting increase in iron loss remains within acceptable limits, while the reduction in thermal resistance exceeds 40 percent compared to a baseline architecture with no copper interlayers. The copper laminations are bonded to the iron laminations using a high-temperature, thermally conductive epoxy, ensuring low contact resistance and mechanical stability under thermal cycling. The copper sheets are oriented such that their high-conductivity plane is aligned with the radial direction of heat flow, maximizing the efficiency of heat transfer from the center of the winding to the outer surface of the yoke.

The ceramic coil holder is fabricated from a high-thermal-conductivity, electrically insulating material such as aluminum nitride, and is machined into a C-shaped geometry that conforms to the curvature of the stator’s inner diameter. Each segment of the holder is designed with a thin base that interfaces directly with the inner surface of the yoke and a series of thin fins that extend radially inward to separate and support the individual conductors. The base thickness is minimized to reduce its contribution to the series thermal resistance, while the fin thickness is optimized to balance thermal conduction area with the need to preserve sufficient copper volume for current carrying capacity. The holder is assembled axially by stacking multiple machined segments, each aligned to match the axial length of the active winding region, and bonded to the yoke using a thermally conductive adhesive. This configuration creates a parallel thermal path from the sides of the conductors directly to the yoke, bypassing the high-resistance insulation layers that would otherwise dominate the thermal path in conventional designs.

The invention further includes a method of manufacturing the integrated architecture, wherein the copper laminations are formed from thin foil of high-purity copper alloy, cut to precise dimensions via wire electrical discharge machining, and interleaved with iron laminations in a controlled sequence. The laminations are stacked and bonded under pressure and elevated temperature to ensure intimate contact and minimize interfacial thermal resistance. The ceramic coil holder is fabricated using waterjet cutting of thick ceramic plates to achieve the required geometry with minimal microcracking, and the resulting segments are stacked and aligned axially to match the length of the stator. The form-wound Litz conductors are then inserted into the spaces between the ceramic fins, and the entire assembly is encapsulated with a low-viscosity, thermally conductive potting compound to ensure uniform thermal contact and mechanical stability.

The thermal performance of the invention is quantified by a reduction in the equivalent thermal resistance from the center of the windings to the outer surface of the yoke by more than 50 percent compared to a baseline slotless motor with conventional insulation and no thermal shunts. This reduction enables the motor to operate at higher current densities without exceeding the maximum allowable temperature of the insulation system, thereby increasing the power density and torque capability of the machine. Electromagnetic simulations confirm that the introduction of copper laminations at a 20 percent fill fraction reduces torque production by less than one percent, while iron losses increase by approximately 60 percent—still well within acceptable limits for transient operation. Eddy current losses induced at the copper-iron interfaces are negligible, measuring less than two watts under full load, and are orders of magnitude below the copper loss component. The ceramic coil holder further enhances the dielectric strength of the system, increasing the breakdown voltage between phases and between phase and ground, thereby reducing the likelihood of partial discharge activity and extending the long-term reliability of the insulation system.

The invention is particularly advantageous in applications requiring repeated high-power pulses, such as the takeoff and landing cycles of eVTOL aircraft, where sustained thermal management is critical to operational safety and system longevity. By integrating thermal management directly into the stator structure, the invention eliminates the need for complex external cooling systems, reduces the overall mass of the motor, and improves the reliability of the electrical insulation under transient thermal stress. The architecture is scalable to different power levels and can be adapted to various motor geometries, including outrunner and inrunner configurations, without compromising its core functional benefits. The combination of thermal shunting, parallel heat conduction, and enhanced dielectric isolation represents a fundamental advancement in electric motor design for high-performance aerospace applications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The preferred embodiment of the invention is a 300-kilowatt slotless permanent magnet synchronous machine designed for use in electric vertical takeoff and landing aircraft, wherein the stator assembly integrates an innovative combination of interleaved copper and iron laminations with a precision-machined ceramic coil holder to achieve unprecedented thermal and electrical performance. The machine is configured as an outrunner topology, with a rotating outer rotor containing a Halbach permanent magnet array and a stationary inner stator housing the armature windings. The stator core is constructed without iron teeth, allowing the full cross-sectional area of the stator bore to be occupied by copper conductors, thereby maximizing electrical loading and torque density. However, the absence of iron teeth eliminates the natural thermal conduction path that exists in conventional slotted machines, creating a significant thermal bottleneck in the region surrounding the windings. To address this, the invention introduces a laminated yoke structure composed of alternating layers of magnetic iron and high-purity copper, arranged in a periodic pattern such that copper sheets are inserted every third lamination, resulting in a copper fill fraction of approximately 20 percent by volume. These copper laminations are fabricated from 0.005-inch-thick Copper 110 alloy foil, selected for its high thermal conductivity, excellent formability, and compatibility with high-temperature bonding processes. The iron laminations are made from 0.010-inch-thick HF-10 electrical steel, a material chosen for its low hysteresis loss and high permeability under the operating flux densities of the machine. Each lamination is coated on both sides with a thin layer of EB-548 epoxy, a high-temperature, thermally conductive adhesive with a thermal conductivity of approximately 1.2 W/m·K, which is applied uniformly using a precision roll-coating process to ensure consistent interfacial bonding. The laminations are then stacked in a controlled sequence—iron, copper, iron, iron, copper, iron, and so on—under a controlled pressure of 150 psi and cured at a temperature of 180°C for a duration of four hours to ensure complete polymerization and minimal void formation at the interfaces. The resulting bonded stack forms a mechanically rigid, axially continuous structure that serves as both the magnetic flux path and the primary thermal conduit from the windings to the outer surface of the stator.

The ceramic coil holder is fabricated from aluminum nitride, a material selected for its combination of high thermal conductivity (approximately 170 W/m·K), excellent electrical insulation properties (dielectric strength exceeding 15 kV/mm), and machinability using non-contact methods such as waterjet cutting. The holder is designed in a C-shaped cross-section to conform to the inner curvature of the stator bore, with a base thickness of 0.030 inches and a fin thickness of 0.030 inches, dimensions determined through steady-state thermal finite element analysis to optimize the balance between thermal conduction and conductor space utilization. The base of the holder is intended to make direct contact with the inner surface of the bonded lamination stack, forming a low-resistance thermal interface that allows heat to flow laterally from the sides of the conductors into the yoke. The fins extend radially inward between adjacent conductors, providing both mechanical support and thermal coupling. The holder is manufactured by cutting multiple identical segments from a single 7-millimeter-thick plate of aluminum nitride using a non-articulated waterjet system operating at a pressure of 60,000 psi with a 0.010-inch abrasive nozzle. The waterjet process is carefully calibrated to minimize taper and microcracking, ensuring dimensional accuracy and structural integrity. Each segment is then stacked axially to match the active length of the stator, which is 4.5 inches, resulting in a total of 12 stacked segments. The stacked holder is aligned using precision dowel pins and bonded to the inner surface of the lamination stack using a thin layer of thermally conductive silicone adhesive, which fills any microscopic gaps and ensures uniform thermal contact across the entire interface. The form-wound Litz conductors, consisting of 1,200 individually insulated strands of 0.003-inch-diameter copper wire twisted into a helical configuration to minimize skin and proximity effects, are then inserted into the spaces between the ceramic fins. The conductors are arranged in a three-phase configuration, with each phase occupying a distinct radial sector of the stator bore, and are secured in place using a low-viscosity, thermally conductive potting compound that fully encapsulates the winding assembly while maintaining electrical isolation between phases. The potting compound, composed of a silicone-based matrix filled with aluminum oxide particles, has a thermal conductivity of 2.5 W/m·K and cures at room temperature, ensuring no thermal stress is introduced during assembly.

The electromagnetic performance of the machine was analyzed using a three-dimensional finite element analysis model of a one-pole section of the motor, representing half the axial length of the full stator. The model incorporated the precise geometry of the interleaved laminations, including the 20 percent copper fill, and simulated the magnetic flux distribution under rated operating conditions. The results demonstrated that the peak flux density in the iron laminations increased from 1.06 tesla in the baseline design to 1.30 tesla in the MAGICA architecture, a 22.6 percent increase attributable to the reduced effective cross-sectional area of the iron due to the presence of copper. This increase in flux density led to a corresponding rise in iron losses, which were calculated using a modified Steinmetz model to account for the non-uniform flux distribution and the presence of copper interlayers. The total iron loss increased from 667 watts in the baseline design to 1,095 watts in the MAGICA design, an increase of 64 percent. However, this increase remained within acceptable limits for transient operation, given that copper losses under the same conditions were approximately 6,893 watts in the baseline and 7,012 watts in the MAGICA design, indicating that the dominant loss mechanism remained resistive and that the thermal benefits far outweighed the marginal increase in iron loss. Torque production was evaluated under full-load conditions, and the results showed a reduction of only 1.1 percent, from 790 newton-meters in the baseline design to 781 newton-meters in the MAGICA design. This minimal reduction was attributed to the fact that the copper interlayers, while non-magnetic, occupied a relatively small volume and did not significantly perturb the magnetic field in the core, especially when the copper fill was maintained below 25 percent. The eddy current losses induced at the copper-iron interfaces were analyzed using a high-resolution 3D model with laminations of 0.010-inch iron and 0.005-inch copper, and the results showed that the peak eddy current density reached 2.3 amperes per square millimeter at the 33.3 percent copper fill level, but at the 20 percent fill level used in the preferred embodiment, the peak density was limited to 0.7 amperes per square millimeter. The total eddy current loss was calculated to be less than 0.1 watts, a value that is negligible compared to the copper and iron losses and poses no thermal or efficiency concern.

The thermal performance of the architecture was evaluated using a lumped-element thermal equivalent circuit model, which represented the thermal resistance network from the center of the windings to the outer surface of the yoke. The model included the thermal resistances of the conductor insulation, the ceramic coil holder, the interfacial bonding layers, the iron laminations, and the copper interlayers, with each element calculated based on the material’s thermal resistivity, the geometric dimensions of the stator, and the effective cross-sectional area for heat flow. The baseline architecture, which consisted of a pure iron yoke with conventional polymer insulation and no coil holder, exhibited a total thermal resistance of 0.85 K/W from the conductor center to the yoke surface. In contrast, the MAGICA architecture, with the interleaved copper laminations and ceramic coil holder, achieved a total thermal resistance of 0.41 K/W, representing a 51.8 percent reduction. This dramatic improvement was attributed to two parallel thermal pathways: the first through the copper interlayers, which provided a direct radial conduction path from the windings to the yoke, and the second through the ceramic fins, which conducted heat laterally from the sides of the conductors into the yoke. The copper interlayers alone reduced the thermal resistance by 40 percent, while the addition of the ceramic holder contributed an additional 10 percent reduction, demonstrating the synergistic effect of combining multiple thermal enhancement techniques. The thermal resistance of the ceramic holder itself was calculated to be 0.12 K/W, which was significantly lower than the 0.35 K/W contribution of the polymer insulation in the baseline design, confirming that the ceramic material not only provided superior electrical isolation but also acted as a highly effective thermal conductor.

To validate the thermal performance predictions, a prototype of the MAGICA architecture was constructed and subjected to a comparative thermal test against a baseline stator with identical dimensions and winding configuration but without copper interlayers or a ceramic holder. The test setup consisted of two identical cylindrical stator cores, each 4.5 inches in length and 42.5 inches in diameter, mounted on a custom fixture that allowed a uniform heat load to be applied to the inner surface of the windings using a resistive ceramic heater. The heater was calibrated to simulate the power dissipation of the motor under full-load conditions, delivering a total heat flux of 120 watts per square centimeter to the inner surface of the windings. A thermal imaging camera with a spatial resolution of 0.05 millimeters and a temperature accuracy of ±0.5°C was used to monitor the outer surface temperature distribution of both stators over an 8.5-minute period. The results showed that the baseline stator developed a pronounced temperature gradient, with a hotspot temperature of 142°C at the inner surface of the windings and a maximum outer surface temperature of 89°C, indicating that heat was not effectively conducted radially outward. In contrast, the MAGICA prototype exhibited a much more uniform temperature profile, with a maximum hotspot temperature of 108°C on the inner surface and a maximum outer surface temperature of 72°C, a reduction of 34°C in the outer surface temperature and 34°C in the inner hotspot. The temperature gradient across the stator wall was reduced from 53°C in the baseline to 36°C in the MAGICA design, demonstrating a 32 percent improvement in radial heat spreading. The thermal images also revealed that the MAGICA stator reached thermal equilibrium approximately 40 percent faster than the baseline, indicating a higher effective thermal diffusivity and more efficient heat dissipation. These experimental results confirmed the predictions of the thermal model and demonstrated that the integrated architecture significantly improves the motor’s ability to manage transient thermal loads.

The mechanical integrity of the ceramic coil holder was evaluated under conditions simulating the vibration and thermal cycling expected during aircraft operation. The holder was subjected to 100 cycles of thermal shock, cycling between -40°C and 120°C at a rate of 10°C per minute, followed by mechanical vibration testing at frequencies ranging from 5 Hz to 2,000 Hz with a peak acceleration of 15 g. No cracks, delamination, or dimensional changes were observed after testing, and the electrical insulation resistance between phases remained above 10 GΩ, confirming that the ceramic material retained its structural and dielectric properties under extreme conditions. The bonding interface between the ceramic holder and the lamination stack was inspected using ultrasonic imaging, which revealed no voids or debonding, and the adhesive layer was found to have maintained its integrity throughout the testing. The copper laminations were examined using X-ray computed tomography, which confirmed that the bonding epoxy had fully penetrated the interfacial regions and that no delamination or oxidation had occurred. The Litz conductors were tested for partial discharge activity under a 5-kilovolt AC potential, and no discharge inception voltage was observed below 8 kV, compared to 5.5 kV for the baseline insulation system, indicating a significant improvement in dielectric robustness attributable to the ceramic holder.

The manufacturing process for the invention is scalable and compatible with high-volume production techniques. The copper laminations can be produced using continuous roll-to-roll stamping or laser cutting, and the iron laminations can be sourced from standard electrical steel suppliers. The bonding process can be automated using a multi-zone vacuum press that applies uniform pressure and temperature across the entire stack, enabling the production of hundreds of stator cores per day. The ceramic coil holder can be manufactured using robotic waterjet systems capable of cutting complex geometries with sub-millimeter precision, and the stacking and alignment process can be automated using vision-guided robotic arms. The potting process can be performed using automated dispensing systems that inject the thermal compound under controlled pressure, ensuring complete void-free encapsulation. The entire assembly can be integrated into a modular stator housing that facilitates easy replacement and maintenance, making the architecture suitable for both new aircraft platforms and retrofit applications.

The invention provides a fundamental advancement in electric motor design by transforming the stator from a passive structural component into an active thermal management system. By integrating thermal shunting and parallel heat conduction directly into the core structure, the invention eliminates the need for external cooling systems such as liquid jackets or heat pipes, thereby reducing system mass, complexity, and failure points. The ceramic coil holder not only enhances thermal performance but also provides a robust dielectric barrier that reduces the risk of insulation failure under high-voltage switching conditions, a critical consideration for aerospace applications. The combination of these features enables the motor to operate at higher power densities than previously possible, with improved reliability, longer service life, and greater safety margins. The architecture is not limited to the specific dimensions or materials described herein; variations in copper fill fraction, ceramic composition, lamination thickness, and winding configuration can be implemented to suit different power levels, operating environments, and performance requirements. The invention thus represents a new paradigm in high-power electric motor design, one in which thermal management, electrical insulation, and electromagnetic performance are no longer treated as separate design challenges but are instead unified into a single, synergistic system.
