# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) inertial sensors, and more specifically to a high-frequency, axis-symmetric vibratory gyroscope that employs a substrate-decoupled bulk-acoustic wave resonator structure to achieve exceptional environmental robustness, minimal bias drift, and immunity to shock and vibration. The invention is particularly suited for applications requiring high reliability under dynamic environmental conditions, including automotive navigation, personal inertial navigation systems, aerospace guidance, industrial robotics, and consumer electronics where long-term stability and resistance to external mechanical disturbances are critical. The device operates in the megahertz frequency range using degenerate in-plane vibrational modes of a silicon-based resonator, with electrostatic actuation and capacitive readout, and incorporates a uniquely engineered stress isolation architecture that decouples the resonant structure from substrate-induced energy dissipation mechanisms. This architecture fundamentally alters the damping behavior of the system, eliminating the dominant source of environmentally sensitive offset drift that has historically plagued high-performance gyroscopes fabricated on anisotropic substrates such as (100) single-crystal silicon. The resulting device exhibits a quality factor exceeding 60,000 at moderate vacuum levels, maintains mode symmetry across wide temperature ranges, and demonstrates negligible sensitivity to linear accelerations and broadband mechanical vibrations, thereby enabling unprecedented performance in uncontrolled environments without requiring complex calibration or redundant proof masses.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Vibratory gyroscopes have long served as the cornerstone of inertial sensing in applications ranging from consumer electronics to precision navigation systems. Among the most widely deployed configurations are low-frequency flexural tuning-fork gyroscopes, which exploit the Coriolis-induced excitation of orthogonal vibrational modes to detect angular rotation. While these devices have achieved commercial success due to their relatively simple fabrication and integration with complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) electronics, they suffer from inherent vulnerabilities to environmental perturbations. Specifically, their resonant frequencies typically fall within the bandwidth of common mechanical disturbances—such as shock, vibration, and acoustic pressure fluctuations—rendering them susceptible to false outputs that manifest as drift in angular rate measurements. These limitations have become increasingly problematic as the demand for inertial sensors in mobile and wearable devices grows, where prolonged operation under uncontrolled mechanical conditions necessitates exceptional stability and noise resilience.

To mitigate the effects of linear acceleration and vibration, some tuning-fork designs incorporate redundant proof masses arranged symmetrically to reject common-mode disturbances. However, this approach introduces significant trade-offs: the addition of supplementary masses increases the overall footprint of the device, complicates the fabrication process due to stringent symmetry requirements, and often necessitates post-fabrication electromechanical calibration to compensate for residual asymmetries introduced during microfabrication. Furthermore, such architectures remain vulnerable to cross-modal excitation caused by capacitive sidewall imperfections and exhibit poor rejection of higher-order resonant modes that may still couple to the sensing axis under broadband excitation. Consequently, these solutions are economically and practically unsuitable for high-volume, cost-sensitive applications where miniaturization and reliability are paramount.

An alternative class of gyroscopes based on bulk-acoustic wave (BAW) resonators has emerged as a promising candidate for overcoming these limitations. These devices operate at significantly higher frequencies—typically in the megahertz range—and leverage the axis-symmetric geometry of a vibrating disk or ring to support degenerate vibrational modes that are inherently insensitive to linear acceleration due to their high resonant frequencies and distributed mass distribution. The high-frequency operation places the primary resonant modes well above the spectral content of most environmental vibrations, thereby suppressing the mechanical coupling that plagues low-frequency designs. Moreover, the high quality factors achievable in BAW structures under moderate vacuum conditions—often exceeding 10,000—enable precise detection of minute Coriolis-induced displacements, making them theoretically ideal for high-performance applications.

However, despite their theoretical advantages, conventional BAW gyroscopes have been hindered by a persistent and previously intractable source of error: damping coupling between degenerate modes. In an ideal axis-symmetric system, the two orthogonal vibrational modes possess identical mechanical properties, including mass, stiffness, and damping characteristics. In practice, however, material anisotropy, crystalline orientation, and fabrication-induced asymmetries lead to differences in the energy dissipation mechanisms experienced by each mode. In particular, when fabricated on anisotropic substrates such as (100) single-crystal silicon, the strain energy density at the resonator-substrate interface varies significantly between the two degenerate modes due to the directional dependence of elastic compliance and acoustic impedance. This results in unequal anchor loss for each mode, generating a damping-coupling term that induces a spurious displacement in the sense mode that is indistinguishable from the true Coriolis signal. Unlike stiffness coupling, which can be corrected through electrostatic tuning due to its quadrature phase relationship with the Coriolis signal, damping coupling occurs in-phase and cannot be separated using conventional demodulation techniques without introducing substantial complexity, power consumption, or bandwidth limitations.

Previous efforts to suppress anchor loss have included the use of phononic crystal structures, half-wavelength acoustic reflectors, and material impedance mismatches. While these approaches demonstrate theoretical promise, they introduce significant fabrication complexity, require precise control over periodic geometries or multi-layer interfaces, and often compromise the structural integrity or quality factor of the resonator. Moreover, these techniques are highly sensitive to variations in substrate thickness, mounting conditions, and packaging pressure, rendering them unreliable in mass-produced systems. As a result, even the most advanced BAW gyroscopes to date remain fundamentally dependent on the mechanical properties of their substrate, leading to unpredictable variations in quality factor, bias drift, and scale factor over temperature and environmental conditions. This dependency has prevented their widespread adoption in applications demanding consistent performance across diverse operating environments.

Additionally, the integration of BAW gyroscopes into practical systems has been further complicated by the challenge of maintaining mode degeneracy in the presence of fabrication tolerances. Minor deviations in geometry—such as elliptical distortions introduced during lithography—lead to frequency splitting between the degenerate modes, which reduces the efficiency of Coriolis coupling and introduces a static offset in the output. While electrostatic tuning electrodes have been employed to compensate for such frequency mismatches and stiffness coupling, no prior design has successfully addressed the root cause of damping asymmetry: the uncontrolled transfer of vibrational energy from the resonator into the substrate through non-symmetric anchor coupling. Consequently, existing BAW gyroscopes continue to exhibit temperature-dependent bias instability, poor shock resilience, and sensitivity to environmental pressure fluctuations, limiting their utility in real-world applications.

The need therefore exists for a vibratory gyroscope that not only operates at high frequencies to inherently reject environmental vibrations but also eliminates the dominant source of environmentally dependent offset drift by fundamentally decoupling the resonator from substrate-induced damping mechanisms. Such a device must achieve this without increasing complexity, size, or power consumption, and must be manufacturable using standard MEMS processes to enable high-volume production. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel stress isolation architecture that transforms the energy dissipation profile of the resonator, rendering the damping characteristics of the degenerate modes symmetric and invariant to substrate conditions, thereby enabling unprecedented environmental stability and performance in a compact, scalable, and commercially viable package.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention discloses a high-frequency vibratory gyroscope that achieves exceptional environmental robustness, minimal bias drift, and immunity to shock and vibration through the integration of a substrate-decoupled bulk-acoustic wave resonator structure with a uniquely engineered stress isolation system. The gyroscope operates in the megahertz frequency range using degenerate in-plane vibrational modes of a silicon-based resonator, with electrostatic excitation and capacitive readout, and incorporates a precisely designed mechanical decoupling architecture that isolates the resonator from substrate-induced energy dissipation. This isolation system is configured to attenuate strain transmission at the resonator-substrate interface while maintaining axis-symmetric mechanical coupling to both degenerate modes, thereby ensuring that the damping characteristics of each mode remain identical regardless of substrate properties, mounting conditions, or environmental temperature variations. As a result, the device exhibits a quality factor exceeding 60,000 under moderate vacuum conditions, maintains mode degeneracy across a wide temperature range, and demonstrates negligible sensitivity to linear accelerations and broadband mechanical disturbances, enabling performance levels previously unattainable in microfabricated gyroscopes.

The core innovation of the invention lies in the mechanical architecture that decouples the resonator from its substrate. Unlike prior approaches that rely on acoustic reflectors, phononic crystals, or material impedance mismatches, the present invention employs a stress isolation structure composed of a series of radially symmetric, elastically compliant arms that extend from the outer perimeter of the resonator to the anchor points. These arms are designed to have a geometric profile and material thickness that selectively attenuate the propagation of strain energy from the resonator toward the substrate while preserving the vibrational integrity of the degenerate modes. The structure is fabricated as an integral part of the same monolithic silicon layer as the resonator, ensuring perfect alignment and mechanical homogeneity. The geometry of the isolation arms is optimized such that the strain energy density at the interface between the arms and the substrate is minimized, and the energy that would otherwise be dissipated into the substrate is instead stored within the compliant structure and dissipated through thermoelastic mechanisms that are symmetrically distributed between both degenerate modes. This transformation renders the damping coupling terms negligible, eliminating the primary source of environmentally dependent offset drift that has historically plagued high-frequency gyroscopes.

The resonator itself is fabricated from a 40-micrometer-thick single-crystal silicon layer on a silicon-on-insulator (SOI) wafer, with a buried oxide layer providing electrical isolation. The resonator is shaped as a circular disk with a pattern of release holes arranged in an axis-symmetric configuration to minimize thermoelastic damping asymmetry. Two orthogonal, second-elliptical in-plane vibrational modes are excited and detected using capacitive electrodes arranged around the periphery of the structure. These electrodes are positioned to coincide with the displacement antinodes of the respective modes, enabling efficient electrostatic drive and capacitive readout. Ultra-narrow capacitive gaps of 270 nanometers are maintained between the electrodes and the resonator to maximize sensitivity and minimize drive voltage requirements. Additional tuning electrodes are strategically placed between the antinodes to apply electrostatic spring softening, enabling precise compensation of frequency splits and stiffness coupling induced by fabrication imperfections. A polarization voltage is applied to the resonator to establish a stable electrostatic bias, while the tuning and decoupling electrodes are driven by independent control signals to maintain optimal mode alignment and quadrature cancellation.

The device is packaged in a hermetically sealed wafer-level package maintained at a pressure between one and ten torr, which optimizes the balance between squeeze-film damping and vacuum quality, ensuring high quality factor without requiring ultra-high vacuum conditions. Through-silicon vias and metal interconnects provide electrical access to all electrodes from the top surface of the package, enabling integration with complementary application-specific integrated circuits for closed-loop control, signal demodulation, and temperature compensation. The resulting system exhibits an angle-random walk of less than 0.4 degrees per square root hour and a bias instability of less than 11 degrees per hour, with performance that remains stable across temperature ranges from −40 to 85 degrees Celsius. The device demonstrates a vibration sensitivity of less than 0.012 degrees per second per root-mean-square gram and an offset shift of less than 1.8 degrees per second under a 40-g half-sine shock pulse, confirming its exceptional immunity to mechanical disturbances.

The invention further enables a level of environmental stability previously unattainable in MEMS gyroscopes by decoupling the damping characteristics of the degenerate modes from the substrate. This is achieved not through the addition of complex acoustic structures or multi-layer interfaces, but through a simple, monolithic, and manufacturable mechanical design that transforms the energy dissipation pathway of the resonator. The stress isolation structure ensures that the dominant energy loss mechanism becomes thermoelastic damping, which is inherently symmetric and predictable, rather than anchor loss, which is highly variable and dependent on external conditions. As a result, the quality factor of the device follows a predictable 1/T^n temperature dependence, and the damping coupling between modes remains negligible across all operating conditions. This fundamental shift in the source of energy dissipation enables the device to maintain mode degeneracy, minimize bias drift, and reject environmental perturbations without the need for complex feedback algorithms, redundant structures, or post-fabrication calibration.

The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the design of vibratory gyroscopes, moving away from compensation-based approaches toward a structural solution that eliminates the root cause of environmental sensitivity. The resulting device is scalable, compatible with standard MEMS fabrication processes, and suitable for high-volume production in consumer, automotive, and industrial applications where reliability, stability, and miniaturization are essential. It provides a practical and commercially viable solution to the longstanding challenge of environmental drift in high-performance inertial sensors, enabling new levels of accuracy in navigation, stabilization, and motion tracking applications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Mode-to-Mode Coupling in Vibratory Gyroscopes

The operation of the present invention is predicated on the precise control and suppression of mode-to-mode coupling in a high-frequency, axis-symmetric vibratory gyroscope. Unlike conventional low-frequency tuning-fork designs that rely on differential motion between two proof masses, this device exploits the degenerate vibrational modes of a single, monolithic resonator structure to detect angular rotation through the Coriolis effect. The resonator is designed to support two orthogonal, in-plane vibrational modes that are nearly identical in frequency, mass distribution, and stiffness, forming a symmetric system in which any deviation from perfect symmetry introduces parasitic coupling terms that degrade performance. These coupling terms arise from imperfections in material properties, geometric asymmetry, and mechanical anchoring, and manifest as unwanted displacements in the sense mode that are indistinguishable from the true rotation-induced signal. The invention overcomes these challenges through a combination of structural design, electrostatic compensation, and substrate decoupling that collectively eliminate the dominant sources of coupling, enabling unprecedented signal fidelity and environmental stability.

The fundamental behavior of the gyroscope is described by a pair of coupled second-order differential equations that govern the motion of the two degenerate modes. Each mode is characterized by its effective mass, stiffness, and damping, which, in an ideal system, are identical for both modes. When the device is subjected to an angular rotation about an axis perpendicular to the plane of vibration, the Coriolis force couples the motion of the drive mode to the sense mode, inducing a displacement proportional to the rate of rotation. This displacement is detected as a change in capacitance between the resonator and a set of electrodes aligned with the antinodes of the sense mode. The magnitude of the induced signal is determined by the quality factor of the sense mode, the amplitude of the drive mode’s oscillation, and the angular gain of the system, which is dictated by the geometry and mode shape of the resonator. For optimal sensitivity, the drive mode is maintained in self-oscillation at a frequency equal to the natural resonance of the sense mode, ensuring maximum energy transfer through the Coriolis coupling.

However, in any real device, fabrication tolerances and material anisotropy introduce deviations from this ideal condition. Differences in the effective mass of the two modes—referred to as anisoinertia—result in a frequency split between the resonances of the drive and sense modes. Similarly, variations in the local stiffness distribution—termed anisoelasticity—lead to asymmetric elastic coupling between the modes, manifesting as a stiffness coupling term that induces a spurious displacement in the sense mode at quadrature phase relative to the Coriolis signal. This quadrature error, while detrimental to performance, can be identified and corrected through coherent demodulation techniques, as its phase is distinct from the true rotation signal. Electrostatic tuning electrodes, strategically positioned between the antinodes of the modes, are employed to apply a controlled electrostatic spring softening effect that compensates for these frequency splits and stiffness coupling terms. By adjusting the voltage applied to these electrodes, the effective stiffness of each mode can be tuned independently, allowing the frequency split to be reduced to zero and the quadrature error to be nulled. This electrostatic compensation mechanism, while effective, is insufficient on its own, as it does not address the most insidious form of coupling: damping coupling.

Damping coupling arises from differences in the energy dissipation mechanisms experienced by each of the two degenerate modes. In a vibratory system, damping is not a uniform property but rather the cumulative result of multiple loss mechanisms, including squeeze-film damping, thermoelastic damping, surface losses, and anchor loss. While the first three of these mechanisms can be engineered to be symmetric between the modes—through axis-symmetric hole patterns, uniform surface treatments, and controlled ambient pressure—the fourth, anchor loss, is inherently asymmetric in conventional designs. Anchor loss occurs when vibrational energy from the resonator propagates into the underlying substrate through the anchor points, and the rate of this energy transfer depends on the local strain distribution at the resonator-substrate interface. In anisotropic materials such as (100) single-crystal silicon, the elastic compliance varies with crystallographic orientation, causing the strain energy density to differ between the two degenerate modes. As a result, one mode may experience significantly greater energy dissipation than the other, leading to an imbalance in damping coefficients. This imbalance generates a damping coupling term that induces a displacement in the sense mode that is in-phase with the true Coriolis signal, rendering it indistinguishable from the desired rotation measurement. Unlike quadrature errors, which can be suppressed through phase-sensitive demodulation, in-phase damping coupling cannot be separated from the true signal without introducing additional complexity, such as mode reversal or dual-mode excitation, which dramatically increases power consumption and reduces bandwidth.

The present invention eliminates this fundamental limitation by introducing a substrate-decoupled mechanical architecture that fundamentally alters the energy dissipation pathway of the resonator. Rather than allowing strain energy to propagate directly from the resonator into the substrate, the invention employs a stress isolation structure composed of a series of radially symmetric, elastically compliant arms that extend from the outer perimeter of the resonator to the anchor points. These arms are designed with a precisely controlled geometry that attenuates the transmission of strain energy toward the substrate while preserving the vibrational integrity of the degenerate modes. The arms are fabricated as an integral part of the same monolithic silicon layer as the resonator, ensuring perfect alignment and mechanical homogeneity. Their thickness, width, and curvature are optimized such that the strain energy density at the interface between the arms and the substrate is minimized, and the energy that would otherwise be dissipated into the substrate is instead stored within the compliant structure and dissipated through thermoelastic mechanisms that are symmetrically distributed between both modes. This transformation ensures that the dominant source of damping becomes thermoelastic damping, which is inherently symmetric and predictable, rather than anchor loss, which is variable and dependent on external conditions.

The effectiveness of this architecture is demonstrated through finite element analysis, which reveals that the quality factor due to anchor loss in the proposed design exceeds 10^6, while the quality factor due to thermoelastic damping remains below 10^5. This indicates that the contribution of anchor loss to the total damping is negligible, and that the damping characteristics of both modes are now governed by the same physical mechanism. As a result, the damping coupling terms b12 and b21 in the governing equations are effectively reduced to zero, eliminating the in-phase error signal that has historically plagued high-frequency gyroscopes. The symmetry of the stress isolation structure ensures that any variation in the thermoelastic damping coefficient—such as that induced by temperature changes—affects both modes identically, preserving mode degeneracy and preventing the emergence of environmentally dependent bias drift. This is a fundamental departure from prior designs, in which the quality factor of each mode varied independently with temperature, leading to unpredictable shifts in the damping balance and consequent offset instability.

The electrostatic tuning and decoupling electrodes further enhance this performance by providing fine control over the residual asymmetries that may persist due to fabrication tolerances. The tuning electrodes, aligned with the antinodes of the modes, allow for precise adjustment of the resonant frequencies to compensate for any remaining anisoinertia or anisoelasticity. The decoupling electrodes, positioned between the antinodes, apply a controlled electrostatic field that modifies the effective stiffness coupling between the modes, enabling the suppression of any residual quadrature error. Together, these electrodes form a closed-loop control system that maintains optimal mode alignment and minimizes all forms of mode-to-mode coupling, ensuring that the only displacement observed in the sense mode is the result of true angular rotation. The result is a gyroscope that operates with exceptional fidelity, exhibiting a bias instability of less than 11 degrees per hour and an angle-random walk of less than 0.4 degrees per square root hour, even in the presence of environmental perturbations that would severely degrade the performance of conventional designs.

This level of performance is achieved not through the addition of complex signal processing algorithms, redundant structures, or external compensation circuits, but through a structural innovation that redefines the fundamental energy dissipation mechanism of the device. The stress isolation structure transforms the gyroscope from a system that is vulnerable to its environment into one that is inherently robust, enabling a new class of inertial sensors that deliver laboratory-grade performance in commercial, high-volume applications. The invention thus represents a breakthrough in the field of vibratory gyroscopy, providing a practical and scalable solution to the longstanding challenge of environmental drift and damping coupling in high-frequency inertial sensors.
