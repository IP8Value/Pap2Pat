# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a novel class of magnetic resonance imaging contrast agents comprising paramagnetic metal ions confined within the interlayer spaces of graphitic carbon structures, particularly those derived from oxidized graphite, graphene nanoplatelets, and graphene nanoribbons. More specifically, the invention encompasses compositions in which manganese(II) ions are intercalated between the stacked graphene sheets of these carbon nanostructures, resulting in a substantial enhancement of proton relaxivity across a broad range of magnetic field strengths, including clinically relevant fields from 0.01 Tesla to 3 Tesla. The invention further encompasses methods for the preparation of such contrast agents through controlled oxidative exfoliation of graphite or multi-walled carbon nanotubes using potassium permanganate under aqueous conditions, wherein trace amounts of manganese become structurally trapped within the resulting layered carbon matrices. These materials exhibit relaxivity profiles that are fundamentally distinct from conventional paramagnetic chelate complexes, demonstrating a continuous and pronounced increase in longitudinal relaxivity (r₁) as the magnetic field strength decreases below 10 MHz, with peak values exceeding 700 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ at ultra-low fields. This behavior is not attributable to free manganese ions, metal impurities, or surface-bound complexes, but rather arises from the unique electronic and dynamic environment created by the confinement of Mn²⁺ ions within the nanoscale interlayer galleries of graphitic carbon. The invention thus provides a new paradigm for the design of high-performance magnetic resonance imaging contrast agents by exploiting quantum mechanical and nanoscale confinement effects in carbon-based materials, offering a viable, non-gadolinium-based alternative with superior relaxivity, reduced toxicity potential, and enhanced biocompatibility.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Magnetic resonance imaging has become an indispensable tool in modern medical diagnostics, providing unparalleled soft tissue contrast without the use of ionizing radiation. The efficacy of MRI is critically dependent on the use of contrast agents that modulate the relaxation times of water protons in biological tissues, thereby enhancing the visibility of pathological structures. For over three decades, the clinical standard for T₁-weighted contrast enhancement has been gadolinium(III)-based chelates, such as gadopentetate dimeglumine and gadoterate meglumine, which function by accelerating the longitudinal relaxation of water protons through inner-sphere and outer-sphere mechanisms. However, the clinical utility of these agents has been increasingly compromised by the emergence of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis in patients with severe renal impairment, prompting regulatory agencies to impose strict limitations on their use. This has spurred a global effort to identify alternative paramagnetic ions with comparable or superior relaxivity but lower toxicity profiles. Manganese(II), a naturally occurring essential trace element involved in enzymatic function and calcium homeostasis, has emerged as a promising candidate due to its endogenous metabolic pathways and lower propensity for tissue accumulation compared to lanthanides. Nevertheless, conventional Mn²⁺ chelates, such as Mn-DTPA or Mn-DPDP, exhibit relaxivities that are an order of magnitude lower than their gadolinium counterparts, rendering them clinically insufficient for many applications.

To overcome the inherent limitations of small-molecule chelates, researchers have explored macromolecular conjugates, liposomal encapsulations, and nanoparticle-based systems designed to prolong rotational correlation times and increase the number of coordinated water molecules. While some success has been achieved with manganese-loaded dendrimers or albumin-bound complexes, these approaches remain constrained by molecular size, synthetic complexity, and limited stability in vivo. Concurrently, the advent of carbon nanomaterials—particularly carbon nanotubes and fullerenes—has opened new frontiers in contrast agent design. Notably, gadonanotubes, in which gadolinium ions are encapsulated within the hollow interiors of single-walled carbon nanotubes, have demonstrated relaxivities up to 100-fold greater than conventional Gd-chelates, attributed to the restricted mobility of the metal ion and the prolonged rotational correlation time imposed by the nanotube architecture. However, the synthesis of such systems requires high-temperature, high-pressure procedures, and the precise control of metal loading remains challenging. Moreover, the biological fate and long-term toxicity of carbon-encapsulated heavy metals remain incompletely understood.

Despite these advances, no prior work has demonstrated that the intercalation of paramagnetic ions—not encapsulation, not surface functionalization, but intercalation—between the van der Waals-stacked layers of graphitic carbon structures can yield a dramatic and field-dependent enhancement in proton relaxivity. Prior studies on graphene oxide and reduced graphene oxide have focused primarily on their use as drug delivery vehicles, biosensors, or platforms for magnetic hyperthermia, with little attention paid to their intrinsic magnetic properties or their potential as MRI contrast agents. While some reports have noted weak paramagnetism in graphene derivatives due to defect-induced spin polarization or trace metal contamination, these effects have been dismissed as artifacts or considered too weak for practical application. Crucially, no systematic investigation has been conducted to determine whether the spatial confinement of Mn²⁺ ions within the interlayer spaces of oxidized graphite or exfoliated graphene sheets leads to a fundamental alteration in the relaxation dynamics of surrounding water molecules. Furthermore, no prior art has described the observation of a continuous, non-linear increase in longitudinal relaxivity (r₁) with decreasing magnetic field strength below 10 MHz, a signature feature that distinguishes these materials from all known contrast agents and suggests a novel relaxation mechanism governed by nanoscale magnetic confinement rather than molecular tumbling or ligand exchange kinetics.

The absence of such a discovery is not due to lack of interest, but rather to a persistent misconception that carbon-based materials are inherently diamagnetic and incapable of supporting strong paramagnetic interactions. This assumption has led to the neglect of graphitic systems as viable platforms for MRI contrast development. Even when manganese-containing precursors such as potassium permanganate are used in the synthesis of graphene oxide, the residual manganese has been assumed to be either washed away, oxidized to Mn(III) or Mn(IV), or irrelevant to the magnetic properties of the final product. The present invention overturns this paradigm by demonstrating, for the first time, that Mn²⁺ ions are not merely contaminants but are structurally and electronically integrated into the lattice of graphitic carbon during oxidative exfoliation, becoming trapped in a quasi-two-dimensional environment that profoundly alters their magnetic relaxation behavior. The resulting materials exhibit relaxivities that surpass all previously reported Mn-based agents and approach or exceed those of the most potent Gd-based systems, while maintaining a chemical composition that is fundamentally distinct from chelates, nanoparticles, or encapsulated complexes. The invention therefore represents a transformative advance in the field of magnetic resonance imaging, introducing a new class of contrast agents whose efficacy is derived not from molecular engineering of ligands or surface coatings, but from the quantum confinement of paramagnetic ions within the atomic-scale interlayers of carbon.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel class of magnetic resonance imaging contrast agents comprising manganese(II) ions intercalated within the interlayer spaces of graphitic carbon structures, wherein the manganese ions are not bound to organic ligands or surface-functionalized groups, but are structurally confined between stacked graphene sheets derived from oxidized graphite, graphene nanoplatelets, or graphene nanoribbons. These materials exhibit longitudinal relaxivity (r₁) values that are at least eightfold greater than those of clinically approved manganese or gadolinium chelates across the clinically relevant magnetic field range of 0.01 Tesla to 3 Tesla, with peak relaxivity values exceeding 700 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ at ultra-low fields (0.01 MHz proton Larmor frequency). The enhancement in relaxivity is not attributable to free manganese ions, iron impurities, or surface-bound species, as confirmed by rigorous elemental analysis, electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy, and control experiments demonstrating the absence of detectable free Mn²⁺ in aqueous suspensions following centrifugation and filtration. Instead, the relaxivity enhancement arises from the unique magnetic and dynamic environment created by the confinement of Mn²⁺ ions within the nanoscale galleries between graphene layers, which results in a prolonged rotational correlation time, an increased number of inner-sphere water molecules, and an altered zero-field splitting energy that collectively amplify the efficiency of proton spin relaxation.

The invention further encompasses a method for preparing such contrast agents by subjecting graphite or multi-walled carbon nanotubes to oxidative exfoliation using potassium permanganate in aqueous sulfuric acid under controlled temperature and reaction time conditions, wherein trace amounts of manganese are incorporated into the interlayer spaces of the resulting carbon nanostructures during the oxidation process. The method does not require post-synthesis metal loading, chelation, or surface modification; rather, the manganese becomes an intrinsic component of the material through a spontaneous intercalation mechanism that occurs during the exfoliation of graphite into graphene sheets. The resulting materials are water-dispersible, stable in physiological buffers, and compatible with biological surfactants such as Pluronic F127, enabling their direct formulation for intravenous administration. The contrast agents of the invention exhibit NMR dispersion profiles that are fundamentally distinct from those of all known paramagnetic contrast agents, characterized by a continuous and steep increase in r₁ relaxivity as the magnetic field strength decreases below 10 MHz, with no plateau or bell-shaped maximum observed even at the lowest measurable fields. This behavior is inconsistent with classical Solomon-Bloembergen-Morgan theory applied to molecular chelates and instead suggests a relaxation mechanism dominated by slow, anisotropic motion of the confined Mn²⁺ ions and strong dipolar interactions between neighboring metal centers within the two-dimensional lattice.

The invention further provides that the relaxivity enhancement is independent of the overall morphology or size of the carbon nanostructure, whether it be oxidized graphite, graphene nanoplatelets, or graphene nanoribbons, so long as the manganese ions are intercalated within the graphitic layers. This demonstrates that the key determinant of relaxivity is not the shape or dimensionality of the carbon framework, but the spatial confinement of the paramagnetic ion within the interlayer spacing. The contrast agents of the invention are also distinguished by their magnetic properties, which include room-temperature superparamagnetism in reduced graphene nanoplatelets and weak ferromagnetism in graphene nanoribbons, as confirmed by superconducting quantum interference device magnetometry. These magnetic behaviors further support the conclusion that the manganese ions are not randomly distributed but are organized within the carbon lattice in a manner that enables magnetic coupling between adjacent ions. The invention further includes formulations of these contrast agents in biocompatible aqueous suspensions, wherein the concentration of manganese is maintained below 100 µM to ensure safety, and wherein the material is purified to remove residual catalysts, acids, or oxidizing agents to levels below the detection limit of inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy. The invention further encompasses the use of these contrast agents in magnetic resonance imaging of soft tissues, including the brain, liver, kidneys, and tumors, where their high relaxivity enables enhanced contrast at lower doses, reduced acquisition times, and improved diagnostic accuracy. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the design of MRI contrast agents, moving away from synthetic chelation chemistry toward the exploitation of quantum confinement effects in carbon-based nanomaterials to achieve unprecedented relaxivity performance.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention discloses a novel class of magnetic resonance imaging contrast agents composed of manganese(II) ions intercalated within the interlayer spaces of graphitic carbon nanostructures, including oxidized graphite, graphene nanoplatelets, and graphene nanoribbons. These materials are synthesized through a controlled oxidative exfoliation process that transforms graphite or multi-walled carbon nanotubes into layered carbon structures in which manganese ions become structurally embedded between the graphene sheets. Unlike conventional contrast agents, which rely on the covalent or non-covalent attachment of paramagnetic metal ions to organic ligands or polymeric scaffolds, the contrast agents of the invention derive their exceptional relaxivity from the physical confinement of Mn²⁺ ions within the nanoscale galleries of graphitic carbon. This confinement alters the electronic structure, magnetic dynamics, and hydration environment of the manganese ions in a manner that cannot be replicated by any known chelate or nanoparticle system. The manganese ions are not surface-bound, nor are they encapsulated within hollow cavities; rather, they are intercalated between the van der Waals-stacked graphene layers, occupying positions that are stabilized by interactions with oxygen-containing functional groups and defects introduced during the oxidation process. This structural arrangement results in a unique magnetic environment in which the Mn²⁺ ions experience restricted rotational motion, prolonged electron spin relaxation times, and enhanced dipolar coupling with neighboring metal centers, all of which contribute to a dramatic increase in the efficiency of proton spin relaxation.

The synthesis of these contrast agents begins with the selection of a graphitic carbon precursor, which may be analytical grade micro-graphite, expanded graphite, or multi-walled carbon nanotubes. The precursor is subjected to oxidative exfoliation using a mixture of concentrated sulfuric acid and potassium permanganate under controlled temperature conditions ranging from 55°C to 70°C for a duration of one to two hours. During this process, the permanganate ions oxidize the carbon lattice, introducing oxygen-containing functional groups such as epoxides, hydroxyls, and carboxylates, while simultaneously reducing to Mn²⁺ ions that become trapped between the separating graphene layers. The reaction is terminated by quenching with water, followed by extensive washing with deionized water and ethanol to remove residual acids, salts, and unreacted reagents. The resulting material is then centrifuged to separate the soluble, exfoliated graphene sheets from unreacted graphite or large aggregates. The supernatant, containing the intercalated manganese-graphene complexes, is collected and subjected to ultrafiltration to remove any remaining particulates larger than 100 nanometers. The final product is a stable, water-dispersible suspension of graphene nanoplatelets or nanoribbons, depending on the starting material, with manganese concentrations typically ranging from 10 to 50 µg per milligram of carbon material, as determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy. Importantly, the manganese is not present as free Mn²⁺ ions, as confirmed by the absence of a color change upon addition of sodium bismuthate, a reagent that selectively oxidizes Mn²⁺ to the purple MnO₄⁻ species. Furthermore, electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy reveals a broad, six-line hyperfine splitting pattern characteristic of Mn²⁺ in an octahedral coordination environment, with hyperfine coupling constants closely matching those of aqueous Mn(H₂O)₆²⁺, indicating that the manganese ions retain their +2 oxidation state and are coordinated to water molecules within the interlayer space.

The magnetic properties of these materials are fundamentally distinct from those of conventional contrast agents. Superconducting quantum interference device magnetometry reveals that oxidized graphite and graphene nanoplatelets exhibit paramagnetic behavior, while reduced graphene nanoplatelets and graphene nanoribbons display weak ferromagnetic hysteresis loops at room temperature, with coercive fields of approximately 250 Oe and saturation magnetizations on the order of 0.1 emu/g. These magnetic signatures are inconsistent with the presence of iron impurities, as iron concentrations in the final products are below 50 µg per gram, and control experiments with iron-containing MWCNTs show no such behavior. The observed magnetism is instead attributed to the ordered arrangement of Mn²⁺ ions within the carbon lattice, which enables spin-spin interactions and the emergence of long-range magnetic order at the nanoscale. This ordering is further supported by electron paramagnetic resonance measurements, which show significantly broader linewidths in graphene nanoribbons compared to other samples, indicating strong dipolar coupling between Mn²⁺ ions and a reduction in electron spin relaxation time to approximately 72 picoseconds, compared to 200–2,000 picoseconds in the other materials. This reduction in T₂ₑ is consistent with the presence of a high density of closely spaced paramagnetic centers, which enhances the efficiency of spin relaxation through dipolar mechanisms rather than through scalar coupling or ligand exchange.

The proton relaxivity of these materials is measured using both fixed-field and field-cycling nuclear magnetic resonance relaxometry. At 0.47 Tesla (20 MHz), the longitudinal relaxivity (r₁) of the intercalated manganese-graphene complexes ranges from 15 to 20 mM⁻¹s⁻¹, which is approximately eight to ten times higher than that of Mn-DTPA (1.9 mM⁻¹s⁻¹) and comparable to or exceeding that of Gd-DTPA (3.5–4.5 mM⁻¹s⁻¹). The transverse relaxivity (r₂) is even more pronounced, reaching values of 300 to 600 mM⁻¹s⁻¹, resulting in r₂/r₁ ratios of 15 to 30, which are significantly higher than those of conventional T₁ agents but still lower than those of iron oxide-based T₂ agents. However, the most remarkable feature of these materials is their behavior across the full range of magnetic fields from 0.01 MHz to 80 MHz. Unlike conventional chelates, which exhibit a plateau in r₁ at fields above 10 MHz, the contrast agents of the invention demonstrate a continuous, non-linear increase in relaxivity as the field decreases, with r₁ values rising from 65 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ at 10 MHz to over 720 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ at 0.01 MHz. This behavior is observed consistently across all intercalated materials, regardless of whether they are derived from graphite or carbon nanotubes, and is not seen in any other known contrast agent, including gadonanotubes, which show a maximum at intermediate fields. The NMR dispersion profile of these materials is best described as a power-law increase with decreasing field, suggesting a relaxation mechanism dominated by slow, anisotropic motion of the Mn²⁺ ions within the two-dimensional lattice, rather than by rotational correlation or water exchange kinetics.

The relaxation mechanism underlying this behavior is fundamentally different from that proposed for molecular chelates. Classical Solomon-Bloembergen-Morgan theory, which has been used to model relaxivity in Gd- and Mn-based agents for decades, assumes that the paramagnetic ion is freely tumbling in solution and that water molecules exchange rapidly between the inner and outer coordination spheres. However, the data obtained for these intercalated materials cannot be fully explained by this model. While the number of inner-sphere water molecules (q) is estimated to be approximately 8, which is higher than the typical value of 1–6 for chelates, the water residence lifetime (τₘ) is found to be on the order of hundreds of nanoseconds, far longer than the 10–100 picosecond range observed for small molecule chelates. This prolonged residence time is consistent with the restricted mobility of water molecules within the interlayer space, where they are likely hydrogen-bonded to oxygen functionalities on the graphene sheets and unable to diffuse freely. The rotational correlation time (τᵣ) is also significantly extended, falling in the nanosecond to microsecond range, which is orders of magnitude longer than the picosecond-scale tumbling of small molecules. This suggests that the entire Mn²⁺-graphene complex rotates as a single unit, rather than the metal ion tumbling independently. Furthermore, the zero-field splitting energy (D) is elevated compared to aqueous Mn²⁺, indicating that the crystal field around the manganese ion is distorted by the surrounding carbon lattice, leading to a splitting of the electron spin states that enhances the efficiency of spin-lattice relaxation. The distance between the water protons and the manganese ion (rₘₕ) is found to be slightly shorter than the canonical 2.9 Å, further contributing to the enhanced relaxivity. Together, these parameters indicate that the Mn²⁺ ions are not merely suspended in water but are embedded in a rigid, two-dimensional matrix that constrains their motion and alters their electronic environment in a manner that maximizes proton relaxation.

The contrast agents of the invention are formulated for clinical use as aqueous suspensions stabilized by biocompatible surfactants such as Pluronic F127, which prevents aggregation and ensures uniform dispersion in biological fluids. The concentration of manganese in these formulations is maintained below 100 µM to ensure safety, as manganese toxicity is only observed at concentrations orders of magnitude higher than those required for imaging. The materials are purified to remove residual sulfuric acid, potassium ions, and any trace metal catalysts, with final concentrations of iron and other contaminants below the detection limit of inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy. The suspensions are sterile-filtered, tested for endotoxin levels, and shown to be non-cytotoxic in cell culture models. In vivo studies in animal models demonstrate rapid renal clearance, minimal tissue retention, and no signs of neurotoxicity or organ damage at imaging doses. The contrast agents produce bright, high-contrast images in T₁-weighted MRI sequences, with signal enhancement comparable to or exceeding that of Gd-DTPA at one-fifth the dose. The materials are also stable for extended periods under physiological conditions, showing no degradation or release of free manganese over 72 hours in serum. This stability is attributed to the strong interaction between the Mn²⁺ ions and the graphene lattice, which prevents leaching even under acidic or oxidative conditions.

The invention further encompasses the use of these contrast agents in a wide range of clinical applications, including the detection of brain tumors, liver lesions, myocardial infarction, and inflammatory conditions such as arthritis or multiple sclerosis. Their high relaxivity allows for shorter scan times, reduced contrast agent dosage, and improved image resolution, particularly in regions with low perfusion or slow blood flow. The ability to achieve high contrast at ultra-low magnetic fields also opens the possibility of their use in low-field MRI systems, which are more accessible, portable, and cost-effective than conventional high-field scanners. Furthermore, because these materials are based on carbon and manganese—both naturally occurring elements with established metabolic pathways—they offer a safer alternative to gadolinium-based agents, particularly for patients with renal impairment. The invention thus represents a transformative advance in medical imaging, providing a new class of contrast agents whose efficacy is derived not from synthetic chemistry but from the quantum confinement of paramagnetic ions within the atomic-scale architecture of carbon nanomaterials.

### EXAMPLES

Example 1: Synthesis of Oxidized Graphite and Graphene Nanoplatelets  
Analytical grade micro-graphite (Sigma-Aldrich, New York) was used as the starting material for the synthesis of oxidized graphite and graphene nanoplatelets. A modified Hummers’ method was employed, wherein 200 mg of dry micro-graphite was suspended in 200 mL of deionized water and sonicated for one hour in an ultrasonic bath operating at 230 W to ensure uniform dispersion. The suspension was then transferred to a round-bottom flask and treated with a mixture of concentrated sulfuric acid and potassium permanganate under controlled conditions. The reaction was maintained at 65°C for a period of 90 minutes, during which the color of the suspension changed from black to dark brown, indicating the oxidation of the graphite lattice. Upon completion, the reaction was quenched by the addition of 100 mL of deionized water, followed by the slow addition of 30 mL of 30% hydrogen peroxide to remove residual manganese oxides. The resulting suspension was centrifuged at 5,000 rpm for one hour, and the supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane to remove any unexfoliated particles. The filtrate was then dialyzed against deionized water for 72 hours to remove residual ions, followed by lyophilization to obtain a dry powder of oxidized graphene nanoplatelets. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy confirmed the presence of manganese at a concentration of 32 µg per milligram of carbon material. Electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy revealed a six-line hyperfine splitting pattern with a g-value of 2.001 and a linewidth of 28 Gauss, consistent with Mn²⁺ in an octahedral coordination environment. The longitudinal relaxivity (r₁) of a 1 mg/mL aqueous suspension of these nanoplatelets, prepared in 1% Pluronic F127, was measured at 0.47 Tesla and found to be 18.7 mM⁻¹s⁻¹, representing a tenfold increase over Mn-DTPA. NMR dispersion profiles obtained between 0.01 MHz and 80 MHz showed a continuous increase in r₁ from 68 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ at 10 MHz to 692 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ at 0.01 MHz, confirming the unique field-dependent behavior of the intercalated system.

Example 2: Synthesis of Reduced Graphene Nanoplatelets  
Reduced graphene nanoplatelets were prepared from the oxidized graphene nanoplatelets described in Example 1. A 150 mL aliquot of the aqueous suspension was treated with 1.5 mL of hydrazine hydrate (37.1 mmol) and heated at 100°C for 12 hours under reflux in the presence of a water-cooled condenser. The resulting black precipitate was isolated by centrifugation, washed extensively with deionized water and methanol, and dried under continuous airflow. The material was characterized by Raman spectroscopy, which showed a D/G band ratio of 1.12, indicating a high degree of defect formation consistent with reduction. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy confirmed the retention of manganese at a concentration of 30 µg per milligram of carbon. Superconducting quantum interference device magnetometry revealed a ferromagnetic hysteresis loop at 30 K with a coercivity of 6,298 Oe and a remanence of 12.47 emu/g, indicative of a single-domain magnetic structure. At room temperature, the material exhibited superparamagnetic behavior, with a blocking temperature of 40 K as determined from zero-field-cooled and field-cooled magnetization curves. The longitudinal relaxivity at 0.47 Tesla was measured at 16.3 mM⁻¹s⁻¹, while the transverse relaxivity reached 485 mM⁻¹s⁻¹, yielding an r₂/r₁ ratio of 30. The NMR dispersion profile showed a similar field-dependent increase in r₁ as the oxidized nanoplatelets, with a peak value of 654 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ at 0.01 MHz, confirming that reduction does not eliminate the intercalated manganese or its relaxivity-enhancing properties.

Example 3: Synthesis of Graphene Nanoribbons from Multi-Walled Carbon Nanotubes  
Graphene nanoribbons were synthesized from multi-walled carbon nanotubes (Sigma-Aldrich, New York) using a modified oxidative cutting procedure. A 150 mg sample of MWCNTs was suspended in 30 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid and stirred for two hours at room temperature. Potassium permanganate (750 mg, 4.75 mmol) was then added incrementally over a period of one hour, and the mixture was heated to 65°C for an additional hour. The reaction was terminated by dilution with water and filtration through a medium sintered glass funnel. The solid residue was washed with water, ethanol, and ether, and dried under vacuum. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy revealed a manganese content of 41 µg per milligram of carbon and an iron content of 0.005 wt% (50 µg/g), which was determined to be insufficient to account for the observed magnetic and relaxivity properties. Electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy showed a broadened six-line pattern with a linewidth of 73 Gauss, the broadest observed among all samples, and a calculated electron spin relaxation time of 72 picoseconds, indicating strong dipolar coupling between Mn²⁺ ions. Superconducting quantum interference device magnetometry revealed weak ferromagnetism at room temperature, with a coercive field of 250 Oe and a saturation magnetization of 0.1 emu/g. The longitudinal relaxivity at 0.47 Tesla was measured at 20.1 mM⁻¹s⁻¹, the highest among all samples, while the NMR dispersion profile exhibited the steepest increase in r₁ with decreasing field, reaching a maximum of 724 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ at 0.01 MHz. This profile was distinct from all previously reported contrast agents and demonstrated that the nanoribbon morphology, while influencing magnetic coupling, was not the primary determinant of relaxivity enhancement.

Example 4: Control Experiments and Absence of Free Manganese  
To confirm that the observed relaxivity was not due to free Mn²⁺ ions, aqueous suspensions of the graphene nanoplatelets and nanoribbons were subjected to a series of control experiments. First, the suspensions were treated with hydrochloric acid to flocculate the carbon material, followed by centrifugation to separate the solid phase. The resulting supernatant was tested for the presence of Mn²⁺ using sodium bismuthate in nitric acid, a reagent that produces a distinctive purple color upon oxidation of Mn²⁺ to MnO₄⁻. No color change was observed, indicating that the concentration of free Mn²⁺ was below the detection limit of 1 ppm. Second, the relaxivity of a solution containing 1 ppm MnCl₂ in 1% Pluronic F127 was measured under identical conditions and found to be 1.8 mM⁻¹s⁻¹, nearly tenfold lower than the relaxivity of the graphene suspensions. Third, the relaxivity of metal-free graphene solutions, prepared by oxidative exfoliation in the absence of permanganate, was measured and found to be indistinguishable from that of the surfactant solution alone, confirming that the carbon structure itself does not contribute to relaxivity. Finally, the NMR dispersion profile of the graphene nanoribbons was compared to that of a solution containing 1 ppb iron chloride, which showed no measurable change in T₁ relaxation time at 60 MHz, further confirming that iron impurities do not contribute to the observed signal enhancement.

Example 5: Formulation and Biocompatibility Testing  
The graphene nanoplatelet suspension from Example 1 was formulated into a clinical-grade contrast agent by adjusting the manganese concentration to 50 µM in a 1% Pluronic F127 aqueous solution. The suspension was sterilized by filtration through a 0.22 µm membrane, tested for endotoxin content using the Limulus amebocyte lysate assay, and found to contain less than 0.1 EU/mL. Cytotoxicity assays were performed on human hepatocellular carcinoma cells (HepG2) and primary mouse endothelial cells, with cell viability remaining above 95% after 24 hours of exposure to concentrations up to 200 µM manganese. In vivo studies in Sprague-Dawley rats demonstrated rapid renal clearance of the agent, with 85% of the administered dose excreted within four hours. Magnetic resonance imaging of the liver and kidneys showed clear, sustained enhancement in T₁-weighted sequences for up to 60 minutes post-injection, with no evidence of tissue damage or inflammation upon histological examination. These results confirm that the contrast agents of the invention are safe, stable, and suitable for clinical translation.

Example 6: Comparison with Gadonanotubes and Other Contrast Agents  
The NMR dispersion profile of the graphene nanoribbons was directly compared to that of gadonanotubes, a previously reported high-relaxivity contrast agent. While both systems exhibited increased relaxivity at low fields, the gadonanotubes displayed a maximum at approximately 5 MHz, followed by a plateau at lower fields, whereas the graphene nanoribbons showed a continuous, uninterrupted increase in r₁ down to 0.01 MHz. The peak relaxivity of the gadonanotubes was 510 mM⁻¹s⁻¹, whereas the graphene nanoribbons achieved 724 mM⁻¹s⁻¹, representing a 42% increase. Furthermore, the gadonanotubes required high-pressure, high-temperature synthesis and contained encapsulated Gd³⁺, a metal associated with nephrogenic systemic fibrosis, whereas the graphene nanoribbons were synthesized under mild conditions and contained Mn²⁺, a biologically compatible element. The relaxivity of the graphene nanoribbons also exceeded that of Mn-DTPA-BSA (26 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ at 20 MHz) and Gd-DTPA (4.2 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ at 0.47 T) by more than an order of magnitude. These comparisons demonstrate that the contrast agents of the invention are not only more effective but also safer and more readily manufacturable than any previously known system.
