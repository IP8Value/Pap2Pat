# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to hybrid quantum-classical optimization algorithms for solving combinatorial optimization problems using noisy intermediate-scale quantum computers. More specifically, the invention pertains to a novel method for aggregating measurement outcomes from parameterized quantum circuits by employing the Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) as an objective function, thereby improving the convergence rate and solution quality of variational quantum algorithms such as the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) and the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA). The invention is particularly suited for applications in finance, logistics, supply chain management, scheduling, network design, and resource allocation, where high-quality approximate solutions to NP-hard optimization problems are required under practical constraints of limited quantum coherence and gate fidelity. The method leverages the diagonal structure of Hamiltonians derived from classical combinatorial problems to focus optimization efforts on the most promising regions of the probability distribution of measurement outcomes, rather than on the statistical mean, thus aligning the quantum algorithm’s objective more closely with the practical goal of identifying high-performing solutions rather than achieving average-case performance.

## BACKGROUND

Combinatorial optimization problems form the backbone of numerous real-world decision-making processes across industries, including manufacturing, transportation, telecommunications, and financial portfolio management. These problems, which involve selecting the best configuration from a finite set of discrete possibilities, are frequently NP-hard, meaning that no known classical algorithm can guarantee optimal solutions in polynomial time for arbitrary instances. Despite their computational intractability, heuristic and approximate methods have been developed to find high-quality solutions in practice, often leveraging local search, simulated annealing, genetic algorithms, or constraint programming techniques. The advent of quantum computing has spurred significant interest in developing quantum-enhanced heuristics capable of outperforming classical counterparts, particularly in the era of noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices, where full fault-tolerant quantum computation remains years away.

Among the most prominent candidates for NISQ-era optimization are the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) and the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA). Both are hybrid quantum-classical algorithms that rely on a parameterized quantum circuit—referred to as a variational form—to prepare a trial quantum state, which is then measured to extract classical information about the problem’s objective function. The parameters of the quantum circuit are iteratively adjusted by a classical optimizer to minimize an objective function derived from the measurement outcomes. In the original formulations of VQE and QAOA, this objective function is defined as the expected value of the problem Hamiltonian, computed as the arithmetic mean of a finite number of independent measurements. This approach is well-justified in quantum chemistry applications, where the Hamiltonian represents a physical observable such as molecular energy, and minimizing its expectation corresponds to finding the ground state of a quantum system. However, when applied to combinatorial optimization problems, the underlying Hamiltonian is diagonal in the computational basis, meaning that its eigenvalues correspond directly to the objective function values of classical bitstrings. In this context, the goal is not to approximate an energy level but to identify one or more bitstrings that yield near-optimal values of the combinatorial objective.

The use of the expected value as the optimization criterion in this setting introduces a fundamental misalignment between the algorithm’s objective and the practical goal. Since the optimal solution corresponds to a single (or a small set of) basis states with minimal (or maximal) eigenvalue, the success of the algorithm depends not on the average performance across all possible outcomes, but on the ability to generate a quantum state with sufficient amplitude on the optimal or near-optimal solutions. The expected value, however, is sensitive to the entire distribution of outcomes, including those with poor objective values, and may therefore be driven by states that are far from the optimum. This can lead to slow convergence, poor sample efficiency, and a failure to identify high-quality solutions even when they are accessible within the expressivity of the variational form. Moreover, the flatness of the resulting optimization landscape—particularly in algorithms with limited parameterization such as QAOA—can cause the classical optimizer to become trapped in regions where the gradient is negligible, even when the quantum state contains non-negligible overlap with the optimal solution.

Previous attempts to improve the performance of VQE and QAOA have focused on modifying the variational form through deeper circuits, adaptive parameter initialization, or enhanced classical optimizers. While these approaches have yielded marginal gains, they often come at the cost of increased circuit depth, which exacerbates the effects of decoherence and gate errors on NISQ hardware. Alternative strategies, such as using the minimum observed outcome as the objective, have been considered but discarded due to the non-smooth, discontinuous nature of the resulting function, which renders it incompatible with gradient-based optimization methods. There remains, therefore, a critical gap in the art: a method that preserves the structure and efficiency of existing hybrid quantum-classical algorithms while redefining the optimization objective in a way that better aligns with the practical goal of identifying high-quality solutions, without requiring additional quantum resources or deeper circuits.

The present invention addresses this gap by introducing the Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) as a novel aggregation function for the measurement outcomes obtained from variational quantum circuits. CVaR, a risk measure widely adopted in financial portfolio theory, quantifies the expected value of the worst-case outcomes within a specified tail of the distribution. By focusing optimization on the lower α-tail of the objective function distribution—where α is a confidence level between 0 and 1—the method prioritizes the improvement of the best-performing samples while maintaining a smooth, differentiable objective function suitable for gradient-based optimization. This approach effectively shifts the algorithm’s focus from the average performance to the most promising region of the probability distribution, thereby increasing the likelihood of sampling optimal or near-optimal solutions with fewer iterations and greater robustness to noise. Empirical results demonstrate that CVaR-based variants of VQE and QAOA achieve significantly faster convergence and higher solution quality across a diverse set of combinatorial optimization benchmarks, including maximum cut, portfolio optimization, number partitioning, and maximum satisfiability. Furthermore, the method exhibits remarkable resilience to hardware noise, as it naturally filters out low-quality measurement outcomes that are likely corrupted by decoherence or gate errors, allowing the classical optimizer to be guided by the most reliable information available. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the design of quantum optimization algorithms, moving from expectation-based optimization to tail-focused optimization, and enables practical quantum advantage on current and near-future quantum hardware without requiring hardware improvements or algorithmic complexity beyond a simple modification of the objective function.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a method for solving combinatorial optimization problems using a hybrid quantum-classical algorithm that employs the Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) as the objective function for guiding the optimization of a parameterized quantum circuit. The method comprises preparing a trial quantum state using a variational form consisting of a sequence of parameterized single-qubit rotations and entangling gates, measuring the state multiple times to obtain a set of classical bitstrings and corresponding objective function values, and computing the CVaR of these values at a specified confidence level α, where α is a real number strictly between zero and one. The classical optimizer then adjusts the parameters of the variational form to minimize the computed CVaR, iteratively refining the quantum state until convergence is achieved. Upon termination, the bitstring yielding the lowest objective function value among all measured outcomes is returned as the solution to the combinatorial optimization problem.

The variational form may be implemented using any parameterized quantum circuit capable of generating a superposition of computational basis states, including but not limited to the layered structure of VQE, which employs alternating layers of single-qubit Y-rotations and controlled-Z entanglement gates, or the alternating unitary structure of QAOA, which employs layers of Pauli-X and Pauli-Z rotations derived from the problem Hamiltonian. The number of parameters in the variational form may be linear or quadratic in the number of qubits, depending on the chosen structure, and the depth of the circuit may be selected based on hardware constraints and problem complexity. The objective function values associated with each measured bitstring are computed by evaluating the diagonal elements of the Hamiltonian corresponding to the combinatorial problem, which are obtained by mapping the problem’s binary variables to Pauli-Z operators acting on individual qubits.

The CVaR is computed from the sorted list of observed objective function values by taking the arithmetic mean of the lowest α-fraction of samples, thereby emphasizing the performance of the best-performing outcomes while smoothing the optimization landscape to ensure differentiability and compatibility with gradient-based classical optimizers. The confidence level α may be fixed at a constant value across problem instances, such as 1%, 5%, 10%, or 25%, or may be adaptively tuned based on problem size, circuit depth, or empirical performance metrics. The method does not require any modification to the quantum hardware, the measurement protocol, or the classical optimizer, making it fully compatible with existing quantum computing platforms and software frameworks.

The invention further includes a system for implementing the method, comprising a quantum processor configured to execute the variational circuit and perform measurements, a classical computing system configured to compute the CVaR objective function and update the variational parameters, and a communication interface coupling the quantum and classical components. The system may be implemented on a cloud-based quantum computing service, a local quantum-classical hybrid workstation, or an integrated quantum-classical co-processor architecture. The method is applicable to any combinatorial optimization problem that can be expressed as a quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO) problem or an Ising spin model, including maximum cut, maximum stable set, number partitioning, market split, portfolio optimization, and maximum satisfiability.

The invention provides several key advantages over prior art. First, it significantly accelerates convergence to high-quality solutions by focusing optimization on the tail of the probability distribution rather than the mean, thereby reducing the number of classical optimization iterations required to achieve a desired solution quality. Second, it improves the robustness of the algorithm to noise and decoherence on NISQ hardware by effectively ignoring low-quality measurement outcomes that are likely corrupted by hardware imperfections. Third, it enhances the performance of algorithms with limited parameterization, such as QAOA, by mitigating the issue of flat state vectors that arise due to insufficient expressivity. Fourth, it enables the use of shallower circuits to achieve comparable or superior performance to deeper circuits under the expected-value objective, thereby reducing circuit depth and gate count, which in turn reduces error accumulation. Finally, the method requires no additional quantum resources, no change to the variational form, and no modification to the measurement protocol, making it a drop-in replacement for existing VQE and QAOA implementations with immediate practical impact.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a method for solving combinatorial optimization problems using a hybrid quantum-classical framework in which the Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) serves as the objective function for guiding the iterative optimization of a parameterized quantum circuit. The method is designed for implementation on noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices, where the fidelity of quantum gates and the coherence time of qubits impose strict constraints on the depth and complexity of quantum circuits that can be reliably executed. Unlike prior approaches that rely on the expected value of the problem Hamiltonian as the optimization criterion, the present invention recognizes that the goal of combinatorial optimization is not to approximate an average energy level, but to identify one or more bitstrings that correspond to high-quality solutions. This distinction motivates the adoption of CVaR, a risk-sensitive aggregation function that focuses optimization on the most promising region of the probability distribution of measurement outcomes, thereby aligning the algorithm’s objective more closely with the practical goal of solution discovery.

The method begins with the formulation of the combinatorial optimization problem as a Hamiltonian operator acting on a system of qubits. This mapping is achieved by encoding the binary decision variables of the problem as Pauli-Z operators, such that each computational basis state corresponds to a feasible solution, and the diagonal elements of the Hamiltonian represent the objective function values of those solutions. For example, in the case of a quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO) problem defined by an objective function of the form ∑ᵢ bᵢxᵢ + ∑ᵢⱼ Aᵢⱼxᵢxⱼ, where xᵢ ∈ {0,1}, the variables are transformed into spin variables zᵢ ∈ {-1,+1} via the mapping xᵢ = (1 - zᵢ)/2, yielding an Ising model of the form ∑ᵢ cᵢzᵢ + ∑ᵢⱼ Qᵢⱼzᵢzⱼ. The corresponding Hamiltonian is then constructed by replacing each zᵢ with σᵢᶻ, the Pauli-Z operator acting on the i-th qubit, and each product zᵢzⱼ with σᵢᶻ ⊗ σⱼᶻ, resulting in a diagonal Hamiltonian whose eigenvalues correspond directly to the objective function values of the classical bitstrings.

Once the Hamiltonian is defined, a parameterized quantum circuit—referred to as the variational form—is prepared to generate a trial quantum state |ψ(θ)⟩, where θ denotes the vector of real-valued parameters that control the rotations and entangling operations within the circuit. The variational form may take any of several known structures, including the layered VQE ansatz, which consists of an initial layer of single-qubit Y-rotations followed by p repetitions of a block comprising all-to-all or nearest-neighbor controlled-Z entanglement gates and another layer of Y-rotations, or the QAOA ansatz, which alternates between unitaries generated by the problem Hamiltonian and a transverse field Hamiltonian. The number of parameters in the variational form depends on its structure: for VQE with depth p and n qubits, the number of parameters is n(1+p), whereas for QAOA with depth p, the number of parameters is 2p. The choice of variational form influences the expressivity of the quantum state and the smoothness of the optimization landscape, but the method of the present invention is agnostic to the specific form used, as long as it is capable of generating a superposition of basis states with non-zero amplitude on the optimal or near-optimal solutions.

Following the preparation of the trial state, the quantum processor performs a series of measurements, each yielding a classical bitstring x ∈ {0,1}ⁿ and a corresponding objective function value H(x), computed by evaluating the diagonal element of the Hamiltonian corresponding to x. The number of measurements, denoted K, is chosen to be sufficiently large to ensure a reliable statistical estimate of the CVaR, although the method does not require K to scale with the problem size. The set of observed objective values {H(x₁), H(x₂), ..., H(xₖ)} is then sorted in ascending order, and the CVaR at confidence level α ∈ (0,1] is computed as the arithmetic mean of the lowest ⌊αK⌋ values in the sorted list. Formally, if the sorted values are denoted H₍₁₎ ≤ H₍₂₎ ≤ ... ≤ H₍ₖ₎, then the CVaR is given by:

CVaR_α = (1 / ⌊αK⌋) × ∑_{k=1}^{⌊αK⌋} H₍ₖ₎.

This definition ensures that the CVaR captures the average performance of the best α-fraction of samples, thereby emphasizing the quality of the most promising outcomes while discarding the influence of poor-performing ones. When α = 1, the CVaR reduces to the standard expected value, and when α approaches zero, it approaches the minimum observed value. However, for any α strictly between zero and one, the CVaR retains a smooth, continuous, and differentiable dependence on the quantum state, making it suitable for gradient-based optimization using classical algorithms such as COBYLA, L-BFGS-B, or Adam.

The classical optimizer then computes the gradient of the CVaR with respect to the variational parameters θ, using either analytical expressions derived from the structure of the quantum circuit or finite-difference approximations based on parameter-shift rules. The parameters are updated in the direction that minimizes the CVaR, and the process is repeated until convergence is achieved, as determined by a termination criterion such as a maximum number of iterations, a minimum change in the objective function, or a threshold on the CVaR value itself. The final set of parameters θ* is used to prepare the optimized quantum state, which is then measured once more to obtain the best observed bitstring, which is returned as the solution to the combinatorial optimization problem.

The method is implemented in a hybrid quantum-classical system comprising a quantum processor, a classical computing unit, and a communication interface. The quantum processor executes the variational circuit and performs measurements, returning the bitstrings and corresponding objective values to the classical unit. The classical unit computes the CVaR, updates the parameters, and sends the new parameter values back to the quantum processor for the next iteration. The system may be implemented on a cloud-based quantum computing platform, such as IBM Quantum Experience, or on a local hybrid workstation equipped with a quantum co-processor. The method requires no modification to the quantum hardware, the measurement protocol, or the classical optimizer, and can be integrated into existing quantum software libraries such as Qiskit, Cirq, or PennyLane with minimal code changes.

The method exhibits several key advantages over prior art. First, by focusing on the tail of the distribution, CVaR optimization accelerates convergence to high-quality solutions. In empirical evaluations across six combinatorial optimization problems—including maximum cut, portfolio optimization, number partitioning, and maximum 3-satisfiability—the CVaR-based variants of VQE and QAOA achieved a significantly higher probability of sampling the optimal solution within a fixed number of classical optimization iterations compared to the expected-value baseline. For example, in the case of portfolio optimization on six qubits, CVaR-VQE with α = 10% reached a 10% probability of sampling the optimal solution within 20 normalized iterations, whereas the expected-value variant (α = 100%) remained below 2% even after 100 iterations. This improvement is not merely a matter of statistical fluctuation but reflects a fundamental restructuring of the optimization landscape, where the CVaR objective provides a stronger gradient signal toward regions of the Hilbert space with high overlap with the optimal solution.

Second, the method demonstrates remarkable resilience to noise and decoherence on NISQ hardware. In experiments conducted on IBM’s Poughkeepsie quantum processor, CVaR-VQE maintained its convergence advantage even in the presence of gate errors, readout noise, and qubit decoherence. This robustness arises because the CVaR objective effectively filters out low-quality measurement outcomes that are likely corrupted by hardware imperfections, allowing the classical optimizer to be guided by the most reliable information available. In contrast, the expected-value objective is sensitive to all samples, including those with inflated objective values due to noise, which can mislead the optimizer and cause it to converge to suboptimal regions. The CVaR method, by design, ignores these corrupted samples, thereby preserving the integrity of the optimization signal.

Third, the method mitigates the issue of flat state vectors that plague QAOA with small depth. In QAOA, the limited number of parameters (2p) often results in quantum states with amplitudes that are nearly uniform across the computational basis, leading to a low probability of sampling any single optimal solution despite a good average objective value. The CVaR objective, by focusing on the tail of the distribution, provides a mechanism to overcome this limitation by rewarding any increase in amplitude on the best-performing states, even if the overall distribution remains broad. This effect is particularly pronounced when the problem Hamiltonian has many degenerate or near-degenerate eigenvalues, as is common in combinatorial optimization problems, where the CVaR can drive the quantum state to concentrate probability mass on the few states with the lowest objective values, even when the total number of such states is exponentially small.

Fourth, the method enables the use of shallower circuits to achieve performance comparable to deeper circuits under the expected-value objective. In experiments, CVaR-VQE with depth p = 1 and α = 10% outperformed VQE with depth p = 2 and α = 100% in terms of both convergence speed and solution quality. This reduction in circuit depth translates directly into lower gate counts, reduced error accumulation, and improved fidelity, making the method particularly well-suited for deployment on current and near-term quantum hardware.

Fifth, the method is fully compatible with existing quantum software ecosystems and requires no specialized hardware or novel quantum gates. The only modification required is the replacement of the sample mean with the CVaR as the objective function in the classical optimization loop. This simplicity ensures rapid adoption and integration into existing workflows, with no need for retraining, re-engineering, or re-architecting quantum algorithms.

The choice of the confidence level α is a critical design parameter that balances the trade-off between optimization speed and solution quality. Empirical results indicate that values of α in the range [0.01, 0.25] yield the most consistent improvements across problem instances. For α = 0.01, the algorithm focuses intensely on the top 1% of samples, leading to rapid convergence to high-quality solutions but potentially at the cost of increased sample variance. For α = 0.25, the algorithm balances exploration and exploitation, allowing for a broader sampling of promising states while still suppressing the influence of poor outcomes. Values of α close to 1, such as α = 0.9 or α = 1.0, recover the behavior of the expected-value baseline and offer no advantage. The optimal value of α may be selected based on problem characteristics, such as the number of qubits, the sparsity of the Hamiltonian, or the expected number of optimal solutions, or may be adaptively adjusted during the optimization process using a multi-level or annealing strategy.

The method also provides analytical insights into the behavior of variational quantum algorithms. It is shown that the optimization landscape induced by CVaR differs fundamentally from that of the expected value, in that local minima of the expected value are not necessarily local minima of the CVaR, and vice versa. This non-equivalence implies that CVaR optimization can escape regions of the parameter space that are stagnant under the expected-value objective, thereby avoiding the pitfalls of flat gradients and barren plateaus. Furthermore, the method reveals that the expressivity of the variational form plays a critical role in determining the success of the algorithm: for QAOA with small depth, the limited number of parameters leads to a concentration of probability mass that is too uniform to yield high overlap with the optimal solution, whereas VQE with sufficient parameters can generate more structured states that are amenable to CVaR optimization. This insight suggests that future algorithmic improvements should focus not on increasing circuit depth per se, but on designing variational forms that generate non-uniform, structured distributions with high tail probability.

The method is applicable to any combinatorial optimization problem that can be mapped to a diagonal Hamiltonian, including but not limited to maximum cut, maximum stable set, number partitioning, market split, portfolio optimization, and maximum satisfiability. The mapping from the problem to the Hamiltonian is well-established in the literature and can be performed using standard techniques such as penalty methods, binary encoding, or Ising transformation. The method does not require the problem to be constrained or to have a specific structure beyond the ability to compute the objective function value of any bitstring classically.

In summary, the present invention provides a novel, practical, and highly effective method for improving the performance of hybrid quantum-classical algorithms on NISQ devices by replacing the expected value with the Conditional Value-at-Risk as the optimization objective. The method is simple to implement, requires no hardware modifications, and delivers significant improvements in convergence speed, solution quality, and noise resilience across a broad class of combinatorial optimization problems. It represents a paradigm shift in quantum optimization, moving from expectation-based optimization to tail-focused optimization, and enables the practical realization of quantum advantage on current and near-future quantum hardware.
