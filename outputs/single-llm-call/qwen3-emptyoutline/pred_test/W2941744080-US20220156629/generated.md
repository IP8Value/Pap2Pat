# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to quantum computing architectures, specifically to a scalable, fault-tolerant surface code implementation employing silicon-based spin qubits that inherently mitigates charge leakage errors through the integration of mediator dots and charge reservoirs. The invention is particularly directed to a two-dimensional array of electrostatically defined quantum dots in silicon, wherein data qubits are implemented as single-electron spin states, ancilla qubits are implemented as spin-paired double quantum dots initialized in a singlet state, and elongated mediator dots serve as non-computational intermediaries for two-qubit gate operations. The architecture leverages the natural charge relaxation dynamics between qubit dots and mediator dots, coupled with reservoir-based reset mechanisms, to convert otherwise uncorrectable charge leakage errors into correctable computational errors within the surface code’s stabilizer framework. This approach is designed for compatibility with conventional complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) fabrication processes and enables high-density qubit packing while simultaneously addressing the critical challenge of leakage error propagation that plagues other quantum dot architectures. The invention further encompasses methods for performing stabilizer measurements, single-qubit rotations, and ancilla readout using global or local electromagnetic fields, with optimized gate sequences that minimize circuit depth and eliminate the need for explicit single-qubit gates on ancilla qubits through symmetry-based cancellation. The resulting system achieves a fault-tolerance threshold for both gate errors and charge leakage errors that is sufficiently high to enable practical, scalable quantum computation using silicon spin qubits without requiring additional hardware for active leakage detection or correction.

## BACKGROUND

Quantum computing architectures based on semiconductor spin qubits have emerged as a leading candidate for scalable, fault-tolerant quantum information processing due to their compatibility with existing semiconductor manufacturing infrastructure, long coherence times in isotopically purified silicon, and potential for dense two-dimensional integration. Among the most promising error correction schemes for such platforms is the surface code, which offers a high error threshold, local stabilizer measurements, and a planar qubit layout that aligns well with lithographic fabrication techniques. However, the practical realization of surface code implementations using silicon quantum dots has been hindered by a class of errors known as leakage errors—specifically, those arising from the unintended escape of electrons from the computational subspace defined by the ground charge configuration of the quantum dots. Unlike bit-flip or phase-flip errors, which are confined within the computational Hilbert space and can be corrected by standard quantum error correction protocols, leakage errors result in the system transitioning into non-computational states, such as empty quantum dots or excited charge configurations, and are therefore invisible to conventional stabilizer measurements. These errors accumulate over time and, if left unaddressed, can corrupt the logical qubit encoding even when their per-gate probability is low.

Prior approaches to scaling silicon spin qubit arrays have focused on minimizing qubit spacing to maximize density, often employing direct exchange interactions between neighboring quantum dots. While these architectures achieve high gate fidelities, they are highly susceptible to charge leakage due to the proximity of control gates, the small energy gaps between ground and excited charge states, and the absence of dedicated structures to absorb or reset leaked charge. Alternative proposals have introduced modular architectures with electron shuttling between isolated quantum dot clusters to provide space for individual control lines and reduce cross-talk. However, such schemes introduce new sources of leakage during the shuttling process itself, as electrons must traverse multiple tunnel barriers under adiabatic conditions, and any deviation from ideal control voltages can result in permanent loss of charge or decoherence. Moreover, these architectures require complex routing of control signals, additional ancilla qubits for leakage detection, or active reset circuits that increase the physical footprint and operational complexity of the system.

Other quantum computing platforms, such as superconducting circuits or trapped ions, have developed methods to mitigate leakage through ancilla-based detection and post-selection, or through engineered leakage reduction units that actively drive leaked states back into the computational subspace. However, these techniques are not readily transferable to silicon quantum dot systems due to the fundamental differences in physical implementation. In particular, the use of additional ancilla qubits for leakage detection introduces prohibitive qubit overhead in dense arrays, and the integration of charge sensors or reset circuits directly into the qubit plane is constrained by the limited space available between quantum dots. Furthermore, leakage reduction protocols often require additional gate operations that increase circuit depth and runtime, thereby exacerbating decoherence and reducing the effective error correction rate. The absence of a mechanism to passively and efficiently restore leaked charge without introducing new sources of noise or requiring additional hardware has thus remained a critical bottleneck in the development of scalable, fault-tolerant silicon spin qubit processors.

The present invention addresses these longstanding challenges by introducing a novel architectural design in which elongated mediator dots, originally introduced to extend the range of exchange interactions and facilitate readout, are repurposed as natural charge sinks for leaked electrons. By connecting these mediator dots to integrated charge reservoirs, the architecture enables a passive, in situ restoration of charge deficits in qubit dots via fast relaxation processes, effectively converting charge leakage errors into depolarizing errors that fall within the computational subspace. Crucially, this transformation occurs without requiring additional qubits, sensors, or active correction cycles, and the resulting errors are temporally and spatially decorrelated through a carefully designed partitioning of stabilizer measurement cycles. This innovation not only eliminates the need for complex leakage mitigation hardware but also enhances the overall fault-tolerance threshold of the surface code by ensuring that leakage errors do not propagate or correlate across the array. The invention further optimizes the stabilizer measurement circuit by exploiting the symmetry of the double-dot ancilla structure to eliminate unnecessary single-qubit gates, reducing circuit depth and operational time. The combination of these features—mediator-mediated charge restoration, reservoir-based reset, symmetric ancilla design, and partitioned stabilizer scheduling—constitutes a fundamentally new approach to leakage-resilient quantum computing that is uniquely suited to the physical constraints and material properties of silicon spin qubit platforms.

## SUMMARY

The invention discloses a quantum computing architecture for fault-tolerant universal quantum computation based on silicon spin qubits, wherein charge leakage errors are rendered correctable by the surface code through an integrated system of mediator dots and charge reservoirs. The architecture comprises a two-dimensional array of electrostatically defined quantum dots arranged in a surface code lattice, with data qubits implemented as single-electron spin states confined within individual quantum dots, and ancilla qubits implemented as spin-paired double quantum dots initialized in a singlet state. Elongated mediator dots, positioned between each data qubit and one half of an ancilla double-dot, serve as non-computational intermediaries for two-qubit gate operations via mediated exchange interactions, and are electrically coupled to integrated charge reservoirs that supply electrons for qubit initialization and reset. When an electron leaks from a data or ancilla qubit dot, the resulting charge deficit is rapidly restored by relaxation of an electron from an adjacent mediator dot, a process occurring on a timescale significantly shorter than the duration of quantum gate operations. This relaxation transfers the charge imbalance to the mediator dot, which is subsequently reset to its ground charge configuration by controlled tunneling to the charge reservoir during idle periods of the stabilizer cycle. Because the mediator dots carry no quantum information, their reset does not introduce computational errors, and the resulting residual error in the qubit system is equivalent to a depolarizing error confined within the computational subspace. The surface code stabilizer checks are performed using a partitioned sequence of operations, wherein the array is divided into four disjoint subsets of plaquettes, each activated sequentially, such that leakage errors occurring during one partition do not propagate to subsequent ones. During the activation of one partition, the mediator dots associated with inactive partitions are simultaneously reset, ensuring that any charge imbalance introduced during active stabilizer cycles is fully resolved before the next cycle begins. This temporal partitioning eliminates both spatial and temporal correlations in leakage-induced errors, rendering them Markovian and compatible with standard surface code decoding algorithms. The two-qubit gates required for stabilizer measurements are implemented using either a dipole-dipole interaction in the Ω ≫ J regime or a √SWAP gate in the Ω ≪ J regime, both mediated by the elongated mediator dots, with the choice of regime determined by the presence or absence of localized magnetic field gradients generated by an array of micromagnets. Single-qubit gates are applied globally via electron spin resonance or locally via electrically driven spin resonance, with the latter enabled by the spatial separation afforded by the mediator dots, which allows micromagnets to be placed without interfering with ancilla qubits. The ancilla qubits, being initialized in a spin-singlet state and subject to symmetric exchange interactions, enable the omission of all single-qubit Z rotations except one, which is sandwiched between two √SWAP gates, thereby reducing circuit depth and operational time. Virtual Z rotations are employed for all other single-qubit corrections, eliminating the need for physical gate pulses and further reducing error accumulation. The readout of ancilla qubits is performed via Pauli spin blockade, detected through dispersive charge sensing or gate-based capacitive readout, with the singlet-triplet distinction providing a direct measurement of the stabilizer parity without requiring ancilla qubit state tomography. The entire architecture is designed for compatibility with CMOS fabrication processes, with a gate pitch of 30–40 nm and mediator dot dimensions of 30 nm × 300 nm, enabling high-density qubit packing while preserving sufficient space for control lines, reservoirs, and readout electronics. Simulations demonstrate that the architecture achieves a fault-tolerance threshold for gate errors of approximately 0.76% to 0.86%, depending on the two-qubit gate implementation, and a charge leakage error threshold of up to 0.66% in the absence of gate errors, with thresholds of 0.23% to 0.27% under realistic gate error rates of 0.5%. These thresholds are comparable to or exceed those of conventional surface code implementations, while simultaneously eliminating the need for additional hardware for leakage detection or correction. The invention thus provides a self-contained, scalable, and manufacturable quantum computing platform that inherently suppresses the most damaging form of error in silicon spin qubit systems—charge leakage—through passive, integrated physical mechanisms rather than active, resource-intensive protocols.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The quantum computing architecture disclosed herein is a fully integrated, scalable, and fault-tolerant surface code implementation based on silicon spin qubits, engineered to overcome the persistent challenge of charge leakage errors that have historically limited the viability of quantum dot platforms for large-scale quantum computation. The architecture is composed of a two-dimensional lattice of electrostatically defined quantum dots fabricated on a silicon substrate, with each unit cell of the surface code comprising a data qubit, an ancilla qubit, and two mediator dots arranged in a specific geometric configuration that enables both high-fidelity quantum operations and passive leakage error mitigation. The data qubits are implemented as single-electron spin states confined within individual quantum dots, each of which is defined by a set of metallic gate electrodes patterned on the surface of a silicon-on-insulator wafer. These quantum dots are sized to be approximately 30 nanometers in diameter, with a Coulomb blockade energy on the order of 2 terahertz, which ensures that the addition of a second electron to any data qubit dot is energetically forbidden under normal operating conditions. The spin degeneracy of the electron is lifted by a uniform external magnetic field of approximately 30 millitesla, enabling spin manipulation via electron spin resonance or electrically driven spin resonance. The coherence time of these spin qubits is extended through the use of isotopically enriched silicon-28 substrates, which suppress magnetic noise from nuclear spins, and through dynamical decoupling techniques that mitigate decoherence from charge noise and lattice vibrations. The quantum information encoded in these data qubits is protected by the surface code, which requires the periodic measurement of stabilizer operators corresponding to the parity of spin states across neighboring qubits. These stabilizer measurements are performed using ancilla qubits, each of which is implemented as a double quantum dot containing two electrons in a spin-singlet state. Unlike prior architectures that treat one of the two dots in the ancilla as a passive reference, the present invention treats both dots as active participants in the stabilizer measurement, allowing for parallel interaction with two data qubits simultaneously and thereby halving the time required to complete a stabilizer cycle. The singlet state of the ancilla is prepared by initializing the double dot in a (0,2) charge configuration, where the ground state is a singlet due to strong Pauli spin blockade, and then relaxing the system through a charge transition to the (1,1) configuration, where the singlet state is stabilized by hyperfine and spin-orbit coupling effects. The readout of the ancilla state is performed via Pauli spin blockade, which manifests as a suppression of electron tunneling between the two dots when the spin state is triplet, and is detected using a nearby charge sensor such as a quantum point contact or a radio-frequency single-electron transistor, enabling single-shot readout with fidelity approaching 98%. The key innovation of the architecture lies in the introduction of elongated mediator dots, which are positioned between each data qubit and one half of the ancilla double-dot, and serve as non-computational intermediaries for the two-qubit exchange interactions required to implement the stabilizer gates. These mediator dots are fabricated as elongated quantum dots with dimensions of approximately 30 nanometers in width and 300 nanometers in length, and are designed to contain two electrons in their ground state, though configurations with four or more electrons may be employed to mitigate valley-orbit splitting effects. The mediator dots do not store quantum information; rather, they mediate the exchange interaction between the spins of the data and ancilla qubits via Ruderman-Kittel-Kasuya-Yosida coupling, with the strength of the interaction controlled by tuning the on-site energy of the mediator dot relative to the side dots. When the mediator dot is tuned to a low detuning state (∆on ≈ 10 gigahertz), the exchange interaction is activated, enabling the implementation of √SWAP or S gates depending on the magnetic field configuration, while when tuned to a high detuning state (∆off ≈ 1 terahertz), the interaction is suppressed to a residual level of approximately 100 hertz, which is negligible compared to the surface code threshold. The use of mediated exchange, as opposed to direct exchange, provides two critical advantages: first, the residual interaction in the off state is suppressed by a factor of (∆on/∆off)^2 compared to direct exchange, making it orders of magnitude smaller and thus less likely to induce unintended gate errors; second, the next-nearest-neighbor interactions are suppressed by a factor of (∆on/∆off)^4, effectively eliminating crosstalk between distant qubits and simplifying the error model. The two-qubit gates required for the stabilizer circuit are implemented in one of two regimes: in the Ω ≫ J regime, where the Zeeman splitting between the data and ancilla qubits is much larger than the exchange coupling, a dipole-dipole interaction is induced that directly implements the S gate; in the Ω ≪ J regime, where the Zeeman splitting is negligible, the exchange interaction implements the √SWAP gate, which is then combined with a single Zπ gate to form a controlled-Z gate. The choice of regime is determined by the application of localized magnetic field gradients, which are generated by an array of micromagnets deposited on the surface of the chip above the data qubits but not above the ancilla qubits. This spatial separation is made possible by the increased inter-qubit spacing afforded by the mediator dots, which allows the micromagnets to be placed without interfering with the delicate spin states of the ancilla double-dots. The single-qubit gates required for the stabilizer cycle are implemented using global electron spin resonance for uniform rotation of all data qubits, or using electrically driven spin resonance for localized control, with the latter enabled by the magnetic field gradients. Crucially, the ancilla qubits do not require any single-qubit gates, as their state is always initialized in the singlet and measured in the singlet-triplet basis, and all necessary phase corrections are implemented virtually by shifting the reference frame of subsequent pulse sequences, thereby eliminating the need for physical gate pulses on the ancilla and reducing the total circuit depth. The stabilizer measurement cycle is divided into four disjoint partitions, each corresponding to a distinct color in the surface code lattice, such that no two plaquettes of the same color share a common data qubit. During each partition, stabilizer checks are performed simultaneously on all plaquettes of that color, while the mediator dots associated with the other three partitions are simultaneously reset to their ground charge configuration via controlled tunneling to the integrated charge reservoirs. These reservoirs are located at the periphery of the array and are connected to each mediator dot through tunable tunnel barriers, allowing for rapid reset during idle periods without introducing noise into the active stabilizer cycles. The reset time is optimized to be less than half the duration of a stabilizer cycle, ensuring that any charge imbalance introduced during the active phase is fully resolved before the next cycle begins. This partitioning strategy ensures that leakage errors occurring during one partition do not propagate to subsequent ones, and that the residual errors after charge restoration are temporally and spatially uncorrelated, thereby satisfying the Markovian error assumption required for surface code decoding. When an electron leaks from a data or ancilla qubit dot—due to decoherence during exchange interactions, fluctuations in gate voltages, or thermal excitation—the resulting charge deficit is rapidly filled by an electron from an adjacent mediator dot, a process governed by the charge relaxation time, which has been experimentally measured to be on the order of 10 nanoseconds in silicon quantum dot systems. This relaxation time is significantly shorter than the duration of any quantum gate operation, which occurs on the microsecond timescale, ensuring that the charge configuration is restored before the next gate sequence begins. The electron that fills the vacancy in the qubit dot originates from the mediator, leaving behind a charge excess in the mediator dot. However, because the mediator dot carries no quantum information and is not part of the computational subspace, this excess charge does not corrupt the quantum state of the system. Instead, during the next idle period, the mediator dot is reset by allowing the excess electron to tunnel into the charge reservoir, which acts as an infinite sink for charge. The tunnel coupling between the mediator and the reservoir is tuned to be weak enough to prevent noise injection during active operations but strong enough to enable full reset within the allotted idle time. This entire process—leakage, restoration, and reset—occurs without any additional hardware, control lines, or ancilla qubits, and without introducing any new sources of decoherence or crosstalk. The resulting error in the system is equivalent to a depolarizing error on the affected qubit and its neighboring qubits, which is fully correctable by the surface code. Simulations of the stabilizer circuit, performed using classical Monte Carlo methods and minimum-weight perfect matching decoders, demonstrate that the architecture achieves a gate error threshold of 0.86% when using S gates and 0.76% when using √SWAP gates, both of which are comparable to the theoretical threshold of 0.75% for the surface code under depolarizing noise. The charge leakage error threshold, defined as the maximum probability of leakage per two-qubit gate before logical error rates begin to increase, is found to be 0.66% in the absence of gate errors, and remains above 0.23% even under realistic gate error rates of 0.5%. These thresholds are significantly higher than those of prior architectures that rely on active leakage correction or shuttling, and are sufficient to enable fault-tolerant quantum computation with logical qubit error rates below 10^-15 using code distances of 15 or greater. The architecture is fully compatible with CMOS fabrication processes, with a gate pitch of 30–40 nanometers, enabling the integration of millions of qubits on a single chip. The elongated mediator dots provide the necessary spacing for the placement of control electrodes, charge reservoirs, and readout sensors without compromising qubit density, and the use of global ESR and localized EDSR for single-qubit gates allows for parallel operation across the entire array. The absence of micromagnets over the ancilla qubits further simplifies fabrication and reduces parasitic magnetic noise. The entire system operates at cryogenic temperatures below 100 millikelvin, with microwave and voltage pulses delivered through planar metallic interconnects fabricated using standard lithographic techniques. The stabilizer cycle time, determined by the exchange interaction duration and the duration of Hadamard gates, is estimated to be between 8 and 10 microseconds, which is sufficiently fast to suppress decoherence from spin relaxation and dephasing, as the measured T2 times in isotopically enriched silicon exceed 20 milliseconds. The architecture thus represents a complete, self-contained solution to the problem of charge leakage in silicon spin qubit systems, combining physical design, operational protocol, and error correction into a unified framework that requires no additional hardware beyond what is already necessary for qubit control and readout. The invention thereby enables the realization of large-scale, fault-tolerant quantum computers using silicon spin qubits, a platform that is uniquely positioned to leverage decades of investment in semiconductor manufacturing and to scale to the millions of qubits required for practical quantum advantage.
