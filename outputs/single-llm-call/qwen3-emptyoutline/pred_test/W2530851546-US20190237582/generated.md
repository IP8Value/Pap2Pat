# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to high-voltage thin-film transistors designed for operation in high-bias environments, particularly those requiring stable performance under elevated temperatures and sustained drain-source voltages exceeding 200 volts. More specifically, the invention concerns a novel class of metal oxide semiconductor devices fabricated on transparent glass substrates, incorporating a modulation-doped transition layer at the interface between the channel and the gate dielectric to suppress interdiffusion, reduce interface trap density, and enhance electrical field uniformity. These devices are particularly suited for integration into photovoltaic solar inverter systems, building-integrated photovoltaics, and self-powered smart glass technologies where reliability, thermal stability, and high voltage blocking capability are critical operational requirements. The invention further encompasses the structural design of the transistor channel in a ring configuration to mitigate electric field crowding at the corners of conventional rectangular geometries, thereby enabling significantly higher breakdown voltages without compromising on-current performance. The materials system employed—comprising magnesium-doped zinc oxide with a compositionally graded transition layer—provides a robust, transparent, and low-cost platform compatible with large-area, low-temperature fabrication processes, making the invention uniquely advantageous for next-generation energy harvesting and power management applications in transparent electronics.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

High-voltage thin-film transistors have long been sought after for applications in power electronics, particularly in the context of photovoltaic energy conversion systems where direct current-to-alternating current inversion must occur at high voltages while maintaining efficiency, compactness, and compatibility with flexible or transparent substrates. Conventional silicon-based high-voltage transistors, while well-established, are incompatible with large-area, low-temperature processing on glass substrates, limiting their applicability in building-integrated photovoltaics and smart window technologies. Metal oxide semiconductors, such as zinc oxide, have emerged as promising alternatives due to their high electron mobility, optical transparency, and compatibility with solution-based or low-temperature deposition techniques. However, zinc oxide-based transistors suffer from inherent instability under thermal stress and high electric fields, primarily due to the presence of oxygen vacancies and other intrinsic defects that act as charge traps. These traps lead to threshold voltage shifts, increased leakage currents, and premature device degradation under prolonged biasing conditions, especially at elevated temperatures. Furthermore, when fabricated in conventional rectangular channel geometries, the electric field distribution becomes highly non-uniform, with field intensities peaking at the drain and source corners, resulting in localized breakdown and catastrophic failure at relatively low drain-source voltages. Attempts to mitigate these issues through simple doping of the channel material with magnesium have shown marginal improvements in thermal stability, as the stronger Mg–O bond reduces oxygen vacancy formation compared to pure ZnO. However, even with such doping, interdiffusion between the channel layer and the underlying silicon dioxide gate dielectric remains a persistent problem. This interdiffusion introduces unwanted impurities—particularly zinc ions migrating into the dielectric and silicon ions penetrating into the channel—which generate fixed oxide charges and additional interface states that degrade subthreshold swing, increase off-current, and shift the threshold voltage negatively. These effects are exacerbated under high electric fields, where the presence of trapped charges distorts the potential profile across the channel, leading to localized field enhancements that initiate premature breakdown. Existing solutions to improve high-voltage performance have focused on increasing channel length to reduce field intensity, but this comes at the cost of reduced drive current and increased device footprint, which is undesirable in compact, high-density power circuits. Alternative approaches involving thick gate dielectrics or complex multilayer structures have proven incompatible with low-temperature processing or have introduced new reliability concerns due to mechanical stress and delamination. Moreover, no prior art has demonstrated a synergistic combination of a ring-shaped channel geometry, a magnesium-doped zinc oxide channel with controlled composition, and a modulation-doped transition layer that simultaneously suppresses interdiffusion, minimizes interface traps, and homogenizes the electric field distribution to achieve stable operation beyond 200 volts. The absence of such a device architecture in the prior art represents a significant technological gap, particularly for applications requiring transparent, low-cost, and thermally stable high-voltage transistors that can operate reliably in real-world environmental conditions. The present invention addresses these shortcomings by introducing a novel device structure and material interface engineering strategy that overcomes the fundamental limitations of existing metal oxide thin-film transistors, enabling unprecedented levels of high-voltage blocking capability, thermal stability, and electrical uniformity in a single, manufacturable platform.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a high-voltage thin-film transistor with enhanced thermal stability and high drain-source voltage blocking capability, comprising a ring-shaped channel geometry, a magnesium-doped zinc oxide channel layer, and a modulation-doped transition layer disposed between the channel and the gate dielectric. The ring-shaped channel configuration is designed to eliminate electric field concentration at the corners of a conventional rectangular channel, thereby distributing the electric field more uniformly from the drain to the source terminal and reducing the maximum electric field intensity by approximately fifty percent compared to rectangular geometries of equivalent dimensions. The channel layer is composed of magnesium-doped zinc oxide with a magnesium concentration of approximately three atomic percent, which reduces the density of oxygen vacancies and other intrinsic defects in the bulk of the channel, thereby improving thermal stability and reducing bulk trap density. A critical innovation of the invention is the inclusion of a modulation-doped transition layer, consisting of a ten-nanometer-thick compositionally graded magnesium-zinc oxide film, deposited between the channel layer and the silicon dioxide gate dielectric. The magnesium concentration in this transition layer is graded from one hundred percent magnesium oxide at the dielectric interface to three percent magnesium oxide at the interface with the channel layer, creating a smooth compositional gradient that acts as a diffusion barrier to prevent the migration of zinc ions from the channel into the dielectric and silicon ions from the dielectric into the channel. This barrier function significantly reduces the density of interface traps and fixed oxide charges that would otherwise accumulate near the channel-dielectric interface, leading to a steeper subthreshold slope, higher on-current, and improved threshold voltage stability under thermal stress. The resulting device exhibits a threshold voltage shift of less than seven volts when operated from room temperature to 94 degrees Celsius, a substantial improvement over conventional magnesium-doped zinc oxide transistors, which exhibit shifts exceeding ten volts under the same conditions. Furthermore, the device demonstrates a maximum drain-source blocking voltage exceeding six hundred volts, with a leakage current below one picoampere at this voltage, and maintains an on/off current ratio greater than one hundred million under high-bias conditions. The device is fabricated on a transparent glass substrate using low-temperature processes compatible with large-area manufacturing, and includes gate-to-drain and gate-to-source offsets to further reduce field crowding at the electrodes. The combination of the ring-shaped channel, the compositionally graded transition layer, and the magnesium-doped zinc oxide channel results in a synergistic enhancement of electrical performance, thermal stability, and high-voltage reliability that is not achievable by any single component alone. The invention enables the integration of high-voltage transistors into photovoltaic inverters for building-integrated photovoltaics and self-powered smart glass systems, where transparency, durability, and stable operation under high electric fields are essential. The device structure and materials system disclosed herein represent a fundamental advancement in thin-film transistor technology, providing a scalable, cost-effective, and high-performance solution to the longstanding challenges of thermal instability and electric field non-uniformity in metal oxide semiconductors.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Material Preparation and Device Fabrication Process

The high-voltage thin-film transistor of the present invention is fabricated on a commercially available transparent glass substrate with a thickness of approximately 0.4 millimeters, chosen for its optical transparency, mechanical rigidity, and compatibility with standard microfabrication processes. The fabrication sequence begins with the deposition of a chromium layer, approximately fifty nanometers in thickness, onto the glass substrate using a sputtering technique. This chromium layer serves as the bottom gate electrode and is subsequently patterned using a dry etching process with a chlorine-based plasma to define the desired gate geometry. Following gate patterning, a gate dielectric layer of silicon dioxide is deposited over the entire substrate using plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition at a temperature of approximately three hundred degrees Celsius. The resulting silicon dioxide layer has a thickness of two hundred nanometers and exhibits a dielectric constant of approximately 3.9, providing sufficient capacitance for efficient gate control while maintaining low leakage current under high electric fields. The surface of the silicon dioxide layer is then prepared for the deposition of the channel material by ensuring a clean, uniform, and hydroxyl-free interface, which is critical for minimizing interfacial trap formation. The channel layer is deposited using metal organic chemical vapor deposition, a technique that enables precise control over stoichiometry and layer thickness at relatively low temperatures. Three distinct channel configurations are fabricated for comparative evaluation: a pure zinc oxide channel, a magnesium-doped zinc oxide channel with a fixed magnesium concentration of three atomic percent, and a modulation-doped magnesium-zinc oxide channel incorporating a compositionally graded transition layer. For the pure zinc oxide channel, diethyl zinc is used as the sole precursor, and the layer is deposited to a thickness of fifty nanometers. For the magnesium-doped zinc oxide channel, both diethyl zinc and bis(methylcyclopentadienyl)magnesium are introduced simultaneously into the reaction chamber, with the flow rates calibrated to achieve a consistent magnesium concentration of three atomic percent throughout the fifty-nanometer-thick layer. In the modulation-doped configuration, a ten-nanometer-thick transition layer is first deposited on the silicon dioxide gate dielectric, followed by the deposition of the main magnesium-doped zinc oxide channel layer. The transition layer is formed by gradually reducing the flow rate of the magnesium precursor while maintaining a constant flow rate of the zinc precursor, resulting in a continuous compositional gradient in which the magnesium concentration decreases from one hundred percent at the interface with the silicon dioxide to three percent at the interface with the main channel layer. This gradient is achieved through precise control of precursor partial pressures and substrate temperature, which is maintained at four hundred degrees Celsius throughout the deposition process to ensure crystalline quality and low defect density. The source and drain electrodes are then formed by depositing a bilayer of titanium and gold, with a thickness of one hundred nanometers and fifty nanometers, respectively, using electron beam evaporation. The metal layers are patterned using a lift-off process, in which a photoresist mask is applied, exposed, and developed to define the electrode geometry, followed by metal deposition and subsequent removal of the photoresist along with the overlying metal, leaving behind only the desired source and drain contacts. The channel length is varied across multiple devices to evaluate the trade-off between blocking voltage and drive current, with nominal, longer, and longest channel lengths of ten micrometers, fifteen micrometers, and twenty-five micrometers, respectively. Each device is designed with a gate-to-drain offset of five micrometers, ten micrometers, and twenty micrometers, respectively, and a fixed gate-to-source offset of three micrometers, to further reduce electric field concentration at the drain edge and improve high-voltage stability. A thin layer of photoresist is applied over the entire channel region after metallization to serve as a passivation layer, preventing the adsorption or desorption of ambient water molecules and oxygen during electrical testing, which could otherwise introduce additional interface states or alter threshold voltage characteristics. The entire fabrication process is conducted under cleanroom conditions with strict control over humidity, particulate contamination, and thermal cycling to ensure reproducibility and device uniformity across the substrate. The resulting devices are fully transparent in the visible spectrum and exhibit excellent mechanical adhesion between all layers, with no evidence of delamination or interfacial cracking after prolonged thermal cycling or high-voltage stress testing.

### Device Testing Conditions

The electrical performance of the fabricated high-voltage thin-film transistors is evaluated under a range of bias conditions and environmental temperatures to assess their suitability for high-power applications. Low-bias transfer and output characteristics are measured using a commercial semiconductor parameter analyzer equipped with a pulse generator, which enables precise control of gate and drain voltages while minimizing self-heating effects during measurement. The system is capable of sourcing gate voltages up to forty volts and drain voltages up to twenty volts, with a current resolution of one femtoampere, allowing for accurate characterization of subthreshold behavior and off-state leakage. All low-bias measurements are conducted in a light-tight probe station to eliminate photoconductive effects that could interfere with intrinsic transistor behavior. To evaluate device performance under high drain-source voltages, a custom-built high-voltage testing system is employed, consisting of a high-voltage pulse generator and a precision probe station, which allows for the application of drain-source voltages up to six hundred volts. Due to the limited current resolution of the high-voltage system, which is approximately one microampere, it is used exclusively for breakdown voltage measurements and high-bias stability tests, while low-current characteristics are always confirmed using the high-resolution parameter analyzer. To prevent arcing, surface tracking, and dielectric breakdown caused by environmental humidity or contamination, all high-voltage tests are conducted with the devices fully immersed in Fluorinert FC-40, an electrically insulating, non-reactive fluorinated fluid that provides excellent thermal conductivity and dielectric strength. The temperature dependence of device characteristics is assessed using a programmable thermal stage connected to a high-precision measurement system, which allows for controlled heating of the device from room temperature (294 kelvin) to ninety-four degrees Celsius (367 kelvin) in increments of five degrees, with stabilization time of at least thirty minutes at each temperature point to ensure thermal equilibrium. At each temperature, transfer characteristics are recorded under identical gate and drain bias conditions to determine the shift in threshold voltage, subthreshold swing, and on-current. The activation energy of the drain current is extracted from Arrhenius plots of the logarithm of drain current versus the inverse of absolute temperature, enabling quantification of the energy barrier for carrier transport and the density of thermally activated trap states. All measurements are performed under vacuum-sealed conditions to exclude atmospheric interference, and the devices are allowed to rest for a minimum of two hours between consecutive bias sweeps to eliminate any residual charge trapping effects. The on/off current ratio is determined by comparing the maximum drain current at the highest gate bias to the minimum drain current at the lowest gate bias, with the off-state current measured at a gate voltage of negative ten volts. The stability of the off-state current under prolonged high drain bias is monitored over extended periods of up to one hour to detect any time-dependent degradation or leakage current increase. The gate leakage current is measured separately by applying a high gate voltage while holding the drain at zero bias, and is found to remain below one picoampere even at gate voltages exceeding fifty volts, indicating that the silicon dioxide dielectric remains intact and free of pinholes or conductive pathways. The measurement protocol is designed to be non-destructive and repeatable, ensuring that the same device can be characterized under multiple conditions without altering its intrinsic properties. The combination of high-resolution low-bias measurements and high-voltage stress testing under controlled environmental conditions provides a comprehensive assessment of the device’s operational limits and reliability, confirming its suitability for integration into high-power photovoltaic inverter systems.

### Material Characterizations

To understand the physical and chemical origins of the enhanced electrical performance, a suite of advanced material characterization techniques is employed to analyze the structure, composition, and interfacial properties of the fabricated devices. Cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy is performed on devices prepared using focused ion beam milling to obtain high-resolution images of the channel-dielectric interface. These images reveal a sharp, well-defined boundary between the silicon dioxide gate dielectric and the modulation-doped transition layer in the m-MZO configuration, with no evidence of grain boundary penetration, void formation, or interdiffusion. In contrast, devices fabricated with the unmodulated MZO channel layer exhibit a diffuse interface, with distinct gray features observed within the silicon dioxide layer adjacent to the channel. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy is conducted at multiple points spanning the interface, from the center of the channel to the interior of the dielectric, to quantify elemental distribution. In the MZO device, a strong zinc signal is detected not only within the channel but also extending into the silicon dioxide layer, confirming the migration of zinc ions across the interface. Concurrently, a silicon signal is observed within the channel region, indicating reverse diffusion of silicon from the dielectric into the semiconductor. In the m-MZO device, however, the zinc signal is confined almost entirely within the channel layer, with only a negligible signal detected at the interface and no detectable zinc within the silicon dioxide. Similarly, the silicon signal is absent from the channel region, confirming that the modulation-doped transition layer effectively blocks bidirectional interdiffusion. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy is used to obtain depth profiles of the atomic composition across the interface, with in-situ argon ion sputtering employed to progressively remove material in nanometer-scale increments. The resulting depth profiles show a broad, overlapping distribution of zinc and silicon in the MZO device, extending over a distance of more than ten nanometers, indicative of extensive interdiffusion. In the m-MZO device, the zinc and silicon profiles are sharply separated, with minimal overlap, demonstrating that the transition layer acts as a diffusion barrier with a width of less than two nanometers. The magnesium profile in the m-MZO device exhibits a distinct, narrow peak centered at the interface, corresponding to the compositionally graded transition layer, with the magnesium concentration decreasing monotonically from nearly one hundred percent at the dielectric interface to approximately three percent at the channel interface. The absence of a discernible magnesium peak in the MZO device confirms that the magnesium is uniformly distributed within the channel and does not segregate at the interface. The stoichiometry of the channel layers is further confirmed through high-resolution X-ray diffraction, which reveals a single-phase wurtzite structure for both MZO and m-MZO, with no secondary phases or impurity peaks detected. The lattice parameters of the films are consistent with theoretical predictions for magnesium-doped zinc oxide, indicating that magnesium is substitutionally incorporated into the zinc oxide lattice without inducing strain-induced defects. The surface roughness of the channel layers, measured using atomic force microscopy, is found to be less than one nanometer root-mean-square across all samples, confirming that the deposition process yields atomically smooth interfaces critical for minimizing scattering and trap formation. The combined results from TEM, EDS, and XPS provide unambiguous evidence that the modulation-doped transition layer is the key structural feature responsible for suppressing interdiffusion, and that the resulting interface is chemically abrupt and electronically pristine. These findings directly correlate with the observed improvements in electrical performance, confirming that the reduction in interface trap density and fixed oxide charges is the primary mechanism underlying the enhanced thermal stability and high-voltage blocking capability of the invention.

### Device Design

The design of the high-voltage thin-film transistor is fundamentally reimagined to address the inherent limitations of conventional rectangular channel geometries, which are plagued by electric field crowding at the corners where the source and drain contacts meet the channel. In such geometries, the electric field intensity can reach values several times higher than the average field across the channel, leading to localized breakdown and premature device failure even at moderate drain-source voltages. To eliminate this issue, the present invention employs a ring-shaped channel configuration, in which the channel forms a continuous, closed-loop structure surrounding the gate electrode, with the source and drain contacts positioned at diametrically opposite points on the ring. This geometry ensures that the electric field lines emanating from the drain terminal propagate radially inward toward the source terminal, rather than converging at sharp corners, resulting in a significantly more uniform field distribution across the entire channel length. The ring structure is fabricated using photolithographic patterning of the channel material, with the inner and outer radii of the ring carefully chosen to maintain a constant channel width of five micrometers while maximizing the effective channel length. The gate electrode is positioned concentrically within the ring, with a gate-to-drain offset of five micrometers, ten micrometers, or twenty micrometers, depending on the device variant, to further reduce field concentration at the drain edge. The gate-to-source offset is maintained at a fixed value of three micrometers to ensure balanced field distribution and minimize the risk of punch-through. The ring geometry is not merely a geometric modification but a structural innovation that fundamentally alters the physics of charge transport and field distribution in the device. Finite element simulations confirm that the maximum electric field in the ring configuration is reduced by approximately fifty percent compared to an equivalent rectangular device with the same channel length and width, with the highest field intensity occurring along the inner edge of the ring rather than at discrete corners. This reduction in peak field intensity directly translates into a higher breakdown voltage, as the probability of avalanche breakdown or tunneling-induced leakage is exponentially dependent on the maximum local field. The ring geometry also provides a larger perimeter for current flow, which reduces current density and minimizes self-heating effects under high drain bias. The gate electrode is connected to a probing pad through a narrow, serpentine metal trace that minimizes parasitic capacitance and ensures uniform potential distribution across the entire gate. The source and drain contacts are designed with rounded edges and are positioned at a sufficient distance from the ring’s inner and outer boundaries to avoid field enhancement due to contact geometry. The overall layout is optimized for minimal parasitic resistance and capacitance, with all metal interconnects designed to have a width greater than ten micrometers to reduce current crowding. The device is designed to be fully planar, with no vertical stacking of layers beyond the gate-channel-drain-source sequence, ensuring compatibility with low-temperature fabrication and large-area processing. The ring structure is scalable to any size, and multiple rings can be connected in parallel to increase drive current without compromising voltage blocking capability. The design also facilitates the integration of multiple transistors into a single circuit, with each transistor operating independently under its own gate bias while sharing a common substrate. The combination of the ring geometry with the modulation-doped transition layer and magnesium-doped channel creates a device architecture that is structurally, electrically, and thermally optimized for high-voltage operation, representing a departure from conventional transistor design principles and establishing a new paradigm for high-performance metal oxide semiconductors.

### Transfer Characteristics and Thermal Stability

The transfer characteristics of the high-voltage thin-film transistors reveal a marked improvement in subthreshold swing, on-current, and threshold voltage stability when the modulation-doped transition layer is incorporated into the device structure. Under low drain bias conditions, the subthreshold swing of the pure zinc oxide device is measured at approximately 1.8 volts per decade, indicating a high density of interface and bulk traps that impede efficient gate control. The magnesium-doped zinc oxide device without the transition layer exhibits a reduced subthreshold swing of 1.3 volts per decade, demonstrating that the incorporation of magnesium into the zinc oxide lattice reduces the density of oxygen vacancies and other bulk defects, thereby improving the efficiency of carrier transport. However, the most significant improvement is observed in the modulation-doped device, which achieves a subthreshold swing of 0.8 volts per decade, representing a reduction of more than fifty percent compared to the pure zinc oxide device and nearly forty percent compared to the unmodulated magnesium-doped device. This dramatic improvement is directly attributable to the suppression of interface trap density by the modulation-doped transition layer, as confirmed by material characterization. The on-current of the modulation-doped device is approximately one order of magnitude higher than that of the unmodulated magnesium-doped device at a gate bias of twenty volts, indicating a substantial increase in carrier mobility and a reduction in scattering mechanisms at the interface. The threshold voltage, defined as the gate voltage at which the drain current reaches one nanoampere, is shifted toward zero volts in the modulation-doped device, with a value of approximately 0.5 volts, compared to negative four volts in the unmodulated device and negative eight volts in the pure zinc oxide device. This shift is a direct consequence of the reduction in positive fixed oxide charges that would otherwise accumulate in the silicon dioxide dielectric due to zinc ion diffusion. The thermal stability of the devices is assessed by measuring the shift in threshold voltage as the temperature is increased from room temperature to ninety-four degrees Celsius. The pure zinc oxide device exhibits a threshold voltage shift of negative ten point five volts, indicating a strong thermally activated release of trapped electrons from deep states in the channel and at the interface. The magnesium-doped device without the transition layer shows a reduced shift of negative six volts, reflecting the lower density of bulk traps due to the stronger Mg–O bonding. However, the modulation-doped device exhibits a threshold voltage shift of only negative four point five volts, demonstrating superior thermal stability and a significantly reduced density of thermally activated traps. The activation energy of the drain current, extracted from Arrhenius plots, reveals that the slope of the activation energy versus gate bias is steepest for the modulation-doped device, with a value of 0.246 electronvolts per volt, compared to 0.177 electronvolts per volt for the unmodulated device and 0.107 electronvolts per volt for the pure zinc oxide device. This steeper slope indicates a lower total trap density and a more efficient gating mechanism, as the Fermi level can be modulated more readily with gate voltage in the absence of deep traps. The on/off current ratio of the modulation-doped device exceeds one hundred million at room temperature and remains above ten million even at elevated temperatures, confirming that the device maintains high switching performance under thermal stress. The leakage current at zero gate bias remains below one picoampere across the entire temperature range, demonstrating that the interface remains electrically intact and that no new conduction pathways are activated by thermal excitation. These results collectively demonstrate that the modulation-doped transition layer not only improves the intrinsic electrical performance of the device but also confers exceptional thermal resilience, making the invention uniquely suited for applications in environments with fluctuating temperatures, such as outdoor photovoltaic installations and automotive power electronics.

### High Voltage Blocking Capability

The high-voltage blocking capability of the invention is demonstrated through a series of rigorous breakdown and operational tests that establish the device’s ability to sustain drain-source voltages far beyond the limits of conventional thin-film transistors. When subjected to increasing drain-source bias, the unmodulated magnesium-doped zinc oxide device exhibits a sharp increase in drain leakage current at approximately ninety volts, followed by catastrophic failure within milliseconds, as evidenced by permanent degradation of the transfer characteristics and visible damage to the channel region. In contrast, the modulation-doped device continues to operate with a leakage current below one picoampere even at a drain-source voltage of two hundred volts, which is the maximum limit of the high-resolution measurement system. When tested using a custom high-voltage setup capable of applying up to six hundred volts, the modulation-doped device with a channel length of twenty-five micrometers demonstrates a blocking voltage exceeding six hundred volts, with the leakage current remaining below ten picoamperes and the on-current unchanged from its low-bias value. This represents a more than six-fold improvement in blocking voltage compared to the unmodulated device and a more than twenty-fold improvement over pure zinc oxide devices. The ability to sustain such high voltages without degradation is attributed to the combined effects of the ring-shaped channel geometry, which homogenizes the electric field distribution, and the modulation-doped transition layer, which suppresses interdiffusion and eliminates fixed oxide charges that would otherwise create localized field enhancements. The output characteristics of the device at various gate biases reveal a well-defined saturation region, with the drain current increasing linearly with gate voltage and plateauing at high drain bias, indicating that the channel is fully modulated and that no premature breakdown occurs. The kink effect observed at high gate bias is attributed to channel length modulation induced by self-heating, rather than to dielectric breakdown or interface degradation, as the device recovers its original characteristics after cooling. The maximum operating drain bias, defined as the highest drain voltage at which the on-current remains within ten percent of its low-bias value, is measured at seventy volts, one hundred ten volts, and two hundred volts for devices with channel lengths of ten micrometers, fifteen micrometers, and twenty-five micrometers, respectively. This demonstrates a clear trade-off between drive current and blocking voltage, with longer channels enabling higher voltage tolerance at the expense of reduced current density. Despite this trade-off, the on/off current ratio remains consistently above one hundred million across all devices and all operating voltages, confirming that the device maintains excellent switching performance even under extreme bias conditions. The off-state current remains constant regardless of the applied drain voltage, indicating that the gate dielectric is not compromised and that no tunneling or impact ionization mechanisms are activated. The device exhibits no signs of time-dependent degradation during prolonged operation at two hundred volts for over one hour, and repeated cycling between zero and two hundred volts for one thousand cycles results in no measurable change in threshold voltage or subthreshold swing. These results confirm that the invention not only achieves unprecedented high-voltage blocking capability but also operates with exceptional reliability and stability, making it the first thin-film transistor capable of functioning as a high-voltage switch in photovoltaic inverter systems without requiring external protection circuits or complex packaging.

### Statistical Data of Electrical Performance

A comprehensive statistical analysis of the electrical performance of the high-voltage thin-film transistors is conducted across a population of one hundred and twenty devices fabricated on three separate substrates, each containing forty devices with varying channel lengths and configurations. The mean threshold voltage of the modulation-doped devices is measured at 0.6 volts, with a standard deviation of 0.15 volts, indicating excellent uniformity across the wafer. The subthreshold swing of the modulation-doped devices has a mean value of 0.82 volts per decade, with a standard deviation of 0.07 volts per decade, demonstrating that the interface quality is highly reproducible and that the modulation-doped transition layer is consistently effective across the entire fabrication batch. The on-current at a gate bias of twenty volts and a drain bias of ten volts has a mean value of 3.8 × 10⁻⁵ amperes for the ten-micrometer channel devices, with a standard deviation of 0.3 × 10⁻⁵ amperes, confirming that the device performance is not sensitive to minor variations in channel width or thickness. The off-current, measured at a gate bias of negative ten volts, has a mean value of 2.1 × 10⁻¹³ amperes, with a standard deviation of 0.4 × 10⁻¹³ amperes, indicating that the leakage current is uniformly low across all devices. The on/off current ratio has a mean value of 1.8 × 10⁸, with a standard deviation of 0.3 × 10⁸, and no device in the population exhibits an on/off ratio below ten million. The threshold voltage shift under thermal stress, measured after heating to ninety-four degrees Celsius, has a mean value of negative four point three volts, with a standard deviation of 0.2 volts, confirming that the thermal stability is not only superior but also highly consistent. The maximum blocking voltage, determined by the point at which the leakage current exceeds one picoampere, has a mean value of 587 volts for the twenty-five-micrometer devices, with a standard deviation of 19 volts, and the lowest recorded blocking voltage in the population is 550 volts, well above the 200-volt operational requirement for photovoltaic inverters. The gate leakage current, measured at a gate bias of fifty volts, has a mean value of 0.8 × 10⁻¹² amperes, with a standard deviation of 0.1 × 10⁻¹² amperes, confirming that the dielectric integrity is maintained across all devices. The variation in device performance is found to be primarily attributable to minor differences in channel length due to photolithographic alignment tolerances, with no correlation observed between performance and substrate location, deposition time, or ambient humidity during fabrication. The coefficient of variation for all key parameters is less than ten percent, indicating that the fabrication process is robust and scalable. The statistical data confirms that the invention is not merely a laboratory curiosity but a manufacturable technology with predictable, repeatable, and commercially viable performance characteristics, suitable for integration into high-volume production lines for building-integrated photovoltaics and smart glass applications.

### Simulation of Electrical Field Distribution

To elucidate the physical mechanisms underlying the enhanced high-voltage blocking capability of the invention, a three-dimensional electrostatic simulation is performed using SILVACO TCAD software, incorporating the exact geometry, material properties, and interfacial charge distributions of both the modulation-doped and unmodulated devices. The simulation domain includes the full cross-section of the device, from the gate electrode through the dielectric, transition layer, channel, and into the source and drain contacts, with mesh refinement at the critical interface regions to ensure numerical accuracy. The dielectric constant of silicon dioxide is set to 3.9, the channel material is assigned a mobility of 15 square centimeters per volt-second, and the modulation-doped transition layer is modeled as a graded dielectric with a compositionally varying permittivity that transitions from 9.9 at the dielectric interface to 3.9 at the channel interface, reflecting the change in magnesium concentration. The interface trap density is modeled as a continuous distribution of states with an energy distribution centered at 0.3 electronvolts below the conduction band, with a density of 8.4 × 10¹¹ per square centimeter for the unmodulated device and 1.4 × 10¹¹ per square centimeter for the modulation-doped device, consistent with experimental measurements. The fixed oxide charge density is set to 6.5 × 10¹¹ per square centimeter for the unmodulated device, based on the measured threshold voltage shift, and zero for the modulation-doped device. The simulation reveals that in the unmodulated device, the maximum electric field reaches 1,520 kilovolts per centimeter at the drain edge, where the channel meets the dielectric, due to the presence of positive oxide charges that attract electrons and create a localized field enhancement. In contrast, the modulation-doped device exhibits a maximum electric field of only 1,410 kilovolts per centimeter, a reduction of approximately seven percent, despite having a longer channel and higher applied voltage. This reduction is not attributable to the thickness of the transition layer, which is too thin to contribute significantly to voltage division, but rather to the elimination of fixed oxide charges and the resulting smoother potential profile across the interface. The electric field distribution in the ring geometry is further analyzed, showing that the field lines are uniformly distributed along the entire perimeter of the channel, with no localized hot spots, and that the peak field intensity is reduced by an additional thirty percent compared to a rectangular geometry with the same channel length. The simulation also reveals that the electric field in the channel remains below one megavolt per centimeter even at a drain-source voltage of six hundred volts, well below the theoretical breakdown field of zinc oxide, confirming that the device operates in a sub-breakdown regime. The potential profile across the channel is nearly linear in the modulation-doped device, indicating that the gate can effectively modulate the entire channel without being compromised by trapped charges or interfacial barriers. The simulation further predicts that the threshold voltage shift under thermal stress is minimized in the modulation-doped device because the absence of fixed charges eliminates the need for additional gate bias to compensate for trapped positive ions. These simulation results provide a quantitative and visual confirmation that the invention’s performance enhancement arises from the synergistic interaction of the ring geometry and the modulation-doped transition layer, which together eliminate the two primary causes of high-voltage failure: field crowding and interfacial charge accumulation. The simulation not only validates the experimental observations but also provides a predictive framework for optimizing future device designs, including variations in channel length, transition layer thickness, and doping concentration, ensuring that the invention remains at the forefront of high-voltage thin-film transistor technology.
