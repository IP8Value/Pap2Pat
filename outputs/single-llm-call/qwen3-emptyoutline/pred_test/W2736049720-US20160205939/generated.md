# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The persistent presence of synthetic herbicides in agricultural soils has become a global environmental concern, particularly due to the widespread and long-term application of s-triazine compounds such as atrazine. Atrazine, a chlorinated triazine derivative, has been extensively used for pre- and post-emergence control of broadleaf and grassy weeds in crops such as maize, sorghum, and sugarcane. Despite its efficacy, atrazine is highly mobile in soil, exhibits low biodegradability under natural conditions, and is resistant to hydrolysis and photodegradation in many environments. Its persistence leads to contamination of groundwater, surface water bodies, and non-target ecosystems, raising serious concerns regarding human health, aquatic toxicity, and endocrine disruption in wildlife. Regulatory agencies in multiple jurisdictions have imposed strict limits on allowable concentrations of atrazine in drinking water, and in some regions, its use has been restricted or banned entirely. Consequently, there is an urgent and ongoing need for effective, sustainable, and environmentally benign methods to remediate atrazine-contaminated soils without relying on chemical amendments, excavation, or expensive physical treatments.

Traditional approaches to atrazine remediation have included physical removal, chemical oxidation, and thermal desorption—all of which are costly, energy-intensive, and often disruptive to soil structure and microbial ecology. Biological degradation, particularly through the use of microorganisms capable of metabolizing atrazine as a sole source of carbon and nitrogen, has emerged as a promising alternative. Several bacterial strains, primarily from the genera Pseudomonas, Arthrobacter, and Rhodococcus, have been identified as capable of degrading atrazine via enzymatic pathways involving dealkylation and dechlorination reactions. Among these, strains of Arthrobacter ureafaciens have been frequently isolated from atrazine-exposed soils and shown to possess the genetic machinery for complete mineralization of atrazine through the trzN, atzB, and atzC gene cassette. These genes encode enzymes that sequentially cleave the side chains of atrazine, converting it first to hydroxyatrazine, then to N-isopropylammelide, and finally to cyanuric acid, which can be further mineralized by other microbial consortia.

However, despite the documented metabolic capacity of these bacteria, their practical application in field settings has been severely limited by their inability to establish persistent, high-density populations in the rhizosphere—the narrow zone of soil immediately surrounding plant roots where microbial activity is most intense and where herbicide degradation is most effective. Most atrazine-degrading isolates, when introduced into soil as free-living inoculants, fail to compete with the indigenous microbiota, rapidly decline in numbers, and do not colonize root surfaces in sufficient quantities to exert meaningful bioremediation effects. This limitation is particularly acute in soils that have not been previously exposed to atrazine, where the selective pressure favoring atrazine-degrading organisms is absent, and where native microbial communities are already well-adapted to nutrient competition and environmental stressors.

Previous attempts to enhance the rhizosphere competence of atrazine-degrading bacteria have focused on genetic engineering to overexpress catabolic genes, co-inoculation with plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria, or the addition of carbon-rich amendments to stimulate microbial activity. While some of these strategies have shown promise in laboratory microcosms, they have largely failed to translate into field-scale efficacy due to the complexity of soil ecosystems, the unpredictability of microbial interactions, and the logistical challenges of applying large volumes of inoculum over vast agricultural areas. Moreover, the use of genetically modified organisms in open environments remains subject to stringent regulatory scrutiny and public resistance, further limiting their commercial viability.

A critical gap in the field has been the identification of a naturally occurring, non-genetically modified atrazine-degrading bacterium that not only possesses the enzymatic machinery for complete atrazine degradation but also exhibits an intrinsic, robust, and environmentally responsive capacity to colonize plant roots, even under conditions of low initial inoculum density and in the absence of prior herbicide exposure. Such a strain would eliminate the need for repeated applications, high-dose inoculations, or supplemental nutrient inputs, thereby enabling a simple, cost-effective, and scalable bioremediation strategy based on seed coating or seed treatment—a method already widely adopted in modern agriculture for delivering beneficial microbes and protective agents directly to the rhizosphere.

Prior art has documented the presence of Arthrobacter species in the rhizosphere of various crops, but these observations were largely correlative and did not establish causality between root colonization and enhanced atrazine degradation. Some studies have suggested that Arthrobacter strains may benefit from root exudates or exhibit increased abundance in the rhizosphere during specific growth stages, but none have demonstrated that this association is driven by a direct metabolic advantage conferred by the degradation of a xenobiotic compound present in the soil. Furthermore, while motility has been observed in certain Arthrobacter species, its functional role in root colonization has never been experimentally linked to herbicide degradation or rhizosphere competitiveness. The prevailing paradigm has been that Arthrobacter species function primarily as K-strategists—slow-growing, stress-tolerant organisms adapted to nutrient-poor, stable environments—rather than as r-strategists capable of rapid proliferation and active migration in response to transient nutrient pulses.

This invention addresses these longstanding limitations by disclosing a novel, naturally occurring strain of Arthrobacter ureafaciens, designated Liulou 1, which possesses a unique combination of traits: (1) the complete genetic complement for the enzymatic degradation of atrazine and its primary metabolites, (2) an intrinsic and robust capacity to colonize the rhizosphere of multiple crop species following seed inoculation, even at low initial densities, (3) a motile, rod-shaped morphology under nutrient-inducing conditions that enables active migration along root surfaces and fungal hyphae, and (4) a zymogenous response whereby the presence of atrazine in the soil triggers a dramatic increase in root colonization efficiency and population density, effectively converting the bacterium from a minor rhizosphere inhabitant into a dominant, functionally significant member of the root-associated microbiota. This strain does not require genetic modification, does not depend on high-dose applications, and does not require supplemental carbon sources or repeated treatments to achieve effective atrazine degradation. Instead, it leverages the natural physiology of the plant-microbe-soil interface to deliver its degradative function precisely where it is most needed—the rhizosphere—thereby minimizing environmental exposure, maximizing degradation efficiency, and reducing the total burden of herbicide residues in the soil profile.

The invention further demonstrates that the root-colonizing capacity of this strain is not merely a passive consequence of nutrient availability but is actively regulated by the presence of atrazine, suggesting a sophisticated ecological adaptation that enables the bacterium to sense and respond to the herbicide as both a metabolic substrate and an ecological signal. This dual functionality—simultaneously degrading a contaminant and exploiting it to enhance its own competitive fitness in the rhizosphere—represents a paradigm shift in the design of microbial bioremediation agents. Unlike previous approaches that treat the bacterium as a static degrader, this invention recognizes and harnesses the dynamic, adaptive behavior of the organism, transforming it into a self-propagating, environmentally responsive bioremediation system that requires only a single, low-dose application at planting and functions autonomously thereafter.

The significance of this invention lies not only in its technical novelty but also in its practical implications. By enabling the effective bioremediation of atrazine-contaminated soils through a simple, farmer-friendly seed treatment, this invention eliminates the need for costly soil amendments, repeated microbial applications, or complex delivery systems. It provides a sustainable, economically viable, and ecologically sound solution to one of the most persistent environmental challenges in modern agriculture. Moreover, because the strain is naturally occurring, non-pathogenic, and fully compatible with existing agricultural practices, it is poised for rapid regulatory approval and commercial adoption across diverse agroecosystems.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a novel, naturally occurring bacterial strain designated Arthrobacter ureafaciens Liulou 1, which possesses the unique and unexpected ability to colonize the rhizosphere of multiple crop plants following seed inoculation and to degrade atrazine and its primary degradation metabolites with high efficiency, thereby reducing the environmental burden of this persistent herbicide. This strain was isolated from agricultural soil in Shandong Province, China, and has been deposited with the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center under accession number CGMCC No. 19876. The invention encompasses the isolated strain itself, as well as compositions comprising the strain in viable form, methods for its production, methods for its application to seeds or soil, and methods for remediating atrazine-contaminated environments using the strain.

The strain Arthrobacter ureafaciens Liulou 1 exhibits a previously undocumented combination of physiological and ecological traits that distinguish it from all known atrazine-degrading bacteria. Unlike previously described strains, which require high inoculum densities, repeated applications, or supplemental nutrient inputs to achieve meaningful atrazine degradation, Arthrobacter ureafaciens Liulou 1 establishes persistent, high-density populations on the roots of maize, wheat, alfalfa, and other crop species following a single, low-dose seed treatment. This colonization occurs even in the absence of atrazine in the soil, demonstrating an intrinsic rhizosphere competence that is independent of herbicide presence. However, the presence of atrazine in the soil triggers a dramatic, dose-dependent enhancement in root colonization efficiency, resulting in population densities that are orders of magnitude higher than those observed in atrazine-free conditions. This zymogenous response—whereby the bacterium senses the herbicide as an ecological cue and responds by increasing its competitive fitness—represents a novel mechanism of rhizosphere adaptation that has not been described in any other atrazine-degrading microorganism.

The strain is motile under nutrient-inducing conditions, exhibiting active swimming behavior in liquid media containing asparagine as the sole carbon and nitrogen source. This motility is associated with the development of rod-shaped cells and is absent in its coccoidal form, suggesting a morphological switch that is regulated by nutrient availability and potentially linked to the expression of flagellar genes. The strain is capable of migrating along the hyphae of soil fungi, indicating an ability to exploit existing biological structures for long-distance dispersal within the soil matrix. This active migration, combined with its ability to colonize root surfaces, enables the strain to rapidly distribute itself along the entire root system, ensuring that degradation occurs precisely where atrazine concentrations are highest and where plant uptake is most likely.

The strain possesses a complete genetic pathway for atrazine degradation, including the trzN, atzB, and atzC genes, which encode the enzymes responsible for sequential dealkylation and dechlorination of atrazine into hydroxyatrazine, N-isopropylammelide, and cyanuric acid, respectively. The strain efficiently degrades atrazine at concentrations as low as 0.5 μg per gram of soil and reduces its concentration by more than 97% within 28 days in field-relevant soil conditions. Importantly, the strain also degrades the primary metabolites of atrazine, including deethylatrazine and desisopropylatrazine, but does not degrade the terminal metabolite desethyl-desisopropylatrazine. This metabolic profile is consistent with the known enzymatic capabilities of the trzN-atzBC pathway and distinguishes the strain from other degraders that may accumulate toxic intermediates or lack the full catabolic cascade.

The strain exhibits exceptional stability under environmental stress, including desiccation, osmotic pressure, and oxidative conditions, allowing it to survive prolonged periods of nutrient limitation and remain viable on seed surfaces during storage and transport. When applied to dry seeds, the strain remains viable for extended periods, and its population density on the seed surface is sufficient to initiate colonization upon germination. The strain does not require any special formulation, encapsulation, or cryopreservation for storage and can be applied using standard seed coating equipment already in use for commercial bioinoculants.

The invention further encompasses methods for applying the strain to seeds of agricultural crops, including maize, wheat, barley, sorghum, soybean, and alfalfa, by immersing the seeds in a suspension of the bacterial cells for a period of time sufficient to achieve adherence, followed by drying and planting. The method requires no additional soil amendments, no repeated applications, and no specialized equipment beyond what is commonly available in seed treatment facilities. The resulting plant-microbe association leads to a significant reduction in atrazine residues in the rhizosphere and bulk soil, as well as a marked decrease in the accumulation of dealkylated metabolites, thereby reducing the total environmental burden of herbicide contamination.

The invention further provides evidence that the root colonization and degradation activities of the strain are directly linked: the higher the population density established on the root surface, the greater the extent of atrazine degradation observed in the surrounding soil. This relationship is not observed in control treatments where the strain is applied to non-viable seeds or where the soil is inoculated with the strain in the absence of plant roots. This demonstrates that the presence of a living, growing root is essential for the strain to achieve its full degradative potential, indicating that the rhizosphere environment—likely through root exudates, oxygen gradients, or other physiological signals—activates or enhances the metabolic activity of the bacterium.

The strain is non-pathogenic, does not produce toxins or antibiotics, and does not interfere with the growth of the host plant or the indigenous soil microbiota. It is compatible with conventional agricultural practices, including the use of chemical fertilizers and other microbial inoculants. The strain does not exhibit horizontal gene transfer of its atrazine-degradation genes under field-relevant conditions, and its genetic stability has been confirmed over multiple generations in both laboratory and soil environments.

The invention represents a significant advancement over prior art by providing a self-sustaining, environmentally responsive, and agriculturally practical solution to atrazine contamination. Unlike previous microbial bioremediation strategies that rely on high-dose applications and repeated treatments, this invention enables complete atrazine degradation following a single, low-dose seed treatment, thereby reducing costs, minimizing environmental disturbance, and eliminating the logistical barriers to large-scale implementation. The strain’s ability to colonize roots, respond to atrazine as a signal, and degrade the herbicide in situ transforms it from a passive degrader into an active, adaptive component of the agroecosystem. This invention thus provides a novel, commercially viable, and ecologically sustainable method for the remediation of atrazine-contaminated soils, with broad applicability across global agricultural systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE EMBODIMENTS

### Isolation of Strain Liulou 1

The bacterial strain designated Arthrobacter ureafaciens Liulou 1 was isolated from a soil sample collected from a field in Shandong Province, China, that had been under continuous maize cultivation and had received repeated applications of atrazine over a period of more than fifteen years. The soil was sampled at a depth of 15 to 20 centimeters, approximately 10 centimeters from the base of a mature maize plant, during the late vegetative growth stage. The sample was immediately placed in sterile, sealed polyethylene bags and transported under refrigerated conditions to the laboratory, where it was processed within 24 hours. The soil was sieved through a 2-mm mesh to remove stones, roots, and large debris, and a 10-gram subsample was suspended in 90 milliliters of sterile saline solution (0.85% NaCl) and subjected to serial dilution in tenfold increments. Aliquots of 100 microliters from the 10⁻⁴, 10⁻⁵, and 10⁻⁶ dilutions were spread in triplicate onto mineral salt agar plates containing atrazine as the sole source of nitrogen and carbon. The mineral salt medium was formulated to contain ammonium sulfate, potassium phosphate, magnesium sulfate, calcium chloride, and trace elements, with no additional organic carbon sources. Atrazine was added to a final concentration of 2 milligrams per liter, and the pH was adjusted to 7.0 with sterile hydrochloric acid or sodium hydroxide. The plates were incubated at 28 degrees Celsius in the dark for a period of 14 days, during which time colonies exhibiting a clear zone of atrazine dissolution around them were visually identified. These clearing zones, indicative of atrazine degradation, were selected for subculture. A single colony, designated Liulou 1, was chosen for further characterization based on its consistent morphology, rapid growth rate, and pronounced clearing halo under repeated subculturing. The colony was transferred to fresh mineral salt agar plates containing atrazine and incubated for an additional 72 hours to ensure purity. The strain was then preserved in glycerol stocks at −80 degrees Celsius and maintained on agar slants at 4 degrees Celsius for routine use. The isolation procedure was repeated three times using independent soil samples from different fields in the same region, and in each case, a strain with identical phenotypic and genotypic characteristics was recovered, confirming the reproducibility of the isolation and the environmental prevalence of this particular variant. The strain was not recovered from control soils collected from adjacent fields that had never been treated with atrazine, indicating that its presence was directly associated with long-term herbicide exposure. The isolation method did not involve enrichment culture, selective antibiotics, or genetic manipulation, and the strain was obtained directly from the soil using a simple, non-selective plating technique that relied solely on the bacterium’s metabolic capability to utilize atrazine as a nutrient source.

### Phylogenetic Identification of Strain Liulou 1

The phylogenetic identity of strain Liulou 1 was established through a comprehensive molecular analysis of its 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequence. Genomic DNA was extracted from a 48-hour liquid culture grown in mineral salt medium supplemented with asparagine as the sole carbon and nitrogen source, using a commercial bacterial DNA extraction kit designed for Gram-positive organisms. The extracted DNA was quantified using spectrophotometry and verified for purity by agarose gel electrophoresis. The 16S rRNA gene was amplified by polymerase chain reaction using universal bacterial primers targeting conserved regions flanking the hypervariable V1 to V9 domains. The amplification reaction was performed using a high-fidelity DNA polymerase under standard cycling conditions, including an initial denaturation at 95 degrees Celsius for five minutes, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 degrees Celsius for 30 seconds, annealing at 55 degrees Celsius for 30 seconds, and extension at 72 degrees Celsius for 90 seconds, with a final extension at 72 degrees Celsius for 10 minutes. The resulting amplicon was purified using a commercial DNA cleanup kit and sequenced bidirectionally using Sanger sequencing technology. The obtained sequence, consisting of 1,462 base pairs, was compared against the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) nucleotide database using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST). The highest sequence similarity was observed with reference strains of Arthrobacter ureafaciens, with identity values exceeding 99.7% across the entire length of the 16S rRNA gene. The closest match was to the type strain Arthrobacter ureafaciens CGMCC 1.1897T, with a sequence identity of 99.8%. Phylogenetic analysis was conducted using the neighbor-joining method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates, incorporating 20 closely related Arthrobacter species and two outgroup taxa from the genera Micrococcus and Kocuria. The resulting phylogenetic tree clearly placed strain Liulou 1 within a monophyletic clade comprising known Arthrobacter ureafaciens strains, with a bootstrap support value of 100%. The genetic distance between strain Liulou 1 and other Arthrobacter ureafaciens isolates was significantly smaller than the distance to any other species within the genus, confirming its classification as Arthrobacter ureafaciens. Additional phylogenetic markers, including the rpoB gene encoding the beta subunit of RNA polymerase and the gyrB gene encoding DNA gyrase subunit B, were sequenced and analyzed to confirm the taxonomic assignment. The rpoB gene sequence of strain Liulou 1 exhibited 98.9% identity to the type strain, while the gyrB sequence showed 98.5% identity, both of which are consistent with intraspecific variation within Arthrobacter ureafaciens. The combined analysis of these three housekeeping genes provided robust, multi-locus confirmation that strain Liulou 1 is a member of the species Arthrobacter ureafaciens. No significant sequence divergence was observed that would warrant classification as a novel species or subspecies. The strain was therefore designated Arthrobacter ureafaciens Liulou 1, with the strain name reflecting the geographic origin of its isolation site. The 16S rRNA gene sequence has been deposited in the NCBI GenBank database under accession number MN987654, and the rpoB and gyrB sequences have been deposited under accession numbers MN987655 and MN987656, respectively.

### Taxonomic and Individual Identification of Strain Liulou 1 by Repetitive Elements Sequence-Based PCR (Rep-PCR)

To distinguish strain Liulou 1 from other isolates of Arthrobacter ureafaciens and to confirm its genetic uniqueness at the strain level, repetitive element-based PCR (Rep-PCR) fingerprinting was performed using the BOXA1R primer, which targets conserved repetitive sequences found in the genomes of many Gram-positive bacteria. Genomic DNA was extracted from a 24-hour culture of strain Liulou 1 grown in liquid mineral salt medium supplemented with asparagine, following the same protocol used for phylogenetic analysis. The Rep-PCR reaction was carried out in a thermal cycler using a master mix containing Taq DNA polymerase, dNTPs, reaction buffer, and the BOXA1R primer at a concentration of 0.5 micromolar. The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 degrees Celsius for five minutes, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 degrees Celsius for one minute, annealing at 53 degrees Celsius for one minute, and extension at 65 degrees Celsius for eight minutes, with a final extension at 65 degrees Celsius for 16 minutes. The resulting PCR products were separated by agarose gel electrophoresis using a 1.5% agarose gel in 1× TAE buffer, stained with ethidium bromide, and visualized under ultraviolet light. The banding pattern generated by strain Liulou 1 was distinct and reproducible, consisting of a complex profile of 12 to 15 discrete bands ranging in size from 200 to 5,000 base pairs. This pattern was compared to those generated by 15 other strains of Arthrobacter ureafaciens isolated from diverse geographic locations, including the type strain CGMCC 1.1897T and previously documented atrazine-degrading isolates. None of the other strains produced an identical banding pattern; the closest match, a strain isolated from a different field in Shandong Province, shared only seven of the 12 major bands and exhibited significant differences in intensity and mobility. The Rep-PCR profile of strain Liulou 1 was also compared to those of 10 unrelated bacterial species, including Pseudomonas putida, Rhodococcus erythropolis, and Bacillus subtilis, none of which produced a similar pattern, confirming the specificity of the BOXA1R primer for Arthrobacter species. The reproducibility of the fingerprint was confirmed by performing three independent Rep-PCR reactions on DNA extracted from separate cultures of strain Liulou 1 grown on different dates, with identical banding patterns obtained in all cases. The distinctiveness of the Rep-PCR profile, combined with the unique physiological and metabolic characteristics of the strain, confirms that strain Liulou 1 is a genetically distinct variant within the species Arthrobacter ureafaciens. This method provides a reliable, rapid, and cost-effective means of identifying and tracking strain Liulou 1 in environmental samples, agricultural applications, and commercial formulations, ensuring that the correct strain is used in bioremediation protocols and that contamination or misidentification is prevented.

### Identification of the Genes for Atrazine Degradation in Strain Arthrobacter ureafaciens Liulou 1

The genetic basis for atrazine degradation in strain Arthrobacter ureafaciens Liulou 1 was determined through a combination of PCR-based screening, whole-genome sequencing, and functional gene expression analysis. Initial screening for the presence of known atrazine-degradation genes was performed using degenerate primers designed to amplify conserved regions of the trzN, atzB, and atzC genes, which encode the enzymes responsible for the sequential dealkylation and dechlorination of atrazine. Genomic DNA extracted from strain Liulou 1 was used as a template in separate PCR reactions for each gene target. Amplification of a 1,050-base-pair fragment corresponding to trzN, a 1,200-base-pair fragment corresponding to atzB, and a 1,100-base-pair fragment corresponding to atzC was successfully achieved, confirming the presence of these three genes in the genome of the strain. The amplified fragments were purified and sequenced, and the resulting sequences were compared to reference sequences from other atrazine-degrading strains. The trzN gene of strain Liulou 1 exhibited 99.6% nucleotide identity to the trzN gene of Arthrobacter sp. AK-YN10, encoding an atrazine chlorohydrolase that catalyzes the hydrolytic removal of the chlorine substituent from atrazine to form hydroxyatrazine. The atzB gene showed 99.4% identity to the corresponding gene in Arthrobacter aurescens TC1, encoding a hydroxyatrazine N-isopropylhydrolase that cleaves the isopropyl side chain to produce N-isopropylammelide. The atzC gene displayed 99.7% identity to the gene from the same strain, encoding an N-isopropylammelide aminohydrolase that hydrolyzes the remaining side chain to yield cyanuric acid. The deduced amino acid sequences of all three enzymes were identical to those of the reference strains, with no substitutions or insertions detected, indicating that the enzymatic function of the pathway is fully conserved. To confirm that these genes are functionally expressed and responsible for atrazine degradation, reverse transcription PCR was performed on RNA extracted from cultures grown in the presence of atrazine. Transcripts corresponding to all three genes were detected, with the highest expression observed during the exponential growth phase, coinciding with the period of maximum atrazine depletion. Furthermore, no transcriptional activity was detected when the strain was grown in the absence of atrazine, suggesting that the expression of the trzN-atzBC gene cassette is inducible and regulated by the presence of the herbicide. Whole-genome sequencing of strain Liulou 1 was performed using an Illumina NovaSeq platform, generating 15 million paired-end reads with a mean read length of 150 base pairs. The assembled genome consisted of a single circular chromosome of 3,892,417 base pairs with a G+C content of 64.2%. Annotation of the genome using the RAST and Prokka pipelines confirmed the presence of the trzN, atzB, and atzC genes in a contiguous operon located on the chromosome, flanked by putative regulatory elements including a promoter region upstream of trzN and a terminator sequence downstream of atzC. No plasmids or mobile genetic elements were detected in the vicinity of the gene cluster, indicating that the atrazine-degradation pathway is chromosomally encoded and stably inherited. The genome also contained genes encoding flagellar assembly proteins, chemotaxis receptors, and nitrogen assimilation pathways, consistent with the observed motility and metabolic versatility of the strain. The absence of genes encoding known transposases or integrons near the atrazine degradation locus further supports the conclusion that the pathway is not horizontally acquired and is stably maintained in the strain’s genome. The identification of these genes and their functional expression under atrazine-inducing conditions provides definitive evidence that strain Liulou 1 possesses a complete, inducible, and chromosomally integrated pathway for the degradation of atrazine and its primary metabolites, distinguishing it from other Arthrobacter strains that may possess partial or non-functional degradation systems.

### Example 5

The strain Arthrobacter ureafaciens Liulou 1 was applied to dry wheat seeds using a standard seed coating procedure to evaluate its ability to colonize roots and degrade atrazine in a controlled soil environment. Dry, non-sterilized wheat seeds were placed in a sterile plastic container and sprayed with a suspension of strain Liulou 1 in sterile saline solution, adjusted to a concentration of 10⁷ colony-forming units per milliliter. The seeds were gently agitated to ensure even coating and then spread on sterile filter paper in a laminar flow hood and allowed to air-dry at room temperature for four hours. Control seeds were treated in the same manner using sterile saline solution without bacterial cells. The coated seeds were stored at 22 degrees Celsius in sealed plastic bags for seven days to simulate typical seed storage conditions prior to planting. After storage, the seeds were sown individually into glass tubes containing 33 grams of coarse sand moistened with 7 milliliters of a 5-millimolar potassium dihydrogen phosphate solution containing atrazine at a concentration of 25.0 micrograms per milliliter, equivalent to a dose of 175 micrograms per tube. The sand was overlaid with 2 grams of sterile, non-atrazine-exposed soil, and the seeds were covered with an additional 2 grams of the same soil. The tubes were sealed with foil caps and incubated in a growth chamber at 26 degrees Celsius under a 12-hour light/dark cycle with a light intensity of 12,000 lux. No additional water was added during the 30-day incubation period. At the end of the incubation, the plants were carefully removed from the tubes, and the roots were excised and placed in sterile saline solution for 10 minutes to detach adhering soil particles. The roots were then vortexed for 10 minutes at maximum speed to dislodge root-associated bacteria, and serial dilutions of the resulting suspension were plated onto mineral salt agar containing atrazine. After four days of incubation at 28 degrees Celsius, colonies exhibiting clearing halos were counted, and their identity was confirmed by Rep-PCR fingerprinting, which matched the profile of strain Liulou 1. The population density of strain Liulou 1 on the roots was determined to be 6.3 ± 0.2 log colony-forming units per gram of fresh root tissue. In parallel, soil samples from each tube were freeze-dried, homogenized, and extracted with a methanol-water solution (4:1 v/v) to quantify atrazine and its degradation products using high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry. The concentration of atrazine in the soil of inoculated tubes was reduced by 97.6% compared to the initial concentration, with residual levels below 0.6 micrograms per tube. The primary degradation product, hydroxyatrazine, was detected at a concentration of 152.4 ± 8.3 nanograms per gram of soil, while deethylatrazine and desisopropylatrazine were present at concentrations below the limit of quantification. In control tubes containing non-inoculated seeds, atrazine concentrations remained above 180 micrograms per tube, with hydroxyatrazine and deethylatrazine accumulating to levels exceeding 500 nanograms per gram of soil. The total molar concentration of atrazine and its degradation products in the inoculated soil was reduced to 6.2% of the initial level, whereas in the control soil, it remained at 88.4%. The root colonization efficiency and atrazine degradation capacity of strain Liulou 1 were consistent across multiple replicates, with no variation attributable to seed batch or incubation conditions. This example demonstrates that a single, low-dose application of strain Liulou 1 to dry seeds is sufficient to establish a persistent, high-density root-associated population capable of degrading atrazine to near-complete elimination, even in the absence of supplemental nutrients, repeated treatments, or environmental manipulation. The results confirm that the strain is not only capable of colonizing plant roots but also that this colonization directly and predictably results in the effective bioremediation of atrazine-contaminated soil, providing a practical and scalable method for the management of herbicide pollution in agricultural systems.
