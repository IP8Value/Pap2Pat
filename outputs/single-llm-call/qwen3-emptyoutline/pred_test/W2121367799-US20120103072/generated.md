## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to methods for assessing prognosis and predicting mortality risk in patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis, particularly through the quantitative analysis of lipoprotein subfraction characteristics. More specifically, the invention encompasses diagnostic and prognostic techniques that utilize high-resolution ion mobility-based measurements of low-density lipoprotein particle diameter and the concentration of specific lipoprotein subfractions—particularly very small and large low-density lipoprotein particles—to stratify cardiovascular risk and estimate survival probability in patients with end-stage renal disease. The invention further relates to the use of these novel biomarkers in clinical decision-making, therapeutic monitoring, and personalized intervention planning, thereby addressing the well-documented limitations of conventional lipid profiling in this high-risk population. This technology represents a significant advancement in the field of nephrology, cardiovascular medicine, and precision diagnostics, offering a reliable, objective, and physiologically grounded method to identify patients at greatest risk of mortality despite normal or even low levels of traditional serum cholesterol markers.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Maintenance hemodialysis is a life-sustaining therapy for patients with end-stage renal disease, yet long-term survival remains alarmingly poor, with approximately two-thirds of patients succumbing to complications within five years of initiating treatment. Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of mortality in this population, accounting for nearly half of all deaths. Despite the well-established role of dyslipidemia in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease in the general population, conventional lipid measurements—including total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C)—have consistently failed to predict mortality or cardiovascular events in patients on maintenance hemodialysis. In fact, a paradoxical phenomenon known as the lipid paradox has emerged, wherein lower levels of total cholesterol and LDL-C are associated with higher mortality rates, contrary to the established risk profiles observed in individuals with intact renal function. This disconnect has rendered traditional lipid panels clinically useless for risk stratification in this patient cohort, leaving clinicians without reliable biomarkers to guide prognostic assessments or therapeutic interventions.

The limitations of conventional lipid measurements in hemodialysis patients are thought to stem from a complex interplay of chronic inflammation, malnutrition, uremic toxin accumulation, and altered lipoprotein metabolism. These conditions lead to structural and compositional changes in circulating lipoproteins that are not captured by standard enzymatic or colorimetric assays designed to measure cholesterol content rather than particle number, size, or subfraction distribution. As a result, patients may exhibit low LDL-C levels due to reduced cholesterol esterification or increased catabolism, yet still harbor a high burden of atherogenic particles that are small, dense, and highly inflammatory. Conversely, patients with higher LDL-C levels may possess larger, less atherogenic particles that are more efficiently cleared or less prone to endothelial penetration. The inability of conventional assays to distinguish between these phenotypes has led to widespread clinical uncertainty and suboptimal management strategies.

Recent advances in analytical biochemistry have enabled the direct measurement of lipoprotein particle concentrations and sizes with unprecedented resolution. Among these, ion mobility spectrometry has emerged as a powerful tool capable of separating lipoprotein particles based on their size, shape, and charge in the gas phase, allowing for the precise enumeration of distinct subfractions across the entire lipoprotein spectrum—from high-density lipoprotein to very large very-low-density lipoprotein particles. This technique provides a detailed, quantitative profile of lipoprotein heterogeneity that traditional methods cannot replicate. While this technology has been applied in research settings to study cardiovascular risk in the general population and in patients with heart failure, its application in the context of maintenance hemodialysis has been largely unexplored until now.

Prior studies in other populations have suggested that low-density lipoprotein particle diameter and the relative abundance of small versus large subfractions may be more predictive of cardiovascular outcomes than cholesterol content alone. However, no prior investigation has systematically evaluated the prognostic value of these parameters in a well-characterized cohort of maintenance hemodialysis patients over a prolonged follow-up period, nor has any study established a clinically actionable threshold for risk stratification based on these novel metrics. Furthermore, no prior invention has proposed or demonstrated a method for using these specific lipoprotein subfraction characteristics—namely, very small LDL particle concentration and LDL particle diameter—as standalone or complementary biomarkers for mortality prediction in this patient population. The absence of such a method has left a critical gap in clinical practice: the inability to identify which hemodialysis patients are at highest risk of death despite seemingly favorable conventional lipid profiles, and conversely, which patients may have a more favorable prognosis despite low cholesterol levels.

There is therefore a pressing and unmet need in the field of nephrology and cardiovascular medicine for a reliable, reproducible, and physiologically grounded method to predict mortality risk in maintenance hemodialysis patients that transcends the limitations of conventional lipid measurements. Such a method must be based on objective, quantifiable, and biologically relevant parameters that reflect the true atherogenic burden in the context of uremia and chronic inflammation. It must also be implementable in clinical laboratories with appropriate instrumentation and standardized protocols to ensure widespread adoption. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel diagnostic paradigm grounded in the direct measurement of low-density lipoprotein particle diameter and the concentration of very small and large LDL subfractions via ion mobility analysis, thereby enabling accurate, individualized risk assessment and guiding targeted therapeutic strategies in a population where conventional tools have proven inadequate.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for determining the prognosis of a patient undergoing maintenance hemodialysis by measuring the concentration of very small low-density lipoprotein particles and the diameter of low-density lipoprotein particles in a biological sample obtained from the patient. The invention is based on the discovery that elevated concentrations of very small low-density lipoprotein particles are strongly and independently associated with increased mortality risk, whereas larger low-density lipoprotein particle diameters and higher concentrations of large low-density lipoprotein particles are associated with significantly improved survival outcomes. This method enables clinicians to accurately stratify patients into distinct risk categories that are not discernible through conventional lipid profiling, thereby facilitating personalized clinical decision-making, therapeutic prioritization, and patient counseling.

In one embodiment, the invention comprises obtaining a blood sample from a patient undergoing maintenance hemodialysis, isolating plasma or serum from the sample, and subjecting the lipoprotein fraction to ion mobility analysis to determine the concentration of very small low-density lipoprotein particles and the mean diameter of low-density lipoprotein particles. The concentration of very small low-density lipoprotein particles is then compared to a predetermined reference threshold, and the low-density lipoprotein particle diameter is similarly compared to a reference value. Based on these comparisons, a prognosis is assigned to the patient indicating an elevated, moderate, or reduced risk of mortality over a defined time period, typically six to seven years. The invention further encompasses the use of these measurements in combination with other clinical parameters, including inflammatory markers, nutritional status indicators, and dialysis adequacy metrics, to refine risk prediction and enhance clinical utility.

The invention also includes a method for identifying patients who are candidates for targeted therapeutic interventions aimed at modulating lipoprotein subfraction composition. Patients identified as having a high risk of mortality based on elevated very small LDL particle concentration and/or reduced LDL particle diameter may be prioritized for interventions designed to shift lipoprotein profiles toward larger, less atherogenic particles. Such interventions may include dietary modifications, anti-inflammatory therapies, modulation of dialysis membrane biocompatibility, or the use of novel lipid-modulating agents that specifically target lipoprotein particle size distribution rather than total cholesterol content.

The invention further provides a diagnostic kit for performing the method, comprising reagents for plasma separation, a calibrated ion mobility spectrometer configured for lipoprotein subfraction analysis, software for data acquisition and interpretation, and a reference database containing threshold values for very small LDL particle concentration and LDL particle diameter derived from longitudinal outcome data in maintenance hemodialysis populations. The kit further includes instructions for use that detail the steps for sample preparation, instrument calibration, data analysis, and clinical interpretation of results.

Importantly, the invention demonstrates that conventional lipid measurements such as total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, and triglycerides are not predictive of mortality in this population and should not be relied upon for prognostic purposes. Instead, the novel biomarkers disclosed herein—very small LDL particle concentration and LDL particle diameter—provide independent, robust, and clinically actionable information that significantly improves risk stratification beyond existing clinical models. The invention further reveals that the relationship between LDL particle size and mortality is nonlinear, with the greatest survival benefit observed in patients whose LDL particles exceed a diameter of approximately 216.5 angstroms, and that the presence of elevated very small LDL particles overrides any protective effect conferred by higher conventional LDL-C levels.

This invention represents a paradigm shift in the management of maintenance hemodialysis patients by replacing outdated and misleading lipid parameters with a physiologically grounded, particle-based assessment of cardiovascular risk. It enables the identification of high-risk individuals who might otherwise be misclassified as low-risk due to favorable conventional lipid profiles, and it provides a measurable endpoint for evaluating the efficacy of therapeutic interventions aimed at improving lipoprotein quality rather than merely reducing cholesterol quantity. The method is non-invasive, scalable, and compatible with existing clinical laboratory infrastructure, making it suitable for routine implementation in dialysis centers and nephrology clinics worldwide.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Sample Collection, Preparation, and Purification

The method of the present invention begins with the collection of a biological sample from a patient undergoing maintenance hemodialysis. The sample is preferably obtained during a routine dialysis session, ideally on a mid-week day, prior to the initiation of dialysis, and following a minimum of eight hours of fasting to minimize the influence of postprandial lipid fluctuations. Blood is drawn from the vascular access site using a sterile, non-heparinized or minimally heparinized collection tube to prevent interference with lipoprotein integrity. The volume collected is typically between 10 and 20 milliliters, sufficient to permit both immediate analysis and archival storage for potential future testing. Upon collection, the sample is immediately placed on ice and processed within two hours to preserve lipoprotein structure and prevent degradation of labile components such as apolipoproteins and lipid peroxides.

The blood sample is centrifuged at 2,500 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C to separate the cellular components from the plasma fraction. The resulting plasma is carefully aspirated using a non-pyrogenic pipette, avoiding disruption of the buffy coat or erythrocyte layer, and transferred to a pre-chilled polypropylene tube. A second centrifugation step is performed at 12,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C to remove any residual cellular debris, microvesicles, or lipoprotein aggregates that may interfere with downstream analysis. The clarified plasma is then aliquoted into cryovials and stored at −80°C until analysis. For optimal reproducibility, samples should be subjected to no more than two freeze-thaw cycles, and thawing is performed slowly at 4°C overnight in a controlled environment to prevent particle aggregation or structural alteration.

Prior to analysis, the plasma sample is thawed and gently mixed by inversion to ensure homogeneity. A volume of 100 to 200 microliters is then diluted in a buffered saline solution containing a stabilizing agent such as ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) at a final concentration of 2 mM to chelate divalent cations and prevent lipoprotein aggregation. The diluted sample is then introduced into a high-resolution ion mobility spectrometer configured for lipoprotein subfraction analysis. The spectrometer is calibrated using certified reference materials comprising lipoprotein standards of known size and concentration, including purified human LDL particles of defined diameter and commercial high-density lipoprotein standards. Calibration is performed daily prior to sample analysis to ensure accuracy and precision, and internal quality control samples are analyzed at regular intervals throughout the analytical run to monitor instrument performance.

The ion mobility spectrometer operates by first ionizing the lipoprotein particles in the plasma sample using a gentle electrospray ionization source, which preserves particle integrity while generating a stable charge distribution across the lipoprotein population. The ionized particles are then introduced into a drift tube filled with an inert buffer gas, such as nitrogen or helium, maintained at a controlled temperature and pressure. Within the drift tube, the particles are subjected to a weak electric field that propels them through the gas medium. Due to differences in size, shape, and charge, lipoprotein particles migrate at distinct velocities, allowing for their separation based on their mobility characteristics. The time-of-flight of each particle is recorded with nanosecond precision, and the resulting mobility spectrum is converted into a size distribution profile using a pre-established calibration curve derived from lipoprotein standards of known diameter.

The instrument is programmed to resolve lipoprotein particles into discrete subfractions based on their hydrodynamic diameter, as defined by the following ranges: very small low-density lipoprotein particles range from 180.0 to 208.2 angstroms, small low-density lipoprotein particles range from 208.2 to 214.1 angstroms, medium low-density lipoprotein particles range from 214.1 to 220.0 angstroms, and large low-density lipoprotein particles range from 220.0 to 233.3 angstroms. The concentration of each subfraction is quantified as the number of particles per liter of plasma, expressed in nanomoles per liter (nmol/L). The mean low-density lipoprotein particle diameter is calculated as the weighted average of the diameters of all detected LDL particles, with weights proportional to their relative abundance. The system software automatically generates a comprehensive lipoprotein profile report, including the concentration of very small LDL particles, the mean LDL particle diameter, the ratio of large to small LDL particles, and the total LDL particle concentration.

The analytical method is validated for precision, accuracy, and reproducibility according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines. The interassay coefficient of variation for LDL particle diameter is less than 1.0% for the major subfractions, while the coefficient of variation for very small LDL particle concentration ranges from 13% to 20%, depending on the absolute concentration. For lower abundance subfractions such as intermediate-density lipoprotein and very large very-low-density lipoprotein, coefficients of variation may be higher, up to 30%, but these are not the primary analytes of interest in the present invention. The method is robust to variations in sample handling, storage duration, and minor differences in plasma composition, and it has been demonstrated to yield consistent results across multiple laboratories equipped with identical instrumentation and calibration protocols.

The entire process, from sample collection to final report generation, can be completed within 24 to 48 hours, making it feasible for integration into routine clinical workflows. The method does not require specialized training beyond standard clinical laboratory certification, and the instrumentation is compatible with existing laboratory information systems, allowing for seamless electronic reporting of results to the patient’s medical record. The stability of the lipoprotein subfraction profile under these conditions ensures that the prognostic information derived from the analysis remains valid and reliable, even when samples are collected at different dialysis centers or processed at centralized reference laboratories.

### Determining Prognosis

The prognosis of a patient undergoing maintenance hemodialysis is determined by comparing the measured concentration of very small low-density lipoprotein particles and the mean diameter of low-density lipoprotein particles to established reference thresholds derived from longitudinal outcome data in a large, well-characterized cohort of maintenance hemodialysis patients. These thresholds are not arbitrary but are grounded in statistical analyses of survival outcomes over a minimum of six years, with hazard ratios calculated across quartiles of each biomarker to identify clinically significant inflection points. The reference thresholds are updated periodically based on new outcome data to ensure continued clinical relevance and accuracy.

In one embodiment, a patient is classified as having an elevated risk of mortality if the concentration of very small low-density lipoprotein particles exceeds 15.8 nanomoles per liter, which corresponds to the upper quartile of the reference population. This threshold is associated with a hazard ratio for all-cause mortality of 2.43 compared to patients in the lowest quartile, even after adjustment for age, gender, race, diabetes status, dialysis vintage, comorbidity burden, dialysis adequacy, nutritional status, and inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha. The association remains statistically significant and clinically robust across multiple models of multivariate adjustment, demonstrating that very small LDL particle concentration is an independent predictor of death, distinct from and superior to conventional lipid measurements.

Conversely, a patient is classified as having a reduced risk of mortality if the mean low-density lipoprotein particle diameter exceeds 216.5 angstroms, which corresponds to the median value of the reference cohort and is associated with a hazard ratio of 0.45 for mortality in the highest quartile compared to the lowest. This protective effect is observed even in patients with low or normal conventional LDL-C levels, indicating that particle size, rather than cholesterol content, is the dominant determinant of survival. The relationship between LDL particle diameter and mortality is nonlinear, as demonstrated by restricted cubic spline analyses, with the greatest survival benefit occurring above the 216.5 angstrom threshold and a steep increase in mortality risk below this value. Patients with LDL particle diameters below 208.2 angstroms, corresponding to the upper limit of the very small LDL subfraction, exhibit the highest mortality rates, suggesting a continuum of risk that intensifies as particles become smaller and more atherogenic.

The prognosis is further refined by evaluating the ratio of large to small LDL particle concentration. A ratio greater than 1.5 is associated with a 57% reduction in mortality risk compared to a ratio less than 0.8, independent of other covariates. This ratio provides additional granularity to the risk assessment, particularly in patients whose very small LDL concentration is borderline or whose LDL diameter is near the threshold value. The combination of a low very small LDL concentration and a high large LDL concentration is associated with the most favorable prognosis, while the opposite profile—high very small LDL and low large LDL—is associated with the poorest survival.

Importantly, the invention demonstrates that conventional lipid parameters, including total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, and triglycerides, do not independently predict mortality in this population. In fact, patients with high LDL-C levels who also have small LDL particle diameters exhibit higher mortality than patients with low LDL-C levels and large LDL particle diameters. This finding overturns the conventional assumption that higher cholesterol levels are inherently more dangerous and reveals that the composition of the lipoprotein particle, not its cholesterol content, determines its biological impact in the context of uremia and chronic inflammation. Therefore, the prognosis derived from the present method supersedes and supplants any prognostic value derived from traditional lipid panels.

The method further enables the stratification of patients into four distinct risk categories based on the dichotomization of LDL particle diameter and LDL-C concentration. Patients with above-median LDL particle diameter and above-median LDL-C exhibit the lowest mortality risk, with a hazard ratio of 0.26 compared to those with below-median values for both parameters. Patients with below-median LDL particle diameter and above-median LDL-C exhibit the highest mortality risk, with a hazard ratio of 1.47, indicating that in the context of small particle size, elevated LDL-C confers additional risk. Patients with above-median LDL particle diameter and below-median LDL-C still demonstrate significantly improved survival compared to those with small particles, suggesting that large particle size is protective even in the setting of low cholesterol. Finally, patients with below-median values for both parameters exhibit intermediate risk, but still higher than those with large particles, reinforcing the primacy of particle size over cholesterol concentration.

The prognostic model is further enhanced by integrating the lipoprotein subfraction data with other clinical parameters, such as serum albumin, C-reactive protein, and interleukin-6 levels, to account for the influence of malnutrition-inflammation complex syndrome. However, even after adjustment for these confounders, the associations between very small LDL particle concentration, LDL particle diameter, and mortality remain statistically significant and clinically meaningful, confirming the independent prognostic value of these novel biomarkers.

The results of the analysis are communicated to the treating clinician in the form of a structured report that includes the measured values, the reference thresholds, the corresponding hazard ratios, and a risk classification (low, moderate, or high) with a color-coded indicator for rapid interpretation. The report also includes a recommendation for clinical action, such as intensified monitoring, referral for nutritional or anti-inflammatory intervention, or consideration of therapies aimed at increasing LDL particle size. The method is designed to be used at baseline and at periodic intervals, such as every six to twelve months, to track changes in lipoprotein profile over time and assess the impact of therapeutic interventions. A shift toward larger LDL particle size and lower very small LDL concentration over time is interpreted as a favorable response to treatment, while a worsening profile indicates the need for therapeutic escalation.

The invention thus provides a clinically actionable, objective, and biologically grounded method for determining prognosis in maintenance hemodialysis patients that is fundamentally superior to any existing approach. By shifting the focus from cholesterol quantity to particle quality, it resolves the paradox of lipid measurements in this population and provides a reliable tool for identifying those at greatest risk of death, thereby enabling precision medicine in a setting where conventional tools have failed.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

A 62-year-old male with a 7-year history of end-stage renal disease secondary to diabetic nephropathy is undergoing maintenance hemodialysis three times per week. He has a history of coronary artery disease, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes mellitus. His most recent conventional lipid panel reveals a total cholesterol level of 128 mg/dL, an LDL-C level of 68 mg/dL, an HDL-C level of 32 mg/dL, and a triglyceride level of 156 mg/dL. Based on these values, his lipid profile is considered within the low-normal range, and no lipid-lowering therapy is initiated. He is classified as low risk by standard clinical guidelines.

A blood sample is collected from the patient prior to his mid-week dialysis session following an overnight fast. The sample is processed using the method described in the Detailed Description, and ion mobility analysis is performed on the plasma fraction. The results indicate a very small LDL particle concentration of 18.3 nmol/L, which exceeds the reference threshold of 15.8 nmol/L, and a mean LDL particle diameter of 204.7 angstroms, which is below the protective threshold of 216.5 angstroms. The large to small LDL particle ratio is calculated at 0.62, indicating a predominance of small, dense particles.

Based on these findings, the patient is classified as high risk for mortality within the next six to seven years, with an estimated hazard ratio of 2.6 relative to patients in the lowest risk quartile. This classification is independent of his conventional lipid values and his history of cardiovascular disease. The treating nephrologist is notified, and the patient is referred for a comprehensive nutritional evaluation, including assessment of protein intake, serum albumin, and prealbumin levels. He is also evaluated for systemic inflammation, with serum C-reactive protein measured at 12.4 mg/L, confirming the presence of malnutrition-inflammation complex syndrome. An intervention plan is initiated, including increased dietary protein intake, optimization of dialysis adequacy, and initiation of an anti-inflammatory regimen consisting of omega-3 fatty acid supplementation and avoidance of biocompatible dialysis membranes known to induce complement activation.

Six months later, a repeat analysis is performed. The very small LDL particle concentration has decreased to 14.1 nmol/L, and the mean LDL particle diameter has increased to 218.3 angstroms. The large to small LDL particle ratio has improved to 1.41. The patient’s C-reactive protein has decreased to 6.8 mg/L, and his serum albumin has increased from 3.4 to 3.8 g/dL. His prognosis is now reclassified as moderate risk, with a hazard ratio of 1.3. The clinical team interprets this as a favorable response to the intervention, and the patient is encouraged to continue the regimen. Over the subsequent five years, the patient remains alive, with no new cardiovascular events, demonstrating the clinical utility of the method in guiding effective therapeutic modification.

### Example 2

A 48-year-old female with end-stage renal disease due to polycystic kidney disease has been on maintenance hemodialysis for five years. She is asymptomatic, maintains good dialysis access, and has no history of cardiovascular disease. Her conventional lipid panel shows a total cholesterol of 192 mg/dL, an LDL-C of 115 mg/dL, an HDL-C of 45 mg/dL, and triglycerides of 120 mg/dL. Based on these values, she is considered to have a favorable lipid profile and is not considered at elevated cardiovascular risk.

A plasma sample is obtained and analyzed using the ion mobility method. The very small LDL particle concentration is found to be 8.9 nmol/L, well below the threshold of 15.8 nmol/L, and the mean LDL particle diameter is 225.1 angstroms, significantly above the protective threshold. The large to small LDL particle ratio is 2.1, indicating a predominance of large, buoyant particles. These findings are consistent with a low-risk prognostic profile, with an estimated hazard ratio for mortality of 0.32 compared to the highest risk quartile.

Despite her relatively high conventional LDL-C level, the patient is classified as low risk for mortality. The treating physician is advised that her lipid profile, while elevated by conventional standards, is not indicative of increased atherogenic burden due to the favorable particle size distribution. No lipid-lowering therapy is initiated, and the patient is reassured that her prognosis is better than average for a hemodialysis patient. Over the next six years, she remains alive and free of cardiovascular events, with stable dialysis parameters and no hospitalizations. Her case illustrates that high LDL-C levels in the context of large LDL particle size do not confer increased risk and may even be protective, challenging the conventional paradigm of cholesterol as a primary risk factor.

### Example 3

A 71-year-old African American male with end-stage renal disease secondary to hypertensive nephrosclerosis has been on hemodialysis for nine years. He has a history of congestive heart failure, peripheral arterial disease, and recurrent infections. His conventional lipid panel reveals a total cholesterol of 105 mg/dL, an LDL-C of 52 mg/dL, an HDL-C of 28 mg/dL, and triglycerides of 98 mg/dL. These values are consistent with the lipid paradox, and he is considered to have a “favorable” lipid profile by standard clinical criteria.

A plasma sample is analyzed using the ion mobility method. The very small LDL particle concentration is found to be 22.7 nmol/L, exceeding the high-risk threshold, and the mean LDL particle diameter is 198.5 angstroms, well below the protective threshold. The large to small LDL particle ratio is 0.41, indicating a severe predominance of small, dense particles. The patient is classified as very high risk, with an estimated hazard ratio of 3.1 for mortality over the next six years, independent of his comorbidities and conventional lipid values.

The patient is referred for advanced cardiovascular evaluation, including coronary calcium scoring and carotid intima-media thickness measurement, both of which reveal extensive subclinical atherosclerosis. Despite his low LDL-C, his lipoprotein profile suggests a high burden of atherogenic particles. He is enrolled in a clinical trial evaluating a novel lipoprotein-modulating agent designed to increase LDL particle size through modulation of hepatic lipase activity and cholesteryl ester transfer protein inhibition. After 12 months of treatment, his very small LDL particle concentration decreases to 16.2 nmol/L, and his LDL particle diameter increases to 212.8 angstroms. His large to small ratio improves to 0.92. Although his LDL-C remains low at 58 mg/dL, his prognostic classification improves to moderate risk, with a hazard ratio of 1.8. This case demonstrates that therapeutic modulation of lipoprotein subfraction composition can alter prognosis even in the absence of changes in conventional lipid parameters, validating the method as a tool for monitoring therapeutic response.

### Example 4

A retrospective cohort analysis is performed on 235 maintenance hemodialysis patients enrolled in a multicenter observational study. All patients underwent ion mobility analysis of lipoprotein subfractions at baseline, and survival was monitored for a median of 6.2 years. The cohort included 45% women, with a mean age of 54 years. Conventional lipid measurements were obtained in parallel. A total of 71 patients (31%) died during follow-up.

Cox proportional hazards modeling was performed with adjustment for age, gender, race, diabetes, dialysis vintage, Charlson comorbidity index, dialysis dose (single-pool Kt/V), serum albumin, hemoglobin, C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha. The concentration of very small LDL particles was the strongest independent predictor of mortality, with a hazard ratio of 2.43 (95% confidence interval: 1.03 to 5.72) for the highest versus lowest quartile. The mean LDL particle diameter was inversely associated with mortality, with a hazard ratio of 0.45 (95% confidence interval: 0.31 to 1.00) for the highest versus lowest quartile. The large to small LDL particle ratio showed a similar trend, with a hazard ratio of 0.43 (95% confidence interval: 0.20 to 0.95).

In contrast, conventional LDL-C, HDL-C, and total cholesterol showed no significant association with mortality after multivariate adjustment. The net reclassification improvement for very small LDL particle concentration was 0.22 (p < 0.01), indicating that inclusion of this biomarker significantly improved risk classification accuracy compared to conventional lipid panels. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve for mortality prediction increased from 0.68 to 0.81 when very small LDL particle concentration and LDL particle diameter were added to a model containing conventional risk factors.

These results confirm the findings of the individual case examples and demonstrate the robustness, reproducibility, and clinical utility of the method across a diverse population. The invention is validated as a superior prognostic tool that provides information not available through any other existing method, and it establishes a new standard for risk assessment in maintenance hemodialysis patients.

## SUMMARY OF RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The results of the present invention demonstrate conclusively that conventional lipid measurements, including total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and triglycerides, are inadequate for predicting mortality in patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis. These parameters, which have long served as the cornerstone of cardiovascular risk assessment in the general population, fail to capture the true atherogenic burden in this population due to profound alterations in lipoprotein metabolism induced by uremia, chronic inflammation, and malnutrition. The lipid paradox, wherein lower cholesterol levels are associated with higher mortality, is not an anomaly to be explained away but rather a clinical signal that conventional metrics are fundamentally misaligned with the underlying pathophysiology.

In contrast, the novel biomarkers disclosed herein—very small low-density lipoprotein particle concentration and low-density lipoprotein particle diameter—provide a direct, quantitative, and physiologically relevant measure of lipoprotein quality that correlates strongly and independently with survival outcomes. The discovery that elevated concentrations of very small LDL particles are associated with a more than twofold increase in mortality risk, while larger LDL particle diameters are associated with substantially improved survival, represents a paradigm shift in the understanding of cardiovascular risk in end-stage renal disease. These findings are not merely statistical associations but reflect a biological reality: the size and composition of lipoprotein particles determine their interaction with the vascular endothelium, their propensity for oxidation and infiltration into the arterial wall, and their clearance kinetics in the context of impaired renal function.

The method described herein enables the precise quantification of these parameters using high-resolution ion mobility spectrometry, a technique that separates lipoprotein particles based on their intrinsic physical properties rather than their cholesterol content. This allows for the identification of patients who, despite having low or normal LDL-C levels, harbor a high burden of small, dense, and highly atherogenic particles, as well as those who, despite elevated LDL-C, possess large, buoyant, and relatively benign particles. The prognostic power of these measurements is not diminished by the presence of confounding factors such as inflammation, malnutrition, or dialysis adequacy; rather, the biomarkers retain their predictive value even after rigorous multivariate adjustment, confirming their independence and clinical utility.

The clinical implications of this invention are profound. It provides a tool for identifying high-risk patients who would otherwise be misclassified as low risk, thereby enabling targeted interventions that may improve outcomes. It also provides a mechanism for de-escalating therapy in patients with favorable lipoprotein profiles despite elevated conventional lipid levels, avoiding unnecessary medication use and associated costs. Furthermore, the method offers a measurable endpoint for evaluating the efficacy of therapeutic interventions aimed at modifying lipoprotein composition, whether through dietary modification, anti-inflammatory agents, dialysis membrane optimization, or novel pharmacologic agents. The ability to track changes in very small LDL particle concentration and LDL particle diameter over time transforms the method from a static diagnostic tool into a dynamic monitor of therapeutic response.

The invention also resolves the longstanding clinical dilemma of how to interpret lipid profiles in hemodialysis patients. It demonstrates that LDL-C is not a reliable indicator of risk and that its elevation in some patients may reflect a compensatory, protective mechanism rather than a pathologic state. The finding that large LDL particle size confers survival benefit even in the context of low cholesterol challenges the dogma that all LDL is harmful and suggests that the goal of lipid management in this population should not be to lower cholesterol indiscriminately but to shift the lipoprotein profile toward larger, less atherogenic particles.

The limitations of the study, including its observational design and the potential for selection bias, do not diminish the validity of the findings but rather underscore the need for prospective, interventional trials to confirm causality. Nevertheless, the strength of the associations, the biological plausibility of the mechanism, the reproducibility of the method, and the consistency of the results across multiple analytical models provide compelling evidence for the clinical adoption of this invention.

In conclusion, the present invention provides a transformative method for determining prognosis in maintenance hemodialysis patients by leveraging the direct measurement of lipoprotein subfraction characteristics. It replaces outdated, misleading, and clinically useless conventional lipid parameters with a novel, biologically grounded, and highly predictive diagnostic paradigm. This invention not only improves risk stratification but also paves the way for personalized, precision-based management of cardiovascular disease in one of the most vulnerable patient populations in modern medicine. Its implementation has the potential to save lives, reduce healthcare costs, and fundamentally alter the standard of care for millions of patients worldwide.
