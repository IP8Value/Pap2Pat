# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to intelligent lighting systems and, more specifically, to methods and apparatuses for estimating spatial occupancy distributions within enclosed environments using modulated visible light and sparse non-imaging color sensors. The invention enables the realization of privacy-preserving, energy-efficient, and human-centric lighting control systems by inferring the approximate location and extent of occupants or objects within a room without capturing or processing any visually identifiable imagery. The system leverages the inherent physical properties of light transport—namely, the attenuation and redistribution of luminous flux due to occlusion or reflection—to construct a low-resolution, non-identifying representation of spatial occupancy. This is achieved through the controlled modulation of multi-channel LED lighting fixtures and the measurement of corresponding changes in luminous flux using low-cost, passive photodiode-based color sensors. The invention is particularly suited for deployment in residential, commercial, healthcare, and industrial environments where occupant privacy is paramount, traditional imaging sensors are deemed intrusive or legally restricted, and energy conservation is a critical operational objective. Unlike conventional occupancy detection systems that rely on infrared motion sensors, ultrasonic transducers, or video cameras, the disclosed system operates entirely within the visible spectrum, utilizes existing illumination infrastructure, and requires no additional hardware beyond a minimal array of color-controllable light sources and multi-spectral photodetectors. The resulting occupancy estimates are sufficiently coarse to avoid privacy violations while retaining sufficient spatial fidelity to enable adaptive lighting strategies that optimize luminance distribution, spectral composition, and power consumption in real time.

## BACKGROUND

Modern lighting systems have evolved from simple, fixed-output incandescent and fluorescent fixtures to sophisticated, digitally controllable LED arrays capable of dynamic spectral and intensity modulation. This evolution has enabled new paradigms in lighting design, including tunable white lighting, circadian rhythm alignment, and task-specific illumination. Concurrently, the demand for intelligent lighting systems that respond to occupancy patterns has grown significantly, driven by the dual imperatives of energy efficiency and human well-being. Traditional occupancy sensing technologies, such as passive infrared (PIR) sensors, ultrasonic detectors, microwave radars, and pressure mats, have been widely adopted in commercial and residential settings. However, these technologies suffer from inherent limitations: PIR sensors are prone to false negatives when occupants are stationary, ultrasonic sensors are susceptible to environmental noise and false positives due to air currents or moving objects unrelated to human presence, and microwave sensors can penetrate walls, leading to cross-room interference and privacy concerns. Moreover, all of these non-imaging sensors provide only binary or coarse-grained occupancy information, lacking any spatial resolution beyond a few discrete zones.

Imaging-based solutions, such as RGB cameras, depth sensors, or thermal imagers, offer superior spatial resolution and can detect fine-grained occupancy patterns. However, their deployment raises significant privacy and legal challenges. The capture and storage of visual data—even in anonymized or low-resolution form—can violate expectations of bodily privacy, trigger regulatory compliance burdens under data protection statutes such as GDPR or CCPA, and foster user distrust. In environments such as restrooms, bedrooms, private offices, and healthcare facilities, the use of imaging sensors is often prohibited or strongly discouraged. Even in public spaces, the perception of being visually monitored can induce psychological discomfort and reduce productivity. Consequently, there exists a critical gap in the market for a sensing modality that provides meaningful spatial occupancy information without capturing or transmitting any form of visual imagery.

Existing approaches to non-imaging occupancy sensing have largely focused on passive detection of emitted or reflected energy—such as infrared radiation from human bodies or acoustic echoes from ultrasonic pulses. These methods are fundamentally limited by their reliance on physical properties that are either too weak (infrared emission from stationary subjects), too diffuse (acoustic reflections), or too easily confounded by environmental variables (temperature fluctuations, background noise, or non-human obstructions). Furthermore, these sensors typically operate in isolation from the primary illumination system, requiring separate hardware, power, and data acquisition infrastructure. This fragmentation increases cost, complexity, and installation overhead.

The advent of visible light communication (VLC) and Li-Fi technologies has demonstrated that modern LED fixtures can be modulated at frequencies far exceeding human perceptual thresholds—up to gigahertz rates—enabling high-speed data transmission via ambient illumination. This capability opens a novel pathway for sensing: by introducing imperceptible, high-frequency perturbations into the lighting field and measuring the resulting changes in multi-spectral luminous flux at distributed sensor locations, one can infer the physical properties of the environment through the lens of light transport physics. Unlike structured light systems used in computer graphics, which rely on high-resolution projectors and cameras to map scene geometry with precision, the present invention operates under severe constraints: a small number of fixtures, sparse sensor placement, overlapping illumination fields, and a requirement that all perturbations remain imperceptible to human observers. These constraints render conventional reconstruction techniques inapplicable. The challenge, therefore, is not merely to detect occupancy, but to extract meaningful spatial information from a highly underdetermined, noisy, and non-linear system with minimal hardware and maximum privacy.

Previous attempts to use light transport modeling for occupancy estimation have been confined to laboratory settings with dense sensor arrays or have assumed idealized conditions such as known room geometry, uniform surface reflectance, or the absence of ambient light. None have addressed the practical realities of real-world lighting environments: non-uniform fixture distributions, variable surface materials, uncontrolled ambient illumination, and the necessity of operating within strict perceptual and power constraints. Moreover, prior work has not integrated the sensing modality directly into the operational lighting control loop, nor has it proposed a systematic method for generating perturbation patterns that simultaneously maximize information gain and minimize perceptual disturbance. The present invention fills this gap by introducing a complete, end-to-end framework for occupancy-sensitive lighting control that combines a novel perturbation-modulated sensing protocol, a robust method for recovering the light transport matrix under real-world constraints, and two complementary reconstruction algorithms—one for wall-mounted sensors and another for ceiling-mounted sensors—that yield spatial occupancy estimates sufficient for lighting control while preserving privacy by design.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a system and method for estimating spatial occupancy distributions within an enclosed environment using modulated visible light and sparse non-imaging color sensors, enabling privacy-preserving, energy-efficient lighting control without the use of imaging devices. The system comprises a plurality of color-controllable light-emitting diode (LED) fixtures, each capable of independently adjusting the intensity of at least three spectral channels—red, green, and blue—over a continuous range, and a plurality of non-imaging color sensors, each comprising an array of photodiodes with spectral filters corresponding to the same color channels, configured to measure the total luminous flux incident upon them without resolving spatial imagery. The system operates in alternating sensing and adjustment phases, during which imperceptible perturbations are superimposed upon a base illumination condition, and the resulting changes in sensor output are captured and analyzed to reconstruct a low-resolution representation of occupancy.

In the sensing phase, a base illumination pattern is established by the lighting control system to meet ambient lighting requirements. A sequence of perturbation signals, each representing a small, temporally discrete variation in the intensity of one or more LED channels, is then applied to the fixtures. These perturbations are designed to be below the human flicker fusion threshold and to avoid perceptible changes in color or brightness, ensuring occupant comfort. The sensor outputs are sampled at a high temporal rate during the application of each perturbation, yielding a set of differential measurements that isolate the effect of the perturbations from ambient light and steady-state illumination. These measurements are organized into a matrix equation, where the unknown light transport matrix describes the linear relationship between the input perturbations and the observed sensor responses. The light transport matrix is recovered using a pseudo-inverse solution derived from an overdetermined system of linear equations, thereby eliminating the influence of ambient light and capturing the spatial characteristics of light propagation through the environment.

Two distinct reconstruction algorithms are employed to estimate the spatial occupancy distribution based on the recovered light transport matrix. For systems in which color sensors are mounted on vertical surfaces such as walls, a light blockage model is applied. This model computes a difference matrix between the light transport matrix of a reference (empty) configuration and the current configuration. The difference matrix is aggregated across color channels to produce a simplified matrix representing the attenuation of direct light paths between each fixture and each sensor. A three-dimensional confidence volume is then generated by computing, for each point in space, a weighted sum of Gaussian kernels centered along the direct line-of-sight paths between fixtures and sensors, where the weight is determined by the magnitude of attenuation in the aggregated difference matrix. This volume represents the probability that a physical obstruction—such as a human body or furniture—is present at each spatial location, with higher confidence values indicating greater likelihood of occupancy.

For systems in which color sensors are mounted on horizontal surfaces such as ceilings, a light reflection model is applied. This model assumes that light reaches the sensors exclusively via diffuse reflection from surfaces within the room, particularly the floor plane. The aggregated difference matrix is used to weight a pre-computed set of reflection kernels, each representing the expected luminous flux contribution from a specific fixture-sensor pair to every point on the floor plane, derived from photometric principles and geometric optics. The resulting two-dimensional confidence map indicates regions on the floor plane where occupancy is most likely, accounting for the angular dependence of luminous intensity, surface albedo, and path distances. A normalization and sharpening parameter may be applied to enhance spatial contrast and compensate for non-uniform fixture and sensor distributions.

The system further includes a perturbation ordering module that optimizes the sequence in which perturbation patterns are applied to minimize perceptual disturbance. This module formulates the problem as a traveling salesman problem on a graph whose vertices represent perturbation patterns and base illumination, with edge weights corresponding to the magnitude of luminous change between adjacent states. A heuristic optimization algorithm, such as a genetic algorithm, is employed to find a near-optimal traversal sequence that ensures smooth, gradual transitions between perturbations, thereby reducing the likelihood of perceptible flicker or color shifts.

The reconstructed occupancy estimates—whether three-dimensional volumes or two-dimensional floor maps—are then fed into a lighting control strategy module, which adjusts the base illumination pattern to optimize energy efficiency, visual comfort, and productivity. For example, lighting intensity may be reduced in unoccupied zones, spectral composition may be tuned to enhance alertness in occupied workspaces, and luminance gradients may be adjusted to minimize glare and shadowing. The system operates continuously, periodically re-calibrating the light transport matrix and updating occupancy estimates to adapt to dynamic environmental changes, such as the movement of occupants or the introduction of new objects.

The invention provides a novel, privacy-by-design solution to the problem of spatial occupancy sensing in indoor environments. It eliminates the need for imaging sensors, avoids the collection of any visually identifiable data, and operates seamlessly within existing lighting infrastructure. The resulting occupancy estimates are intentionally coarse, providing sufficient spatial resolution to guide lighting control decisions while remaining incapable of identifying individuals, their activities, or their personal belongings. The system is scalable, cost-effective, and compatible with existing LED lighting standards, making it suitable for deployment in homes, offices, hospitals, schools, and other sensitive environments where privacy, safety, and energy efficiency are paramount.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Rank Minimization

The core of the disclosed invention lies in the accurate and robust recovery of the light transport matrix, which encodes the spatial relationships between light sources and sensors through the physical propagation of luminous flux. This matrix, denoted as A, is a real-valued matrix of dimensions m₂ × m₁, where m₁ represents the total number of independently controllable light channels and m₂ represents the total number of sensor output channels. In a typical implementation, each LED fixture provides three spectral channels (red, green, blue), and each color sensor provides four output channels (red, green, blue, and unfiltered white), resulting in a system where m₁ = 3Nₗ and m₂ = 4Nₛ, with Nₗ and Nₛ denoting the number of fixtures and sensors, respectively. The relationship between the input perturbations and the sensor responses is modeled as an affine transformation: y = Ax + b, where y is the vector of sensor measurements, x is the vector of input signals to the fixtures, and b is a bias vector representing the sensor response to ambient light and other uncontrolled illumination sources. The challenge is to isolate the matrix A, which is invariant to ambient conditions and solely dependent on the geometric and material properties of the environment.

To eliminate the influence of the bias term b, the system first establishes a reference illumination state, denoted x₀, under which the sensor readings are recorded as y₀. Subsequently, a sequence of n perturbation signals, δx₁, δx₂, ..., δxₙ, each of dimension m₁, is applied to the fixtures in rapid succession, with each perturbation being sufficiently small to remain imperceptible to human observers yet large enough to produce a measurable change in sensor output. For each perturbation δxᵢ, the corresponding change in sensor output, δyᵢ, is measured as the difference between the sensor reading under the perturbed condition and the baseline reading y₀. This yields a set of n differential measurements: δyᵢ = Aδxᵢ. By arranging the perturbation vectors as columns of an m₁ × n matrix X and the differential sensor responses as columns of an m₂ × n matrix Y, the system of equations becomes Y = AX. This is a linear system in which A is the unknown matrix to be solved.

Under normal operating conditions, the number of perturbations n is chosen to exceed the number of input channels m₁, resulting in an overdetermined system. This ensures that the solution for A is unique in the least-squares sense and minimizes the Frobenius norm of the residual error. The solution is obtained via the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse: A = YX⁺, where X⁺ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀ. This formulation is computationally efficient and numerically stable, provided that the perturbation matrix X is full rank. To ensure full rank, the perturbation patterns are generated as random vectors drawn from a uniform distribution over a bounded interval, ensuring sufficient variation across the spectral and spatial dimensions. The magnitude of each perturbation is constrained to a range that balances signal-to-noise ratio with perceptual invisibility, typically between 0.01 and 0.05 on a normalized intensity scale from 0 to 1.

In cases where the number of perturbations is insufficient to ensure an overdetermined system—for instance, due to hardware latency or sensor integration time constraints—the system may employ alternative estimation techniques such as recursive least squares (RLS), low-rank approximation, or sparse recovery methods. However, in the preferred embodiment, the system is designed to operate with n ≥ 2m₁, ensuring that the pseudo-inverse solution remains the dominant and most reliable method. The use of an overdetermined system also provides inherent redundancy, which enhances robustness to sensor noise, quantization errors, and transient environmental disturbances such as brief changes in ambient light. The resulting matrix A is inherently a low-rank object, as the number of physically distinct light transport paths in a room is limited by the number of surfaces, their reflectance properties, and the geometric constraints of the environment. This low-rank structure is exploited implicitly by the pseudo-inverse solution, which naturally favors solutions with minimal Frobenius norm, thereby suppressing noise and unphysical high-frequency components.

The recovery of A is performed during a calibration or sensing phase that occurs in the background, independent of the primary lighting function. This phase is executed during periods of low occupancy or during scheduled maintenance windows to avoid interference with normal operations. The matrix A is not static; it may change over time due to the movement of furniture, the addition or removal of objects, or even seasonal variations in ambient light. Therefore, the system periodically re-computes A to maintain accuracy. The frequency of re-computation is determined by the dynamics of the environment and the stability of the lighting infrastructure. In a typical office setting, A may be recomputed once every few hours, while in a dynamic environment such as a conference room, it may be updated every 15 to 30 minutes. The computational cost of matrix inversion is modest, given the small dimensions of A (typically less than 50 × 50), and can be executed on embedded microcontrollers or low-power processors integrated into the lighting control unit.

The resulting light transport matrix A serves as a unique signature of the spatial configuration of the environment. Unlike camera-based systems that capture pixel-level imagery, A encodes only the aggregate effect of light propagation—how much flux from each source reaches each sensor, modulated by the intervening geometry and materials. Because A contains no spatial coordinates, no texture, no shape, and no identity information, it is inherently privacy-preserving. It cannot be inverted to reconstruct an image of the room, nor can it be used to identify individuals. It is merely a mathematical representation of how light moves through space. This property distinguishes the invention from all prior occupancy sensing systems, which either capture visual data or infer occupancy through indirect physical proxies that may still reveal sensitive information. The rank minimization process, therefore, is not merely a technical step in signal recovery; it is the foundational mechanism by which privacy is mathematically guaranteed.

### Perturbation-Modulated Lighting

The success of the disclosed system hinges on the careful design and sequencing of perturbation patterns applied to the lighting fixtures during the sensing phase. These perturbations must satisfy three conflicting constraints: they must be sufficiently diverse to encode rich spatial information, sufficiently small to remain imperceptible to human observers, and sufficiently large to produce a measurable signal above the noise floor of the sensors. The invention introduces a novel framework for generating, ordering, and applying these perturbations to achieve an optimal balance among these constraints.

Each perturbation pattern is defined as a vector δx ∈ ℝᵐ¹, where each element corresponds to the intensity change applied to a specific LED channel. The magnitude of each perturbation is constrained by a maximum deviation ρ, defined as the infinity norm of the perturbation vector: ρ = maxᵢ ||δxᵢ||∞. Empirical studies have shown that values of ρ below 0.01 result in sensor responses that are dominated by noise and quantization error, while values above 0.05 become perceptible to human observers under normal lighting conditions. The preferred embodiment selects ρ = 0.025, which provides a signal-to-noise ratio sufficient for reliable matrix recovery while remaining below the threshold of conscious perception for flicker and color shift. This value is chosen based on psychophysical thresholds for human visual sensitivity to temporal luminance modulation and chromatic contrast, ensuring that the perturbations are experienced as a subtle, non-distracting variation in ambient lighting rather than as a visible flicker or color change.

The generation of perturbation patterns begins with the creation of a pool of n random vectors, each drawn independently from a uniform distribution over the interval [−ρ, ρ]. These vectors are designed to span the full range of possible spectral and spatial combinations, ensuring that the resulting perturbation matrix X is full rank and that the light transport matrix A can be uniquely recovered. The number of perturbations n is selected to be at least twice the number of input channels m₁ to ensure an overdetermined system, as previously described. However, the order in which these perturbations are applied is critical to human comfort. A naive sequential application of random perturbations would result in abrupt, jarring transitions between lighting states, potentially triggering perceptual flicker or discomfort, even if each individual perturbation is below the perceptual threshold.

To address this, the invention introduces a perturbation ordering module that determines the optimal sequence of perturbations to minimize perceptual disturbance. This is formulated as a traveling salesman problem (TSP), in which each perturbation pattern and the base illumination state are treated as vertices in a complete, weighted graph. The weight of an edge between two vertices is defined as the Euclidean distance between their corresponding perturbation vectors, representing the magnitude of luminous change required to transition from one state to another. The base illumination state is represented as the zero vector, and the goal is to find the shortest Hamiltonian cycle that visits each perturbation pattern exactly once and returns to the base state. This ensures that the lighting transitions are as smooth and gradual as possible, minimizing the total luminous variation experienced by occupants over the course of the sensing cycle.

The TSP is NP-hard, and exact solutions are computationally infeasible for large n. Therefore, the system employs a heuristic optimization algorithm, specifically a genetic algorithm, to find a near-optimal solution. The genetic algorithm encodes potential sequences as chromosomes, where each gene represents the index of a perturbation pattern. The fitness function evaluates the total path length of the sequence, corresponding to the sum of edge weights between consecutive perturbations. Genetic operators—selection, crossover, and mutation—are applied iteratively to evolve better sequences. The mutation operator, in particular, is implemented as a two-edge swap: two non-adjacent edges in the current sequence are randomly selected and their endpoints are cross-linked, effectively reversing a segment of the path. This operator preserves the cyclic nature of the solution while enabling significant improvements in path length with minimal computational cost. The algorithm terminates after a fixed number of generations or when the improvement in fitness falls below a predefined threshold.

The resulting sequence of perturbations is applied to the fixtures in rapid succession, with each perturbation lasting between 50 and 200 milliseconds, depending on the sensor integration time and communication latency. The entire sequence is completed within a few seconds, ensuring that the sensing phase does not interfere with normal lighting operations. During this time, the sensors sample the output at a rate commensurate with the perturbation duration, capturing the transient response of each sensor to each perturbation. The perturbation ordering module operates independently of the main lighting control loop and may be executed on a separate processor or during off-peak hours to reduce computational load. The output of this module is a deterministic sequence of perturbations that, when applied, ensures maximum information gain with minimum perceptual impact.

This approach represents a radical departure from prior art, which either applies perturbations in random order or ignores perceptual consequences entirely. By explicitly optimizing the temporal sequence of lighting changes, the invention ensures that the sensing process is not only technically effective but also human-centered. It transforms what could be a source of discomfort into a seamless, invisible background process. This is critical for user acceptance and long-term deployment in sensitive environments such as bedrooms, hospitals, and private offices. The perturbation-modulated lighting technique, therefore, is not merely a signal acquisition method; it is a human factors innovation that enables the practical realization of privacy-preserving sensing in everyday environments.

### Analysis of the Light Transport Matrix

Once the light transport matrix A has been recovered through the pseudo-inverse solution, it becomes the central data structure upon which all subsequent occupancy estimation is based. Unlike traditional imaging systems that produce pixel arrays representing spatial intensity, A is a high-dimensional matrix whose entries encode the aggregate luminous coupling between each light source and each sensor. Each element Aᵢⱼ represents the total luminous flux received by sensor i when a unit perturbation is applied to light channel j. This value is determined by the cumulative effect of all possible light transport paths between the source and the sensor, including direct line-of-sight paths, diffuse reflections from walls, ceilings, and floors, specular reflections from glossy surfaces, and interreflections between multiple surfaces. The matrix A, therefore, is not a simple mapping of geometry but a complex, integrated representation of the room’s optical properties.

The key insight of the invention is that changes in the physical environment—such as the presence of an occupant or the movement of furniture—alter the light transport matrix by attenuating or redirecting specific light paths. This change is captured in the difference matrix E = A₀ − A, where A₀ is the light transport matrix measured during a reference state, typically when the room is unoccupied or in a known baseline configuration. The matrix E is of the same dimensions as A and contains positive values where the current configuration exhibits reduced luminous flux compared to the reference state, indicating that a physical obstruction has blocked or attenuated one or more light paths. The magnitude of each entry in E corresponds to the degree of attenuation, which is directly related to the size, position, and reflectance properties of the obstructing object.

However, the matrix E is not directly interpretable as a spatial map. Each entry in E aggregates the effect of multiple light paths, and each light path may be shared by multiple fixture-sensor pairs due to the overlapping illumination fields inherent in real-world lighting systems. For example, a single LED fixture may illuminate an entire room, and a single sensor may receive light from multiple directions. Therefore, the raw matrix E contains redundant and overlapping information that must be aggregated and interpreted through physical models to extract meaningful spatial occupancy information.

The invention introduces two complementary aggregation strategies, tailored to the physical configuration of the sensors. For wall-mounted sensors, the matrix E is aggregated across the three color channels (red, green, blue) for each fixture-sensor pair to produce a simplified matrix Ê of dimensions Nₛ × Nₗ, where Nₛ and Nₗ are the number of sensors and fixtures, respectively. This aggregation is performed as a weighted sum: Êᵢⱼ = wᵣEᵢⱼᴿ + w₉Eᵢⱼᴳ + wᵦEᵢⱼᴮ, where Eᵢⱼᴿ, Eᵢⱼᴳ, and Eᵢⱼᴮ are the entries in E corresponding to the red, green, and blue channels of fixture j and sensor i, and wᵣ, w₉, wᵦ are weighting coefficients that compensate for differences in sensor sensitivity across spectral bands. These weights are determined during system calibration using known reference objects and are stored as system parameters. The resulting matrix Ê is a compact representation of the relative attenuation of direct light paths between each fixture and each sensor. A large value in Êᵢⱼ indicates that the direct path from fixture j to sensor i is likely obstructed, suggesting the presence of an object along that line-of-sight.

For ceiling-mounted sensors, where direct paths do not exist, the matrix E is similarly aggregated, but the interpretation shifts from blockage to reflection. In this configuration, the difference matrix E reflects changes in the diffuse reflection patterns from the floor and other surfaces. A large value in Êᵢⱼ indicates that the total reflected flux from the floor plane to sensor i via fixture j has changed, which may occur when an occupant stands on the floor and alters the reflectance properties of the illuminated area. The interpretation of Ê in this case is probabilistic rather than deterministic, as the change in flux is distributed across a broad area rather than localized to a single line-of-sight.

The analysis of the light transport matrix is further enhanced by the incorporation of physical constraints. For instance, the system may apply a threshold to eliminate entries in E that are below a noise floor, determined empirically as the standard deviation of E under static conditions. Entries above this threshold are retained as significant indicators of occupancy. Additionally, the system may apply spatial smoothing to the aggregated matrix Ê to reduce noise and enhance spatial coherence. This is achieved through convolution with a low-pass kernel or by applying a Gaussian blur to the matrix as if it were a two-dimensional image. The resulting smoothed matrix is then used as input to the reconstruction algorithms.

Importantly, the light transport matrix A and its difference E are not used to reconstruct the room geometry or to identify objects. Instead, they are used solely to infer the presence and approximate location of obstructions that affect light transport. The matrix is inherently low-resolution, containing no texture, shape, or identity information. It cannot be inverted to produce a visual representation of the room. It cannot be used to determine the height, size, or movement of occupants. It cannot be used to identify clothing, facial features, or personal belongings. It is, in essence, a mathematical abstraction of physical obstruction, stripped of all visual content. This is the essence of the invention’s privacy-by-design philosophy: the system does not capture data that could be misused; it only measures the effect of obstruction on light, and nothing more.

The analysis of the light transport matrix, therefore, is not a signal processing task in the traditional sense. It is a physical inference problem, grounded in photometry and geometric optics, and constrained by the fundamental limits of human perception and data privacy. The matrix A is not a camera image; it is a fingerprint of light interaction. And by analyzing this fingerprint, the system can infer occupancy without ever seeing a face, a body, or a silhouette.

### Volume Rendering

The final stage of the occupancy estimation process is the transformation of the aggregated difference matrix Ê into a spatial representation of occupancy. For systems with wall-mounted sensors, this is achieved through a three-dimensional volume rendering algorithm that computes a confidence map over the entire volume of the room. This algorithm is inspired by the principles of the inverse Radon transform used in medical imaging, but adapted to the sparse, non-uniform sampling conditions inherent in lighting-based sensing.

The volume rendering algorithm begins by discretizing the three-dimensional space of the room into a regular grid of voxels, with each voxel representing a small cubic volume element—typically on the order of 1 inch³. For each voxel located at position P = (x, y, z), the algorithm computes a confidence value C(P) that represents the likelihood that an obstruction is present at that location. This confidence is derived from the aggregated difference matrix Ê and the geometric relationship between each fixture-sensor pair and the voxel.

For each fixture-sensor pair (j, i), the algorithm defines a direct line-of-sight path connecting the position of fixture j to the position of sensor i. The distance from the voxel P to this line is computed as the perpendicular point-to-line distance dᵢⱼ(P). The confidence contribution of this path to the voxel is then modeled as a Gaussian kernel: G(dᵢⱼ(P), σ) = exp(−dᵢⱼ(P)² / 2σ²), where σ is a spatial smoothing parameter that determines the extent of influence of each path. This kernel reflects the physical intuition that an obstruction is more likely to be located near the direct path between a fixture and a sensor, and that the likelihood of occupancy decays smoothly with distance from that path. The value of σ is chosen based on the expected size of typical obstructions—such as human bodies or furniture—and is typically set to 15–25 inches to ensure that the confidence volume is sufficiently smooth and does not produce spurious peaks due to noise.

The total confidence at voxel P is then computed as a weighted sum of all such Gaussian contributions: C(P) = Σᵢ Σⱼ Êᵢⱼ · G(dᵢⱼ(P), σ) / Z, where Z is a normalization factor that accounts for the non-uniform spatial distribution of fixtures and sensors. This normalization ensures that the confidence values remain comparable across different regions of the room, even where the number of intersecting paths is sparse. The normalization term Z is computed as the sum of the absolute values of Ê over all fixture-sensor pairs, scaled by the number of paths that intersect each voxel. This prevents regions with few fixtures or sensors from being assigned artificially low confidence values simply due to sparse sampling.

The resulting confidence volume V(x, y, z) = C(P(x, y, z)) is a three-dimensional scalar field that represents the spatial probability distribution of occupancy. This volume is not a high-resolution image; it is a coarse, blurred representation that intentionally lacks fine detail. It does not reveal the shape of a person, the orientation of a chair, or the presence of small objects. It only indicates, in broad strokes, which regions of the room are more likely to contain obstructions. The volume is rendered in real time using a graphics processing unit (GPU) or a dedicated hardware accelerator, with each voxel updated every few seconds as new perturbation data becomes available. The output of the volume rendering module is a low-resolution 3D occupancy map that is passed to the lighting control system.

The algorithm is computationally efficient due to its local structure: each voxel’s confidence depends only on its distance to the direct paths, and the Gaussian kernels can be pre-computed and stored in memory. For a room discretized into 100 × 100 × 100 voxels and 12 fixtures with 12 sensors, the system requires approximately 150 million kernel evaluations per volume update. This computation, while intensive, is feasible on modern embedded systems due to the simplicity of the operations involved—only multiplication and addition—and the ability to parallelize the computation across voxels. The use of a GPU enables real-time rendering at 1–2 frames per second, sufficient for dynamic lighting control.

The volume rendering algorithm is deliberately designed to be insensitive to the exact geometry of the room. It does not require knowledge of wall positions, ceiling height, or surface reflectance. It operates solely on the positions of the fixtures and sensors and the aggregated difference matrix. This makes the system highly portable and adaptable to arbitrary room geometries without the need for manual calibration or 3D modeling. The algorithm is also robust to sensor drift, fixture misalignment, and minor environmental changes, as these are absorbed into the ongoing re-estimation of the light transport matrix.

Importantly, the volume rendering process does not produce any visual output that could be interpreted as an image. The confidence volume is not rendered as a 3D mesh or point cloud; it is not displayed to users or transmitted over networks. It is used internally by the lighting control system as a numerical input to determine which zones of the room require increased or decreased illumination. The system never generates a picture of a person. It only generates a probability field. This distinction is fundamental to the invention’s privacy guarantee. The volume is a mathematical construct, not a visual representation. It cannot be used to identify, track, or surveil individuals. It is, by design, incapable of revealing more than the broadest spatial occupancy pattern.

This approach stands in stark contrast to all prior occupancy sensing systems, which either capture images or infer occupancy through indirect means that may still reveal sensitive details. The volume rendering algorithm is the culmination of the invention’s privacy-first philosophy: it extracts only the minimal information necessary to perform its function, and nothing more. It is not a surveillance tool. It is a lighting optimizer. And it achieves this by transforming the problem of occupancy sensing into a problem of light transport analysis—where the only thing that matters is how light moves, not what it sees.
