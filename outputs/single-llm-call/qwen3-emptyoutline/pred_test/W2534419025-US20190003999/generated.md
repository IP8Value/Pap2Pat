## BACKGROUND

The preparation of high-resolution structural models of biological macromolecules using cryo-electron microscopy has long been constrained by the limitations of conventional grid substrates and the challenges of isolating target molecules from complex biological matrices. Traditional grids, such as those coated with amorphous carbon or lipid monolayers, suffer from inherent drawbacks including poor electron transparency, inconsistent surface chemistry, and an inability to selectively capture target proteins while excluding non-specific contaminants. The presence of cellular debris, nucleic acids, lipids, and other non-target biomolecules in crude lysates often results in high background noise, reduced signal-to-noise ratios, and compromised image quality, necessitating extensive purification steps prior to grid preparation. These purification protocols typically involve multiple centrifugation, filtration, and chromatographic steps that are time-consuming, labor-intensive, and prone to sample loss, particularly when working with low-abundance or fragile targets. Furthermore, the physical and chemical properties of conventional grid surfaces often lead to non-uniform sample distribution, preferential orientation, and structural distortion due to interfacial denaturation or adsorption artifacts.

Recent advances in nanomaterials have introduced graphene-based substrates as promising alternatives due to their exceptional thinness, high electrical conductivity, and mechanical stability. Graphene oxide, in particular, offers a chemically tunable surface rich in oxygen-containing functional groups—such as carboxyl, hydroxyl, and epoxide moieties—that can be exploited for covalent modification and molecular recognition. However, prior attempts to utilize graphene oxide for affinity-based purification on grids have been hindered by the uncontrolled reactivity of these functional groups, which promote non-specific binding of proteins and cellular components through hydrophobic interactions, electrostatic attractions, or covalent adduct formation with lysine, arginine, or cysteine residues. This non-specific adsorption overwhelms the intended affinity signal, rendering the substrate ineffective for selective capture in complex biological environments. Additionally, the large lateral dimensions and irregular shapes of graphene oxide sheets make it difficult to form uniform, single-layer films using conventional deposition techniques such as drop-casting, which frequently result in multilayered, aggregated, or unevenly distributed coatings that are unsuitable for high-resolution imaging.

The development of ligands capable of reversible, high-affinity binding to polyhistidine tags has revolutionized protein purification, particularly through the use of nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA) chelators complexed with divalent metal ions such as nickel. NTA-based systems have been successfully incorporated into lipid bilayers and polymer-coated surfaces to enable on-grid capture of His-tagged proteins. Yet, these lipid-based platforms suffer from instability under cryogenic conditions, poor mechanical integrity during blotting and vitrification, and limited surface density of functional ligands, which restricts capture efficiency and reproducibility. Polymer-based coatings, while more robust, often introduce excessive thickness and heterogeneity, degrading image contrast and resolution. There remains a critical unmet need for a substrate that combines the atomic-scale thinness and electron transparency of graphene oxide with the specificity and reversibility of NTA-metal affinity chemistry, while simultaneously suppressing non-specific interactions from complex biological samples without compromising the structural integrity of the captured target.

Moreover, the ability to perform affinity capture directly on the electron microscopy grid—bypassing traditional purification steps—would represent a paradigm shift in structural biology workflows. Such an approach would minimize sample handling, reduce the risk of aggregation or degradation, conserve scarce material, and accelerate the transition from lysate to high-resolution reconstruction. However, achieving this requires not only precise chemical functionalization of the graphene oxide surface but also the strategic deactivation of its intrinsic reactive moieties that drive non-specific adsorption. The identification of effective, biocompatible blocking agents capable of passivating these reactive sites without interfering with the NTA-metal-His6 binding motif has remained elusive. Furthermore, the formation of a monolayer of functionalized graphene oxide at the air-water interface, with controlled lateral packing, thickness, and transfer efficiency onto diverse grid types, has not been systematically addressed in prior art. Conventional Langmuir-Blodgett techniques are ill-suited for graphene oxide due to its large size, low volatility, and poor interfacial mobility, and attempts to use surfactants to facilitate dispersion compromise the intrinsic surface chemistry of the graphene oxide. The use of volatile co-solvents such as 2-propanol to modulate surface tension and enable controlled Langmuir-Schaefer transfer has not been previously applied to NTA-functionalized graphene oxide, nor has the combination of PABA-mediated surface deactivation with BSA blocking been demonstrated as a synergistic strategy for achieving ultra-low background in cryo-EM applications.

Thus, there exists a significant gap in the art for a novel, chemically engineered graphene oxide-based substrate that enables direct, selective, and high-efficiency capture of His-tagged proteins from crude cell lysates on electron microscopy grids, while simultaneously providing a uniform, single-layer coating with minimal non-specific binding, optimal electron transparency, and compatibility with cryogenic sample preparation protocols. The invention disclosed herein fulfills this need by introducing a functionalized graphene oxide material, chemically modified with NTA chelators and rendered non-reactive toward non-specific interactions through a dual-passivation strategy, that can be deposited as a monolayer film via Langmuir-Schaefer transfer onto standard EM grids, enabling on-grid affinity purification and subsequent high-resolution structural analysis without intermediate purification steps.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel substrate for the direct capture and high-resolution structural analysis of histidine-tagged biomolecules from complex biological samples using transmission electron microscopy. The substrate comprises a monolayer of graphene oxide sheets chemically functionalized with nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA) chelating groups, wherein the graphene oxide sheets are arranged in a continuous, two-dimensional film with minimal thickness and maximal surface coverage, and wherein the surface of the graphene oxide is rendered non-reactive toward non-specific adsorption of non-target biomolecules through a two-step surface passivation protocol involving covalent modification with 4-aminobenzoic acid followed by non-covalent adsorption of bovine serum albumin. The NTA groups are positioned at the periphery of the graphene oxide sheets and are complexed with divalent metal ions, enabling selective, reversible binding to polyhistidine tags on target proteins. The monolayer film is formed by Langmuir-Schaefer transfer from an air-water interface stabilized by a mixture of water and 2-propanol, which facilitates the controlled spreading, compression, and transfer of large-area graphene oxide sheets without the use of surfactants or other interfering agents. The resulting substrate exhibits exceptional electron transparency, uniform thickness, and high ligand density, allowing for the direct application of clarified cell lysates, followed by on-grid capture of histidine-tagged targets, washing, cryofixation, and high-resolution imaging without prior purification. The invention further enables the acquisition of structural data with resolution sufficient for de novo model building and refinement, as demonstrated by the successful reconstruction of a histidine-tagged GroEL complex from Escherichia coli lysate at 8.1 Å resolution using cryo-electron microscopy and single-particle analysis. The substrate is compatible with standard EM grid formats, including lacey carbon-supported copper grids and bare metal grids, and can be stored under dry conditions for extended periods prior to use. The dual-passivation strategy comprising 4-aminobenzoic acid and bovine serum albumin effectively eliminates background noise from non-target cellular components, including nucleic acids, lipids, and endogenous proteins, thereby enabling high signal-to-noise imaging directly from crude lysates. The invention represents a transformative advancement in sample preparation for structural biology, eliminating the need for multi-step purification protocols, reducing sample consumption, minimizing structural perturbations, and accelerating the workflow from lysate to high-resolution structure.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention disclosed herein comprises a chemically engineered substrate designed for the direct, on-grid affinity capture and high-resolution structural analysis of histidine-tagged biomolecules from complex biological samples. The substrate is composed of a monolayer of graphene oxide sheets that have been covalently functionalized with nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA) chelating groups, arranged in a continuous, two-dimensional film with controlled lateral packing and uniform thickness, and rendered non-reactive toward non-specific adsorption through a sequential surface passivation protocol involving 4-aminobenzoic acid and bovine serum albumin. The graphene oxide sheets are derived from the oxidation of graphite, resulting in a two-dimensional material with a basal plane composed of sp²-hybridized carbon atoms and a periphery decorated with oxygen-containing functional groups, including carboxyl, hydroxyl, epoxide, and ketone moieties. These functional groups serve as chemical handles for the covalent attachment of the NTA ligands, which are introduced via esterification and subsequent deprotection reactions involving a lysine-based tris-t-butyl NTA ester. The attachment of the NTA groups occurs preferentially at the edges and defect sites of the graphene oxide sheets, preserving the integrity of the basal plane while maximizing the density of functional ligands available for metal chelation and histidine-tag recognition. The resulting NTA-functionalized graphene oxide material, referred to herein as GO-NTA, exhibits a unique amphiphilic character due to the hydrophobic nature of the graphene core and the hydrophilic distribution of the NTA groups and residual oxygen functionalities at the sheet periphery. This amphiphilicity enables the spontaneous organization of GO-NTA sheets at the air-water interface, where the hydrophobic core orients toward the vapor phase and the hydrophilic periphery remains solvated in the aqueous subphase, facilitating the formation of a monolayer film without the need for surfactants or other interfacial stabilizers.

The formation of a uniform, single-layer film of GO-NTA at the air-water interface is achieved through a Langmuir-Schaefer transfer process that employs a subphase composed of a mixture of water and 2-propanol. The addition of 2-propanol, a volatile, polar protic solvent, reduces the surface tension of the aqueous subphase, thereby enhancing the mobility and lateral diffusion of the large graphene oxide sheets and enabling their controlled compression and packing. The surface pressure-area isotherm obtained during compression reveals a characteristic progression from isolated, non-interacting sheets to close edge-to-edge packing, followed by folding, wrinkling, and sliding of adjacent sheets upon further compression, analogous to the behavior of phospholipid monolayers. The optimal surface pressure for transfer is determined to be approximately 15 mN/m, at which point the sheets are densely packed but not yet subject to irreversible coagulation due to dominant van der Waals interactions. Transfer of the monolayer onto solid substrates is performed using the Langmuir-Schaefer technique, in which a substrate is vertically lowered through the air-water interface and brought into contact with the compressed monolayer, resulting in the transfer of a single layer of GO-NTA sheets with an efficiency of 75–85%. The use of 2-propanol in the subphase is essential for achieving uniform, single-layer coverage; transfer from pure aqueous subphases results in heterogeneous, multilayered films with significant thickness variations and overlapping sheets, which are unsuitable for high-resolution imaging. The thickness of the transferred monolayer, as determined by atomic force microscopy and scanning electron microscopy, is approximately 1.3 nm, consistent with the expected thickness of a single layer of graphene oxide. Selected area electron diffraction analysis of the transferred films reveals a hexagonal diffraction pattern, confirming the preservation of the graphenic lattice structure and the absence of multilayer stacking.

Following transfer, the GO-NTA surface is subjected to a two-step passivation protocol to eliminate non-specific binding. The first step involves the covalent modification of residual reactive functional groups on the graphene oxide surface, particularly epoxide and aldehyde moieties, with 4-aminobenzoic acid (PABA). PABA is a small, aromatic carboxylic acid bearing an amino group that reacts with electrophilic sites on the graphene oxide surface through nucleophilic addition, forming stable amide or ester linkages that block the reactive centers responsible for non-specific protein adsorption. This reaction is carried out by incubating the GO-NTA-coated substrate in an aqueous solution of PABA under gentle agitation for 24 hours, allowing sufficient time for diffusion and covalent attachment. The use of PABA is critical, as alternative blocking agents such as glycine or ethanolamine fail to fully quench the reactivity of the graphene oxide surface, resulting in persistent background noise. The second step involves the non-covalent adsorption of bovine serum albumin (BSA), a large, globular protein with a high isoelectric point and a surface rich in hydrophobic patches and charged residues. BSA is applied as a dilute solution (0.1 mg/mL) to the PABA-modified surface and allowed to adsorb for five minutes, after which unbound protein is removed by rinsing with deionized water. The adsorbed BSA forms a dense, conformal monolayer that fills any remaining voids or defects in the PABA-passivated surface, creating a physical and chemical barrier that prevents non-target biomolecules from interacting with the underlying substrate. The combination of PABA and BSA is synergistic: PABA chemically deactivates the most reactive functional groups, while BSA provides a comprehensive, steric blockade against non-specific adsorption. This dual-passivation strategy is uniquely effective in complex biological matrices, as demonstrated by the near-complete absence of background signal from cellular components in lysates, even in the absence of Ni²⁺.

The resulting substrate, referred to herein as BSA-PABA-GO-NTA, is then ready for use in affinity capture experiments. Prior to sample application, the NTA groups are activated by incubation with a solution of divalent metal ions, typically nickel sulfate (NiSO₄), at a concentration of 1 mM for five minutes. The Ni²⁺ ions coordinate with the carboxylate and amine groups of the NTA chelators, forming a tetrahedral complex that presents a high-affinity binding site for polyhistidine tags. The metal-chelated substrate is then rinsed with buffer to remove unbound metal ions and immediately exposed to the biological sample, which may be a clarified cell lysate containing the histidine-tagged target protein. The histidine-tagged protein binds selectively to the Ni²⁺-NTA complexes, while non-target biomolecules are excluded by the BSA-PABA passivation layer. After a brief incubation period of two minutes, the substrate is rinsed with buffer and deionized water to remove unbound material, and then prepared for cryo-electron microscopy by blotting and plunge-freezing in liquid ethane. The entire process—from lysate application to cryofixation—can be performed directly on the grid, eliminating the need for centrifugation, filtration, chromatography, or any other purification step. The captured protein remains in its native conformation, as the substrate does not induce denaturation or aggregation, and the thinness of the graphene oxide layer ensures minimal electron scattering, resulting in high-contrast images suitable for single-particle analysis.

The invention is compatible with a variety of standard electron microscopy grid formats, including 400 mesh, 1500 mesh, and 2000 mesh copper grids, with or without Formvar or lacey carbon support films. The size of the GO-NTA sheets, which is approximately 16 μm × 16 μm, is well-matched to the dimensions of the grid holes in 1500 mesh and 2000 mesh grids, ensuring complete coverage and uniform transfer. The use of 400 mesh grids, which have larger holes (37 μm × 37 μm), is less effective due to the mismatch in size, resulting in incomplete coverage and reduced ligand density. The substrate can be stored under dry, desiccated conditions for extended periods without degradation of the NTA-metal complex or the passivation layer, allowing for batch preparation and long-term use. The reversibility of the NTA-Ni²⁺-His6 interaction enables elution of the captured protein using imidazole, allowing for downstream analysis such as mass spectrometry or functional assays. The invention is particularly advantageous for the study of low-abundance, unstable, or aggregation-prone proteins, as it minimizes sample handling and preserves structural integrity throughout the workflow.

The functionalization of graphene oxide with NTA is achieved through a multi-step synthetic procedure that begins with the oxidation of graphite to produce graphene oxide via a modified Hummers method. The resulting graphene oxide is purified and dispersed in a mixture of methanol and water to yield a stable suspension. The carboxylic acid groups on the graphene oxide surface are activated by reaction with thionyl chloride, forming reactive acyl chloride intermediates. These intermediates are then reacted with the tris-t-butyl ester of lysine NTA, a protected form of the NTA chelator, under anhydrous conditions at elevated temperature to form an ester linkage between the graphene oxide and the NTA ligand. The t-butyl protecting groups are subsequently removed by acidolysis using trifluoroacetic acid, yielding the final GO-NTA product with free carboxylate groups on the NTA moiety. The extent of functionalization is confirmed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, which shows the disappearance of the t-butyl C–H stretching vibration at 2933 cm⁻¹ and the persistence of carboxylate and amide peaks consistent with successful deprotection. The concentration of GO-NTA is quantified by UV-visible spectroscopy, with an absorption maximum at 280 nm corresponding to the π–π* transitions of the graphenic core and the n–π* transitions of the carbonyl groups. The extinction coefficient is determined empirically for each batch to ensure accurate concentration measurements.

The Langmuir-Schaefer transfer process is performed using a Kibron μTrough equipped with a syringe pump and a Wilhelmy plate for surface pressure measurement. The GO-NTA dispersion is introduced slowly into the subphase at a rate of 100 μL/min until the surface pressure reaches 15 mN/m, at which point the film is allowed to equilibrate for five minutes. The subphase is then supplemented with 2-propanol to achieve a final concentration of 20% v/v, which reduces surface tension and enhances sheet mobility. The film is slowly compressed to 15 mN/m, and a substrate is lowered vertically through the interface to effect transfer. The transfer efficiency is calculated by measuring the change in surface area before and after transfer, and values between 75% and 85% are consistently achieved. The transferred films are dried overnight in a desiccator before passivation with PABA and BSA. The PABA modification is carried out by incubating the grids in a 3 mg/mL solution of PABA in phosphate-buffered saline for 24 hours at room temperature with gentle agitation. The grids are then rinsed with deionized water and dried before BSA adsorption. The BSA solution is prepared at 0.1 mg/mL in deionized water and applied as a 10 μL drop to the grid surface, which is incubated for five minutes before three washes with 20 μL of deionized water. The grids are then stored in a desiccator until use.

The application of the substrate to biological samples is straightforward. For purified proteins, a solution of the histidine-tagged target at a concentration of 10¹⁰ to 10¹² particles/mL is applied to the Ni²⁺-activated grid and incubated for two minutes. For crude lysates, clarified supernatants from bacterial cultures expressing the target protein are applied directly without prior purification. In the case of His6-T7 bacteriophage, the lysate is generated by infecting BL21 cells with the phage and allowing replication for one hour, followed by centrifugation to remove cells. For His6-GroEL, the ASKA library strain expressing the N-terminal His6-tagged protein is grown to mid-log phase, induced with IPTG, and lysed by sonication in the presence of protease inhibitors. The clarified lysate is diluted tenfold and applied to the grid. In all cases, the capture is specific to the Ni²⁺-NTA complex, as demonstrated by the absence of binding in the absence of metal ions or in the presence of excess imidazole. The captured protein is then cryofixed using a Vitrobot device with controlled blotting and plunge-freezing in liquid ethane, and imaged on a 300 kV transmission electron microscope equipped with a direct electron detector. The resulting micrographs are processed using standard single-particle analysis software, including motion correction, contrast transfer function estimation, particle picking, and 3D reconstruction. The reconstructed density maps exhibit high resolution and structural fidelity, with features corresponding to known domains of the target protein clearly resolved. The presence of additional electron density in the central pore of the GroEL complex, attributable to the extended His6-tag and linker sequences, confirms that the protein is captured in its native state and that the substrate does not induce conformational artifacts.

The invention represents a significant departure from conventional approaches to cryo-EM sample preparation. Unlike lipid-based affinity grids, which are fragile, unstable under cryogenic conditions, and limited in ligand density, the graphene oxide-based substrate is mechanically robust, chemically stable, and capable of supporting high-density ligand presentation. Unlike polymer-coated grids, which introduce excessive thickness and heterogeneity, the monolayer graphene oxide film is atomically thin and uniform, ensuring minimal electron scattering and maximum contrast. Unlike traditional grids that require extensive purification, the BSA-PABA-GO-NTA substrate enables direct capture from crude lysates, reducing sample loss, minimizing handling time, and preserving the native state of the target. The dual-passivation strategy is uniquely effective, as neither PABA nor BSA alone is sufficient to eliminate background noise; their combination is essential for achieving the low background required for high-resolution imaging. The use of 2-propanol in the subphase is critical for achieving uniform monolayer transfer, a feature not disclosed in any prior art. The invention is broadly applicable to any histidine-tagged protein, peptide, or viral particle, and can be readily adapted for other affinity systems by replacing the NTA ligand with alternative chelators such as IDA or TAC. The substrate can also be functionalized with other recognition elements, such as antibodies or aptamers, for non-histidine-tagged targets. The invention thus provides a universal platform for streamlined, high-fidelity structural analysis of biological macromolecules directly from complex biological samples.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The synthesis of graphene oxide functionalized with nitrilotriacetic acid (GO-NTA) was initiated by the preparation of graphene oxide via a modified Hummers method, wherein graphite powder was oxidized using a mixture of sodium nitrate, potassium permanganate, and concentrated sulfuric acid under controlled temperature conditions. The resulting graphite oxide was washed extensively with deionized water and hydrochloric acid to remove residual salts and metal impurities, followed by dialysis against deionized water until a neutral pH was achieved. The purified graphite oxide was then subjected to sonication in deionized water for 30 minutes to exfoliate the stacked layers into individual graphene oxide sheets, resulting in a stable, dark brown dispersion. The concentration of the graphene oxide solution was determined by UV-visible spectroscopy, with an absorption maximum at 230 nm corresponding to the π–π* transitions of the aromatic core. The graphene oxide dispersion was then concentrated by centrifugation and re-dispersed in anhydrous dimethylformamide (DMF) to facilitate subsequent chemical reactions. To activate the carboxylic acid groups on the graphene oxide periphery, the dispersion was treated with thionyl chloride in the presence of catalytic DMF at 70°C for three days under an inert nitrogen atmosphere. The reaction mixture was then evaporated under reduced pressure to remove excess thionyl chloride and residual DMF, leaving behind a solid residue of acyl chloride-functionalized graphene oxide. This intermediate was washed three times with dry dichloromethane to remove any unreacted reagents and dried under vacuum at 60°C for 24 hours. The acyl chloride-activated graphene oxide was then suspended in acetonitrile and treated with triethylamine to neutralize any residual acid. Subsequently, the tris-t-butyl ester of lysine NTA was added in a molar excess of 1.5 equivalents relative to the estimated carboxylic acid content of the graphene oxide. The reaction mixture was heated to 100°C and stirred for three days under nitrogen, allowing for esterification between the activated carboxyl groups and the hydroxyl termini of the NTA ester. The resulting product, GO-NTA-(O-t-Bu)₃, was recovered by centrifugation at 9,000 rpm for 15 minutes and washed three times with tetrahydrofuran and three times with deionized water to remove unreacted reagents and byproducts. The washed product was dried under vacuum at 60°C for 24 hours to yield a dry, powdery solid. The t-butyl protecting groups on the NTA moieties were then removed by dissolving the solid in a mixture of trifluoroacetic acid and tetrahydrofuran (1:3 v/v) and stirring at 60°C for five hours. The deprotected product, GO-NTA, was recovered by centrifugation at 11,000 rpm for 15 minutes and washed three times with tetrahydrofuran and three times with deionized water. The final product was dried under vacuum at 60°C for 24 hours and stored in a desiccator until further use. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy confirmed the successful functionalization, with the disappearance of the aliphatic C–H stretch at 2933 cm⁻¹ and the persistence of carboxylate and amide vibrations at 1720 cm⁻¹ and 1650 cm⁻¹, respectively. The degree of functionalization was estimated to be approximately one NTA group per 15 carbon atoms on the graphene oxide periphery, as determined by elemental analysis and quantitative titration of free carboxyl groups.

### Example 2

The exfoliation and dispersion of GO-NTA for Langmuir-Schaefer transfer was performed by suspending 1 mg of the dried GO-NTA powder in 1 mL of a 5:1 mixture of methanol and deionized water. The suspension was subjected to probe sonication using a 150 W ultrasonic probe for five cycles, each consisting of 45 seconds of sonication followed by 45 seconds of rest to prevent overheating. After each sonication cycle, the suspension was centrifuged at 1,200 g for 10 minutes to pellet any aggregated or unexfoliated material. The supernatant, containing the exfoliated GO-NTA sheets, was carefully collected and subjected to an additional five rounds of sonication and centrifugation to maximize the yield of single-layer material. The final supernatant, now containing a stable dispersion of exfoliated GO-NTA sheets at a concentration of approximately 1 mg/mL, was stored at 4°C and used within 48 hours for Langmuir-Schaefer transfer. The concentration of the dispersion was verified by UV-visible spectroscopy, with an absorption maximum at 280 nm corresponding to the π–π* transitions of the graphenic core and the n–π* transitions of the carbonyl groups. The extinction coefficient for this batch was determined empirically by preparing a series of dilutions in 5:1 MeOH:H₂O and measuring absorbance at 280 nm. The slope of the linear regression of absorbance versus concentration yielded an extinction coefficient of 1.2 × 10⁵ M⁻¹cm⁻¹. The dispersion was found to remain stable for up to 72 hours at 4°C without significant aggregation, as confirmed by dynamic light scattering and atomic force microscopy. The size distribution of the sheets, as determined by transmission electron microscopy, ranged from 5 to 25 μm in lateral dimension, with a median size of 16 μm × 16 μm, consistent with the dimensions of the graphene oxide sheets produced by the modified Hummers method. The stability of the dispersion was critical for achieving uniform Langmuir-Schaefer transfer, as aggregation or sedimentation resulted in non-uniform film formation and reduced transfer efficiency.

### Example 3

Langmuir-Schaefer transfer of the GO-NTA monolayer was performed using a Kibron μTrough equipped with a Wilhelmy plate for surface pressure measurement and a syringe pump for controlled deposition. The trough was filled with 200 mL of deionized water, and the surface pressure was monitored until a stable baseline of 0.5 mN/m was achieved. The exfoliated GO-NTA dispersion (1 mL) was loaded into a 20 mL syringe fitted with a 20-gauge needle and slowly introduced at the air-water interface at a rate of 100 μL/min. As the GO-NTA sheets spread at the interface, the surface pressure increased gradually, reaching 15 mN/m after approximately 15 minutes of deposition. The film was then allowed to relax for five minutes to permit reorganization of the sheets into a more ordered arrangement. The subphase was then supplemented with 2-propanol to achieve a final concentration of 20% v/v, which reduced the surface tension from 72 mN/m to approximately 55 mN/m. The film was then slowly compressed using the movable barriers of the trough until the surface pressure returned to 15 mN/m, at which point the film was deemed ready for transfer. A silicon wafer, cut to dimensions of 2.25 cm² and glued to a transfer tube, was vertically lowered through the air-water interface until it made contact with the compressed monolayer. The wafer was then slowly raised, carrying with it a monolayer of GO-NTA sheets. The transfer efficiency was calculated by measuring the change in surface area before and after transfer, yielding values between 75% and 85%. The transferred film was allowed to dry overnight in a desiccator at room temperature. Atomic force microscopy of the dried film revealed a uniform, continuous coating with a thickness of 1.3 ± 0.1 nm, consistent with a single layer of graphene oxide. Scanning electron microscopy confirmed the lateral continuity of the film, with no evidence of multilayer stacking or aggregation. The hexagonal diffraction pattern observed in selected area electron diffraction confirmed the preservation of the graphenic lattice structure. Transfer was also successfully performed onto 1500 mesh copper grids, both with and without Formvar coating, demonstrating the versatility of the method across different EM grid types. Transfer onto 400 mesh grids, however, resulted in incomplete coverage due to the mismatch between the average sheet size and the grid hole dimensions.

### Example 4

The surface passivation of the GO-NTA monolayer was performed in two sequential steps. First, the dried GO-NTA-coated grids were immersed in a 3 mg/mL aqueous solution of 4-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) in phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4) and incubated for 24 hours at room temperature with gentle agitation on a rotary mixer. The PABA solution was prepared by dissolving 30 mg of PABA in 10 mL of buffer, and the grids were submerged in 1 mL of this solution per grid. After incubation, the grids were removed and rinsed three times with 20 μL of deionized water to remove unbound PABA. The PABA-modified grids were then dried overnight in a desiccator. The effectiveness of the PABA modification was confirmed by fluorescence microscopy using a fluorescein-labeled analog of PABA, which showed strong covalent binding to the GO-NTA surface, indicating successful reaction with electrophilic sites. The second passivation step involved the adsorption of bovine serum albumin (BSA). The PABA-modified grids were placed on a Teflon strip and 10 μL of a 0.1 mg/mL BSA solution in deionized water was applied to the surface. The grids were incubated for five minutes at room temperature to allow for non-covalent adsorption of BSA. Unbound BSA was then removed by washing the grids three times with 20 μL of deionized water. The grids were dried overnight in a desiccator and stored until use. Control experiments were performed using grids treated with PABA alone, BSA alone, or neither agent. Grids treated with PABA alone exhibited residual non-specific binding of non-target proteins in lysates, as evidenced by background staining in negative-stain TEM. Grids treated with BSA alone showed even greater background, as the reactive epoxide and aldehyde groups on the graphene oxide surface promoted covalent attachment of lysine-rich proteins. Grids treated with neither agent showed complete non-specific adsorption of all cellular components. Only the dual-passivated BSA-PABA-GO-NTA grids exhibited near-complete suppression of background noise, with clear, isolated binding of the target protein and minimal contamination from non-target material.

### Example 5

The activation of the NTA chelators and capture of His6-T7 bacteriophage was performed on BSA-PABA-GO-NTA-coated 1500 mesh copper grids. The grids were placed on a Teflon strip and 10 μL of a 1 mM solution of NiSO₄ in HEPES buffer (pH 7.4) was applied to the surface. The grids were incubated for five minutes at room temperature to allow for coordination of Ni²⁺ ions with the NTA ligands. The grids were then washed twice with 20 μL of deionized water and once with 20 μL of HEPES buffer to remove unbound metal ions. Purified His6-T7 bacteriophage, prepared at a concentration of 10¹⁰ particles/mL in HEPES buffer, was applied to the grid surface in a volume of 3.5 μL and incubated for two minutes. The grid was then washed twice with 20 μL of HEPES buffer and once with 20 μL of deionized water before staining with 5 μL of 2% uranyl acetate. Negative-stain transmission electron microscopy revealed dense clusters of phage particles bound specifically to the grid surface, with no visible background staining. Control grids treated with NiSO₄ but without BSA-PABA passivation showed extensive non-specific adsorption of contaminating material, obscuring the phage signal. Grids treated with BSA-PABA-GO-NTA but without Ni²⁺ activation showed no phage binding, confirming the specificity of the NTA-Ni²⁺-His6 interaction. Grids treated with 500 mM imidazole after Ni²⁺ activation showed complete elution of the phage particles, demonstrating the reversibility of the binding. The number of bound phage particles per unit area was quantified by manual counting in multiple micrographs, yielding an average of 120 ± 15 particles per square micrometer, indicating high ligand density and efficient capture.

### Example 6

The capture of His6-T7 bacteriophage directly from clarified E. coli lysate was performed using BSA-PABA-GO-NTA-coated grids. BL21 bacterial cells were grown in LB medium to an optical density of 0.8 at 600 nm, and 1 μL of a stock solution of His6-T7 bacteriophage (1 × 10¹² particles/mL) was added to induce infection. The culture was shaken for one hour at 37°C to allow for viral replication and lysis of host cells. The culture was then centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 minutes to pellet cellular debris, and the supernatant was collected as clarified lysate. The BSA-PABA-GO-NTA-coated grids were activated with 1 mM NiSO₄ as described in Example 5. Five microliters of the clarified lysate was applied directly to the grid surface and incubated for two minutes. The grid was then washed twice with 20 μL of HEPES buffer and once with 20 μL of deionized water before staining with 5 μL of 2% uranyl acetate. Negative-stain TEM revealed a high density of phage particles bound to the grid surface, with minimal background staining from cellular components. In contrast, control grids treated with BSA-PABA-GO-NTA but without Ni²⁺ activation showed no phage binding and only trace amounts of non-specific material. Grids treated with Ni²⁺ but without BSA-PABA passivation showed extensive non-specific binding of cellular proteins and nucleic acids, rendering the images unusable. The specificity of the capture was further confirmed by treating the grid with 500 mM imidazole after Ni²⁺ activation but before lysate application, which completely abolished phage binding, demonstrating that the interaction was dependent on the presence of the Ni²⁺-NTA complex. The results demonstrated that the BSA-PABA-GO-NTA substrate enables direct, selective capture of histidine-tagged targets from crude lysates without prior purification.

### Example 7

The capture of His6-GroEL from E. coli lysate was performed using BSA-PABA-GO-NTA-coated grids. The ASKA library strain expressing N-terminal His6-GroEL was grown in LB medium to an optical density of 0.6 at 600 nm, and protein expression was induced with 1 mM IPTG. The culture was incubated for an additional four hours at 37°C, after which cells were harvested by centrifugation. The cell pellet was resuspended in lysis buffer containing 20 mM HEPES, 100 mM NaCl, pH 7.4, 100 μg/mL aprotinin, 174 μg/mL phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride, and 500 μg/mL lysozyme, and allowed to stand for 20 minutes. Cell lysis was completed by probe sonication using 110 W for 35 pulses of one second each, followed by centrifugation at 11,000 g for 10 minutes to remove cellular debris. The clarified supernatant was diluted tenfold in HEPES buffer to reduce viscosity and background noise. BSA-PABA-GO-NTA-coated 400 mesh lacey carbon copper grids were activated with 1 mM NiSO₄ and exposed to 5 μL of the diluted lysate for two minutes. The grids were washed twice with 20 μL of HEPES buffer and once with 20 μL of deionized water. For negative-stain TEM, grids were stained with 5 μL of 2% uranyl acetate, revealing a high density of GroEL complexes bound specifically to the grid surface, with minimal background staining. For cryo-electron microscopy, grids were prepared using a Vitrobot device with controlled blotting (two blots of six seconds each at 80% humidity and an offset setting of −1) and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane. High-resolution micrographs were acquired on a 300 kV Titan Krios microscope equipped with a Gatan K2 Summit direct electron detector, using low-dose conditions and an exposure time of 7.6 seconds. The resulting micrographs showed well-preserved, individual GroEL complexes with high contrast and minimal ice contamination, demonstrating the suitability of the substrate for cryo-EM.

### Example 8

Single-particle analysis of His6-GroEL captured on BSA-PABA-GO-NTA-coated grids was performed using EMAN 2.1 software. A total of 217 micrographs were acquired at 11,000x magnification, corresponding to a pixel size of 1.32 Å per pixel. Motion correction and contrast transfer function estimation were performed using Appion software, with defocus values ranging from 0.4 to 4.0 μm, and 55% of particles falling within the optimal defocus range of 2.0 to 3.0 μm. A total of 5,363 particles were manually picked from the micrographs using a box size of 256 pixels. The particles were binned two-fold for class averaging to improve signal-to-noise ratio. Twelve class averages were selected to generate an initial 3D model with imposed D7 symmetry, reflecting the heptameric ring structure of the GroEL complex. Two independent refinements were performed using even and odd halves of the particle dataset, following the gold standard protocol. After 12 iterations of refinement with an angular sampling of 1.76 degrees, a final 3D reconstruction was obtained with a resolution of 8.1 Å, as determined by the 0.143 Fourier shell correlation criterion. The reconstructed density map showed clear secondary structural features, including α-helices and β-sheets, and resolved the central pore of the GroEL complex. Comparison with the published EMD-5001 map of wild-type GroEL at 4.2 Å resolution, performed using Chimera software, yielded a cross-correlation resolution of 9 Å using the 0.5 criterion, confirming the structural fidelity of the reconstruction. Notably, the density map revealed additional electron density within the central pore, attributable to the extended N-terminal and C-terminal His6-tag and linker sequences (MRGSHHHHHHTDPALRA and GLCGR, respectively) of the engineered GroEL construct. This density appeared as a continuous, amorphous “droplet” filling the pore, consistent with the high flexibility and conformational heterogeneity of the unstructured tag sequences. The presence of this density confirmed that the protein was captured in its native state and that the BSA-PABA-GO-NTA substrate did not induce structural perturbations. The reduced density in the apical regions suggested increased flexibility in these domains, likely due to the combined effects of the His6-tag and NTA chelation.

### Example 9

The performance of the BSA-PABA-GO-NTA substrate was compared across multiple histidine-tagged targets and sample types. In addition to His6-T7 bacteriophage and His6-GroEL, the substrate was tested with His6-ubiquitin, His6-lysozyme, and His6-β-galactosidase, all expressed in E. coli and purified from clarified lysates. In each case, the substrate enabled high-specificity capture with minimal background, as confirmed by negative-stain TEM and cryo-EM. The capture efficiency, defined as the percentage of target protein bound relative to the total input, ranged from 78% to 89% across all targets, with no significant difference observed between viral, enzymatic, and structural proteins. The substrate was also tested with lysates from mammalian cell lines, including HEK293 and CHO cells, expressing His6-tagged membrane proteins. In these more complex matrices, the BSA-PABA-GO-NTA substrate still outperformed conventional lipid-based affinity grids, which exhibited high background due to lipid and detergent interference. The substrate was found to be compatible with detergent-containing buffers, maintaining functionality in the presence of 0.1% Triton X-100 and 0.05% SDS. The stability of the NTA-Ni²⁺ complex was tested after storage for up to six months under dry conditions, and no loss of binding capacity was observed. The substrate was also compatible with downstream mass spectrometry, as eluted proteins retained their integrity and were amenable to tryptic digestion and peptide sequencing. The invention thus demonstrates broad applicability across diverse protein targets, sample matrices, and experimental conditions, establishing it as a versatile, robust platform for on-grid affinity capture and high-resolution structural analysis.
