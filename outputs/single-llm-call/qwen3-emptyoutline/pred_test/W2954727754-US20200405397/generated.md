# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE DISCLOSURE

Interventional cardiology has undergone a profound transformation over the past several decades, evolving from open surgical approaches to minimally invasive transcatheter procedures that are now the standard of care for a broad spectrum of structural heart diseases. These include the closure of atrial septal defects and patent foramen ovale, percutaneous valve repair and replacement, left atrial appendage occlusion, and the deployment of hemodynamic monitoring devices. The clinical adoption of these procedures has been driven by their reduced morbidity, shorter hospital stays, and improved patient outcomes compared to traditional surgical interventions. However, the complexity of navigating catheters and devices within the dynamic, three-dimensional anatomy of the heart presents a persistent challenge for interventionalists. The current standard of imaging guidance relies predominantly on X-ray fluoroscopy, which provides real-time, two-dimensional projection images of radiopaque markers on catheters and devices. While fluoroscopy enables the visualization of device movement and approximate anatomical landmarks, it offers no direct visualization of soft tissue structures such as the myocardium, endocardium, or cardiac valves. As a result, clinicians must rely on indirect cues, such as the silhouette of the heart, the position of bony structures, and the transient opacification provided by iodinated contrast agents, to infer the spatial relationship between the catheter and the target anatomy. This reliance on indirect visualization introduces significant cognitive load, increases procedural time, and elevates the risk of complications, particularly in procedures requiring precise intracardiac navigation such as transseptal puncture or mitral valve repair.

To supplement fluoroscopic guidance, echocardiography—particularly transesophageal echocardiography (TEE)—has been widely adopted as a complementary modality. TEE provides high-resolution, real-time imaging of cardiac structures and blood flow dynamics, enabling direct visualization of the interventional target and immediate feedback on device placement. However, its utility is constrained by several critical limitations. First, TEE requires a highly skilled operator with specialized training in both echocardiography and interventional cardiology, a resource that is not universally available across clinical settings. Second, the physical presence of the transesophageal probe can interfere with procedural workflow, limit access to the femoral venous access site, and introduce additional risks such as esophageal injury or airway compromise. Third, while TEE can provide two-dimensional and, in some cases, three-dimensional reconstructions, these images are still displayed on two-dimensional monitors, requiring the operator to mentally integrate multiple planes and perspectives to form a coherent spatial understanding of the intracardiac environment. Furthermore, the integration of TEE with fluoroscopy necessitates the simultaneous use of multiple imaging systems, each with its own display, controls, and calibration, leading to fragmented attention and increased procedural complexity.

Preoperative imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance (MR) imaging have become integral to procedural planning, offering detailed, high-fidelity three-dimensional reconstructions of cardiac anatomy. These images can be segmented to isolate the heart, great vessels, and surrounding structures, and can be rendered into virtual models that allow for pre-procedural simulation and path planning. In some advanced centers, these preoperative models are overlaid onto real-time fluoroscopic or echocardiographic images via software platforms designed for image fusion. However, these hybrid visualization systems remain fundamentally limited by their two-dimensional display medium. The overlay of a static 3D model onto a live 2D fluoroscopic image does not confer true three-dimensional depth perception. Instead, the clinician must mentally reconstruct the spatial relationships between the overlaid model and the real-time image, a process that is cognitively taxing and prone to error. Moreover, the superimposition of dense anatomical renderings often obscures critical details in the live fluoroscopic image, such as subtle catheter movement, contrast flow patterns, or the presence of thrombus or calcification. The resulting visual clutter can impede rather than enhance decision-making during time-sensitive interventions.

The challenge of aligning anatomical models derived from preoperative imaging with real-time intraoperative imaging has been the subject of extensive research in the field of medical image registration. Numerous algorithms have been developed to register images across different modalities, including intensity-based methods that maximize mutual information, gradient-based methods that exploit edge and texture consistency, and feature-based methods that rely on the detection of distinctive anatomical landmarks such as vessel bifurcations or bone contours. While intensity-based methods have been successfully applied to register CT and MR scans, they are inherently limited in their ability to align images from dissimilar modalities such as CT and fluoroscopy, where pixel intensities vary dramatically due to differences in energy spectra, contrast agents, and detector sensitivities. Gradient-based methods offer improved robustness by focusing on structural transitions rather than absolute pixel values, yet they remain sensitive to noise and partial volume effects, particularly in low-contrast fluoroscopic images where the heart itself is nearly invisible. Feature-based methods, such as SIFT, SURF, and BRIEF, have demonstrated broad applicability in non-medical domains and have been adapted for medical image registration with varying degrees of success. However, their performance in cardiac imaging is severely compromised by the lack of consistent, high-contrast anatomical features in fluoroscopic images. The heart, being composed of soft tissue with minimal inherent X-ray attenuation, appears as a faint, indistinct silhouette, while the surrounding bony structures such as the ribs and spine are often the only reliable anatomical landmarks. Even these landmarks are frequently obscured by overlapping anatomy, motion artifacts, or suboptimal patient positioning. Consequently, feature-based methods often fail to detect sufficient numbers of reliable correspondences between preoperative CT and intraoperative fluoroscopic images, leading to registration failures or inaccurate transformations.

The emergence of augmented reality (AR) technologies has introduced a paradigm shift in medical visualization, offering the potential to bridge the gap between preoperative planning and intraoperative guidance by projecting three-dimensional anatomical models directly into the clinician’s field of view. AR systems such as OpenSight have been cleared by the FDA for use in neurosurgical and orthopedic procedures, where they enable the overlay of preoperative imaging data onto the surgical field through see-through head-mounted displays. These systems have demonstrated improvements in surgical precision, reduced operative time, and enhanced spatial orientation for the surgical team. However, the application of AR to cardiac interventions has remained elusive due to a combination of technical and clinical barriers. Existing AR platforms are not designed to handle the unique demands of intracardiac procedures, which require real-time, high-precision tracking of catheters moving within a dynamically changing anatomical environment. Unlike orthopedic or neurosurgical procedures, where the target anatomy is relatively static, the heart is in constant motion due to cardiac contraction, respiratory displacement, and hemodynamic changes. Furthermore, the spatial scale of cardiac interventions is orders of magnitude smaller than that of open surgical procedures, demanding sub-millimeter accuracy in both image registration and rendering. Current AR systems lack the capability to register fluoroscopic images with high-fidelity CT models in real time, nor do they provide the necessary computational infrastructure to track catheter position from multiple fluoroscopic angles and render the resulting three-dimensional trajectory as a stable, persistent hologram within the anatomical context. The absence of a unified framework that integrates real-time imaging, automated registration, and immersive visualization has prevented the translation of AR from a promising concept into a clinically viable tool for transcatheter interventions.

The limitations of existing technologies have created a critical gap in the field of interventional cardiology: the inability to provide clinicians with an intuitive, three-dimensional, real-time visualization of catheter position relative to the patient’s unique cardiac anatomy. Without such a system, interventionalists are forced to rely on fragmented, two-dimensional visual cues, mental extrapolation, and experience-based estimation to navigate complex intracardiac pathways. This approach is not only error-prone but also contributes to prolonged radiation exposure, increased contrast load, and higher rates of procedural complications. The development of a novel system capable of seamlessly integrating preoperative three-dimensional anatomical models with real-time fluoroscopic tracking, registering these modalities through a robust and automated algorithm, and rendering the resulting data as a persistent, spatially accurate hologram within the clinician’s natural field of view represents a transformative advancement. Such a system would eliminate the cognitive burden of mental image integration, reduce procedural time and radiation exposure, improve accuracy in device placement, and ultimately enhance patient safety. The proposed invention fulfills this unmet clinical need by introducing a comprehensive augmented reality-guided intervention system that leverages the spine as a universal fiduciary marker to achieve sub-millimeter registration accuracy between CT-derived cardiac models and fluoroscopic images, thereby enabling the real-time, three-dimensional holographic visualization of catheter position within the patient’s own anatomy.

## SUMMARY OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present disclosure describes a novel augmented reality-based image guidance system for transcatheter cardiac interventions that enables real-time, three-dimensional holographic visualization of catheter position relative to a patient’s unique cardiac anatomy. The system integrates preoperative computed tomography (CT) imaging, intraoperative fluoroscopic imaging, automated image registration, and wearable augmented reality display technology to provide an intuitive, immersive, and quantitatively accurate visualization environment for interventional cardiologists. The core innovation lies in the use of the patient’s spinal anatomy as a universal fiduciary marker to establish a precise, rigid transformation between the three-dimensional cardiac model derived from preoperative CT scans and the two-dimensional fluoroscopic images acquired during the procedure. This registration method, which employs a Fourier-Mellin transform-based algorithm to decouple and resolve scale, rotation, and translation parameters between the two image modalities, achieves sub-millimeter accuracy without requiring manual landmark identification or contrast-enhanced fiducials. Once registered, the system automatically detects the position and orientation of the catheter from fluoroscopic images captured at two distinct angles, computes its three-dimensional trajectory in space using triangulation principles, and renders this trajectory as a persistent, photorealistic hologram superimposed onto the patient’s segmented cardiac anatomy within a see-through augmented reality headset. The holographic visualization is displayed in real time, allowing the clinician to perceive the catheter’s position, depth, and orientation relative to the heart’s internal structures without diverting gaze from the surgical field. The system further enhances usability through voice-activated commands and hand-gesture recognition, enabling the operator to manipulate the 3D models, toggle between anatomical views, and activate cross-sectional剖面 renderings without interrupting procedural flow. The disclosed system is designed to operate seamlessly within existing catheterization laboratory workflows, requiring no modification to standard fluoroscopy equipment or procedural protocols. It is compatible with standard 12-French catheters and other radiopaque devices commonly used in transcatheter interventions. The system’s architecture is modular, comprising a server-based image processing unit that performs segmentation, registration, and 3D reconstruction tasks, and a wireless augmented reality headset that receives processed data via a secure local network and renders the holographic visualization. The invention eliminates the need for manual image fusion, reduces cognitive load, and provides an unprecedented level of spatial awareness during intracardiac navigation. By enabling clinicians to visualize the catheter’s position in true three-dimensional space relative to the patient’s own anatomy, the system significantly improves procedural precision, reduces radiation and contrast exposure, shortens procedure duration, and lowers the learning curve for complex interventions. The system is applicable to a wide range of transcatheter procedures, including transseptal puncture, atrial septal defect closure, left atrial appendage occlusion, and mitral valve repair, and may be extended to other anatomical regions where rigid fiduciary markers such as the spine or vertebral column are present. The disclosed invention represents a fundamental advancement in image-guided intervention, transitioning from fragmented, two-dimensional visual cues to a unified, immersive, and quantitatively accurate three-dimensional visualization paradigm that enhances both clinical outcomes and procedural efficiency.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention comprises a comprehensive augmented reality-guided system for transcatheter cardiac interventions, designed to provide real-time, three-dimensional holographic visualization of catheter position relative to a patient’s unique cardiac anatomy. The system is composed of three primary functional components: a preoperative imaging and segmentation module, an intraoperative image registration and tracking module, and an augmented reality visualization and interaction module. Each component operates in a coordinated, sequential manner to transform static, preoperative three-dimensional anatomical data into a dynamic, real-time, spatially accurate holographic representation that is seamlessly integrated into the clinician’s field of view during the procedure. The system is designed to operate independently of any proprietary imaging hardware, relying instead on standard clinical equipment such as a fluoroscopy C-arm system, a computed tomography scanner, and a wireless augmented reality headset, thereby ensuring broad clinical compatibility and ease of adoption.

The preoperative imaging and segmentation module begins with the acquisition of high-resolution diagnostic computed tomography (CT) scans of the patient’s thoracic region. These scans are obtained prior to the intervention using standard clinical protocols, with contrast enhancement applied as necessary to delineate vascular structures. The CT data, stored in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format, are imported into a medical image processing workstation where the three-dimensional anatomy of the heart and the vertebral column are segmented using semi-automated thresholding and region-growing algorithms. The segmentation process involves the manual selection of appropriate Hounsfield unit thresholds to isolate the cardiac chambers, great vessels, and bony structures, followed by the application of morphological operations to remove isolated noise, fill internal voids, and smooth surface irregularities. The segmented heart and spine models are then imported into a computer-aided design (CAD) software environment, where non-essential anatomical structures such as the ribs, sternum, and peripheral vasculature are selectively removed to reduce visual clutter and optimize rendering performance. For procedures requiring precise intracardiac navigation, such as transseptal puncture, a virtual procedural path is generated by experienced interventionalists and superimposed onto the 3D model. This path is defined by a series of waypoints and target zones, such as concentric rings representing the ideal puncture site on the interatrial septum. The final 3D models of the heart and spine are exported in a standard stereolithography (STL) file format and are stored on a secure server for subsequent use during the procedure. The segmentation process is designed to be robust to variations in image quality, patient size, and contrast enhancement, ensuring consistent performance across diverse clinical populations.

The intraoperative image registration and tracking module is responsible for establishing a precise spatial correspondence between the preoperative 3D cardiac model and the real-time fluoroscopic images acquired during the procedure. This registration is achieved by leveraging the patient’s vertebral column as a universal fiduciary marker. The spine is selected due to its inherent rigidity, minimal motion during respiration and cardiac cycle, and consistent radiopacity across both CT and fluoroscopic imaging modalities. To ensure alignment between the preoperative and intraoperative imaging sessions, the patient is positioned in the supine position for both the CT scan and the fluoroscopic procedure, minimizing variability in spinal orientation. During the intervention, fluoroscopic images are acquired from two distinct angular projections, typically corresponding to the anterior-posterior (AP) and right anterior oblique (RAO30) views, using a standard C-arm fluoroscopy system. The metadata associated with each fluoroscopic image, including the angular orientation of the C-arm, is automatically recorded and transmitted to the registration server. The registration process begins with the generation of a two-dimensional projection image of the segmented 3D spine model from the preoperative CT scan, using the same angular orientation as the corresponding fluoroscopic image. This projection image serves as the fixed reference, while the actual fluoroscopic image of the spine serves as the moving image. The two images are then processed using a Fourier-Mellin transform-based algorithm that decouples the scale, rotation, and translation parameters between them. First, both images undergo a two-dimensional fast Fourier transform to convert them into the frequency domain. The magnitude spectra of the transformed images are then mapped onto a polar-logarithmic coordinate system, which transforms rotational and scaling differences into translational shifts along the angular and radial axes, respectively. A second Fourier transform is applied to the polar-logarithmic representations, resulting in a phase correlation map where the peak location corresponds to the rotational angle and scaling factor between the two images. Once the scale and rotation are determined, the translation parameters are computed using a standard phase correlation method on the original frequency domain images. The resulting transformation matrix, which includes scale, rotation, and translation components, is applied to the entire 3D cardiac model, effectively aligning it with the coordinate system of the fluoroscopic images. This registration method achieves sub-millimeter accuracy, as validated through quantitative evaluation against ground-truth fiducial markers, and demonstrates a 100% success rate across multiple angular projections, outperforming conventional feature-based methods such as SIFT and SURF in both accuracy and computational efficiency. The registration is performed in near real time, with an average processing latency of less than 150 milliseconds, ensuring that the system remains responsive to procedural changes.

The catheter tracking component of the intraoperative module operates concurrently with the registration process. A standard radiopaque catheter, such as a 12-French transseptal sheath, is inserted into the patient and positioned under fluoroscopic guidance. The catheter’s position is detected from the fluoroscopic images using a pixel-intensity-based edge detection algorithm. The algorithm begins by identifying the distal end of the catheter, typically located near the inferior vena cava or right atrium, by analyzing the bottom rows of the fluoroscopic image for regions of high X-ray attenuation. A region of interest is then extracted around this initial detection, and a series of adaptive thresholding and derivative-based edge detection steps are applied to identify the lateral boundaries of the catheter. The centerline of the catheter is computed as the midpoint between the two detected edges, and this process is iteratively repeated along the length of the catheter, advancing the region of interest upward in a stepwise fashion to trace the full trajectory of the device. The resulting centerline is represented as a sequence of three-dimensional points, each corresponding to a cross-sectional position along the catheter. Because the catheter is detected from two distinct fluoroscopic projections, the three-dimensional position of each point is calculated using triangulation principles. The known angular separation between the two fluoroscopic views, as recorded in the C-arm metadata, is used to solve a geometric transformation that relates the two two-dimensional projections to a single three-dimensional coordinate in space. This calculation yields the precise x, y, and z coordinates of each point along the catheter’s trajectory, with a mean localization error of less than 0.3 millimeters, as demonstrated in phantom validation studies. The three-dimensional catheter trajectory is then transmitted to the augmented reality visualization module, where it is rendered as a persistent holographic object.

The augmented reality visualization and interaction module is implemented using a see-through head-mounted display device, such as Microsoft HoloLens, which overlays the registered 3D cardiac model and the tracked catheter trajectory onto the clinician’s natural field of view. The 3D models of the heart and spine, previously segmented and registered, are imported into the augmented reality environment and scaled to match the patient’s actual anatomical dimensions based on the registration transformation. The catheter trajectory is rendered as a semi-transparent, blue virtual catheter that follows the computed three-dimensional path, with key points along the trajectory represented as small, red spherical markers to indicate discrete positions. The holographic visualization is anchored to the patient’s physical position in the room, ensuring that the 3D model remains fixed relative to the patient’s body regardless of the clinician’s movement. To enhance spatial understanding, a virtual camera is rendered at the distal tip of the catheter, with its orientation aligned to the catheter’s axial direction. This virtual camera provides a first-person perspective of the intracardiac environment, allowing the clinician to visualize the anatomy immediately ahead of the catheter tip as if viewing through the device itself. The system is further enhanced by voice-activated controls and hand-gesture recognition. The clinician may issue voice commands such as “open” to generate a cross-sectional slice through the heart at the current catheter position, revealing internal structures such as the atrial septum, pulmonary veins, or mitral valve apparatus. Additional voice commands allow the operator to switch between standard fluoroscopic views—such as AP, LAO30, and RAO30—each of which triggers a corresponding reorientation of the holographic model to match the perspective of the selected projection. Hand gestures, detected through the headset’s built-in depth sensors, enable the operator to grasp, rotate, or translate the 3D models with intuitive finger movements, facilitating optimal viewing angles without requiring interaction with external control interfaces. The system is designed to operate wirelessly, with all image processing performed on a remote server connected to the headset via a secure local area network. This architecture ensures that the headset remains lightweight and unobtrusive, while the computational demands of segmentation, registration, and rendering are handled by high-performance hardware located outside the sterile field. The entire system is powered by a standard hospital Wi-Fi network and is compatible with existing hospital information systems, allowing for seamless integration of patient data, procedural logs, and imaging archives.

The system is designed to function within the constraints of a standard catheterization laboratory, requiring no modification to existing fluoroscopy equipment, patient positioning protocols, or procedural workflows. The only additional hardware required is the augmented reality headset and a server computer capable of running the registration and rendering algorithms. The system is fully compatible with standard 12-French and smaller catheters, as well as other radiopaque devices such as guidewires, sheaths, and occlusion devices, provided they contain sufficient radiopacity to be reliably detected by the fluoroscopic imaging system. The system does not require the use of additional contrast agents, fiducial markers, or electromagnetic tracking systems, thereby preserving the safety and simplicity of conventional transcatheter procedures. The registration algorithm is robust to minor variations in patient positioning, respiratory motion, and image noise, and has been validated on both phantom models and anonymized clinical datasets. The system’s performance has been quantified in controlled experiments, demonstrating sub-millimeter registration accuracy, millimeter-level catheter localization precision, and real-time rendering latency below 200 milliseconds. These performance metrics are sufficient to support the precise navigation required for complex intracardiac interventions, where deviations of even a few millimeters can lead to complications such as pericardial effusion, atrial perforation, or incomplete device deployment.

The system’s utility extends beyond real-time procedural guidance. The same platform can be used for preoperative planning, allowing clinicians to simulate the procedure in a virtual environment using the patient’s own anatomical model. The virtual path and target zones can be adjusted, tested, and refined prior to the actual intervention, reducing the likelihood of intraoperative surprises. Furthermore, the system serves as a powerful educational tool for training fellows and residents, enabling them to observe the relationship between fluoroscopic projections and three-dimensional anatomy in a way that is impossible with traditional two-dimensional monitors. The system can record and replay procedures, allowing for post-procedural review, performance assessment, and quality improvement initiatives. The holographic visualization can be shared across multiple headsets, enabling collaborative decision-making and remote consultation. The system’s modular architecture allows for future expansion to include real-time cardiac motion modeling, such as gating the 3D model to electrocardiographic signals or integrating real-time echocardiographic data to account for respiratory and contractile dynamics. These enhancements will further improve the system’s accuracy and clinical utility, particularly in procedures involving dynamic anatomical structures such as the mitral valve or left atrial appendage.

The invention represents a fundamental departure from conventional image-guided intervention paradigms. Rather than requiring the clinician to mentally integrate disparate two-dimensional images from multiple modalities, the system provides a unified, immersive, and spatially accurate three-dimensional representation of the catheter and anatomy that is directly perceptible within the natural field of view. This transformation from abstract, cognitive reconstruction to direct, intuitive perception significantly reduces cognitive load, minimizes procedural errors, and enhances spatial awareness. The use of the spine as a universal fiduciary marker eliminates the need for artificial contrast agents or implanted fiducials, preserving patient safety and procedural simplicity. The Fourier-Mellin registration algorithm ensures robust, automated alignment across diverse imaging conditions, while the augmented reality visualization provides an unprecedented level of depth perception and anatomical context. Together, these innovations constitute a comprehensive solution to the longstanding challenge of intracardiac navigation, offering a new standard of care for transcatheter interventions that is precise, intuitive, and clinically transformative.
