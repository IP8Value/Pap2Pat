# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The clinical translation of cell-based therapies has been significantly hindered by the inability to non-invasively and longitudinally monitor the distribution, persistence, and functional activity of therapeutic cells within the living organism. Conventional methods for assessing the localization and survival of administered cells, such as tissue biopsy and histological analysis, are inherently limited by their invasive nature, sampling bias, and inability to provide whole-body, real-time data. These limitations prevent clinicians from correlating the pharmacokinetics of therapeutic cells with clinical outcomes, thereby impeding dose optimization, early detection of treatment failure, and identification of off-target engraftment or adverse effects. The need for a robust, sensitive, and safe imaging modality capable of tracking therapeutic cells across multiple anatomical sites over time remains unmet in the field of regenerative medicine, immunotherapy, and gene therapy.

Positron emission tomography (PET) reporter gene imaging has emerged as a powerful strategy to address this challenge. This approach relies on the genetic engineering of therapeutic cells to express a reporter gene encoding a protein that selectively captures and retains a radiolabeled probe, thereby generating a detectable signal through the emission of positrons. The most widely utilized reporter gene systems to date are derived from viral sources, particularly the herpes simplex virus type 1 thymidine kinase (HSV1-tk) and its engineered variant, sr39tk. These enzymes efficiently phosphorylate a range of nucleoside analogs labeled with positron-emitting isotopes such as fluorine-18, leading to intracellular trapping of the probe due to the addition of a phosphate group that prevents membrane efflux. The resulting signal amplification through catalytic turnover allows for the detection of small numbers of cells, even in deep tissues, making HSV1-tk-based systems highly sensitive in preclinical models. Indeed, HSV1-tk has been successfully employed in early-phase clinical trials to monitor adoptively transferred T cells and stem cells, demonstrating the feasibility of this technology in humans.

However, the clinical utility of viral reporter genes is fundamentally constrained by their immunogenic potential. The human immune system recognizes viral proteins as foreign antigens, leading to the activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes and antibody-mediated responses that can eliminate the very therapeutic cells intended for treatment. This phenomenon has been documented in multiple clinical studies, where the persistence of HSV1-tk-expressing cells was significantly shortened due to immune clearance, thereby compromising therapeutic efficacy and confounding imaging data. The immunogenicity of viral reporter genes not only reduces the longevity of cell engraftment but also introduces an unpredictable variable into the interpretation of PET signals, as signal loss may reflect immune-mediated cell death rather than biological failure of the therapy. Consequently, the development of non-immunogenic reporter gene systems derived from human proteins is not merely an incremental improvement but a necessary evolution for the safe and reliable clinical deployment of cell-based therapies.

Human nucleoside kinases have been proposed as promising alternatives to viral enzymes due to their endogenous origin and presumed low immunogenicity. Among these, mitochondrial thymidine kinase 2 (TK2) and deoxycytidine kinase (dCK) have been investigated as potential reporter genes. TK2, in particular, has garnered attention because of its natural ability to phosphorylate both deoxythymidine and deoxycytidine, and because its truncated form, lacking the mitochondrial targeting sequence, can be expressed in the cytosol where it remains accessible to exogenous nucleoside analogs. Early studies demonstrated that a truncated form of TK2 could phosphorylate 18F-FEAU, a fluorinated pyrimidine analog, and enable PET imaging in murine models. However, the sensitivity of the TK2/18F-FEAU system was found to be substantially lower than that of HSV1-tk/18F-FHBG, limiting its practical utility. Furthermore, both the truncated TK2 and the engineered dCK double mutant retained significant activity toward endogenous nucleosides, raising concerns about metabolic perturbation. The ectopic overexpression of nucleoside kinases in therapeutic cells can lead to unregulated phosphorylation of naturally occurring deoxythymidine and deoxycytidine, resulting in imbalanced intracellular deoxynucleoside triphosphate (dNTP) pools. Such imbalances are known to induce DNA replication stress, increase the risk of mutagenesis, and potentially trigger genotoxic stress responses, particularly in long-lived cell populations such as hematopoietic stem cells or engineered T cells intended for chronic engraftment. The risk is further amplified in preclinical models, where serum concentrations of thymidine in rodents are several-fold higher than in humans, creating a more pronounced substrate competition and metabolic burden.

Another critical limitation of existing reporter probe systems is suboptimal biodistribution. The ideal reporter probe should exhibit rapid clearance from non-target tissues, minimal background accumulation in organs such as the liver, gastrointestinal tract, and lungs, and high specificity for the reporter gene-expressing cells. While 18F-FHBG, the most commonly used probe for HSV1-tk imaging, achieves good sensitivity, its biodistribution profile is hindered by substantial hepatobiliary excretion and prolonged retention in the gallbladder and intestines. This leads to high background signal in the abdominal cavity, obscuring the detection of therapeutic cells localized in the liver, pancreas, or intestinal mesentery. Moreover, the probe’s clearance kinetics are not always compatible with the temporal dynamics of cell engraftment, requiring imaging at multiple time points to distinguish specific signal from non-specific retention. The development of a novel reporter probe with improved pharmacokinetics—particularly one that avoids high hepatic and gastrointestinal accumulation—would significantly enhance the spatial resolution and diagnostic accuracy of PET reporter gene imaging.

In parallel, the chemical structure of the probe itself presents an opportunity for innovation. Naturally occurring nucleosides adopt the D-configuration, and most radiolabeled analogs are synthesized in this form. However, recent studies have suggested that non-natural L-enantiomers of nucleoside analogs may be selectively recognized by certain kinases while being poorly metabolized by endogenous cellular enzymes, thereby reducing off-target uptake and improving specificity. L-2′-deoxy-2′-fluoro-5-methyl-1-β-D-arabinofuranosyluracil (L-18F-FMAU) represents a candidate probe in this class, possessing a fluorine-18 label at the 2′ position and a methyl group at the 5-position of the pyrimidine ring, with the sugar moiety in the unnatural L-configuration. This structural configuration renders L-18F-FMAU a poor substrate for most human nucleoside kinases, except for TK2, which exhibits promiscuous enantiomeric specificity. The potential of L-18F-FMAU as a reporter probe lies in its ability to be efficiently phosphorylated by a genetically engineered variant of TK2 while remaining largely inert to endogenous kinases, thereby minimizing background signal and maximizing target-to-background contrast.

Despite these promising features, the native TK2 enzyme is subject to strong allosteric feedback inhibition by its endogenous product, thymidine triphosphate (dTTP). This regulatory mechanism, essential for maintaining balanced dNTP pools during the cell cycle, severely limits the catalytic efficiency of TK2 under conditions of probe administration. When dTTP levels rise, as they do during S-phase or in metabolically active cells, the enzyme’s activity is suppressed, leading to inconsistent and unpredictable probe accumulation. This dynamic regulation introduces significant noise into PET signal quantification, making it difficult to correlate signal intensity with cell number or gene expression level. Furthermore, the catalytic efficiency of wild-type TK2 for L-18F-FMAU is suboptimal, and its activity toward endogenous substrates remains too high to be considered biologically inert. Therefore, a comprehensive engineering strategy is required to simultaneously enhance the enzyme’s affinity and turnover rate for L-18F-FMAU, reduce its sensitivity to dTTP-mediated inhibition, and minimize its phosphorylation of thymidine and deoxycytidine.

The present invention addresses these longstanding challenges by introducing a novel, human-derived PET reporter gene-probe system comprising a genetically engineered double mutant of human thymidine kinase 2 (TK2-N93D/L109F) and the non-natural nucleoside analog L-18F-FMAU as the reporter probe. This system was developed through a structure-guided, rational design approach that leverages molecular modeling, site-directed mutagenesis, and rigorous biochemical and in vivo validation. The resulting enzyme exhibits a unique combination of properties: dramatically increased catalytic efficiency for L-18F-FMAU, near-complete resistance to dTTP-mediated feedback inhibition, and substantially reduced activity toward endogenous nucleosides thymidine and deoxycytidine. These features collectively enable high-sensitivity, high-specificity imaging of therapeutic cells without perturbing their native metabolism or triggering immune responses. Furthermore, the biodistribution of L-18F-FMAU in mice demonstrates superior clearance kinetics compared to existing probes, with minimal retention in the liver and gastrointestinal tract, and initial human studies confirm its feasibility for clinical translation. The TK2-N93D/L109F/L-18F-FMAU system thus represents a transformative advancement in the field of cell tracking, offering a safe, sensitive, and biologically inert platform for the non-invasive monitoring of cell-based therapies across a broad spectrum of clinical applications.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel positron emission tomography (PET) reporter gene-probe system for the non-invasive, quantitative, and longitudinal imaging of therapeutic cells in vivo. The system comprises a genetically engineered variant of human thymidine kinase 2, designated TK2-N93D/L109F, and a radiolabeled reporter probe, 2-deoxy-2-[18F]-5-methyl-1-β-L-arabinofuranosyluracil (L-18F-FMAU). The TK2-N93D/L109F enzyme is a double mutant of the human thymidine kinase 2 protein, wherein the asparagine residue at position 93 is substituted with aspartic acid and the leucine residue at position 109 is substituted with phenylalanine. This engineered enzyme exhibits a marked increase in catalytic efficiency for the L-18F-FMAU probe, a substantial reduction in sensitivity to feedback inhibition by thymidine triphosphate, and a profound decrease in phosphorylation activity toward the endogenous nucleosides thymidine and deoxycytidine. These properties collectively render the enzyme orthogonal to native cellular metabolism, enabling high-fidelity detection of reporter gene expression without altering the physiological function or genomic integrity of the host cell.

The L-18F-FMAU probe is a non-natural enantiomer of the pyrimidine nucleoside analog, labeled with the positron-emitting isotope fluorine-18 at the 2′-position of the arabinofuranose sugar moiety. The L-configuration of the sugar ring confers resistance to phosphorylation by most endogenous human nucleoside kinases, thereby minimizing off-target accumulation in tissues such as the brain, heart, and skeletal muscle. The 5-methyl substitution enhances the probe’s affinity for the engineered TK2 enzyme while maintaining favorable pharmacokinetic properties, including rapid blood clearance, low retention in non-target organs, and predominant excretion via the hepatobiliary route. In preclinical models, the biodistribution of L-18F-FMAU demonstrates minimal accumulation in the liver and gastrointestinal tract compared to existing probes such as 18F-FHBG, resulting in superior target-to-background contrast and enhanced spatial resolution for imaging cells localized in the abdominal cavity.

The TK2-N93D/L109F/L-18F-FMAU system is designed for use in therapeutic cells that have been genetically modified to express the engineered kinase under the control of a constitutive or inducible promoter. Upon administration of L-18F-FMAU, the probe is selectively taken up by cells expressing TK2-N93D/L109F, phosphorylated intracellularly, and trapped due to the addition of a negatively charged phosphate group. This results in the accumulation of radioactivity proportional to the number of reporter gene-expressing cells and the level of enzyme expression, enabling high-sensitivity PET imaging. The system’s sensitivity is comparable to that of the widely used HSV1-sr39tk/18F-FHBG system, as demonstrated in murine tumor models, while offering the critical advantage of reduced immunogenicity due to its human origin. Moreover, the engineered enzyme’s minimal activity toward endogenous nucleosides ensures that its ectopic expression does not perturb cellular nucleotide pools, thereby preserving the viability, proliferation capacity, and genomic stability of therapeutic cells over extended periods.

The invention further encompasses methods for the production and purification of the recombinant TK2-N93D/L109F enzyme, the synthesis of L-18F-FMAU using automated radiochemical methods, and the genetic modification of therapeutic cells using viral or non-viral delivery systems to stably express the reporter gene. The system is applicable to a wide range of cell-based therapies, including but not limited to adoptive T cell transfer, CAR-T cell therapy, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, mesenchymal stem cell therapy, and engineered regulatory T cell therapies. The invention also includes methods for quantifying the number of viable reporter gene-expressing cells in vivo through standardized uptake value (SUV) measurements derived from dynamic PET imaging, as well as methods for correlating signal intensity with therapeutic efficacy, engraftment kinetics, and potential off-target localization.

The TK2-N93D/L109F/L-18F-FMAU system represents a significant advancement over existing reporter gene technologies by integrating three critical attributes: human origin for reduced immunogenicity, enzymatic orthogonality for metabolic inertness, and optimized probe pharmacokinetics for high-contrast imaging. Unlike prior systems that compromise between sensitivity and safety, this invention achieves both simultaneously, thereby enabling the reliable, long-term, and whole-body monitoring of therapeutic cells in preclinical and clinical settings without introducing confounding biological or immunological variables. The system is suitable for use in both small animal models and human subjects, with preliminary human biodistribution data confirming its translational potential. The invention further provides a generalizable platform for the development of additional reporter gene-probe pairs by applying similar structure-guided engineering principles to other human nucleoside kinases and non-natural nucleoside analogs, thereby expanding the scope of non-invasive cell tracking technologies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a novel positron emission tomography (PET) reporter gene-probe system for the non-invasive, quantitative, and longitudinal imaging of therapeutic cells in vivo. The system is composed of two essential components: a genetically engineered variant of human thymidine kinase 2, designated TK2-N93D/L109F, and a radiolabeled reporter probe, 2-deoxy-2-[18F]-5-methyl-1-β-L-arabinofuranosyluracil (L-18F-FMAU). The invention encompasses the molecular design, biochemical characterization, in vivo validation, and clinical translation of this system, as well as methods for its application in monitoring cell-based therapies. The TK2-N93D/L109F enzyme is a double mutant of the human thymidine kinase 2 protein, wherein the asparagine residue at position 93 is substituted with aspartic acid and the leucine residue at position 109 is substituted with phenylalanine. This engineered enzyme was developed through a rational, structure-guided approach that combined homology modeling, site-directed mutagenesis, and kinetic analysis to optimize its catalytic properties for the L-18F-FMAU probe while minimizing its interaction with endogenous nucleosides.

The human thymidine kinase 2 (TK2) gene encodes a mitochondrial enzyme responsible for the salvage phosphorylation of deoxythymidine and deoxycytidine, playing a critical role in maintaining mitochondrial DNA integrity. The enzyme is subject to allosteric feedback inhibition by thymidine triphosphate (dTTP), a regulatory mechanism that prevents excessive accumulation of dTTP during the S-phase of the cell cycle. While the wild-type enzyme exhibits promiscuous enantiomeric specificity and can phosphorylate both D- and L-configured nucleosides, its catalytic efficiency for non-natural L-nucleosides is low, and its activity toward endogenous substrates is too high for safe use in therapeutic cells. To overcome these limitations, the present invention employs a two-step mutagenesis strategy. The first mutation, N93D, targets a residue located in the nucleotide-binding pocket of the enzyme, which is structurally homologous to a known feedback-resistant mutation in Drosophila melanogaster deoxyribonucleoside kinase. Molecular modeling predicted that substitution of asparagine with aspartic acid at position 93 would disrupt a key hydrogen bond between residue 93 and glutamine 200, thereby destabilizing the closed, active conformation of the enzyme and rendering it less susceptible to dTTP binding. Experimental validation confirmed that the N93D mutant retained 96% of its catalytic activity in the presence of 10 μM dTTP, compared to an 80% reduction in wild-type TK2 activity under identical conditions. This resistance to feedback inhibition ensures consistent probe trapping regardless of the intracellular dNTP pool dynamics, a critical feature for reliable quantitative imaging.

The second mutation, L109F, was identified through structural analysis of homology models derived from human deoxycytidine kinase, which shares 35% sequence identity with TK2. The leucine residue at position 109 is positioned near the pyrimidine-binding site and was hypothesized to influence substrate specificity through steric interactions. Substitution with the bulkier phenylalanine residue was expected to preferentially hinder the binding of D-configured nucleosides while accommodating the L-configured probe. Although initial kinetic analysis revealed that the L109F mutation increased the catalytic efficiency for both D- and L-substrates, it also enhanced the enzyme’s affinity for thymidine, thereby compromising selectivity. However, when combined with the N93D mutation, the L109F substitution synergistically enhanced the enzyme’s catalytic turnover for L-18F-FMAU by more than 100% relative to wild-type TK2, while simultaneously reducing its activity toward thymidine by nearly 50%. The resulting double mutant, TK2-N93D/L109F, exhibited a kcat (L-FMAU)/kcat (D-dT) ratio of 14.9, a fourfold improvement over wild-type TK2 and a 40% improvement over the N93D single mutant. This dramatic enhancement in substrate selectivity ensures that the enzyme preferentially phosphorylates the reporter probe over endogenous nucleosides, thereby minimizing metabolic perturbation and maximizing signal specificity.

The engineered TK2-N93D/L109F enzyme was expressed in recombinant form using an Escherichia coli expression system with a SUMO-tagged purification strategy to ensure high yield and purity. The enzyme was purified through sequential metal affinity chromatography and size exclusion chromatography, yielding a homogeneous preparation suitable for kinetic analysis and structural characterization. Biochemical assays confirmed that the double mutant retained its resistance to dTTP inhibition, with only 11% activity loss at 100 μM dTTP, compared to a 56% reduction in the L109F single mutant. Furthermore, the enzyme’s activity toward deoxycytidine, the other endogenous substrate of TK2, was reduced to 62% of wild-type levels, further supporting its metabolic orthogonality. These properties collectively ensure that the ectopic expression of TK2-N93D/L109F in therapeutic cells does not alter intracellular dNTP pools, preserve normal mitochondrial function, and avoid genotoxic stress, even under prolonged in vivo conditions.

The reporter probe, L-18F-FMAU, is synthesized through a multi-step radiosynthesis protocol involving nucleophilic fluorination of a precursor molecule followed by deprotection and purification via high-performance liquid chromatography. The L-configuration of the arabinofuranose sugar is achieved through chiral synthesis or enzymatic resolution, ensuring enantiomeric purity. The fluorine-18 isotope, with a half-life of approximately 110 minutes, is incorporated at the 2′-position of the sugar ring, enabling high-specific-activity labeling suitable for PET imaging. The 5-methyl substitution on the uracil base enhances the probe’s binding affinity for the engineered TK2 enzyme while maintaining favorable pharmacokinetic properties, including rapid plasma clearance, low nonspecific binding, and minimal uptake in non-target tissues. In murine models, L-18F-FMAU demonstrated significantly lower accumulation in the liver and gastrointestinal tract compared to 18F-FHBG, with gallbladder retention reduced by more than 50% and intestinal signal diminished by a factor of three. This improved biodistribution profile enables high-contrast imaging of therapeutic cells localized in the abdominal cavity, a region previously inaccessible due to high background signal from conventional probes.

The invention further encompasses methods for the genetic modification of therapeutic cells to stably express the TK2-N93D/L109F reporter gene. This is achieved through retroviral or lentiviral transduction using a bicistronic vector containing the TK2-N93D/L109F coding sequence, an internal ribosomal entry site (IRES), and a fluorescent reporter gene such as yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) for cell sorting and verification. The vector is designed to exclude the mitochondrial targeting sequence of native TK2, ensuring cytosolic localization and accessibility to the exogenous probe. Transduced cells are selected by fluorescence-activated cell sorting to ensure uniform expression levels, and their viability, proliferation rate, and differentiation capacity are confirmed to be indistinguishable from untransduced controls. In vivo studies in murine xenograft models demonstrated that cells expressing TK2-N93D/L109F accumulated L-18F-FMAU with signal intensity comparable to that of cells expressing the widely used HSV1-sr39tk reporter gene, as measured by microPET/CT imaging. The signal-to-noise ratio was superior in the abdominal region, and the probe’s rapid clearance allowed for imaging as early as one hour post-injection, with peak accumulation observed at three hours.

The invention also includes methods for quantifying the number of reporter gene-expressing cells in vivo using standardized uptake values (SUVs) derived from dynamic PET imaging. The SUV is calculated as the tissue radioactivity concentration normalized to the injected dose per body weight, and it correlates linearly with the number of transduced cells in a given tissue. This quantitative relationship enables the non-invasive assessment of cell engraftment, persistence, and expansion over time, facilitating dose optimization, early detection of treatment failure, and identification of off-target localization. The system’s sensitivity permits detection of as few as 10^4 cells in a single organ, making it suitable for monitoring low-dose cell therapies such as regulatory T cell infusions or engineered stem cell populations.

The TK2-N93D/L109F/L-18F-FMAU system is applicable to a broad range of cell-based therapies, including adoptive T cell transfer for cancer immunotherapy, CAR-T cell therapy, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation for hematologic malignancies, mesenchymal stem cell therapy for tissue regeneration, and engineered regulatory T cells for autoimmune disease. The human origin of the reporter gene ensures minimal immunogenicity, eliminating the risk of immune-mediated elimination of therapeutic cells that has plagued viral-based systems. Furthermore, the metabolic inertness of the enzyme ensures that the long-term persistence of engineered cells does not lead to genotoxic stress or altered differentiation potential, a critical consideration for stem cell and regenerative applications. The probe’s favorable pharmacokinetics and low background signal enable imaging in all major anatomical compartments, including the abdomen, thorax, and brain, with no significant uptake in the myocardium or skeletal muscle, thereby expanding the scope of detectable therapeutic targets.

The invention further encompasses methods for the clinical translation of this system, including the GMP-compliant production of the reporter gene vector, the automated radiosynthesis of L-18F-FMAU, and the development of standardized imaging protocols for human PET/CT studies. Preliminary human biodistribution data from two healthy volunteers demonstrated that L-18F-FMAU is well tolerated, with rapid clearance from the bloodstream and predominant excretion via the hepatobiliary and renal routes. The probe accumulated in the liver and kidneys, as expected, but showed minimal retention in the gastrointestinal tract compared to 18F-FHBG, confirming its translational potential. Myocardial uptake was observed, suggesting a potential limitation for cardiac imaging, but this can be mitigated through structural modifications to the probe, such as substitution of the 5-methyl group with a bulkier substituent to reduce phosphorylation by endogenous TK2.

The TK2-N93D/L109F/L-18F-FMAU system represents a paradigm shift in PET reporter gene imaging by integrating human biology, enzymatic precision, and probe design into a single, clinically viable platform. It overcomes the fundamental limitations of viral-based systems—immunogenicity and metabolic interference—while achieving sensitivity comparable to the gold standard HSV1-sr39tk system. The invention provides a safe, reliable, and quantitative method for monitoring therapeutic cells in real time, enabling the optimization of cell-based therapies and accelerating their clinical adoption.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The construction of the TK2-N93D/L109F expression vector was initiated with the cloning of a truncated form of human thymidine kinase 2 (TK2) lacking the N-terminal mitochondrial targeting sequence. The cDNA encoding the full-length human TK2 protein (Uniprot ID O00142) was obtained from a commercial source and amplified by polymerase chain reaction using high-fidelity DNA polymerase. The forward primer contained a Kozak sequence and an NdeI restriction site, while the reverse primer included a stop codon and an XhoI restriction site, enabling directional cloning into the pMSCV retroviral vector downstream of the murine stem cell virus long terminal repeat (LTR) promoter. The resulting plasmid, designated pMSCV-WT-TK2, encoded the truncated TK2 protein beginning at residue 51, thus eliminating the mitochondrial localization signal while preserving the catalytic domain. This construct was confirmed by DNA sequencing and used as the template for subsequent site-directed mutagenesis. The N93D mutation was introduced using a QuikChange mutagenesis kit with a forward primer containing the nucleotide change from AAC to GAC and a reverse primer complementary to the mutation site. The L109F mutation was introduced similarly using a forward primer with the codon change from CTC to TTC. Both mutations were sequentially introduced into the WT-TK2 backbone, and the resulting double mutant plasmid, pMSCV-TK2-N93D/L109F, was verified by complete sequencing of the entire TK2 coding region. The integrity of the plasmid was further confirmed by restriction digest analysis and agarose gel electrophoresis. The final plasmid was transformed into competent E. coli DH5α cells, amplified in liquid culture, and purified using a commercial plasmid maxi-prep kit. The purified plasmid was quantified by spectrophotometry and stored at -20°C in TE buffer until use for retroviral packaging.

### Example 2

Retroviral particles encoding the TK2-N93D/L109F reporter gene were produced by transient transfection of the Phoenix amphotropic packaging cell line. The pMSCV-TK2-N93D/L109F plasmid was co-transfected with the pCL-Ampho helper plasmid using Lipofectamine 2000 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Transfection efficiency was monitored by fluorescence microscopy using a co-transfected GFP reporter plasmid, and transfection conditions were optimized to achieve greater than 80% transfection efficiency. The transfection medium was replaced 24 hours post-transfection with fresh RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Viral supernatant was collected at 48 and 72 hours post-transfection, filtered through a 0.45 μm cellulose acetate filter to remove cellular debris, and concentrated by ultracentrifugation at 25,000 rpm for 2 hours at 4°C. The viral pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline containing 1% bovine serum albumin and aliquoted into cryovials for storage at -80°C. The viral titer was determined by transducing L1210 murine leukemia cells with serial dilutions of the viral supernatant and quantifying the percentage of yellow fluorescent protein (YFP)-positive cells 72 hours later using flow cytometry. The titer was calculated as transducing units per milliliter (TU/mL) and was found to be 1.2 × 10^7 TU/mL for the TK2-N93D/L109F construct, comparable to the titers obtained for the wild-type TK2 and sr39tk constructs used as controls. The viral stocks were used immediately for cell transduction or stored at -80°C for future use.

### Example 3

L1210 murine leukemia cells were transduced with retroviral particles encoding TK2-N93D/L109F, wild-type TK2, sr39tk, or an empty vector control. Cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 10^5 cells per well in 24-well plates and incubated overnight at 37°C in 5% CO2. The following day, viral supernatant was added to each well in the presence of 2 μg/mL polybrene to enhance transduction efficiency. Cells were subjected to spinfection at 1000 × g for 120 minutes at 37°C to increase viral contact with the cell membrane. After spinfection, the viral supernatant was removed, and fresh medium was added. Transduced cells were maintained in culture for 72 hours to allow for reporter gene expression. Cells were then harvested, washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline, and analyzed by flow cytometry for YFP expression using a BD FACSCanto II instrument. A population of cells expressing YFP at a median fluorescence intensity of 1000 ± 50 arbitrary units was selected for all subsequent experiments to ensure uniform reporter gene expression across all constructs. The percentage of YFP-positive cells was consistently greater than 95% in all transduced populations, confirming efficient transduction and stable expression. The expression level of the TK2-N93D/L109F protein in the selected population was confirmed by Western blot analysis using a polyclonal anti-TK2 antibody, which detected a single band at approximately 28 kDa, consistent with the expected molecular weight of the truncated enzyme. No cross-reactivity was observed with endogenous murine TK2, confirming the specificity of the antibody.

### Example 4

The enzymatic activity of the TK2-N93D/L109F mutant was characterized using a coupled spectrophotometric assay based on the oxidation of NADH. Recombinant TK2 proteins were expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) C41 cells harboring the pET14b-SUMO-TK2 expression vector. Cells were grown in LB medium at 37°C until an optical density at 600 nm of 0.8 was reached, at which point the temperature was reduced to 18°C, and protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside. Cultures were incubated overnight with shaking, harvested by centrifugation, and stored at -80°C. Cell pellets were lysed by sonication in buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 300 mM NaCl, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, and 10% glycerol. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 30 minutes, and the supernatant was loaded onto a HisTrap HP column equilibrated with binding buffer. The His-SUMO-TK2 fusion protein was eluted with a linear gradient of imidazole (0–500 mM), and fractions containing the fusion protein were pooled and incubated with SUMO protease overnight at 4°C to cleave the SUMO tag. The cleaved TK2 protein was reapplied to the nickel column to remove the His-SUMO tag and protease, and the flow-through containing purified TK2 was collected. The protein was further purified by size exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 200 column equilibrated with 25 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 200 mM NaCl, and 3 mM dithiothreitol. Fractions containing pure TK2 were pooled, concentrated to 10 mg/mL using a 10 kDa molecular weight cutoff centrifugal filter, aliquoted, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80°C. Kinetic assays were performed in triplicate at 37°C in a buffer containing 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 100 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM ATP, and 0.2 mM NADH. The reaction was initiated by adding 400 nM purified enzyme and 200 μM substrate. The decrease in absorbance at 340 nm was monitored for 10 minutes using a Cary UV spectrophotometer, and the initial velocity was calculated from the linear portion of the curve. Michaelis-Menten kinetics were determined by varying substrate concentrations from 15 to 500 μM and fitting the data to the equation v = (Vmax × [S]) / (Km + [S]) using SigmaPlot software. The kcat and Km values for L-FMAU, D-thymidine, and deoxycytidine were determined for wild-type TK2, N93D, L109F, and the double mutant. The kcat/Km ratio for L-FMAU was 7.2-fold higher for the double mutant than for wild-type TK2, demonstrating a dramatic enhancement in catalytic efficiency.

### Example 5

The sensitivity of the TK2-N93D/L109F reporter gene system was evaluated in vivo using a murine xenograft model. SCID mice were injected subcutaneously on day -7 with 1 × 10^6 L1210 cells transduced to express TK2-N93D/L109F, wild-type TK2, sr39tk, or an empty vector control. Tumors were allowed to grow for seven days, at which point they reached a mean diameter of 8–10 mm. Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and injected intravenously with 200 μCi of L-18F-FMAU via the tail vein. Three hours post-injection, mice underwent microPET/CT imaging using an Inveon scanner (Siemens Medical Solutions). A low-dose CT scan was acquired for attenuation correction and anatomical registration. PET images were acquired for 10 minutes per bed position, with six bed positions covering the entire body from the skull base to the upper thighs. Images were reconstructed using a three-dimensional ordered subset expectation maximization algorithm followed by maximum a posteriori reconstruction with 18 iterations and a beta value of 0.1. Regions of interest were drawn around the tumors and surrounding muscle tissue using OsiriX software, and standardized uptake values (SUVs) were calculated as the tissue radioactivity concentration divided by the injected dose per body weight. The mean SUV of the TK2-N93D/L109F-expressing tumors was 19.9 ± 1.5, which was not significantly different from the SUV of sr39tk-expressing tumors imaged with 18F-FHBG (24.1 ± 6.2, p = 0.37). In contrast, tumors expressing wild-type TK2 exhibited a significantly lower SUV of 10.2 ± 1.1 (p < 0.01). No signal was detected in tumors expressing the empty vector. These results demonstrate that the TK2-N93D/L109F system provides PET signal intensity equivalent to the gold-standard HSV1-sr39tk system, validating its suitability for in vivo cell tracking.

### Example 6

The specificity of the TK2-N93D/L109F system was assessed by measuring the uptake of L-18F-FMAU in transduced L1210 cells in the presence of excess endogenous nucleosides. Cells expressing TK2-N93D/L109F, wild-type TK2, or sr39tk were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 10^5 cells per well and incubated overnight. The following day, 5 μCi of L-18F-FMAU was added to each well, along with varying concentrations of D-thymidine (0–100 μM) or deoxycytidine (0–50 μM). After one hour of incubation at 37°C, cells were washed four times with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline and lysed in 1 mL of 1 N NaOH. Radioactivity in the lysates was measured using a gamma counter. The percentage of probe retention relative to the no-competitor control was calculated. In cells expressing wild-type TK2, L-18F-FMAU uptake was reduced by 75% in the presence of 5 μM D-thymidine and by 85% in the presence of 10 μM deoxycytidine. In contrast, uptake in TK2-N93D/L109F-expressing cells was reduced by only 47% with 5 μM D-thymidine and by 58% with 10 μM deoxycytidine. The sr39tk-expressing cells showed no significant reduction in uptake with either nucleoside, as expected due to the distinct substrate specificity of the viral enzyme. These results demonstrate that the TK2-N93D/L109F enzyme retains significant activity toward L-18F-FMAU even in the presence of physiological concentrations of endogenous nucleosides, confirming its metabolic orthogonality and suitability for use in vivo where nucleoside concentrations are high.

### Example 7

The biodistribution of L-18F-FMAU was evaluated in healthy human volunteers to assess its translational potential. Two healthy adult volunteers, a 53-year-old male and a 44-year-old female, were enrolled under an Institutional Review Board-approved protocol. Each volunteer received a bolus intravenous injection of 56 MBq (1.5 mCi) of L-18F-FMAU, synthesized under Good Manufacturing Practice conditions. Whole-body PET/CT imaging was performed using a Biograph 64 scanner (Siemens Healthcare) immediately after injection and at 30, 60, and 120 minutes post-injection. Each scan consisted of six bed positions covering the head to the mid-thigh, with a 5-minute acquisition per position. A low-dose CT scan was acquired for attenuation correction. The standard uptake value (SUV) was calculated for major organs, including the liver, kidneys, spleen, heart, lungs, brain, and bladder. The mean SUV for the liver at 120 minutes was 4.8 ± 0.6, for the kidneys 3.2 ± 0.4, for the bladder 6.1 ± 0.8, and for the myocardium 2.3 ± 0.3. No significant uptake was observed in the brain, skeletal muscle, or bone marrow. The gastrointestinal tract showed minimal activity, with SUV values below 1.0 in the small intestine and colon. The probe was cleared rapidly from the bloodstream, with a plasma half-life of approximately 15 minutes. Urine collection confirmed that 78% of the injected dose was excreted within 2 hours, primarily via the kidneys. These findings demonstrate that L-18F-FMAU is well tolerated in humans, with a biodistribution profile suitable for clinical PET imaging, and that its clearance kinetics support imaging within a single session.

### Example 8

The safety and metabolic inertness of the TK2-N93D/L109F reporter gene were evaluated in primary human hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs). CD34+ HSPCs were isolated from mobilized peripheral blood of healthy donors using magnetic bead separation. Cells were transduced with lentiviral vectors encoding TK2-N93D/L109F or an empty vector control at a multiplicity of infection of 10. Transduced cells were cultured in serum-free medium supplemented with stem cell factor, thrombopoietin, and FLT3 ligand for 14 days. At days 7 and 14, cells were harvested and analyzed for viability, proliferation, colony-forming unit (CFU) potential, and intracellular dNTP pool composition. Flow cytometry revealed no difference in viability between transduced and control cells (95% vs. 94%). Proliferation rates, as measured by CFSE dilution, were identical between groups. CFU assays showed no significant difference in the number or type of colonies formed (granulocyte-macrophage, erythroid, or multipotent). Intracellular dNTP levels were quantified by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry and found to be unchanged in TK2-N93D/L109F-expressing cells compared to controls. The dTTP/dCTP ratio remained within the physiological range, and no increase in DNA strand breaks or γH2AX foci was detected by comet assay or immunofluorescence. These results demonstrate that the expression of TK2-N93D/L109F does not perturb the metabolic homeostasis or functional integrity of primary human stem cells, confirming its suitability for use in clinical cell therapies where long-term engraftment and genomic stability are paramount.
