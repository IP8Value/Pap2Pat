# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The generation of truly random numbers has long been a foundational requirement for secure communication systems, cryptographic protocols, scientific simulations, and stochastic modeling. Unlike pseudo-random number generators, which rely on deterministic algorithms and initial seeds and are therefore inherently predictable given sufficient knowledge of their internal state, true random number generators derive their output from physical processes that are fundamentally unpredictable. Among such physical processes, quantum phenomena stand out as the most robust and theoretically sound source of randomness, owing to the intrinsic indeterminacy embedded in the laws of quantum mechanics. The behavior of individual photons, electrons, or other quantum particles cannot be precisely predicted, even with complete knowledge of prior conditions, making them ideal for generating cryptographic-grade randomness.

Historically, various physical systems have been explored for true random number generation, including thermal noise in resistors, atmospheric noise, radioactive decay, and chaotic electronic circuits. While some of these methods have proven adequate for non-critical applications, they often suffer from environmental sensitivity, slow throughput, scalability limitations, or susceptibility to tampering. For instance, thermal noise sources are heavily influenced by temperature fluctuations and require extensive post-processing to remove bias and correlation. Radioactive decay-based systems, though theoretically sound, involve hazardous materials and are impractical for consumer electronics. Chaotic systems, while fast, may exhibit hidden deterministic structures that compromise long-term security.

In recent years, optical methods based on photon detection have emerged as a leading approach for quantum random number generation. These systems exploit the quantum nature of light—specifically, the probabilistic arrival of photons at a detector—to produce sequences of bits whose outcomes are governed by the laws of quantum physics rather than classical determinism. Among the most prominent implementations are single-photon avalanche diodes (SPADs), homodyne detection systems, and conventional complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) image sensors. SPADs, when operated in Geiger mode, are capable of detecting individual photons with high efficiency and have been widely adopted in quantum key distribution systems. However, they require high bias voltages, typically exceeding 20 volts, which increases power consumption and complicates integration with standard low-voltage CMOS circuitry. Furthermore, SPADs are prone to after-pulsing effects, where a previous detection event triggers a spurious secondary detection, introducing temporal correlations that degrade randomness quality. Their pixel sizes are also relatively large, limiting the density of detection elements per unit area and thereby constraining the overall data throughput.

Conventional CMOS image sensors, on the other hand, offer the advantage of compatibility with mass-produced consumer electronics and low-power operation. However, their read noise levels—typically exceeding one electron root-mean-square (e− r.m.s.)—are too high to resolve individual photon arrivals. As a result, the output signal is dominated by classical noise sources such as thermal noise, 1/f noise, and fixed pattern noise, which are not inherently quantum in origin. These noise components are often temperature-dependent and can be modeled or even partially predicted under controlled conditions, thereby reducing the effective entropy of the generated bit stream. Consequently, while CMOS image sensors can produce random-looking outputs, their randomness is not guaranteed to be of quantum origin, and substantial post-processing is required to extract usable entropy, often at the cost of significant data compression and reduced output rates.

The need for a solution that combines the quantum origin of randomness with high throughput, low power consumption, small form factor, and CMOS compatibility has led to the exploration of novel photodetector architectures. One such architecture is the Quanta Image Sensor (QIS), a revolutionary imaging technology conceived as a digital film sensor capable of resolving individual photons with high temporal and spatial resolution. Unlike conventional sensors that integrate charge over multiple exposure periods, QIS operates at extremely high frame rates, capturing each photon arrival as a discrete event within a single frame. Each pixel in a QIS, referred to as a “jot,” is designed with ultra-low read noise, enabling the detection of fractional electron signals and the clear discrimination of photon-counting states. This capability arises from a combination of high conversion gain and advanced readout circuitry that minimizes electronic noise to levels below 0.5 e− r.m.s., a threshold known as deep sub-electron read noise (DSERN). When operated at an optimal quanta exposure level—typically around 0.7 photoelectrons per jot per frame—the probability distribution of the output signal exhibits distinct quantized peaks corresponding to the number of photons detected, separated by noise-induced broadening. By applying a threshold between these peaks, the output can be binarized into a sequence of 0s and 1s, each representing the outcome of a quantum event.

The QIS architecture thus uniquely bridges the gap between the quantum randomness of photon arrival and the practical requirements of modern digital systems. It leverages the same fabrication processes used in commercial CMOS imaging, enabling seamless integration into existing semiconductor manufacturing lines. Moreover, its high pixel density and frame rate allow for massive parallelization, with billion-pixel arrays capable of generating terabits per second of raw entropy. Crucially, because the randomness originates from the quantum mechanical process of photon absorption and the subsequent generation of photoelectrons, the output is not susceptible to classical noise modeling or environmental tampering in the same way as thermal or electronic noise sources. Even if an attacker were to gain full knowledge of the sensor’s internal circuitry, the unpredictability of photon arrival times and numbers remains inviolable under the principles of quantum theory.

Despite these advantages, the application of QIS to random number generation presents several technical challenges that must be addressed to ensure both high-quality output and operational robustness. First, the quanta exposure—the average number of photons incident per jot per frame—must be carefully calibrated to maximize entropy while maintaining stability against fluctuations in illumination intensity. Second, the readout threshold must be precisely positioned at a valley in the probability distribution function to minimize the impact of small variations in light level or sensor gain. Third, the raw bit stream generated by the sensor, while possessing high quantum entropy, is not uniformly distributed and requires post-processing via a randomness extractor to eliminate statistical bias and ensure cryptographic suitability. Finally, the system must be designed to operate reliably under varying environmental conditions, including temperature, humidity, and electromagnetic interference, without degradation in randomness quality.

Previous attempts to implement quantum random number generators using image sensors have largely failed to achieve the necessary combination of high entropy, high throughput, and operational stability. SPAD-based systems, while fast and quantum-certified, suffer from high power consumption and limited scalability. CMOS-based systems, while scalable and low-power, lack the photon-counting precision needed for high-quality entropy generation. The QIS-based approach, as described herein, resolves these trade-offs by introducing a novel architecture that exploits the intrinsic quantum randomness of photon detection while maintaining compatibility with high-volume semiconductor manufacturing and low-power digital systems. This invention represents a significant advancement over prior art by providing a scalable, high-speed, CMOS-compatible quantum random number generator that delivers provably quantum-derived randomness with minimal post-processing overhead and exceptional resistance to environmental perturbations.

## SUMMARY OF SOME EMBODIMENTS

The present invention relates to a quantum random number generator that utilizes a Quanta Image Sensor to produce cryptographically secure random bit sequences derived from the fundamental randomness of quantum mechanical photon detection events. The generator comprises a photodetector array composed of a plurality of jots, each jot being a pixel-sized photodetection element capable of resolving individual photoelectrons with deep sub-electron read noise, and a readout circuit configured to digitize the output of each jot at high frame rates. The system operates under controlled illumination conditions such that the average number of photoelectrons generated per jot per frame is maintained near a predetermined quanta exposure value, typically between 0.6 and 0.8 photoelectrons, to ensure maximum binary entropy and stability against minor fluctuations in light intensity. A threshold comparator is employed to convert the analog readout signal of each jot into a binary output, with the threshold value selected to lie at a local minimum in the probability distribution of the readout signal, thereby minimizing the sensitivity of the output to variations in illumination or sensor gain. The resulting binary stream, while possessing high quantum entropy, is not uniformly distributed and is therefore processed by a non-deterministic randomness extractor based on universal hashing to produce a compressed output sequence that is statistically indistinguishable from a perfectly uniform random bit string.

In one embodiment, the photodetector array comprises at least one hundred million jots, each with a pitch of less than one micrometer, arranged in a two-dimensional grid and fabricated using standard CMOS processes. Each jot includes a photodiode, a pump-gate transistor for high conversion gain, and a correlated double sampling circuit to suppress reset noise and fixed pattern noise. The readout circuit includes a column-parallel analog-to-digital converter array, a multiplexer for sequential readout, and a programmable gain amplifier to condition the signal prior to digitization. The entire sensor is operated at room temperature, eliminating the need for cryogenic cooling or other environmental controls. The illumination source is a stable, low-coherence light emitter such as a light-emitting diode or laser diode, whose intensity is regulated by a feedback loop to maintain the quanta exposure within a narrow range, ensuring consistent entropy generation across extended periods of operation.

In another embodiment, the threshold comparator is implemented in hardware within the sensor’s readout circuitry, allowing for real-time binarization of the analog signal without requiring external processing. The threshold value is dynamically adjusted during an initial calibration phase using statistical analysis of a large sample set to identify the optimal point of minimum sensitivity in the probability distribution function. This calibration is performed once during manufacturing or periodically during operation to compensate for long-term drift in sensor characteristics. The binary output stream is then transmitted to a post-processing unit that applies a universal-2 hash function to compress the raw bits into a final output stream with entropy density approaching one bit per output bit. The compression ratio is determined based on the measured minimum quantum entropy of the raw stream, ensuring that the probability of deviation from perfect uniformity remains below a predetermined threshold, such as 2^(-100), over the operational lifetime of the device.

In yet another embodiment, the system is integrated into a single integrated circuit chip that includes the photodetector array, readout circuitry, threshold comparator, randomness extractor, and control logic, all fabricated on a single substrate using standard CMOS processes. The chip is packaged in a hermetically sealed enclosure with a transparent window to allow incident light to reach the sensor, and includes an onboard light source such as an integrated LED to eliminate dependency on external illumination. The chip further includes a power management unit that regulates voltage levels to maintain stable operation of the pump-gate transistors and analog circuitry, and a temperature sensor that provides feedback to adjust the quanta exposure or threshold in response to thermal drift. The output of the randomness extractor is made available through a high-speed digital interface, such as a serial peripheral interface or a high-speed serial link, enabling direct integration into cryptographic processors, secure communication modules, or blockchain nodes.

In a further embodiment, the system is configured to operate in a multi-jot mode, wherein the binary outputs from multiple jots are combined in parallel to increase the overall data rate. Each jot operates independently with its own threshold, and the outputs are interleaved or aggregated using a deterministic mixing function to ensure that no single point of failure or correlated noise source can compromise the randomness of the final output. This mode enables the generation of gigabits per second of cryptographically secure random numbers, suitable for high-throughput applications such as quantum key distribution, secure cloud computing, and real-time encryption of high-bandwidth data streams.

In yet another embodiment, the system includes a self-diagnostic module that continuously monitors the statistical properties of the raw and extracted bit streams, including entropy estimates, bit imbalance, autocorrelation, and deviation from uniformity. If any parameter exceeds a predefined tolerance, the system initiates a recalibration sequence, adjusts the illumination intensity or threshold, or temporarily suspends output until stability is restored. This self-correcting capability ensures continuous operation without human intervention and provides a mechanism for detecting potential tampering or environmental degradation.

The invention further encompasses a method for generating random numbers comprising the steps of illuminating a photodetector array composed of jots with a stable light source, collecting photoelectrons generated in each jot during a frame period, reading out the analog signal from each jot with deep sub-electron resolution, applying a threshold to convert the signal into a binary value, and post-processing the binary stream using a universal-2 hash function to produce a compressed output with near-unity entropy density. The method further includes calibrating the quanta exposure and threshold during an initial setup phase to maximize entropy and minimize sensitivity to illumination variations, and continuously monitoring the statistical integrity of the output to ensure ongoing compliance with cryptographic standards.

The invention also includes a system for verifying the quality of random number generation, comprising a statistical analysis module that performs a suite of standardized tests, including the NIST Statistical Test Suite, on the output stream to confirm the absence of bias, periodicity, or compressibility. The system further includes a tamper-detection module that monitors for anomalies in the photon flux, read noise characteristics, or threshold stability that could indicate external interference or device malfunction. In the event of a detected anomaly, the system generates an alert and initiates a secure shutdown protocol to prevent the output of compromised randomness.

The embodiments described herein collectively provide a novel, scalable, and robust quantum random number generator that overcomes the limitations of prior art by leveraging the intrinsic randomness of quantum photon detection, the high precision of QIS technology, and the efficiency of modern CMOS fabrication. The resulting device is capable of producing cryptographically secure random numbers at unprecedented speeds, with minimal power consumption, and without reliance on external environmental controls or complex post-processing infrastructure. It is suitable for deployment in a wide range of applications, from consumer electronics and mobile devices to high-security data centers and quantum communication networks.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF SOME EMBODIMENTS

The quantum random number generator described herein is a fully integrated system designed to exploit the fundamental unpredictability of quantum mechanical events—specifically, the probabilistic arrival of photons at a photodetector—to produce a stream of random bits that are statistically indistinguishable from perfect randomness. The system is composed of several interdependent components: a photodetector array fabricated using advanced CMOS technology, a precision readout circuit capable of resolving sub-electron signals, a thresholding mechanism for binary conversion, a randomness extractor for entropy purification, and a control and monitoring subsystem for operational stability. Each component is carefully engineered to preserve the quantum origin of randomness while ensuring high throughput, low power consumption, and long-term reliability under real-world conditions.

The photodetector array is the core element of the system and consists of a two-dimensional grid of millions to billions of individual photodetection units known as jots. Each jot is a pixel-sized structure fabricated using standard CMOS processes, with dimensions on the order of sub-micrometers, enabling the integration of over one billion jots on a single chip with a total area of less than one square centimeter. Unlike conventional image sensors, which accumulate charge over multiple exposure periods to improve signal-to-noise ratio, each jot in this system is designed to operate in a single-photon counting mode, capturing the arrival of individual photons within a single frame duration. This is achieved through the use of a pump-gate transistor architecture, which provides an exceptionally high conversion gain—on the order of several hundred microvolts per electron—thereby amplifying the tiny charge generated by a single photoelectron into a measurable voltage signal. The pump-gate structure also minimizes dark current and reset noise, two major sources of non-quantum noise in traditional photodetectors. The readout circuitry associated with each jot includes a correlated double sampling stage, which subtracts the reset noise from the signal readout, further reducing the effective noise floor to below 0.25 electron root-mean-square. This level of noise suppression is critical, as it allows the system to resolve the discrete quantization peaks in the photon counting histogram, where each peak corresponds to the detection of a specific number of photoelectrons—zero, one, two, or more—within a single frame.

To ensure that the system operates in the optimal regime for entropy generation, the photodetector array is illuminated by a stable, low-coherence light source, such as a light-emitting diode or a laser diode with a diffuser to eliminate spatial coherence. The intensity of this illumination is carefully controlled to maintain an average quanta exposure—defined as the mean number of photoelectrons generated per jot per frame—within a narrow range centered around 0.7 photoelectrons. This value is not arbitrary; it is derived from theoretical analysis of the Poisson distribution governing photon arrival and the Gaussian noise distribution introduced by the readout electronics. When the quanta exposure is set to approximately 0.7, the probability distribution of the readout signal exhibits two distinct peaks: one centered at zero photoelectrons and another at one photoelectron, separated by a valley in the probability density function. By placing the binary threshold at this valley—typically at 0.5 electron equivalent—the probability of outputting a “0” or a “1” becomes nearly equal, maximizing the binary entropy per bit. Moreover, this configuration renders the system highly insensitive to small fluctuations in illumination intensity. For instance, if the quanta exposure varies by 2% due to ambient temperature changes or power supply drift, the resulting change in output entropy is negligible when the threshold is positioned at the valley, whereas a similar variation would cause a significant entropy drop if the threshold were placed at a peak in the distribution. This stability is a key innovation of the present invention, as it ensures consistent randomness quality over extended periods of operation without requiring continuous recalibration.

The readout circuitry is designed to handle the high-speed, high-precision demands of the photodetector array. Each column of jots is connected to a dedicated analog signal chain that includes a programmable gain amplifier, a sample-and-hold circuit, and a low-noise analog-to-digital converter. The gain amplifier is calibrated to scale the output voltage of each jot so that a single electron corresponds to a well-defined voltage step, typically on the order of hundreds of millivolts. This scaling ensures that the digitized output accurately reflects the number of photoelectrons detected, even in the presence of minor variations in conversion gain across the array. The analog-to-digital converters are implemented with high resolution—typically 14 to 16 bits—to capture the fine-grained differences between adjacent photon-counting levels. The digitized values are then buffered and transmitted to a threshold comparator, which applies a fixed or dynamically adjusted threshold value to convert each sample into a single binary digit. This threshold is determined during an initial calibration phase, in which a large number of samples—on the order of tens of millions—are collected and analyzed to construct a histogram of the readout signal. The median of this histogram is computed, and the threshold is set to the value that minimizes the probability of misclassification, which occurs when a signal from a zero-photoelectron event is incorrectly classified as a one-photoelectron event, or vice versa. This calibration is performed once during manufacturing and may be repeated periodically during operation to account for long-term drift in sensor characteristics.

The binary output stream generated by the threshold comparator possesses high quantum entropy but is not uniformly distributed. This is a natural consequence of the underlying physics: while the probability of detecting zero or one photoelectron is nearly equal, the raw bit stream may still exhibit slight biases due to imperfections in the illumination source, minor variations in pixel response, or residual electronic noise. To address this, the system incorporates a non-deterministic randomness extractor based on universal-2 hash functions. This extractor takes as input a block of raw bits—typically 1024 bits—and applies a matrix multiplication operation using a randomly generated binary matrix of dimensions 1024 by 833, producing 833 output bits. The compression ratio of approximately 1.23 ensures that the output entropy density approaches one bit per output bit, while the probability of deviation from perfect uniformity remains below 2^(-100), a value so small that the likelihood of observing any statistical anomaly over the operational lifetime of the device is less than the probability of a cosmic ray striking the chip and altering its state. The matrix used in the extraction process is generated using multiple independent entropy sources, including thermal noise from a resistor, timing jitter from an oscillator, and the output of the sensor itself, ensuring that the extractor is not vulnerable to prediction or reverse-engineering. Importantly, the extractor is implemented in hardware using dedicated logic circuits to minimize latency and power consumption, allowing the system to sustain output rates of several gigabits per second without introducing bottlenecks.

The entire system is fabricated on a single integrated circuit chip using standard CMOS processes, enabling mass production at low cost and facilitating integration into a wide range of electronic devices. The chip includes not only the photodetector array and readout circuitry but also a control unit that manages the timing of frame acquisition, the operation of the gain amplifier, the selection of the threshold value, and the activation of the randomness extractor. A power management unit regulates the supply voltage to the pump-gate transistors and analog circuitry, ensuring stable operation even under fluctuating environmental conditions. A temperature sensor is embedded on-chip to monitor the ambient temperature, and a feedback loop adjusts the illumination intensity or the threshold value in real time to compensate for thermal drift in the sensor’s response characteristics. This closed-loop control system ensures that the quanta exposure remains within the optimal range regardless of external conditions, eliminating the need for manual intervention or environmental shielding.

In a preferred embodiment, the system includes an integrated light source, such as a low-power LED, mounted directly above the photodetector array within the same package. This eliminates dependency on external illumination, which could be subject to interference, tampering, or environmental variability. The LED is driven by a precision current source and modulated at a frequency that avoids resonance with ambient light sources, ensuring that the photon flux remains stable and predictable. The LED’s output is monitored by a secondary photodiode on the chip, which provides real-time feedback to the control unit, enabling dynamic adjustment of the drive current to maintain the desired quanta exposure. This self-contained design makes the system suitable for deployment in portable devices, embedded systems, and remote sensors where external light sources are unavailable or unreliable.

The output of the randomness extractor is made available through a high-speed digital interface, such as a serial peripheral interface, a universal asynchronous receiver-transmitter, or a high-speed serial link operating at gigabit-per-second rates. This allows the random number generator to be seamlessly integrated into cryptographic processors, secure enclaves, blockchain nodes, or quantum communication modules. The system can be configured to output random bits continuously, in bursts, or on demand, depending on the application requirements. In high-security applications, the output may be authenticated using a hardware-based message authentication code to ensure that the stream has not been altered or substituted by an attacker.

To further enhance reliability and security, the system includes a self-diagnostic module that continuously monitors the statistical properties of both the raw and extracted bit streams. This module performs real-time evaluations of key metrics, including the proportion of ones and zeros, the autocorrelation of the bit sequence, the presence of long-range dependencies, and the compressibility of the output using standard algorithms such as Huffman coding or Lempel-Ziv. If any of these metrics deviate beyond a predefined tolerance—indicative of potential tampering, sensor degradation, or environmental interference—the system triggers a recalibration sequence. During recalibration, the system temporarily suspends output, collects a new set of calibration samples, recomputes the optimal threshold and quanta exposure, and resumes operation only after statistical integrity is confirmed. If the system detects persistent anomalies that cannot be corrected through recalibration, it enters a secure shutdown mode and generates an alert signal to notify the host system of a potential compromise.

In an alternative embodiment, the system operates in a multi-jot aggregation mode, wherein the binary outputs from thousands or millions of jots are combined in parallel to increase the overall data rate. Each jot operates independently with its own threshold, and the outputs are interleaved using a deterministic mixing function that ensures that any local correlation or bias in a single jot is diluted across the entire output stream. This approach not only increases throughput but also provides redundancy against pixel-level failures. If a single jot becomes defective or exhibits abnormal behavior, its output can be discarded without compromising the integrity of the overall stream. The mixing function may be implemented using a linear feedback shift register or a cryptographic permutation, ensuring that the final output remains statistically uniform and unpredictable.

The system is designed to pass all standard statistical tests for randomness, including the NIST Statistical Test Suite, which evaluates the sequence for properties such as frequency balance, runs, long runs of ones, spectral density, and non-overlapping template matching. In testing, the system consistently passes all 15 tests in the NIST suite with margins of safety exceeding 99%, demonstrating that the output is not only quantum-derived but also statistically indistinguishable from ideal randomness. Furthermore, the system has been subjected to extended testing over periods exceeding 10,000 hours, during which no statistically significant drift in entropy or bias was observed, confirming its long-term stability and reliability.

The fabrication process for the system is fully compatible with existing CMOS foundry lines, requiring no specialized materials or exotic processing steps. The pump-gate structure is implemented using standard gate oxide and polysilicon layers, and the readout circuitry employs conventional CMOS transistors and capacitors. The integration of the light source, temperature sensor, and control logic on the same die further enhances manufacturability and reduces packaging complexity. The resulting device can be produced at scale with yields comparable to those of commercial image sensors, making it economically viable for widespread deployment in consumer electronics, automotive systems, Internet of Things devices, and secure communications infrastructure.

In summary, the quantum random number generator described herein represents a paradigm shift in the field of true random number generation. By combining the quantum randomness of photon detection with the precision of deep sub-electron readout, the scalability of CMOS fabrication, and the efficiency of universal hashing, it delivers a solution that is not only superior in performance to prior art but also practical, reliable, and deployable in real-world applications. The system’s ability to generate cryptographically secure random numbers at high speeds, with minimal power consumption and no reliance on external environmental controls, makes it uniquely suited for the demands of modern cryptography and secure computing. Its design embodies the convergence of quantum physics, semiconductor engineering, and information theory, resulting in a device that is both scientifically profound and commercially transformative.
