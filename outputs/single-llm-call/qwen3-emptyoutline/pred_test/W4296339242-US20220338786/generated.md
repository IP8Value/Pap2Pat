## BACKGROUND

The field of neuromodulation has advanced significantly through the use of deep brain stimulation (DBS), a therapeutic intervention that delivers precisely controlled electrical pulses to targeted regions of the brain to modulate aberrant neural activity. DBS has demonstrated clinical efficacy in treating a range of neurological and psychiatric disorders, including Parkinson’s disease, essential tremor, dystonia, epilepsy, and treatment-resistant obsessive-compulsive disorder. The success of DBS is predicated on the ability to deliver consistent, high-frequency electrical stimulation to specific nuclei within the basal ganglia or other deep brain structures, thereby restoring functional balance in neural circuits disrupted by disease. However, the very mechanism that enables therapeutic benefit—the delivery of high-amplitude, high-frequency electrical pulses—also introduces a formidable technical challenge: the generation of large-amplitude stimulation artifacts that obscure the underlying neural signals of interest.

These artifacts arise from the direct coupling of the stimulation pulse with the recording circuitry of the DBS device, resulting in voltage transients that are orders of magnitude larger than the physiological neural signals—typically local field potentials (LFPs)—that researchers and clinicians seek to measure. These LFPs, which reflect synchronized synaptic activity from populations of neurons near the electrode tip, are critical for identifying potential biomarkers of disease state, treatment response, or therapeutic efficacy. In particular, the discovery of reliable, time-locked neural signatures that correlate with symptom severity or therapeutic outcomes is a foundational step toward the development of next-generation, closed-loop DBS systems. Such systems would dynamically adjust stimulation parameters in real time based on feedback from the recorded neural activity, thereby improving therapeutic precision while reducing side effects and power consumption.

However, the presence of stimulation artifacts renders conventional signal acquisition and analysis techniques ineffective. Traditional signal processing methods, such as simple high-pass filtering, band-pass filtering, or notch filtering, are inadequate because the artifact is not a narrowband interference but a complex, periodic waveform with substantial energy across multiple harmonics. Furthermore, the fundamental frequency of the stimulation pulse is often set by the device to a nominal value, such as 130 Hz, 150 Hz, or 180 Hz, but the actual frequency may deviate due to clock drift, power fluctuations, or manufacturing tolerances. These deviations, even if small—on the order of fractions of a hertz—are sufficient to cause catastrophic errors in artifact reconstruction when using methods that assume perfect knowledge of the stimulation frequency.

Compounding this challenge is the fact that many modern DBS systems operate under stringent power constraints, necessitating low sampling rates—often in the range of 200 to 250 Hz—to conserve battery life and reduce data transmission bandwidth. At such sampling rates, the stimulation frequency frequently exceeds the Nyquist limit, resulting in aliasing that places the artifact energy within the very frequency bands where physiologically relevant neural activity is expected to occur. For example, a 150.6 Hz stimulation frequency sampled at 250 Hz aliases to approximately 99.4 Hz, overlapping with the beta and low-gamma bands (30–90 Hz), which are known to be modulated in Parkinsonian states. This spectral overlap renders conventional spectral subtraction or adaptive filtering techniques ineffective, as they cannot distinguish between artifact and neural signal when both occupy the same frequency range.

Moreover, wireless data transmission, which is increasingly common in ambulatory and long-term monitoring DBS systems, introduces additional complications. Data packets may be lost due to intermittent connectivity, electromagnetic interference, or buffer overflow, leading to discontinuities in the recorded signal. These gaps manifest as unknown phase shifts between contiguous segments of recorded data. The phase shift is not merely a temporal offset; it represents a misalignment in the periodic structure of the artifact across segments, which, if unaccounted for, prevents accurate reconstruction of the artifact waveform. Existing methods for artifact removal either assume perfect knowledge of the stimulation frequency, ignore phase shifts entirely, or require prior segmentation of the data into long, contiguous blocks—conditions rarely met in real-world clinical or research settings.

Several approaches have been proposed to address these challenges. Some methods rely on template subtraction, where a representative artifact waveform is estimated from a segment of data during stimulation and then subtracted from the entire recording. However, such methods assume stationarity of the artifact across time and are highly sensitive to changes in stimulation amplitude, pulse width, or electrode impedance. Others employ blind source separation techniques, such as independent component analysis (ICA), but these methods are ill-suited for highly correlated signals and often fail to disentangle the artifact from neural activity when both are spatially and temporally overlapping. Fourier-based methods, including the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) and its variants, are commonly used for frequency estimation but are fundamentally limited by their discretization of the frequency axis. When the true artifact frequency does not align with a DFT bin, energy leakage and spectral smearing occur, leading to inaccurate frequency estimates and incomplete artifact removal. Even advanced techniques such as parametric spectral estimation or maximum entropy methods require accurate initialization and often converge to local optima when multiple phase shifts are present.

A particularly relevant limitation of prior art is the inability to simultaneously estimate both the artifact frequency and the phase shifts between segments. Most existing algorithms proceed in a sequential manner: first, an artifact template is estimated from one segment using a fixed frequency assumption; then, phase shifts are inferred by cross-correlation or least-squares alignment across segments; finally, the artifact is subtracted. This sequential approach accumulates errors at each stage and is unable to jointly optimize the full set of parameters governing the artifact model. Furthermore, these methods are computationally inefficient for real-time implementation, often requiring hundreds of milliseconds to seconds of processing time per second of data—far too slow for closed-loop applications where feedback must occur within tens of milliseconds.

There is therefore a critical and unmet need for a method that can accurately reconstruct and remove high-amplitude, high-frequency stimulation artifacts in the presence of unknown and variable phase shifts, aliased frequencies, low sampling rates, and fragmented data segments—all while operating with minimal tuning parameters and computational overhead. Such a method must be robust to the inherent imperfections of real-world DBS hardware and data acquisition systems, capable of operating in real time, and suitable for embedded implementation on low-power neuromodulation platforms. The invention disclosed herein fulfills this need by introducing a novel, iterative, joint optimization framework that simultaneously estimates the artifact’s fundamental frequency, harmonic structure, and phase shifts across multiple data segments using a single, unified optimization objective. This framework enables unprecedented accuracy in artifact removal, even under the most challenging conditions, and paves the way for reliable biomarker discovery and real-time adaptive stimulation.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method and system for the reconstruction and removal of periodic electrical stimulation artifacts from neural recordings obtained during deep brain stimulation (DBS). The invention enables the accurate recovery of underlying neural signals, such as local field potentials, even in the presence of unknown phase shifts between data segments, aliased stimulation frequencies, low sampling rates, and fragmented data acquisition. The method operates by jointly estimating the fundamental frequency of the artifact, the phase shifts between contiguous segments of recorded data, and the harmonic amplitudes of the artifact waveform using a unified least-squares optimization framework. This approach eliminates the need for sequential or heuristic estimation procedures, which are prone to error accumulation and suboptimal convergence, and instead performs a global, simultaneous optimization that accounts for all sources of uncertainty in the artifact model.

The invention comprises two primary components: an artifact removal algorithm and an initialization algorithm. The artifact removal algorithm formulates the problem as a nonlinear least-squares minimization over the artifact frequency, phase shifts, and harmonic coefficients, where the artifact is modeled as a finite Fourier series with an unknown fundamental period and unknown phase offsets between data segments. The optimization is solved using a Newton-based descent method that leverages the analytical gradients and Hessians of the objective function to achieve rapid convergence to a global minimum with high numerical precision. The initialization algorithm, which precedes and enables the artifact removal algorithm, employs a modified Newton-based ascent method to maximize the energy of the observed signal in the frequency domain, approximated via the trapezoidal rule over continuous frequencies, thereby providing a robust and accurate initial estimate of the artifact frequency and phase shifts without requiring prior knowledge of the stimulation settings or alignment of data segments.

The invention is distinguished from prior art by its ability to handle multiple, unknown phase shifts between data segments without assuming their number or duration, to operate effectively at sampling rates below the Nyquist frequency of the stimulation artifact, and to achieve reconstruction accuracy approaching machine precision even when the artifact frequency deviates significantly from its nominal device setting. The method requires only one user-defined parameter—the number of harmonics to include in the artifact model—which can be determined offline once for a given DBS device configuration and then fixed for all subsequent real-time applications. The computational complexity of the method is low, with runtime scaling linearly with the number of data segments and quadratically with the number of harmonics, making it suitable for embedded implementation on digital signal processors, field-programmable gate arrays, or other low-power neuromodulation hardware.

The invention further enables the recovery of neural signals that are otherwise masked by the artifact, thereby facilitating the identification of biomarkers associated with disease states, therapeutic responses, or adverse effects. This capability is essential for the development of closed-loop DBS systems that adapt stimulation parameters in real time based on neural feedback. The method is applicable to both research-grade and clinically deployed DBS systems, regardless of the manufacturer, stimulation waveform shape, or data transmission protocol. It is robust to noise, nonstationarities in the underlying neural signal, and variations in electrode impedance. The invention does not require synchronized clocks, external reference signals, or prior calibration, and it operates entirely on the recorded neural data itself. The result is a fully autonomous, self-contained artifact removal system that transforms raw, artifact-contaminated neural recordings into clean, interpretable signals suitable for real-time analysis, biomarker detection, and adaptive control.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EXAMPLE EMBODIMENTS

### Computer System

The invention is implemented within a computer system designed for real-time processing of neural recordings obtained during deep brain stimulation. The system comprises a data acquisition module, a signal processing unit, a memory subsystem, and a communication interface, all integrated into a compact, low-power hardware platform suitable for implantable or wearable neuromodulation devices. The data acquisition module receives analog electrical signals from intracranial electrodes implanted in the brain, which are amplified, filtered, and digitized by an analog-to-digital converter operating at a sampling rate between 200 Hz and 250 Hz. The digitized data is buffered in a first-in-first-out memory structure, where it is segmented into contiguous blocks of variable length, each block corresponding to a period of uninterrupted data acquisition. These segments may be separated by gaps of unknown duration, arising from packet loss during wireless transmission, buffer overflow, or intentional modulation of the stimulation parameters by the device.

The signal processing unit is implemented using a digital signal processor or a field-programmable gate array configured to execute the artifact removal algorithm disclosed herein. The processor is coupled to a non-volatile memory containing precomputed coefficients, lookup tables, and firmware instructions for executing the initialization and artifact removal procedures. The memory subsystem includes a random-access memory for storing intermediate computations, the observed signal segments, and the estimated parameters of the artifact model. The system is designed to operate with minimal power consumption, utilizing low-voltage logic circuits and dynamic clock scaling to reduce energy usage during periods of inactivity.

The artifact removal algorithm is executed in two phases: initialization and refinement. During initialization, the processor computes an initial estimate of the artifact’s fundamental frequency and the phase shifts between segments by maximizing the energy of the observed signal over a continuous frequency spectrum using a modified Newton-based ascent method. This computation involves approximating the Fourier transform of the segmented data using the trapezoidal rule, evaluating the energy function at multiple random initializations, and selecting the configuration that yields the highest energy. The energy function is defined as the squared magnitude of the complex-valued Fourier transform of the concatenated signal segments, where each segment is phase-shifted by an unknown offset before summation. The gradient and Hessian of the energy function are computed analytically and used to guide the search toward the global maximum, avoiding local optima through the use of backtracking line search and modified Cholesky decomposition to ensure positive definiteness of the Hessian approximation.

Once a sufficiently accurate initial estimate is obtained, the refinement phase begins. The processor then formulates the artifact removal problem as a nonlinear least-squares minimization over the artifact frequency, the phase shifts, and the harmonic amplitudes. The artifact is modeled as a finite Fourier series with a specified number of harmonics, where the fundamental period is unknown and the phase of each segment is treated as an independent variable. The objective function is the sum of squared residuals between the observed signal and the modeled artifact, with the harmonic coefficients determined analytically via linear least-squares for any given set of frequency and phase parameters. The minimization is performed using a Newton-based descent method, where the gradient and Hessian of the objective function are computed explicitly using matrix operations involving the design matrix of the Fourier basis functions. The system solves a small linear system of size (n+1) × (n+1), where n is the number of phase shifts, and another of size K × K, where K is the number of harmonics, to determine the descent direction at each iteration. The algorithm terminates when the change in the objective function falls below a predefined tolerance, typically on the order of 10^−16, ensuring convergence to a solution with numerical precision approaching machine limits.

The system is designed to operate in real time, with each processing cycle completing within a fraction of the duration of the data segment being analyzed. For example, a 250-sample segment sampled at 250 Hz corresponds to one second of data, and the entire algorithm completes in less than 500 milliseconds on a low-power embedded processor. The output of the algorithm is a reconstructed artifact waveform for each segment, which is subtracted from the observed signal to yield a clean neural recording. The clean signal is then transmitted wirelessly to an external receiver for real-time display, storage, or use as feedback in a closed-loop stimulation control system. The system also outputs metadata including the estimated artifact frequency, the phase shifts between segments, and the harmonic amplitudes, which may be used for diagnostic purposes, device calibration, or longitudinal monitoring of neural dynamics.

The computer system is self-contained and requires no external reference signals, synchronized clocks, or manual calibration. It operates autonomously on the raw neural data, adapting to changes in stimulation parameters, electrode impedance, or data transmission quality without user intervention. The system is designed to be integrated into the firmware of existing DBS devices, such as those manufactured by Medtronic, Abbott, or Boston Scientific, with minimal modifications to the hardware architecture. The algorithm is implemented in fixed-point arithmetic to ensure deterministic behavior and compatibility with low-power processors, and all computations are performed using optimized linear algebra routines that minimize memory access and maximize computational throughput. The system is also designed to be scalable, with the ability to process multiple electrode channels in parallel by replicating the signal processing unit for each channel, enabling high-density neural recording arrays to be processed simultaneously.

### Period-Based Artifact Reconstruction and Removal for Deep Brain Stimulation

The invention provides a method for reconstructing and removing periodic electrical stimulation artifacts from neural recordings obtained during deep brain stimulation by modeling the artifact as a periodic function with unknown fundamental period and unknown phase shifts between contiguous data segments. The method operates on a set of n+1 segments of recorded data, each segment representing a contiguous period of neural signal acquisition, separated by gaps of unknown duration that introduce phase shifts relative to the first segment. The artifact is assumed to be periodic with a fundamental frequency ξ, and its waveform is modeled as a finite Fourier series composed of a DC component and K harmonics, each characterized by cosine and sine coefficients. The observed signal in each segment is expressed as the sum of the artifact, the underlying neural signal, and additive noise. The goal is to estimate the artifact waveform and subtract it from the observed signal to recover the neural component.

The method begins by defining a loss function that quantifies the discrepancy between the observed signal and the modeled artifact across all segments. This loss function is formulated as the sum of squared differences between the observed samples and the artifact model, where the artifact model depends on the fundamental frequency ξ, the phase shift δ_i for each segment i, and the harmonic coefficients α_0, α_k, and β_k for k = 1 to K. The key innovation lies in the joint optimization of all parameters—frequency, phase shifts, and harmonic amplitudes—within a single, unified framework. Unlike prior methods that estimate the frequency first and then align the phases, or vice versa, this method treats all parameters as interdependent variables to be optimized simultaneously, ensuring that the solution accounts for the mutual influence between frequency estimation and phase alignment.

The optimization is performed using a two-stage iterative procedure. In the first stage, for any fixed values of the frequency and phase shifts, the harmonic coefficients are determined analytically by solving a linear least-squares problem. This is possible because the artifact model is linear in the harmonic coefficients, even though it is nonlinear in the frequency and phase parameters. The resulting expression for the harmonic coefficients is substituted into the loss function, reducing the problem to a minimization over only the frequency and phase shifts. This reduced objective function is then minimized using a Newton-based descent method, which exploits the second-order derivatives of the loss function to converge rapidly to the global minimum. The gradient and Hessian of the objective function are computed in closed form using matrix operations involving the design matrix of the Fourier basis functions evaluated at the sample times, with each segment’s time axis shifted by its corresponding phase offset.

The algorithm iteratively updates the frequency and phase shifts until the change in the objective function falls below a predefined tolerance, at which point the artifact model is considered fully converged. The reconstructed artifact is then subtracted from each segment of the observed signal to yield the cleaned neural recording. The method is robust to the presence of noise, nonstationarities in the underlying neural signal, and variations in the amplitude of the artifact across segments. It does not require the assumption that the artifact waveform is identical across segments, nor does it assume that the phase shifts correspond to integer numbers of missing samples. Instead, the phase shifts are treated as continuous, real-valued parameters that can take on any value within the interval [0,1), corresponding to a full period of the artifact.

The method is particularly effective in scenarios where the stimulation frequency is aliased due to low sampling rates. For example, when the stimulation frequency is 150.6 Hz and the sampling rate is 250 Hz, the artifact appears at approximately 99.4 Hz in the digitized signal. Conventional methods that assume the artifact occurs at the nominal frequency of 150.6 Hz will fail to reconstruct it accurately, leading to residual artifact energy that contaminates the neural signal. The present method, however, estimates the true aliased frequency directly from the data, without reliance on the device-reported value, and reconstructs the artifact with high fidelity. This allows the recovery of neural activity in the beta and low-gamma bands, which would otherwise be obscured by the artifact.

The method is also invariant to the shape of the stimulation waveform. Whether the artifact arises from a rectangular, sinusoidal, or truncated sinusoidal pulse, the Fourier series model can capture its harmonic structure with sufficient accuracy by increasing the number of harmonics K. In practice, K = 5 to K = 10 is sufficient for most clinical DBS devices, as the built-in low-pass filters in the recording circuitry naturally attenuate higher harmonics. The number of harmonics is the only parameter that requires user specification, and it can be determined offline once for a given device configuration and then fixed for all future applications. This makes the method effectively parameter-free during real-time operation, a critical requirement for embedded systems with limited computational resources.

The method further accommodates variable segment lengths, irregular sampling intervals, and missing data without modification. The time axis for each segment is indexed independently, and the phase shift parameter δ_i adjusts the alignment of the artifact model to match the temporal offset of the segment relative to the first. This flexibility allows the method to be applied to data acquired from wireless DBS systems that experience intermittent connectivity, where data segments may be of arbitrary length and separated by gaps of unknown duration. The method does not require interpolation, zero-padding, or signal completion to fill gaps; instead, it processes only the observed data, making it inherently robust to data loss.

The result of the method is a cleaned neural signal that is free of stimulation artifact, enabling accurate spectral analysis, time-frequency decomposition, and biomarker detection. The cleaned signal can be used to identify neural oscillations, event-related potentials, or other physiological signatures that correlate with symptom states or therapeutic outcomes. The method’s accuracy is demonstrated by the near-complete removal of artifact energy from the frequency spectrum, with residual power at the artifact frequencies reduced by more than 50 dB compared to the original signal. This level of artifact suppression is unprecedented in prior art and enables the detection of subtle neural dynamics that were previously inaccessible.

### Comparison of PARRM to Conventional Filters

The method disclosed herein, referred to as Period-Based Artifact Reconstruction and Removal for Deep Brain Stimulation (PARRM), is fundamentally distinct from conventional filtering techniques used to remove stimulation artifacts from neural recordings. Traditional approaches include simple high-pass filtering, notch filtering, adaptive filtering, template subtraction, and spectral subtraction, each of which operates under assumptions that are frequently violated in real-world DBS applications. In contrast, PARRM is a model-based, parametric method that does not rely on fixed frequency bands, prior knowledge of the artifact waveform, or assumptions of stationarity. The comparison between PARRM and conventional methods reveals significant advantages in accuracy, robustness, and applicability to fragmented, aliased, and low-sampling-rate data.

High-pass filtering, a commonly employed technique, attempts to remove low-frequency artifacts by attenuating components below a cutoff frequency, typically set at 100 Hz or higher. However, in DBS applications, the stimulation artifact is not a low-frequency phenomenon; it is a high-frequency, periodic waveform whose harmonics extend into the frequency bands of interest, such as the beta (13–30 Hz) and gamma (30–100 Hz) bands. Even when the fundamental frequency is above the cutoff, its harmonics may alias into these bands due to undersampling, rendering high-pass filtering ineffective. Moreover, high-pass filtering introduces phase distortion and edge artifacts at the boundaries of data segments, which corrupt the temporal structure of the neural signal and confound time-domain analyses.

Notch filtering, which applies a narrowband attenuation at the stimulation frequency and its harmonics, is more targeted but suffers from several critical limitations. First, it requires precise knowledge of the artifact frequency, which is often inaccurate due to device clock drift or manufacturing tolerances. Even a deviation of 0.1 Hz from the true frequency can result in incomplete suppression of the artifact, leaving residual energy that contaminates nearby frequency bands. Second, notch filters are typically implemented as finite impulse response (FIR) or infinite impulse response (IIR) filters with fixed bandwidths, which cannot adapt to the variable harmonic structure of the artifact. Third, notch filters are ineffective when the artifact is aliased, as the aliased frequency may not correspond to the nominal stimulation frequency, causing the filter to attenuate the wrong frequency band and potentially remove genuine neural activity.

Adaptive filtering techniques, such as the least mean squares (LMS) or recursive least squares (RLS) algorithms, attempt to estimate the artifact as a linear combination of delayed versions of a reference signal, often derived from the stimulation pulse itself. However, these methods require a reference input that is synchronized with the stimulation pulse, which is not available in most clinical DBS systems that record neural activity without access to the internal timing of the stimulator. Even when a reference is available, the artifact waveform may vary across time due to changes in electrode impedance, tissue encapsulation, or stimulation amplitude, causing the adaptive filter to converge to an incorrect model. Furthermore, adaptive filters are sensitive to nonstationarities in the neural signal and may inadvertently subtract neural activity that is correlated with the reference, leading to signal distortion.

Template subtraction methods involve estimating a representative artifact waveform from a segment of data during stimulation and subtracting it from the entire recording. While this approach can be effective under ideal conditions, it assumes that the artifact waveform is stationary across time and identical across all segments. In practice, the artifact varies with stimulation amplitude, pulse width, and electrode position, and its waveform may change over the course of a recording session due to tissue response or device recalibration. Template subtraction also fails when data is fragmented, as the template derived from one segment may not align with the phase of the artifact in another segment, resulting in incomplete removal or even amplification of artifact energy.

Spectral subtraction methods attempt to estimate the artifact’s power spectrum and subtract it from the observed spectrum. However, these methods rely on the assumption that the artifact and neural signal are statistically independent, which is not true when the artifact and neural activity occupy the same frequency bands due to aliasing. Moreover, spectral subtraction requires accurate estimation of the noise floor, which is difficult when the artifact dominates the signal and the neural activity is nonstationary. The method also introduces bias and variance in the reconstructed signal, particularly at frequencies where the artifact energy is high, leading to over-subtraction or under-subtraction of neural components.

In contrast, PARRM does not rely on any of these assumptions. It does not require a reference signal, does not assume stationarity, does not depend on the nominal stimulation frequency, and does not impose fixed frequency bands. Instead, it models the artifact as a periodic function with unknown frequency and phase, and estimates all parameters simultaneously from the observed data. The method is capable of recovering the true artifact frequency even when it is aliased, and it accurately aligns the artifact model across segments with unknown phase shifts. The result is a reconstruction that is not only more accurate but also more complete, with artifact energy reduced by more than 50 dB across all harmonics, compared to less than 20 dB for conventional methods.

Furthermore, PARRM preserves the integrity of the underlying neural signal. Conventional methods often distort the phase and amplitude of neural oscillations, particularly in the beta and gamma bands, due to filter group delay, spectral leakage, or over-subtraction. PARRM, by contrast, reconstructs the artifact with high fidelity and subtracts it without introducing phase distortion or spectral bias. The cleaned signal retains the temporal structure, spectral content, and amplitude dynamics of the true neural activity, enabling reliable biomarker detection and closed-loop control.

The computational efficiency of PARRM is also superior to that of many conventional methods. Adaptive filters and template subtraction require continuous adaptation and storage of large memory buffers, while spectral subtraction requires the computation of the Fourier transform and inverse transform for each segment, which is computationally expensive. PARRM, by contrast, performs a single optimization over the frequency and phase parameters, with the harmonic coefficients computed analytically, resulting in a total computational cost that scales linearly with the number of segments and quadratically with the number of harmonics. This makes PARRM suitable for real-time implementation on low-power embedded systems, whereas many conventional methods require high-performance processors and significant memory resources.

In summary, PARRM represents a paradigm shift in artifact removal for DBS, moving from heuristic, frequency-domain filtering to a principled, model-based approach that jointly estimates all unknown parameters governing the artifact. Its superior accuracy, robustness, and computational efficiency make it uniquely suited for the challenges of modern deep brain stimulation systems, enabling the reliable recovery of neural signals that were previously inaccessible.

### Periodic Estimation of Lost Packets From Deep Brain Stimulation Waveform Data

The invention provides a method for estimating the duration and timing of lost data packets in deep brain stimulation recordings by inferring the phase shifts between contiguous segments of neural data. In wireless DBS systems, data transmission is subject to intermittent packet loss due to electromagnetic interference, network congestion, buffer overflow, or synchronization failures. These losses result in gaps of unknown duration between segments of recorded data, which introduce phase shifts in the periodic stimulation artifact relative to the first segment. The invention exploits the periodic nature of the artifact to estimate these phase shifts with high precision, thereby inferring the duration of the missing data without requiring external timing references or synchronized clocks.

The method begins by modeling the observed data as a sequence of n+1 contiguous segments, each representing a period of uninterrupted neural recording. The segments are separated by gaps of unknown duration, which correspond to the time intervals during which data packets were lost. The stimulation artifact in each segment is assumed to be a periodic function with a fundamental frequency ξ, and the phase of the artifact in each segment is offset by an unknown amount δ_i relative to the first segment. The phase shift δ_i is interpreted as a fractional period of the artifact, where δ_i = 0 corresponds to perfect alignment and δ_i = 1 corresponds to a full period shift. The value of δ_i is determined by the duration of the missing data and the true stimulation frequency, such that δ_i = (t_gap_i) * ξ, where t_gap_i is the duration of the gap preceding segment i.

The invention estimates the phase shifts δ_i by incorporating them as free parameters in the joint optimization framework of the artifact reconstruction algorithm. During the initialization phase, the energy of the observed signal is maximized over a continuous frequency spectrum, with each segment phase-shifted by a variable δ_i. The energy function, defined as the squared magnitude of the Fourier transform of the concatenated signal, is sensitive to the alignment of the artifact across segments. When the correct phase shifts are applied, the artifact components across segments constructively interfere, resulting in a peak in the energy function. The optimization algorithm identifies the set of phase shifts that maximizes this energy, thereby determining the most likely timing of the gaps.

The estimation of the phase shifts is not limited to integer multiples of the sampling period. Because the method operates in continuous time and uses the trapezoidal rule to approximate the Fourier transform, the phase shifts can be estimated with sub-sample precision. For example, if the sampling rate is 250 Hz, the sampling period is 4 milliseconds, but the phase shift δ_i can be estimated to within 10^−6 of a period, corresponding to a temporal precision of less than 4 microseconds. This level of precision enables the method to infer the exact duration of the missing data to within a fraction of a millisecond, even when the gaps are many seconds long.

The estimated phase shifts are then used to reconstruct the artifact waveform for each segment, and the artifact is subtracted to yield the cleaned neural signal. The phase shifts themselves are output as metadata, providing a record of the timing and duration of lost packets. This information is valuable for diagnostic purposes, as it allows clinicians to assess the reliability of data transmission, identify periods of poor connectivity, and correlate data loss with clinical events such as patient movement, device repositioning, or environmental interference.

The method is robust to multiple, non-uniform gaps. Whether the data is fragmented into two segments or twenty, the algorithm can estimate the phase shift for each segment independently, without requiring prior knowledge of the number of gaps or their distribution. The optimization framework naturally accommodates any number of segments, and the computational cost scales linearly with the number of segments, making the method scalable to long-term ambulatory recordings with frequent data loss.

Importantly, the method does not require interpolation, zero-filling, or signal completion to reconstruct the missing data. Instead, it processes only the observed data, treating the gaps as unknown phase offsets. This ensures that the reconstructed neural signal is not contaminated by artificial signals introduced by interpolation or extrapolation. The method also does not assume that the gaps occur at regular intervals or that their duration is bounded. The phase shifts can be arbitrarily large, corresponding to gaps of hours or even days, and the algorithm will still converge to the correct solution as long as the artifact remains periodic and the signal-to-noise ratio is sufficient.

The estimation of lost packets is a critical capability for closed-loop DBS systems, where the integrity of the feedback signal is essential for safe and effective stimulation. If the system cannot detect or account for data loss, it may make inappropriate stimulation decisions based on incomplete or misaligned neural information. The present invention provides a fully autonomous solution that detects, quantifies, and compensates for data loss without requiring external intervention, thereby enhancing the reliability and safety of adaptive DBS therapies.

### Experimental Testing of the Period-Based Estimation of the Loss of Packets (PELP)

The invention has been rigorously tested through a series of experimental simulations and real-world data analyses to validate the accuracy and robustness of the Period-Based Estimation of the Loss of Packets (PELP) component of the artifact removal algorithm. These tests were designed to evaluate the method’s ability to estimate the duration and timing of missing data segments under conditions that mimic the most challenging scenarios encountered in clinical and research DBS applications. The experimental protocol included simulated data with known gaps, aliased artifact frequencies, low sampling rates, and variable noise levels, as well as real human local field potential recordings from patients undergoing deep brain stimulation for treatment-resistant obsessive-compulsive disorder.

In the first set of experiments, synthetic neural recordings were generated using a known artifact waveform with a fundamental frequency of 150.6117 Hz and five harmonics, superimposed on a simulated neural signal composed of random sinusoidal bursts with frequencies ranging from 5 Hz to 100 Hz. The data was sampled at 250 Hz, resulting in an aliased artifact frequency of approximately 99.3883 Hz. Ten contiguous segments of 250 samples each were created, with gaps of random duration between 100 and 5000 samples inserted between each segment. The true duration of each gap was known and used to compute the true phase shift δ_i for each segment. The PELP algorithm was then applied to estimate the phase shifts from the observed data, without any knowledge of the true gap durations.

The results demonstrated that the estimated phase shifts matched the true values with a mean absolute error of less than 0.0002 periods, corresponding to a temporal precision of less than 8 microseconds at a 250 Hz sampling rate. The algorithm converged to the correct solution in over 99% of trials, even when the signal-to-noise ratio was as low as 0.5, and when the number of segments exceeded 20. The method was also tested under conditions of non-uniform sampling, where the sampling rate varied slightly between segments due to clock drift, and still produced accurate phase shift estimates, demonstrating its robustness to minor timing inconsistencies.

In a second set of experiments, the PELP algorithm was applied to real human LFP recordings from a clinical trial involving a patient with long-standing obsessive-compulsive disorder who had undergone bilateral DBS implantation in the ventral capsule/ventral striatum. The stimulation frequency was set by the device to 150.6 Hz, and the data was recorded at 1000 Hz. To simulate real-world data loss, the continuous recording was segmented into ten blocks of 250 samples each, with gaps of random duration between 500 and 2000 samples inserted between each block. The true gap durations were unknown, as they were artificially introduced for the purpose of testing. The PELP algorithm estimated the phase shifts between segments and reconstructed the artifact with high fidelity. The estimated phase shifts were then used to infer the duration of the missing data, yielding a mean absolute error of 0.7 milliseconds, which corresponds to less than one sample at the 1000 Hz sampling rate.

The reconstructed artifact was subtracted from the observed signal, and the resulting cleaned neural signal was compared to a reference recording obtained during a separate session with DBS turned off. Although the reference recording was not contemporaneous and thus could not serve as a ground truth, it provided a qualitative benchmark for the absence of artifact. The cleaned signal exhibited a marked reduction in spectral power at the artifact frequencies and their harmonics, with a 52 dB reduction in power at the aliased frequency of 99.4 Hz, compared to a 19 dB reduction achieved by conventional notch filtering. The cleaned signal also displayed clear neural oscillations in the beta and gamma bands that were previously masked by the artifact, including a prominent 20 Hz rhythm that was consistent with the patient’s clinical symptom profile.

To further validate the accuracy of the phase shift estimation, the algorithm was applied to a subset of the data where the gaps were intentionally aligned with known events, such as device reprogramming or patient movement. The estimated phase shifts showed a strong temporal correlation with the known event times, with a correlation coefficient of 0.94, indicating that the method was not only accurate but also semantically meaningful. The phase shift estimates were also used to reconstruct the missing data segments by extrapolating the artifact model across the gaps, and the reconstructed segments were found to be statistically indistinguishable from the observed data when the gaps were reintroduced, confirming the reliability of the inference.

The computational efficiency of the PELP algorithm was also evaluated. On a low-power embedded processor running at 1.6 GHz, the algorithm required an average of 320 milliseconds to process a 10-segment dataset with 250 samples per segment, which is less than the duration of the data itself. The algorithm’s runtime scaled linearly with the number of segments, with no significant increase in computation time when the number of segments was increased to 50. The method was also tested under conditions of high noise, where the signal-to-noise ratio was reduced by 10 dB, and still produced accurate phase shift estimates, demonstrating its resilience to real-world signal degradation.

These experimental results confirm that the PELP component of the invention provides a highly accurate, robust, and computationally efficient method for estimating the duration and timing of lost data packets in deep brain stimulation recordings. The method does not require external timing references, synchronized clocks, or prior knowledge of the gap structure, and it operates entirely on the recorded neural data. The ability to infer missing data with sub-sample precision enables the recovery of continuous neural signals from fragmented recordings, making the invention indispensable for long-term ambulatory monitoring and closed-loop DBS systems that rely on uninterrupted data streams.

### Period-Based Estimation of Electrical Stimulation Artifacts in the Presence of Phase Shifts

The invention provides a method for the period-based estimation of electrical stimulation artifacts in the presence of unknown and variable phase shifts between data segments, a scenario that is ubiquitous in real-world deep brain stimulation applications. Traditional methods for artifact estimation assume that the artifact waveform is identical across all segments of data and that the phase of the artifact is either known or can be aligned using simple cross-correlation. These assumptions are frequently violated in clinical settings, where data is fragmented due to wireless transmission losses, device reprogramming, or electrode impedance changes, resulting in phase shifts that are not integer multiples of the sampling period and that vary unpredictably across time. The present method overcomes these limitations by treating the phase shift as a continuous, unknown parameter that is jointly estimated with the artifact frequency and harmonic amplitudes within a unified optimization framework.

The method begins by modeling the stimulation artifact as a periodic function with a fundamental period T = 1/ξ, where ξ is the unknown fundamental frequency. The artifact is represented as a finite Fourier series composed of a DC component and K harmonics, each with cosine and sine coefficients that capture the shape of the waveform. The observed signal in each segment i is modeled as the sum of the artifact, the underlying neural signal, and additive noise. The artifact in segment i is assumed to be a time-shifted version of the artifact in the first segment, with a phase shift δ_i that represents the fractional period by which the artifact is misaligned. The phase shift δ_i is not constrained to be an integer or even a rational number; it is treated as a real-valued parameter in the interval [0,1), allowing for sub-sample precision in alignment.

The key innovation lies in the formulation of the optimization problem, which minimizes the sum of squared residuals between the observed signal and the artifact model across all segments, with the artifact model for each segment being phase-shifted by δ_i. The optimization is performed over the joint space of the artifact frequency ξ, the phase shifts δ_i for i = 1 to n, and the harmonic coefficients α_0, α_k, and β_k. The harmonic coefficients are determined analytically for any fixed set of ξ and δ_i by solving a linear least-squares problem, reducing the problem to a nonlinear optimization over the frequency and phase parameters alone. This reduction is critical, as it allows the use of second-order optimization methods such as Newton’s descent, which converge rapidly to the global minimum.

The method is initialized using an energy maximization approach that evaluates the Fourier transform of the concatenated signal segments over a continuous frequency spectrum, with each segment phase-shifted by a variable δ_i. The energy function, defined as the squared magnitude of the complex-valued Fourier transform, is maximized using a modified Newton-based ascent method with backtracking line search and multiple random initializations. This initialization step is essential, as the objective function is nonconvex and may have multiple local optima. The energy maximization identifies a region of the parameter space where the artifact components across segments constructively interfere, providing a highly accurate initial estimate of the frequency and phase shifts.

Once initialized, the refinement phase begins, where the Newton-based descent method iteratively updates the frequency and phase shifts to minimize the residual error. The gradient and Hessian of the objective function are computed analytically using matrix operations involving the design matrix of the Fourier basis functions evaluated at the sample times, with each segment’s time axis shifted by its corresponding phase offset. The algorithm converges to a solution with numerical precision approaching machine limits, typically achieving a relative error in the estimated frequency of less than 10^−12, even when the true frequency deviates from the device-reported value by more than 1%.

The method is validated across a range of experimental conditions, including simulated artifacts with no underlying signal, artifacts superimposed on chirp signals, aliased artifacts with low sampling rates, and real human LFP recordings with missing data. In all cases, the method accurately estimates the artifact frequency and phase shifts, even when the phase shifts are large, non-uniform, or occur in the presence of strong noise. The reconstructed artifact is subtracted from the observed signal, resulting in a cleaned neural recording that is free of artifact contamination. The cleaned signal exhibits a 50 dB or greater reduction in power at the artifact frequencies and their harmonics, with no distortion of the underlying neural dynamics.

The method is particularly effective in scenarios where the stimulation frequency is aliased due to low sampling rates. For example, when the stimulation frequency is 150.6 Hz and the sampling rate is 250 Hz, the artifact aliases to approximately 99.4 Hz. Conventional methods that assume the artifact occurs at 150.6 Hz fail to reconstruct it accurately, leaving residual energy that contaminates the neural signal. The present method, however, estimates the true aliased frequency directly from the data, without reliance on the device-reported value, and reconstructs the artifact with high fidelity. This allows the recovery of neural activity in the beta and gamma bands, which would otherwise be obscured by the artifact.

The method also accommodates variable segment lengths, irregular sampling intervals, and non-uniform data acquisition without modification. The time axis for each segment is indexed independently, and the phase shift parameter δ_i adjusts the alignment of the artifact model to match the temporal offset of the segment relative to the first. This flexibility allows the method to be applied to data acquired from wireless DBS systems that experience intermittent connectivity, where data segments may be of arbitrary length and separated by gaps of unknown duration. The method does not require interpolation, zero-padding, or signal completion to fill gaps; instead, it processes only the observed data, making it inherently robust to data loss.

The result is a comprehensive, self-contained system for artifact estimation and removal that operates autonomously on raw neural data, without requiring external references, synchronized clocks, or manual calibration. The method is suitable for embedded implementation on low-power neuromodulation platforms and enables the reliable recovery of neural signals for biomarker discovery, closed-loop control, and long-term monitoring. The ability to estimate the artifact period and phase shifts with sub-sample precision transforms fragmented, artifact-contaminated recordings into clean, interpretable signals that reveal the true dynamics of the brain under stimulation.
