## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of genetic therapeutics, specifically to methods and compositions for the treatment of autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa through allele-specific genomic ablation. More particularly, the invention provides a targeted gene editing strategy utilizing CRISPR/Cas9 systems to selectively disrupt a disease-causing mutant allele while preserving the function of the corresponding wild-type allele in photoreceptor cells of the retina. The invention is particularly directed to the treatment of retinal degenerative diseases caused by dominant-negative mutations in the rhodopsin gene, wherein a single nucleotide difference between the mutant and wild-type alleles enables discrimination by the CRISPR/Cas9 system through the presence or absence of a protospacer adjacent motif. The invention further encompasses the use of such allele-specific ablation to halt photoreceptor degeneration, restore proper protein trafficking, preserve retinal architecture, and maintain visual function in vivo. The methods and compositions described herein are applicable to a broad class of dominant retinal disorders characterized by point mutations that generate novel PAM sequences, thereby enabling selective targeting without the need for exogenous gene supplementation.

## BACKGROUND

Retinitis pigmentosa represents a heterogeneous group of inherited retinal degenerative diseases characterized by progressive loss of photoreceptor cells, leading to night blindness, peripheral vision loss, and ultimately, central vision impairment. While many forms of retinitis pigmentosa are inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern, a significant subset of cases arises from autosomal dominant inheritance, wherein a single mutant allele is sufficient to drive disease pathogenesis. Unlike recessive disorders, which result from loss of function and are amenable to gene replacement therapy, autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa is typically caused by gain-of-function or dominant-negative mutations that confer toxic properties to the mutant protein. The most common genetic cause of autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa involves mutations in the rhodopsin gene, which encodes the light-sensitive pigment essential for phototransduction in rod photoreceptors. Among these, the S334ter mutation—resulting in a premature stop codon at position 334 of the rhodopsin protein—has been extensively studied in transgenic rodent models and closely recapitulates the clinical and pathological features observed in human patients with class-I RHO mistrafficking mutations.

In individuals carrying the S334ter mutation, the truncated rhodopsin protein (RHO S334 ) lacks critical C-terminal residues necessary for proper folding, post-translational modification, and trafficking to the photoreceptor outer segment. Instead, the misfolded protein accumulates in the endoplasmic reticulum and other intracellular compartments of the photoreceptor cell body, triggering endoplasmic reticulum stress, activation of the unfolded protein response, and ultimately, apoptotic cell death. The progressive loss of rod photoreceptors leads to secondary degeneration of cone photoreceptors and disruption of synaptic connectivity within the inner retina, culminating in irreversible vision loss. Conventional therapeutic strategies for autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa have been limited by the necessity to suppress or eliminate the mutant transcript without compromising the expression of the wild-type allele, which remains essential for photoreceptor function. Approaches such as RNA interference and antisense oligonucleotides have demonstrated partial efficacy in preclinical models but are inherently transient, require repeated administration, and carry risks of off-target silencing or immune activation. Moreover, these methods do not address the underlying genomic defect and are incapable of achieving permanent correction.

Gene replacement therapy, which has shown success in treating recessive retinal disorders such as Leber congenital amaurosis caused by RPE65 mutations, is not viable for autosomal dominant conditions because the introduction of an exogenous wild-type copy of the gene does not neutralize the toxic effects of the endogenous mutant protein. In fact, co-expression of wild-type and mutant rhodopsin may exacerbate cellular stress through heterodimerization or competitive interference with trafficking machinery. Thus, a therapeutic strategy that selectively inactivates the mutant allele while leaving the wild-type allele intact represents a compelling and unmet need in the field of inherited retinal disease. The emergence of CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing technology has opened new possibilities for precise, permanent correction of disease-causing mutations at the DNA level. However, the application of CRISPR/Cas9 to dominant disorders presents a unique challenge: the requirement to distinguish between two nearly identical alleles differing by a single nucleotide, without introducing off-target mutations or compromising the function of the essential wild-type allele.

Previous attempts to achieve allele-specific editing have relied on strategies such as exploiting single-nucleotide polymorphisms within the guide RNA binding site, using high-fidelity Cas9 variants, or employing base editors to introduce silent mutations that disrupt the mutant allele’s open reading frame. These approaches have been limited by low efficiency, unpredictable cleavage outcomes, or the inability to achieve sufficient discrimination between alleles in vivo. Furthermore, many such strategies require the introduction of additional genetic elements, such as homology-directed repair templates, which are inefficient in post-mitotic cells like photoreceptors and carry risks of insertional mutagenesis. There remains a critical gap in the art for a method that enables robust, selective, and permanent ablation of a dominant disease allele using a single CRISPR/Cas9 construct, without the need for exogenous gene supplementation, without inducing detectable off-target effects, and without compromising the viability or function of the wild-type allele. The present invention fulfills this need by leveraging a naturally occurring, single-nucleotide divergence between the mutant and wild-type rhodopsin alleles that generates a protospacer adjacent motif unique to the mutant allele, thereby enabling Cas9 to cleave the mutant sequence while leaving the wild-type sequence intact.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and non-obvious method for the treatment of autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa by selectively ablating a disease-causing mutant allele of the rhodopsin gene using a CRISPR/Cas9 system that discriminates between mutant and wild-type alleles based on the presence of a protospacer adjacent motif unique to the mutant sequence. The method involves the delivery of a CRISPR/Cas9 construct into photoreceptor cells of a subject suffering from autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa caused by a mutation that generates a novel PAM sequence, wherein the construct comprises a Cas9 endonuclease and a guide RNA designed to hybridize to a target sequence immediately upstream of the mutant-specific PAM, such that cleavage occurs exclusively at the mutant allele. The guide RNA is engineered to contain a sequence complementary to the mutant allele that differs from the wild-type allele by a single nucleotide positioned such that the wild-type allele lacks a functional PAM sequence required for Cas9 activity, thereby preventing cleavage of the wild-type allele. Upon cleavage, the mutant allele undergoes error-prone non-homologous end joining, resulting in insertions or deletions that disrupt the open reading frame and prevent production of the toxic mutant protein. The wild-type allele remains unaltered and continues to express functional rhodopsin protein, which is properly trafficked to the photoreceptor outer segment, thereby restoring phototransduction and halting photoreceptor degeneration.

In a preferred embodiment, the mutation is the S334ter mutation in the rhodopsin gene, wherein the mutant allele contains a thymine-guanine-guanine (TGG) protospacer adjacent motif at a position corresponding to nucleotide 10 of the guide RNA binding site, while the wild-type allele contains a thymine-guanine-cytosine (TGC) sequence at the same position, rendering it non-functional as a PAM for SpCas9. The guide RNA is designed to target a region in exon 1 of the rhodopsin gene immediately upstream of this PAM, such that cleavage occurs only in the presence of the TGG motif. The CRISPR/Cas9 construct is delivered via subretinal injection of a plasmid vector encoding the Cas9 endonuclease and the guide RNA under the control of a photoreceptor-specific or constitutive promoter, optionally linked to a fluorescent reporter such as mCherry to facilitate tracking of transfected cells. Electroporation is employed to enhance transfection efficiency in photoreceptors, which are largely post-mitotic and refractory to conventional viral transduction methods. Following delivery, the mutant rhodopsin transcript is ablated, resulting in the elimination of mislocalized rhodopsin protein from the photoreceptor cell body, restoration of wild-type rhodopsin trafficking to the outer segment, preservation of photoreceptor nuclei in the outer nuclear layer, maintenance of synaptic connectivity with bipolar cells, and significant improvement in visual acuity as measured by optokinetic response.

The invention further encompasses the use of this method for the treatment of other autosomal dominant retinal degenerations caused by point mutations that generate novel PAM sequences in the coding region of disease-associated genes. The method is applicable to any dominant mutation in which a single nucleotide substitution creates a PAM sequence absent in the wild-type allele, and wherein the wild-type allele is haplosufficient for maintaining normal cellular function. The invention demonstrates that even partial ablation of the mutant allele—achieved in as little as 30% of transfected photoreceptors—is sufficient to rescue retinal structure and function, due to the cell-autonomous nature of rhodopsin toxicity and the high expression level of wild-type rhodopsin required for phototransduction. The invention further shows that the therapeutic effect is durable, with preservation of retinal architecture and visual function observed for at least 39 days following a single treatment, and that no detectable off-target cleavage occurs at eight predicted off-target loci with up to four nucleotide mismatches. The method avoids the need for exogenous gene delivery, homology-directed repair, or RNA interference, and instead achieves permanent correction through targeted genomic disruption. The invention thus provides a safe, effective, and broadly applicable strategy for treating a wide range of dominant inherited retinal diseases.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Genomic Editing.

The present invention provides a method for the permanent correction of autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa through targeted genomic editing of the rhodopsin gene in photoreceptor cells. Unlike conventional gene therapy approaches that rely on the addition of exogenous genetic material to compensate for a loss of function, the method described herein achieves therapeutic benefit by selectively eliminating the expression of a dominant-negative mutant allele while preserving the endogenous wild-type allele. This is accomplished through the use of a CRISPR/Cas9 system that exploits a single-nucleotide difference between the mutant and wild-type alleles to achieve allele-specific cleavage. The key innovation lies in the recognition that a point mutation in the rhodopsin gene, specifically the S334ter mutation, not only alters the amino acid sequence of the encoded protein but also introduces a novel protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) that is absent in the wild-type allele. This PAM, which consists of the trinucleotide sequence 5′-TGG-3′, is required for the binding and activation of the Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 endonuclease. In the wild-type rhodopsin allele, the corresponding sequence is 5′-TGC-3′, which is not recognized by Cas9 under physiological conditions, thereby rendering the wild-type allele resistant to cleavage.

The method involves the design of a guide RNA whose target sequence is complementary to a region of the rhodopsin gene immediately upstream of this mutant-specific PAM. The guide RNA is engineered such that its 20-nucleotide spacer sequence is perfectly complementary to the mutant allele, with the critical nucleotide difference located at position 10 relative to the PAM. This positioning ensures that the mismatch between the guide RNA and the wild-type allele does not interfere with hybridization efficiency, while simultaneously preventing Cas9 from recognizing the wild-type sequence as a valid target due to the absence of a functional PAM. Upon delivery of the CRISPR/Cas9 construct into photoreceptor cells, the Cas9 protein, guided by the specific RNA sequence, binds to the mutant allele and introduces a double-strand break approximately three nucleotides upstream of the PAM. This break is repaired by the cell’s endogenous non-homologous end joining pathway, which is inherently error-prone and frequently results in small insertions or deletions at the cleavage site. These indels disrupt the open reading frame of the mutant rhodopsin gene, leading to premature termination of translation, nonsense-mediated decay of the transcript, and complete absence of the truncated, misfolded RHO S334 protein.

The consequence of this targeted disruption is the elimination of the primary driver of photoreceptor toxicity—the accumulation of mislocalized rhodopsin in the cell body. Without the presence of the mutant protein, endoplasmic reticulum stress is alleviated, the unfolded protein response is deactivated, and apoptotic signaling pathways are no longer triggered. The wild-type rhodopsin allele, which remains intact and unaltered, continues to produce full-length, properly folded rhodopsin protein that is efficiently trafficked to the photoreceptor outer segment via the normal ciliary transport machinery. The restoration of proper rhodopsin localization enables the reformation of photoreceptor outer segments, which are essential for the capture of photons and the initiation of the phototransduction cascade. The preservation of outer segment structure, in turn, supports the survival of photoreceptor cell bodies, maintains synaptic connectivity with second-order neurons in the inner retina, and ultimately preserves visual function.

The method is uniquely suited for the treatment of autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa because it does not require the overexpression of exogenous rhodopsin, which could lead to protein aggregation or saturation of trafficking pathways. Moreover, it does not rely on transient suppression mechanisms such as RNA interference, which are subject to degradation, dilution, and immune recognition. Instead, the genomic edit is permanent and heritable within the treated photoreceptor population, which, as terminally differentiated cells, do not divide and therefore do not dilute the edit over time. The therapeutic effect is thus sustained for the lifetime of the cell, and because photoreceptors are long-lived, the potential for long-term vision rescue is substantial. The invention further demonstrates that even partial ablation of the mutant allele—achieved in approximately 30% of photoreceptors—is sufficient to confer significant phenotypic rescue, as the remaining wild-type rhodopsin expression exceeds the threshold required for normal photoreceptor function. This is consistent with prior findings that rhodopsin levels between 50% and 200% of wild-type expression are clinically asymptomatic, and that even 10% of wild-type rhodopsin expression is sufficient to rescue the phenotype in homozygous knockout models. Thus, the method leverages the inherent haplosufficiency of the wild-type rhodopsin allele to achieve therapeutic benefit without the need for gene supplementation, thereby circumventing the major limitations of traditional gene replacement strategies.

The method is also highly specific, as demonstrated by the absence of detectable off-target cleavage at eight predicted off-target loci with up to four nucleotide mismatches to the guide RNA sequence. This specificity arises from the dual requirement for both perfect guide RNA complementarity and the presence of the correct PAM sequence. Even if a mismatch occurs within the guide RNA binding site, cleavage will not occur unless the PAM is present. In the case of the wild-type allele, the PAM is absent, so even a perfectly complementary guide RNA cannot induce cleavage. This provides a built-in safety mechanism that is not available in conventional gene editing approaches that rely solely on guide RNA complementarity. Furthermore, because the mutant allele is present in a single copy in the transgenic model used herein, its ablation is absolute for conferring photoreceptor survival. Each photoreceptor expresses only one mutant allele, and once that allele is disrupted, no further toxic protein can be produced. This contrasts with heterozygous human patients, who carry one mutant and one wild-type allele; in such cases, the method ensures that the wild-type allele remains functional, while the mutant allele is permanently silenced.

The method is applicable not only to the S334ter mutation but to any dominant mutation in the rhodopsin gene or other retinal disease genes that generates a novel PAM sequence through a single nucleotide substitution. For example, other dominant rhodopsin mutations such as P23H, T17M, and G188R have been shown to arise from point mutations that could, in principle, create new PAM sequences depending on the local genomic context. The present invention provides a generalizable framework for identifying such mutations and designing allele-specific guide RNAs that exploit the PAM requirement for Cas9 activity. This approach can be extended to other dominant retinal diseases, such as those caused by mutations in PRPH2, RDS, or IMPDH1, provided that the mutation generates a PAM sequence absent in the wild-type allele. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the treatment of dominant inherited diseases, moving from suppression of mutant transcripts to permanent genomic inactivation of the mutant allele, while preserving the function of the wild-type allele.

### Animal Model

The invention is demonstrated using a well-characterized transgenic rat model of autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa known as the S334ter-3 line. This model was generated by the insertion of a mouse genomic fragment containing the Rho S334ter mutation into the rat genome, resulting in the stable expression of the mutant rhodopsin protein under the control of the native rat rhodopsin promoter. The S334ter-3 rat exhibits a rapid and progressive degeneration of rod photoreceptors that closely mirrors the clinical course observed in human patients with class-I RHO mistrafficking mutations. Beginning at postnatal day 11, photoreceptor nuclei in the outer nuclear layer begin to disappear, and by postnatal day 28, the outer nuclear layer is reduced to a single discontinuous row of surviving nuclei. This degeneration is accompanied by the complete loss of rod-mediated visual function and the subsequent secondary degeneration of cone photoreceptors. The model is particularly valuable for testing allele-specific gene editing strategies because the mutant allele is expressed at physiological levels, the protein is mislocalized in a manner identical to that observed in human patients, and the degenerative cascade is cell-autonomous, meaning that photoreceptor death is driven solely by the presence of the mutant rhodopsin protein within each individual cell.

The S334ter-3 rat model is genetically homogeneous, with all animals carrying a single copy of the mutant transgene on a defined genetic background. This homogeneity ensures reproducible disease progression and allows for precise quantification of therapeutic outcomes. The model also exhibits a clear phenotypic distinction between the mutant and wild-type rhodopsin proteins, which can be resolved using isoform-specific antibodies. An antibody directed against the N-terminal region of rhodopsin detects both the full-length wild-type protein and the truncated mutant protein, while an antibody directed against the C-terminal region detects only the wild-type protein, as the mutant protein lacks the final 15 amino acids that contain the epitope. This differential labeling enables precise assessment of protein localization and abundance in treated versus untreated retinas. In untreated S334ter-3 retinas, N-terminal rhodopsin immunoreactivity is diffuse and concentrated in the cell body, consistent with mislocalization, while C-terminal labeling is absent or severely diminished, confirming the absence of properly trafficked wild-type rhodopsin. In contrast, following treatment with the allele-specific CRISPR/Cas9 construct, N-terminal labeling is absent from the cell body in transfected regions, while C-terminal labeling becomes robust and polarized toward the outer segment, indicating restoration of normal trafficking.

The degeneration of photoreceptors in this model is accompanied by profound structural and functional deficits. The outer nuclear layer, which normally contains multiple rows of photoreceptor nuclei, is reduced to a single layer by postnatal day 33. Photoreceptor outer segments, which are essential for light capture, are completely absent in untreated animals. Synaptic connections between photoreceptors and bipolar cells are disrupted, as evidenced by the loss of protein kinase C-alpha immunoreactivity in rod bipolar cells and the disorganization of synaptic terminals in the outer plexiform layer. Visual function, as measured by the optokinetic reflex, is severely impaired, with treated animals showing no measurable response to moving gratings at spatial frequencies above 0.1 cycles per degree. These features provide a comprehensive and quantifiable set of endpoints for assessing the efficacy of the gene editing intervention.

The S334ter-3 model is particularly advantageous for testing the present invention because the mutant allele contains a single-nucleotide difference from the wild-type allele that generates the TGG PAM sequence required for Cas9 cleavage. This difference is not present in the endogenous rat rhodopsin gene, which is fully wild-type, and is instead introduced by the transgenic insertion of the mouse Rho S334ter sequence. This ensures that any observed cleavage is specific to the mutant allele and not to the endogenous wild-type gene, thereby eliminating confounding effects from off-target editing of the native genome. The model also allows for direct comparison of treated and untreated retinas within the same animal, as each animal receives a unilateral subretinal injection, with the contralateral eye serving as an internal control. This within-animal design eliminates inter-individual variability and provides a highly sensitive measure of therapeutic efficacy.

The utility of the S334ter-3 model extends beyond its phenotypic fidelity to human disease. The model is amenable to surgical manipulation, including subretinal injection and electroporation, which are critical for delivering the CRISPR/Cas9 construct to photoreceptor cells. The timing of intervention is also well-defined, as the degenerative process begins at postnatal day 11, allowing for treatment at postnatal day 0, before significant cell loss has occurred. This early intervention is critical for demonstrating that the method can prevent, rather than merely slow, degeneration. The model also permits the use of fluorescent reporters, such as mCherry, to track transfected cells and correlate gene editing with phenotypic rescue. The presence of mCherry expression in transfected photoreceptors allows for precise mapping of the spatial extent of treatment and enables the correlation of editing efficiency with structural and functional outcomes. The model thus provides a robust, reproducible, and clinically relevant platform for validating the therapeutic potential of allele-specific CRISPR/Cas9 editing in autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa.

### CRISPR/Cas9 Constructs, Generally

The CRISPR/Cas9 constructs employed in the present invention are engineered to achieve highly specific, allele-selective genomic disruption of the mutant rhodopsin allele while leaving the wild-type allele intact. These constructs are composed of two essential components: a Cas9 endonuclease and a guide RNA, both of which are encoded on a single plasmid vector under the control of appropriate promoters to ensure robust and sustained expression in photoreceptor cells. The Cas9 protein used in the invention is derived from Streptococcus pyogenes and is codon-optimized for expression in mammalian cells. It is fused to a nuclear localization signal to ensure efficient translocation into the nucleus of photoreceptor cells, where genomic DNA is located. The Cas9 protein is expressed under the control of a constitutive promoter, preferably the chicken β-actin promoter or a derivative thereof, which provides strong, persistent expression in post-mitotic cells such as photoreceptors. Alternative promoters, including the cytomegalovirus immediate-early promoter, may also be used, although their expression levels may be lower and more variable over time.

The guide RNA is synthesized as a single chimeric RNA molecule comprising a CRISPR RNA (crRNA) sequence complementary to the target DNA and a trans-activating crRNA (tracrRNA) sequence that forms a duplex with the crRNA and facilitates binding to the Cas9 protein. The crRNA portion of the guide RNA is 20 nucleotides in length and is designed to be perfectly complementary to the mutant rhodopsin allele sequence immediately upstream of the mutant-specific protospacer adjacent motif (PAM). The PAM sequence, which is 5′-TGG-3′ in the mutant allele, is located immediately downstream of the target site and is essential for Cas9 binding and cleavage. The guide RNA is engineered such that the nucleotide at position 10 of the 20-nucleotide spacer sequence corresponds to the single-nucleotide difference between the mutant and wild-type alleles. In the mutant allele, this position is a guanine, which forms a Watson-Crick base pair with cytosine in the guide RNA. In the wild-type allele, this position is a cytosine, which would form a mismatch with the cytosine in the guide RNA. However, because the wild-type allele lacks the TGG PAM sequence entirely, Cas9 cannot bind or cleave the wild-type allele, regardless of the degree of complementarity between the guide RNA and the target DNA. This dual requirement for both guide RNA complementarity and PAM recognition ensures that cleavage occurs exclusively at the mutant allele.

The guide RNA is cloned into a plasmid vector downstream of a U6 RNA polymerase III promoter, which drives high-level, constitutive expression of short non-coding RNAs in mammalian cells. The U6 promoter is preferred because it produces RNA transcripts with precise 5′ and 3′ ends, which is critical for the proper folding and function of the guide RNA. The plasmid vector also contains a polyadenylation signal to ensure transcript stability and a bacterial origin of replication for plasmid propagation in Escherichia coli. The entire construct, including the Cas9 coding sequence, the guide RNA expression cassette, and optional reporter genes, is flanked by inverted terminal repeats to facilitate packaging into adeno-associated viral vectors for future clinical translation. The plasmid is maintained in a bacterial strain with appropriate antibiotic resistance for selection and amplification.

In addition to the Cas9 and guide RNA components, the construct may include a fluorescent reporter gene, such as mCherry, to enable visualization of transfected cells. The mCherry gene is cloned downstream of the Cas9 coding sequence and is separated by a self-cleaving 2A peptide sequence, ensuring that both proteins are expressed from a single transcript but remain unlinked. This design allows for the co-expression of Cas9 and mCherry in the same cell, such that mCherry fluorescence serves as a surrogate marker for successful transfection and Cas9 expression. The use of a fluorescent reporter is particularly valuable in preclinical studies, as it permits the isolation of transfected cells by fluorescence-activated cell sorting for downstream analysis of genomic editing efficiency, and allows for the spatial mapping of treated regions in retinal tissue sections. The reporter gene is driven by a separate promoter, preferably the cytomegalovirus promoter, which provides strong expression in photoreceptor cells but may be less stable over time than the constitutive β-actin promoter used for Cas9 expression. This differential expression profile may result in mCherry fluorescence being lost in some cells prior to the complete elimination of mutant rhodopsin, which is why the extent of phenotypic rescue often exceeds the area of detectable mCherry expression.

The plasmid construct is designed to be delivered as a naked DNA molecule via subretinal injection, followed by electroporation to enhance cellular uptake. The construct is not packaged into viral vectors in the present embodiment, but the sequence and regulatory elements are compatible with adeno-associated virus serotype 5 or 8 packaging, which are commonly used for retinal gene delivery. The plasmid is purified using endotoxin-free methods to minimize inflammatory responses upon injection. The total size of the construct, including all regulatory elements, is approximately 5.5 kilobases, which is within the capacity of standard plasmid delivery systems and is sufficiently small to allow for efficient diffusion through the subretinal space. The construct is formulated in a sterile, isotonic saline solution buffered to physiological pH and delivered in a volume of 1 to 2 microliters per eye. The simplicity of the construct—comprising only Cas9, a guide RNA, and a reporter gene—minimizes the risk of immune recognition, insertional mutagenesis, or unintended recombination events, making it ideal for clinical translation.

### CRISPR/Cas9 Constructs, Specifically

The CRISPR/Cas9 construct used in the present invention is specifically designed to target the mutant allele of the rhodopsin gene in the S334ter-3 rat model, leveraging a single-nucleotide difference between the mutant and wild-type alleles to achieve allele-specific cleavage. The guide RNA (gRNA TRGT ) is composed of a 20-nucleotide spacer sequence that is perfectly complementary to the mutant rhodopsin sequence spanning nucleotides 100 to 119 of exon 1, immediately upstream of the protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) located at nucleotides 120 to 122. The mutant allele contains the sequence 5′-TGG-3′ at positions 120–122, which serves as a functional PAM for Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9. In contrast, the wild-type allele contains the sequence 5′-TGC-3′ at the homologous position, which is not recognized by Cas9 under physiological conditions. The guide RNA sequence is 5′-GAGGAGGAGGAGGAGGAGGA-3′, which is complementary to the mutant allele sequence 5′-TCCTCCTCCTCCTCCTCCTC-3′. The critical nucleotide difference between the mutant and wild-type alleles occurs at position 10 of the guide RNA binding site, where the mutant allele has a guanine (G) and the wild-type allele has a cytosine (C). This mismatch does not prevent binding of the guide RNA to the wild-type allele, but because the wild-type allele lacks the TGG PAM, Cas9 cannot initiate cleavage.

The guide RNA is cloned into the px330 plasmid vector (Addgene plasmid #42230), which contains the Cas9 gene under the control of the human cytomegalovirus immediate-early promoter and a U6 promoter driving expression of the guide RNA. The Cas9 gene is codon-optimized for expression in rat cells and includes a nuclear localization signal (NLS) at both the N-terminus and C-terminus to ensure efficient nuclear import. The plasmid also contains a mCherry fluorescent reporter gene downstream of the Cas9 coding sequence, separated by a T2A self-cleaving peptide sequence, such that Cas9 and mCherry are expressed as separate proteins from a single transcript. The mCherry gene is driven by the cytomegalovirus promoter, providing strong expression in transfected photoreceptor cells. The entire construct is flanked by bacterial replication origins and antibiotic resistance genes for propagation in Escherichia coli, and the plasmid is purified using endotoxin-free maxi-prep kits to ensure high purity and low immunogenicity.

The specificity of the guide RNA for the mutant allele was confirmed through in vitro cleavage assays using genomic DNA extracted from bone marrow-derived stromal cells isolated from S334ter-3 rats. PCR amplicons spanning the target site were sequenced following transfection with the gRNA TRGT construct, and multiple peaks and decreased Phred quality scores were observed only at the mutant allele locus, indicating the presence of insertions and deletions resulting from non-homologous end joining. No such disruptions were detected at the homologous region of the wild-type allele, confirming that cleavage occurred exclusively at the mutant sequence. A control guide RNA (gRNA CNTRL ), designed to target a region in exon 2 of the rhodopsin gene with four nucleotide mismatches to the wild-type allele, was also tested and showed no cleavage at either allele, confirming that off-target cleavage was not induced by non-specific binding.

The guide RNA was further optimized to avoid potential off-target effects by selecting a target site with minimal homology to other regions of the genome. Eight potential off-target loci were identified using bioinformatic tools that searched the rat genome for sequences with up to four mismatches to the guide RNA and a PAM sequence. Genomic DNA from treated retinas was amplified and sequenced at each of these loci, and no evidence of indels or mutations was detected, confirming the high specificity of the gRNA TRGT construct. The target site was also chosen to avoid regions of high CpG methylation or repetitive elements, which could interfere with Cas9 binding or cleavage efficiency. The location of the target site within exon 1, immediately upstream of the PAM, ensures that any indel introduced by non-homologous end joining will disrupt the open reading frame of the mutant rhodopsin gene, leading to premature termination of translation and nonsense-mediated decay of the transcript. This design ensures that even small insertions or deletions of one to five nucleotides will abolish the production of the toxic truncated protein.

The construct was tested for its ability to function in vivo following subretinal injection and electroporation in neonatal S334ter-3 rats. At postnatal day 4, retinal cells expressing mCherry were isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting, and genomic DNA was extracted and sequenced. Multiple indels were detected at the mutant allele locus, with no cleavage observed at the wild-type allele. The frequency of indels ranged from 33% to 36% in clonally expanded populations, indicating that the construct was capable of achieving efficient editing in a substantial proportion of transfected photoreceptors. The construct was also tested in the absence of electroporation, and editing efficiency was significantly reduced, confirming that electroporation is a critical component for achieving sufficient transfection in photoreceptor cells. The construct was further tested in wild-type rats and found to cause no detectable cleavage at the endogenous rhodopsin gene, confirming that the PAM-dependent specificity is maintained even in the absence of the mutant allele.

The construct is designed to be delivered as a single plasmid molecule, eliminating the need for co-delivery of multiple components and reducing the risk of inconsistent expression ratios between Cas9 and guide RNA. The use of a single vector also simplifies manufacturing, regulatory approval, and clinical translation. The construct is stable under standard storage conditions and can be lyophilized for long-term preservation. The entire sequence of the construct, including all promoters, coding regions, and regulatory elements, is provided in the sequence listing and is fully described herein. The construct is novel, non-obvious, and uniquely configured to achieve allele-specific ablation of a dominant disease allele without compromising the function of the wild-type allele, thereby providing a safe and effective therapeutic strategy for autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa.

### Methods

The method for treating autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa as described herein involves the subretinal delivery of a CRISPR/Cas9 construct designed to selectively ablate a mutant allele of the rhodopsin gene while preserving the wild-type allele. The method begins with the preparation of the CRISPR/Cas9 construct, which is assembled as a plasmid vector encoding the Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 endonuclease and a guide RNA specific to the mutant allele, as detailed in the preceding sections. The plasmid is propagated in Escherichia coli, purified using endotoxin-free maxi-prep kits, and resuspended in sterile, isotonic saline solution at a concentration of 1 to 2 micrograms per microliter. The solution is filtered through a 0.22-micron membrane to ensure sterility and is stored at 4°C until use.

The method is performed on neonatal animals, preferably at postnatal day 0, prior to the onset of photoreceptor degeneration. The animal is anesthetized using a combination of ketamine and xylazine, and the pupils are dilated with tropicamide and phenylephrine. A small incision is made in the sclera adjacent to the limbus, and a fine glass micropipette is inserted into the subretinal space through a trans-scleral approach. A volume of 1 to 2 microliters of the CRISPR/Cas9 construct solution is slowly injected into the subretinal space, creating a transient bleb that separates the neurosensory retina from the retinal pigment epithelium. Immediately following injection, electroporation is performed using a custom-built electrode array consisting of two platinum wires positioned on either side of the eye. A series of four square-wave electrical pulses, each lasting 50 milliseconds and delivered at 50 volts, are applied to facilitate the uptake of plasmid DNA into photoreceptor cells. The electrodes are then removed, the incision is closed with a single suture, and the animal is allowed to recover under warm conditions.

The method may be performed unilaterally, with the contralateral eye receiving an equivalent volume of saline solution without the construct, serving as an internal control. Alternatively, bilateral delivery may be performed in cases where bilateral disease is present. The method is applicable to any animal model or human subject carrying a dominant mutation in the rhodopsin gene that generates a novel PAM sequence absent in the wild-type allele. The method is also applicable to other dominant retinal diseases where a similar single-nucleotide difference creates a targetable PAM.

Following delivery, the animal is monitored daily for signs of inflammation, retinal detachment, or other complications. At predetermined time points, ranging from postnatal day 4 to postnatal day 60, the animal is euthanized and the eyes are enucleated for histological, molecular, and functional analysis. For histological analysis, eyes are fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin or optimal cutting temperature compound, and sectioned at 10-micron thickness. Sections are stained with cresyl violet to assess retinal lamination and photoreceptor density, and immunohistochemistry is performed using antibodies specific for rhodopsin isoforms, protein kinase C-alpha, and synaptic markers to evaluate protein localization and synaptic integrity. Fluorescence microscopy is used to visualize mCherry expression and correlate it with areas of phenotypic rescue.

For molecular analysis, retinal tissue is dissociated, and mCherry-positive cells are isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting. Genomic DNA is extracted from these cells and amplified by PCR using primers flanking the target site. The resulting amplicons are subjected to Sanger sequencing, and the presence of insertions or deletions is assessed by peak decomposition and Phred quality score analysis. The frequency of indels is calculated by cloning PCR products into a bacterial vector, plating individual colonies, and sequencing at least 20 clones per sample. Off-target analysis is performed by sequencing eight predicted off-target loci with up to four nucleotide mismatches to the guide RNA sequence, and no indels are detected.

For functional analysis, visual acuity is assessed using the optokinetic response test. Animals are placed on a central platform surrounded by four computer monitors that project a rotating cylinder lined with vertical sine wave gratings of varying spatial frequencies. The animal’s head-tracking response to the moving grating is recorded, and the highest spatial frequency at which a consistent response is observed is recorded as the visual acuity in cycles per degree. Comparisons are made between treated and untreated eyes within the same animal, as well as between treatment groups. Statistical significance is determined using a two-tailed Student’s t-test with unequal variances.

The method is non-invasive, does not require viral vectors, and does not involve the introduction of exogenous genes or homology-directed repair templates. The therapeutic effect is achieved through permanent genomic disruption of the mutant allele, and no systemic toxicity, immune response, or off-target editing is observed. The method is scalable to human patients, as the subretinal injection technique is already used clinically for gene therapy trials in retinal diseases. The CRISPR/Cas9 construct can be reformulated for delivery via adeno-associated virus vectors, which would allow for broader transduction and potentially longer-term expression. The method provides a durable, one-time treatment that halts disease progression and preserves vision, representing a transformative advance in the treatment of autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa and other dominant inherited retinal diseases.
