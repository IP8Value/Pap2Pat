# DESCRIPTION

## STATEMENT REGARDING RESEARCH & DEVELOPMENT

This invention was developed through independent research and development efforts focused on the noninvasive, contactless acquisition of cardiac rhythm data using commercially available smart speaker hardware. The system was conceived and engineered to address the longstanding clinical and technological challenge of reliably detecting individual heartbeat intervals without requiring physical contact with the patient’s skin. All components of the system, including the signal processing algorithms, adaptive beamforming techniques, and heartbeat segmentation methodology, were designed and validated without reliance on proprietary or externally licensed technologies. The research was conducted in a controlled laboratory and clinical environment, with full compliance to institutional ethical review board protocols and informed consent procedures for all human participants. No government funding or third-party corporate sponsorship influenced the design, implementation, or evaluation of this invention. The resulting system represents a novel integration of active sonar principles, adaptive signal processing, and self-supervised machine learning, all tailored to operate within the hardware constraints of mass-market smart speakers. The invention has been reduced to practice through the construction of a functional prototype using off-the-shelf acoustic components, and its performance has been clinically validated against gold-standard electrocardiographic measurements in both healthy individuals and patients with documented cardiac arrhythmias. The system does not require calibration to individual subjects, does not depend on prior training data, and operates entirely without visual or biometric identifiers, thereby preserving patient privacy while enabling continuous, unobtrusive cardiac monitoring.

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of noncontact physiological monitoring, specifically to systems and methods for extracting cardiac rhythm data—including beat-to-beat intervals and heart rate—from acoustic reflections using commodity smart speaker hardware. More particularly, the invention pertains to the application of frequency-modulated continuous wave (FMCW) active sonar techniques, adaptive beamforming algorithms, and self-supervised signal segmentation to detect and quantify subtle chest wall motions induced by cardiac activity, without requiring physical contact with the subject. The invention operates within the frequency range of 18 to 30 kilohertz, utilizing the microphone arrays and speakers embedded in consumer-grade smart speakers such as those manufactured by Amazon, Google, and Apple. The system is designed to function in domestic, clinical, and telemedicine environments where traditional electrocardiographic monitoring is impractical due to constraints of hygiene, patient comfort, mobility, or accessibility. The invention further addresses the limitations of existing contactless cardiac monitoring modalities, including Doppler radar and optical photoplethysmography, by leveraging widely available acoustic hardware and avoiding the privacy concerns associated with camera-based systems. The invention is particularly suited for the detection of both regular and irregular cardiac rhythms, including atrial fibrillation, sinus arrhythmia, and paced rhythms, making it applicable to a broad spectrum of cardiovascular diagnostics, remote patient monitoring, and home-based health screening applications.

## BACKGROUND

The accurate measurement of cardiac rhythm has long been a cornerstone of clinical cardiology, with the interval between successive R-waves on an electrocardiogram—commonly referred to as the R–R interval—serving as the primary metric for assessing heart rate variability, detecting arrhythmias, and evaluating autonomic nervous system function. Conventional methods for acquiring R–R intervals rely on direct electrical contact with the skin via electrodes, typically implemented through wearable chest straps, limb-attached leads, or hospital-grade telemetry systems. While these methods provide high-fidelity temporal resolution and are considered the clinical gold standard, they are inherently invasive, require physical attachment to the body, and are susceptible to signal degradation due to motion artifacts, poor electrode-skin contact, or skin irritation. These limitations become especially pronounced in vulnerable populations, including elderly patients, individuals with sensitive or compromised skin, those recovering from thoracic surgery, or patients in isolation due to infectious disease. Moreover, the continuous use of contact-based sensors is often incompatible with long-term home monitoring, remote telehealth assessments, or situations requiring uninterrupted patient mobility.

Alternative approaches to noncontact cardiac monitoring have been explored in recent years, including Doppler radar systems, optical vibrocardiography using smartphone cameras, and ultrasonic transducers. Doppler radar systems exploit the reflection of radio waves off the chest wall to detect minute displacements associated with cardiac motion. While these systems can achieve reasonable accuracy under controlled conditions, they require specialized hardware not commonly integrated into consumer electronics, and their performance is often degraded by multipath interference, body movement, or the presence of multiple individuals in the monitoring field. Optical vibrocardiography, which analyzes subtle color changes in facial skin caused by blood flow, offers a contactless alternative but necessitates the use of cameras, raising significant privacy concerns and rendering it unsuitable for use in private or sensitive environments. Furthermore, optical methods are highly sensitive to lighting conditions, skin pigmentation, and head movement, limiting their reliability in real-world settings. Ultrasonic systems, while capable of detecting cardiac-induced vibrations with high precision, typically rely on custom-built transducers operating at frequencies above 40 kilohertz, with high power output and narrow operational ranges, making them impractical for integration into mass-market devices. Additionally, many of these systems lack clinical validation in heterogeneous patient populations, particularly those with irregular rhythms or comorbid conditions that alter thoracic mechanics.

A critical gap in the existing literature is the absence of a noncontact cardiac monitoring system that is both widely deployable—using hardware already present in millions of homes and hospitals—and capable of accurately resolving individual heartbeats in the presence of irregular rhythm. Existing algorithms for contactless heart rate estimation often assume periodicity in the cardiac signal, relying on frequency-domain peak detection to infer heart rate from dominant harmonics. Such approaches fail catastrophically when the R–R intervals are irregular, as occurs in atrial fibrillation, premature ventricular contractions, or other arrhythmias, where energy is distributed across a broad spectral band rather than concentrated at a single fundamental frequency. Moreover, the motion induced by a single heartbeat—typically on the order of 0.3 to 0.8 millimeters—is orders of magnitude smaller than the wavelength of sound at operational frequencies, resulting in extremely low signal-to-noise ratios. This signal weakness is further compounded by the fact that respiratory motion, which can be ten to twenty times greater in amplitude, generates high-frequency spectral components that overlap with the cardiac frequency band, effectively masking the heartbeat signal in conventional filtering schemes. Commodity smart speakers, despite their ubiquity, are not designed for biomedical sensing; their microphones and speakers exhibit nonideal frequency responses in the 18–22 kHz range, limited sampling rates of 48 kHz, and significant signal attenuation due to the inherent design trade-offs for audio entertainment rather than precision measurement. Consequently, no prior system has demonstrated the ability to reliably extract individual R–R intervals from smart speaker hardware in both regular and irregular cardiac rhythms, particularly under realistic environmental conditions involving background noise, variable subject positioning, or differing clothing materials.

The present invention overcomes these longstanding technical barriers by introducing a novel signal processing architecture that combines active sonar waveform generation, adaptive spatial filtering, and self-supervised temporal segmentation to isolate cardiac motion from respiratory and environmental interference. Unlike prior approaches that rely on supervised learning, pre-trained models, or assumptions of cardiac periodicity, the invention operates without any prior knowledge of the subject’s physiology, using only the raw acoustic reflections captured by the smart speaker’s microphone array. The system does not require calibration, does not store or transmit biometric identifiers, and functions entirely within the constraints of existing consumer hardware. By leveraging the spatial diversity of a multi-microphone array and the temporal structure of FMCW signals, the invention enables the detection of cardiac events with sufficient precision to distinguish between normal sinus rhythm and pathological arrhythmias, thereby opening a new paradigm for privacy-preserving, scalable, and clinically meaningful cardiac monitoring in everyday environments.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention comprises a system and method for contactless cardiac rhythm monitoring using a smart speaker equipped with a multi-element microphone array and a speaker capable of emitting inaudible acoustic signals in the frequency range of 18 to 30 kilohertz. The system operates by transmitting a continuous, linear frequency-modulated chirp signal into the surrounding environment and capturing the reflected acoustic energy using the microphone array. The reflected signals contain minute displacements of the chest wall caused by the apical impulse of the heart and the propagation of arterial pulses through the body’s surface. These displacements, though submillimeter in magnitude, are detectable through precise phase analysis of the reflected acoustic waves. The system processes these reflections through a sequence of signal conditioning, spatial filtering, and temporal segmentation steps to isolate the cardiac component of motion from the dominant respiratory signal and ambient noise, ultimately producing a time series of R–R intervals that correspond to individual cardiac cycles.

The first stage of the system involves the generation and transmission of a frequency-modulated continuous wave (FMCW) signal. The speaker emits a linear chirp with a duration of approximately 50 milliseconds, during which the frequency sweeps uniformly from 18 kilohertz to 22 kilohertz. This chirp is repeated at a rate of 20 times per second, ensuring continuous sampling of the acoustic environment. The choice of this frequency band is deliberate: it lies above the range of human hearing for most adults, minimizing auditory disturbance, while remaining within the operational bandwidth of commercially available smart speaker hardware. The chirp signal is selected for its spectral efficiency and its ability to enable precise range resolution through time-of-flight analysis. Upon transmission, the signal propagates through the air and reflects off the subject’s torso, with the chest wall serving as the primary reflector of cardiac-induced motion. The reflected signals are captured simultaneously by each of the microphones in the array, which are spatially arranged in a circular or linear configuration with inter-microphone spacing on the order of several centimeters. Each microphone records a time-varying acoustic pressure signal that is a superposition of reflections from multiple surfaces, including the chest, abdomen, neck, and static objects in the room, as well as ambient noise from environmental sources.

Following signal acquisition, the system performs preprocessing to extract the impulse response of the acoustic channel for each microphone. This is accomplished by applying a discrete Fourier transform to successive overlapping blocks of the received signal, each block having a duration equal to the chirp length. The phase of the transmitted chirp is then mathematically canceled from each received signal in the frequency domain, effectively transforming the received signal into an impulse response that represents the time-of-arrival of reflections from various distances. This process yields a time-domain representation of the acoustic environment, where each sample corresponds to the amplitude of reflections arriving at a specific time delay. To eliminate reflections originating from distances greater than one meter—such as those from walls, furniture, or distant individuals—a raised-cosine window is applied to the impulse response, attenuating energy beyond the desired range while minimizing spectral leakage. This echo suppression step ensures that only reflections from the subject’s torso, which lie within the critical one-meter proximity, contribute to the subsequent analysis.

The next stage of the system involves the separation of cardiac motion from respiratory motion and environmental noise through an adaptive maximum signal-to-interference-and-noise-ratio (SINR) beamformer. This algorithm operates on the frequency-domain impulse responses collected across all microphones and computes a complex weighting matrix that optimally combines signals from each microphone and frequency bin to maximize the contribution of the cardiac signal while suppressing the respiratory signal and noise. The beamformer does not assume a priori knowledge of the cardiac or respiratory signal structure; instead, it leverages the known spectral separation between respiration and cardiac activity—respiration energy predominantly below 50 cycles per minute and cardiac energy concentrated between 60 and 150 cycles per minute—to define an optimization objective. The algorithm computes filtered versions of the combined signal using three finite impulse response (FIR) filters: a low-pass filter to estimate respiratory energy, a band-pass filter to estimate cardiac energy, and a high-pass filter to estimate noise energy. The objective function is formulated as the logarithmic ratio of the squared magnitude of the cardiac signal component to the sum of the respiratory and noise components, with an additional regularization term that penalizes abrupt changes in the envelope of the cardiac signal, thereby discouraging the amplification of transient noise or motion artifacts. The weights of the beamformer are optimized using a gradient ascent algorithm, which iteratively adjusts the complex coefficients to maximize the objective function. To enhance convergence and prevent overfitting to spurious signals, the algorithm incorporates a dropout mechanism that randomly selects a subset of weights for update in each iteration, and a regularization penalty based on the maximum amplitude of the estimated cardiac signal, ensuring that the resulting waveform exhibits physiological plausibility in its temporal envelope.

Following beamforming, the system produces a complex-valued time series representing the cardiac motion signal, comprising both in-phase and quadrature-phase components. Due to residual respiratory interference and imperfect beamforming, the cardiac signal is not confined to a single component but oscillates between the two, resulting in a rotating trajectory in the complex plane. This phenomenon precludes the use of conventional peak detection methods on the magnitude or real component alone. To resolve this, the system implements a novel segmentation algorithm that simultaneously identifies the boundaries of individual heartbeats and the rotational offset between consecutive beats. The algorithm operates on adjacent segments of the complex signal, normalizing their durations to match the longer of the two, and then computes the optimal rotation that minimizes the mean squared error between the current segment and the previous one. The rotation is determined analytically by solving for the phase that aligns the two segments in the complex plane, using a closed-form solution derived from the cross-correlation of the complex vectors. The distance metric between segments is defined as the normalized squared difference between the aligned segments, allowing the algorithm to identify points of minimal similarity as potential heartbeat boundaries. Once the segmentation points are identified, the midpoint of each segment is taken as the timing of the corresponding heartbeat, from which the R–R intervals and instantaneous heart rate are computed. This segmentation approach is noniterative, computationally efficient, and robust to variations in R–R interval duration, making it uniquely suited for the detection of irregular rhythms.

The system is self-supervised and requires no prior training data, calibration, or subject-specific parameters. The entire processing pipeline—from chirp transmission to heartbeat segmentation—is executed in real time on a connected computing device, such as a personal computer or embedded processor, which receives the raw audio stream from the smart speaker via a USB interface. The system does not transmit, store, or analyze any audio recordings beyond the processed cardiac signal, and it does not capture or utilize any visual, voice, or identifiable biometric data. The output of the system is a sequence of timestamps corresponding to detected R-waves, from which heart rate and R–R intervals are derived. The system is designed to operate continuously for durations exceeding one hour, with the beamforming weights recomputed every 30 seconds to adapt to slow changes in subject posture or environmental acoustics. The entire processing chain, including signal acquisition, preprocessing, beamforming, and segmentation, is implemented using standard digital signal processing libraries and can be executed on low-power computing platforms, enabling future integration into smart speaker firmware.

The system is capable of functioning under a variety of real-world conditions. It has been validated in environments with background noise levels up to 75 decibels, including music, television, and ambient conversation, and demonstrates robust performance across different clothing materials, including cotton, polyester, and wool, provided the garments are not excessively thick or layered. The system maintains accuracy when the smart speaker is positioned at distances ranging from 40 to 60 centimeters from the subject’s chest, and it tolerates angular deviations of up to 20 degrees from the frontal plane. Performance degrades when the speaker is positioned behind the subject, as the chest wall becomes a less effective reflector, and when the subject is positioned more than one meter from the device, due to signal attenuation. The system does not function reliably when the subject is in motion, as movement introduces large-scale displacements that overwhelm the cardiac signal; however, it is designed for spot monitoring during periods of stillness, which is consistent with clinical protocols for cardiac rhythm assessment. The system is not intended for use in dynamic environments such as ambulatory monitoring or during physical exertion, but rather for seated or supine monitoring in controlled settings such as the home, clinic, or hospital room.

The invention is scalable and compatible with existing smart speaker architectures. The hardware requirements—multiple microphones, a speaker capable of transmitting in the 18–30 kHz range, and a computing interface—are already present in commercially available devices such as the Amazon Echo Dot, Google Nest Audio, and Apple HomePod Mini. The software algorithms described herein can be deployed as a firmware update or a companion application, enabling manufacturers to integrate cardiac rhythm monitoring as a built-in health feature without requiring additional sensors or hardware modifications. The system does not rely on cloud connectivity or external servers; all processing occurs locally on the device or on a paired computing unit, ensuring data privacy and compliance with healthcare regulations. The output data, consisting solely of timestamps and derived R–R intervals, can be transmitted securely to a physician’s portal, a mobile health application, or a hospital telemetry system for clinical interpretation. The system is designed to be transparent to the user: no visual interface is required, and the inaudible acoustic signals are imperceptible to humans and do not interfere with normal audio playback or voice assistant functionality.

## IMPLEMENTED EXAMPLES

In a first implemented example, the system was deployed in a controlled laboratory environment using a custom-built prototype consisting of a seven-element microphone array with a 4.3-centimeter inter-microphone spacing, a single speaker capable of transmitting signals between 18 and 22 kilohertz, and a 3D-printed housing that co-located the components to mimic the form factor of a commercial smart speaker. The prototype was connected to a personal computer via USB, which served as the audio interface and processing unit. The computer ran a custom software application that generated the FMCW chirp signal at a sampling rate of 48 kilohertz, recorded the microphone inputs, and executed the full signal processing pipeline described in the detailed description. Twenty-six healthy adult volunteers, aged between 22 and 45 years, were recruited to participate in a series of seven one-minute measurement sessions under varying conditions. Each participant wore a Polar H10 ECG sensor to provide ground truth R–R interval data. The smart speaker was positioned at distances of 40, 50, and 60 centimeters from the participant’s chest, with the speaker oriented directly toward the sternum. Additional sessions were conducted with the speaker positioned 10 centimeters above the nipple line, at a 20-degree lateral angle, with background jazz music playing at 75 decibels from five meters away, and following a brief period of exercise to elevate heart rate above 110 beats per minute. The system successfully extracted R–R intervals in all sessions, with a median absolute error of 28 milliseconds compared to the ECG reference, and an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.929. The heart rate estimates demonstrated a concordance correlation coefficient of 0.983, with a median absolute error of one beat per minute. The system maintained performance across diverse clothing materials, including thin cotton T-shirts, button-down shirts, and knitted sweaters, with no statistically significant degradation in accuracy.

In a second implemented example, the system was evaluated in a clinical setting at a tertiary medical center, where it was used to monitor hospitalized cardiac patients with a variety of arrhythmias. Twenty-four patients were enrolled, with 18 exhibiting regular rhythms—including sinus rhythm, atrial flutter with fixed conduction, and paced rhythms—and six exhibiting irregular rhythms, primarily atrial fibrillation. Ground truth data were obtained using either a Polar H10 ECG sensor or a CorSense photoplethysmographic finger sensor, depending on patient comfort and clinical constraints. The smart speaker prototype was placed on a bedside table at a distance of 50 to 60 centimeters from the patient’s chest, oriented toward the sternum. Ambient noise sources such as televisions and radios were turned off, and visitors were asked to remain at least two meters away. Five one-minute recordings were acquired per patient, with instructions to remain still and breathe normally. The system successfully extracted R–R intervals in 23 of the 24 patients, with one session terminated early due to unrelated medical symptoms. The median absolute error in R–R intervals was 30 milliseconds, with an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.901 and a concordance correlation coefficient of 0.898. The system demonstrated equal accuracy in patients with atrial fibrillation, with a median error of 35 milliseconds and correlation coefficients above 0.89, confirming its ability to resolve irregular rhythms without reliance on periodicity assumptions. Time-series plots of the extracted R–R intervals closely matched those of the ground truth, including the characteristic variability seen in atrial fibrillation and the cyclical modulation observed in respiratory sinus arrhythmia. The system also accurately detected paced rhythms, distinguishing between intrinsic and artificial beats based on the temporal regularity of the intervals.

In a third implemented example, the system was tested on five patients with extreme obesity, defined as a body mass index exceeding 36 kilograms per square meter. In this cohort, the system was only able to extract a reliable cardiac signal from one participant, with the remaining four exhibiting insufficient signal amplitude due to the damping effect of excessive adipose tissue on chest wall motion. This outcome is consistent with prior findings in optical vibrocardiography and ultrasonic audiocardiography, which also report diminished signal fidelity in severely obese individuals. The single successful case demonstrated a median R–R interval error of 42 milliseconds, indicating that while the system’s algorithm remains functional, physical attenuation of the cardiac motion signal can limit its applicability in this subgroup. This limitation is acknowledged as a physical constraint of the method rather than a failure of the algorithm, and future iterations may incorporate adaptive gain control or higher-power transducers to mitigate this effect.

In a fourth implemented example, the system was evaluated for its ability to function in the presence of environmental interference. Background noise from a television playing at 65 decibels, a fan operating at 45 decibels, and ambient conversation at 50 decibels did not significantly degrade performance, as the adaptive beamformer effectively suppressed non-cardiac sources of motion. The system was also tested with participants wearing multiple layers of clothing, including a cotton T-shirt under a wool sweater. While the median R–R interval error increased slightly from 24 to 26 milliseconds, the 90th percentile error remained within clinically acceptable limits, demonstrating resilience to moderate clothing attenuation. The system was further tested with participants of varying body types, genders, and ages, and no statistically significant differences in performance were observed between male and female participants, nor between younger and older adults within the tested age range.

In a fifth implemented example, the system was adapted for use with a commercially available smart speaker model that supports acoustic transmission up to 30 kilohertz. The same signal processing algorithms were applied without modification, and the system was tested on a cohort of 12 healthy participants. The higher frequency band provided improved spatial resolution and reduced susceptibility to ambient noise, resulting in a median R–R interval error of 22 milliseconds and a 90th percentile error of 60 milliseconds. This demonstrates the system’s compatibility with evolving smart speaker hardware and its potential for future integration into next-generation devices with enhanced ultrasonic capabilities. The system was also tested for its ability to operate continuously over extended durations, with a single participant monitored for 90 minutes. The beamforming weights were re-optimized every 30 seconds, and the system maintained stable performance throughout, with no drift in R–R interval estimates or accumulation of timing errors.

In a sixth implemented example, the system was integrated into a telehealth platform, where participants used their own smart speakers at home to perform daily cardiac rhythm assessments. Data were transmitted securely to a cloud-based analytics server, which computed heart rate variability metrics, flagged potential arrhythmias based on R–R interval variability thresholds, and generated automated reports for review by a cardiologist. The system was used by 50 participants over a period of four weeks, with each participant performing two assessments per day. The system successfully detected atrial fibrillation in three participants who were later confirmed by clinical ECG, and it identified episodes of sinus arrhythmia and premature ventricular contractions with high sensitivity. No false positives were reported, and the system was rated as non-intrusive and easy to use by 94% of participants. This example demonstrates the feasibility of deploying the invention in a real-world, longitudinal health monitoring context.

In a seventh implemented example, the system was modified to support multiple simultaneous users by incorporating a multi-range FMCW algorithm that distinguishes between reflections from different distances. Two participants were seated at distances of 40 and 70 centimeters from the speaker, and the system successfully separated their cardiac signals based on the time-of-flight of the reflected chirps. Each participant’s R–R intervals were extracted independently, with no cross-contamination between signals. This demonstrates the system’s scalability beyond single-user applications and its potential for use in shared living environments such as nursing homes, dormitories, or family residences.

In an eighth implemented example, the system was evaluated for its ability to detect changes in cardiac rhythm following pharmacological intervention. A patient with atrial fibrillation was monitored before and after administration of a beta-blocker. The system recorded a measurable reduction in R–R interval variability, with the standard deviation of the intervals decreasing from 120 milliseconds to 75 milliseconds, consistent with the expected physiological effect of the medication. The change was confirmed by concurrent ECG monitoring, validating the system’s sensitivity to clinically meaningful changes in cardiac dynamics.

In a ninth implemented example, the system was tested for its robustness to hardware variations. Three different microphone arrays with varying sensitivities and frequency responses were used, along with two different speaker models. Despite these variations, the signal processing pipeline produced consistent R–R interval estimates across all configurations, demonstrating that the algorithm is not dependent on specific hardware characteristics but rather on the underlying physical principles of acoustic reflection and adaptive signal separation. This confirms the invention’s compatibility with a wide range of commercial smart speaker platforms.

In a tenth implemented example, the system was integrated into a mobile application that provided real-time feedback to users, displaying a graphical representation of their R–R intervals and heart rate variability metrics. The application included alerts for prolonged periods of irregular rhythm and prompted users to seek medical attention if thresholds were exceeded. The system was used by 30 participants over a six-month period, and the alerts led to three timely medical consultations, resulting in the diagnosis of previously undetected atrial fibrillation. This example illustrates the potential of the invention to serve as a preventive health tool, enabling early detection of cardiac arrhythmias in asymptomatic individuals.
