# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF INVENTION

The present invention relates to a novel and highly specific system for the spatio-temporal control of gene expression in plant reproductive tissues, particularly in the tapetum of flowering plants, and more specifically to a reversible male sterility–fertility restoration system engineered for the commercial production of hybrid seeds. The invention encompasses a two-component genetic architecture wherein a male-sterile female line is generated through the high-level, postmeiotic, and tapetum-specific expression of a cytotoxic gene, and male fertility is restored in the F1 hybrid progeny through the transcriptional silencing of the cytotoxic gene via a light-regulated, COP1-mediated protein degradation pathway. The system exploits the molecular interaction between a modified transcription factor component—comprising a fusion of the N-terminal 131 amino acid fragment of the Arabidopsis HFR1 protein and a mutant TATA-box binding protein (TBPm3)—and a nuclear-localized variant of the constitutive photomorphogenic 1 (COP1) E3 ubiquitin ligase, designated COP1L105A. This interaction enables the targeted proteasomal degradation of the TBPm3 component, thereby abolishing transcription from a TGTA-mutated promoter that is otherwise functional only in the presence of TBPm3. The invention is particularly suited for use in dicotyledonous crops such as tobacco, tomato, potato, canola, and other economically significant species where hybrid seed production is desirable but hindered by the lack of efficient, non-transgenic, and environmentally stable male sterility and fertility restoration systems. The system operates without the need for exogenous chemical inducers, temperature shifts, or site-specific recombinases, and is fully reversible, genetically stable, and compatible with conventional breeding protocols.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION AND PRIOR ART

The production of hybrid seeds remains one of the most effective strategies for maximizing agricultural yield, enhancing stress tolerance, and improving uniformity in crop performance. However, the commercial viability of hybrid seed systems is critically dependent on the availability of reliable and scalable male sterility and fertility restoration technologies. Traditional methods of male sterility have relied on cytoplasmic male sterility (CMS), which is inherited maternally and requires the maintenance of a sterile female line and a separate restorer line that carries nuclear fertility-restoring genes. While CMS systems have been successfully deployed in crops such as maize, sorghum, and rice, they are often limited by the narrow genetic base of restorer lines, the difficulty in identifying compatible restorers, and the potential for partial or unstable fertility restoration. Furthermore, CMS systems are not easily transferable across species due to the species-specific nature of mitochondrial-nuclear interactions.

In recent decades, genetic engineering has provided alternative approaches to induce male sterility through the targeted expression of cytotoxic genes in the tapetum, a specialized somatic layer of the anther that supports microspore development. The most widely studied system involves the expression of the bacterial barnase ribonuclease under the control of a tapetum-specific promoter, such as TA29 or A9. The cytotoxic activity of barnase disrupts RNA metabolism in tapetal cells, leading to the collapse of pollen development and complete male sterility. Fertility restoration in this system is achieved by co-expressing the barnase inhibitor barstar, which binds and neutralizes barnase. However, this approach suffers from several critical limitations. First, the identification of restorer lines that express barstar at sufficient levels and in the correct spatio-temporal pattern has proven extremely challenging, particularly in polyploid species such as Brassica juncea, where less than 2% of crosses yield fully fertile F1 progeny. Second, the expression of barstar in vegetative tissues can lead to unintended phenotypic consequences, including yield penalties and reduced plant vigor. Third, the barnase/barstar system requires the co-introduction of two transgenes into the restorer line, increasing the complexity of genetic stacking and regulatory scrutiny.

Other systems have attempted to circumvent these issues by employing alternative mechanisms for fertility restoration. These include RNA interference (RNAi) constructs that degrade the transcript of the male sterility gene, site-specific recombination systems such as Cre/loxP that excise the cytotoxic gene from the genome in the F1 hybrid, and split-gene systems that require the reassembly of a functional gene only in the presence of a complementary transgene from the male parent. While these approaches have demonstrated high levels of restoration in controlled settings, they suffer from inherent drawbacks. RNAi-based systems are often subject to incomplete silencing due to variable efficiency of small RNA processing and the potential for off-target effects. Site-specific recombination systems require the integration of recombinase genes and recognition sites into both parental lines, raising biosafety concerns due to the potential for unintended genomic rearrangements or horizontal gene transfer. Split-gene systems, while elegant in design, require the precise coordination of two independent transgenes and are prone to segregation distortion in segregating populations, reducing the reliability of hybrid seed production.

Another class of systems has explored conditional male sterility based on environmental triggers, such as temperature-sensitive toxins or chemically inducible promoters. For instance, a temperature-sensitive variant of diphtheria toxin (DTA) has been used to confer sterility at low temperatures (18°C) and restoration at higher temperatures (26°C). Similarly, ethanol-inducible promoters have been employed to trigger sterility upon application of ethanol vapor. While these systems offer reversibility, they are impractical for large-scale field production due to the logistical challenges of maintaining precise environmental conditions or applying chemical inducers uniformly across vast agricultural areas. Moreover, such systems are vulnerable to environmental fluctuations and are incompatible with standard agronomic practices.

The fundamental limitation of all prior art systems lies in their inability to achieve complete, consistent, and environmentally robust transcriptional silencing of the male sterility gene in the F1 hybrid without requiring additional transgenes, chemical inputs, or complex genetic manipulations. Most systems rely on post-transcriptional or post-translational mechanisms—such as RNA degradation, protein inhibition, or gene excision—that are inherently leaky, subject to variability, and often incomplete. None have successfully exploited the nuclear dynamics of endogenous light-signaling regulators to achieve spatio-temporally precise, irreversible transcriptional shutdown of a gene expressed under a synthetic promoter. Furthermore, no prior system has combined a TATA-box mutant promoter with a conditionally stabilized transcription factor component and a nuclear-localized ubiquitin ligase to achieve complete, heritable, and predictable transcriptional abolition in a tissue-specific and developmentally timed manner.

The present invention overcomes these longstanding limitations by introducing a novel mechanism of transcriptional control that operates at the level of transcription factor availability rather than transcript stability or protein activity. By fusing a mutant transcription factor (TBPm3) essential for the function of a TGTA-mutated promoter to a degradation tag derived from the HFR1 protein, and by expressing a nuclear-localized variant of COP1—a key E3 ubiquitin ligase in the light-signaling pathway—in the same tissue and developmental window, the invention achieves a previously unattainable level of precision: the complete and irreversible cessation of gene expression in the F1 hybrid, without affecting the viability or fertility of either parental line. This system does not require the expression of an inhibitor protein, the excision of DNA sequences, or the application of external stimuli. Instead, it leverages the natural, evolutionarily conserved machinery of protein degradation to silence gene expression at its source—the transcriptional initiation complex. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the design of hybrid seed systems, moving from protein-level interference to transcriptional-level control, and from leaky, variable restoration to complete, predictable, and genetically stable fertility restoration.

### LIMITATIONS OF PRIOR ART

Prior art systems for male sterility and fertility restoration in plants are fundamentally constrained by their reliance on mechanisms that operate downstream of transcription. The barnase/barstar system, despite its widespread use, suffers from incomplete restoration due to insufficient expression levels of barstar in the tapetum, mis-timing of inhibitor production relative to barnase accumulation, and the metabolic burden imposed by the co-expression of two foreign proteins. Even in optimized versions using chimeric promoters, restoration rarely exceeds 90% of wild-type fertility, and the restorer lines themselves often exhibit reduced vigor or yield. The use of RNA interference to silence the male sterility gene is similarly problematic, as the efficiency of RNAi is highly dependent on the sequence context, the abundance of the target transcript, and the activity of the plant’s endogenous RNAi machinery, all of which vary across genotypes and environmental conditions. In many cases, residual expression of the cytotoxic gene leads to partial pollen abortion, resulting in low seed set and unreliable hybrid seed production.

Site-specific recombination systems, such as those based on Cre/loxP or FLP/FRT, offer the theoretical advantage of complete gene excision. However, these systems require the integration of recombinase recognition sites into the male sterility cassette and the co-expression of the recombinase enzyme in the male parent. The recombinase itself is a foreign protein that may trigger immune responses or unintended recombination events in the plant genome, raising biosafety and regulatory concerns. Moreover, the excision event must occur with 100% efficiency in every cell of the developing anther; even a single cell retaining the cytotoxic gene can lead to pollen abortion and reduced fertility. The probability of achieving such perfect recombination across thousands of cells in a developing anther is exceedingly low, and empirical data from multiple studies indicate that recombination efficiency rarely exceeds 95%, which is insufficient for commercial hybrid seed production.

Split-gene systems, while ingenious, require the division of the cytotoxic gene into two non-functional fragments that must be reassembled through trans-splicing or protein complementation. These systems are highly sensitive to the stoichiometry of the two components, and any imbalance in expression levels—common in heterozygous F1 hybrids—leads to incomplete reassembly and partial sterility. Furthermore, the requirement for two independent transgenes in the male parent increases the complexity of breeding and the risk of segregation distortion, making it difficult to maintain pure restorer lines over multiple generations.

Chemically or environmentally inducible systems, such as ethanol-inducible promoters or temperature-sensitive toxins, are inherently unsuitable for large-scale agriculture. They require precise control of environmental conditions that cannot be reliably maintained in open-field conditions, and they introduce logistical and economic burdens that are prohibitive for seed producers. Moreover, the reversibility of these systems is not absolute; for instance, temperature-sensitive systems may exhibit partial restoration at suboptimal temperatures, leading to unpredictable fertility outcomes. The use of chemical inducers also raises concerns regarding residue accumulation in seeds, environmental contamination, and regulatory approval in food-producing crops.

Perhaps most critically, none of the prior art systems achieve true transcriptional abolition. All existing systems either inhibit the activity of the cytotoxic protein, degrade its transcript, or excise its coding sequence—but none prevent the transcriptional machinery from initiating transcription in the first place. This leaves open the possibility of transcriptional noise, low-level expression, or reactivation under stress conditions, all of which compromise the reliability of the system. The present invention addresses this fundamental gap by introducing a mechanism that renders the transcriptional apparatus incapable of initiating transcription altogether, by removing the essential transcription factor component required for promoter recognition. This approach ensures that no transcript is produced, eliminating the possibility of residual expression, leakiness, or reactivation. The system is thus uniquely suited for applications demanding absolute precision, such as the production of hybrid seeds for human consumption, where even trace levels of cytotoxic gene expression could trigger regulatory or consumer resistance.

### Objectives of the Present Invention

The primary objective of the present invention is to provide a genetically stable, environmentally robust, and completely reversible system for the induction of male sterility in female parent lines and the full restoration of fertility in their F1 hybrid progeny, without the need for chemical inducers, temperature manipulation, or the expression of inhibitory proteins. A secondary objective is to achieve complete transcriptional silencing of the male sterility gene in the F1 hybrid, thereby eliminating any possibility of residual expression that could compromise pollen viability or seed set. A further objective is to ensure that the male sterility phenotype is strictly confined to the tapetum of the anther during the postmeiotic stages of development, with no detectable expression in vegetative tissues, floral organs, or other reproductive structures, thereby preserving the overall fitness and yield potential of the female parent line.

Another key objective is to enable the use of a single, unified genetic cassette in the female parent line that contains both the cytotoxic gene and the transcription factor fusion required for its expression, while allowing the male parent line to carry only the nuclear-localized COP1 variant necessary for transcriptional repression. This design minimizes genetic complexity, reduces the risk of transgene segregation, and simplifies breeding protocols. The invention further aims to demonstrate that the transcriptional repression mechanism is heritable, stable across generations, and not subject to epigenetic silencing or environmental modulation, ensuring consistent performance under diverse agricultural conditions.

A critical objective of the invention is to provide a system that is broadly applicable across dicotyledonous crops, including economically important species such as tomato, potato, canola, sunflower, and soybean, without requiring species-specific optimization of promoters or regulatory elements. The use of conserved plant transcription factors and ubiquitin ligases ensures that the core mechanism is transferable across species, making the invention a universal platform for hybrid seed production. Additionally, the invention seeks to eliminate the need for herbicide or antibiotic selection markers in the final hybrid seed, by ensuring that the male parent line carries no selectable marker genes that could be transmitted to the F1 progeny, thereby facilitating regulatory approval and consumer acceptance.

Finally, the invention aims to establish a new paradigm in plant biotechnology by demonstrating that transcriptional control through targeted degradation of essential transcription factors can serve as a more precise, reliable, and scalable alternative to post-transcriptional or post-translational methods of gene regulation. By leveraging the natural dynamics of light-regulated protein degradation pathways, the invention opens the door to a new class of synthetic gene circuits in plants that operate with the fidelity of endogenous regulatory networks, enabling the design of future systems for precision agriculture, synthetic biology, and trait stacking.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and highly specific system for the controlled induction and restoration of male fertility in flowering plants, comprising a two-component genetic architecture that enables complete male sterility in female parent lines and full fertility restoration in their F1 hybrid progeny. The system operates through the spatio-temporal regulation of gene expression in the tapetum, a somatic cell layer essential for pollen development, by exploiting the molecular interaction between a modified transcription factor and a nuclear-localized ubiquitin ligase. The female parent line contains a single T-DNA cassette that expresses a cytotoxic gene under the control of a TGTA-mutated promoter, which is functionally active only in the presence of a complementing transcription factor. This transcription factor is engineered as a fusion protein comprising a mutant TATA-box binding protein (TBPm3) and the N-terminal 131 amino acid fragment of the Arabidopsis HFR1 protein, such that the fusion protein retains the ability to activate transcription from the TGTA promoter but is rendered susceptible to degradation by the COP1 E3 ubiquitin ligase. The male parent line contains a separate T-DNA cassette that expresses a mutated form of COP1, designated COP1L105A, under the control of a tapetum-specific promoter, which ensures its accumulation in the tapetum during early anther development. The L105A mutation enhances the nuclear localization of COP1 without altering its ubiquitin ligase activity, thereby increasing its concentration in the nucleus where it can efficiently bind and target the HFR1NT131-TBPm3 fusion protein for proteasomal degradation. When the female parent is crossed with the male parent, the resulting F1 hybrid inherits both cassettes. In the tapetum of the F1 hybrid, the COP1L105A protein accumulates at high levels during the critical window of anther development, binds to the HFR1NT131-TBPm3 fusion protein, and mediates its ubiquitination and subsequent degradation. This results in the complete absence of functional TBPm3 protein, rendering the TGTA promoter transcriptionally inactive and abolishing the expression of the cytotoxic gene. Consequently, pollen development proceeds normally, and the F1 hybrid exhibits full male fertility, comparable to wild-type plants. The system is fully reversible, genetically stable, and exhibits no pleiotropic effects on plant growth, development, or seed yield. The invention further provides a method for the production of hybrid seeds by crossing the male sterile female line with the fertility-restoring male line, followed by the collection of F1 seeds that are fully fertile and genetically uniform. The invention is particularly suited for use in dicotyledonous crops and eliminates the need for chemical inducers, temperature control, or the expression of inhibitory proteins, thereby offering a superior alternative to all prior art systems for hybrid seed production.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a sophisticated and highly precise genetic system for the induction and restoration of male fertility in flowering plants, based on the controlled degradation of a transcription factor essential for the activation of a synthetic promoter. The system is composed of two distinct genetic cassettes, each integrated into separate parental lines, which when combined in the F1 hybrid, result in the complete and irreversible silencing of a cytotoxic gene whose expression is otherwise responsible for male sterility. The first cassette, designated the female sterility cassette, is stably integrated into the genome of the female parent line and contains three core components: a tapetum-specific promoter, a TGTA-mutated promoter variant, and a cytotoxic gene under the control of the mutated promoter, along with a fusion gene encoding a transcription factor that is capable of activating transcription from the TGTA promoter but is engineered to be susceptible to degradation by a specific ubiquitin ligase. The second cassette, designated the male fertility restoration cassette, is stably integrated into the genome of the male parent line and contains a tapetum-specific promoter driving the expression of a mutated form of the COP1 E3 ubiquitin ligase, designated COP1L105A, which exhibits enhanced nuclear localization and retains full ubiquitin ligase activity. The key innovation of the invention lies in the molecular design of the transcription factor fusion, which combines the TBPm3 protein—a mutant form of the TATA-box binding protein that specifically recognizes and activates transcription from the TGTA-mutated promoter—with the N-terminal 131 amino acid fragment of the Arabidopsis HFR1 protein, a domain known to interact with COP1. This fusion protein, referred to as HFR1NT131-TBPm3, retains the transcriptional activation function of TBPm3 but is rendered a substrate for COP1-mediated ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. In the absence of COP1, the HFR1NT131-TBPm3 protein accumulates in the nucleus of tapetal cells during postmeiotic stages of anther development, binds to the TGTA promoter, and initiates transcription of the cytotoxic gene, leading to the disruption of tapetal function, collapse of pollen development, and complete male sterility. However, in the presence of the COP1L105A protein, which is expressed in the tapetum of the male parent and inherited by the F1 hybrid, the HFR1NT131-TBPm3 fusion protein is rapidly recognized, ubiquitinated, and degraded before it can assemble into a functional transcription initiation complex. This results in the complete absence of transcription from the TGTA promoter, thereby abolishing the expression of the cytotoxic gene and allowing normal pollen development to proceed. The system is therefore entirely dependent on the combinatorial inheritance of both cassettes: the female parent expresses the cytotoxic gene but lacks the COP1L105A protein, resulting in sterility; the male parent expresses COP1L105A but lacks the cytotoxic gene and the HFR1NT131-TBPm3 fusion, resulting in full fertility; and the F1 hybrid inherits both components, resulting in the degradation of the transcription factor and the restoration of fertility. The invention further includes the use of a modified TATA-box promoter, wherein the native TATATATG sequence is mutated to TGTA, rendering it non-functional in the absence of TBPm3 due to the inability of the endogenous wild-type TBP to bind with sufficient affinity. This mutation ensures that the cytotoxic gene is silent in all plant tissues except those where TBPm3 is provided, thereby achieving exquisite tissue specificity. The use of the HFR1NT131 domain as a degradation tag is critical, as it allows the fusion protein to be recognized by COP1 without interfering with the transcriptional function of TBPm3. The L105A mutation in COP1, which replaces a leucine residue at position 105 with an alanine, enhances the nuclear accumulation of the protein without altering its dimerization interface or its ability to bind HFR1, thereby ensuring that sufficient concentrations of COP1L105A are present in the nucleus at the precise developmental stage when TBPm3 is required for transcription. This temporal and spatial precision is achieved through the use of the Arabidopsis tapetum-specific promoter A9, which drives expression of COP1L105A specifically in the tapetum during early anther development, ensuring that the degradation machinery is in place before the transcription factor begins to accumulate. The system is further enhanced by the use of a constitutive promoter, such as Pcec, to drive the expression of the HFR1NT131-TBPm3 fusion, ensuring that the fusion protein is synthesized continuously and is available for degradation as soon as COP1L105A accumulates. The result is a system in which transcriptional activation is not merely suppressed but is rendered impossible due to the absence of the essential transcription factor, thereby achieving a level of control that is fundamentally superior to any prior art system. The invention has been demonstrated in Nicotiana tabacum, where the cytotoxic gene used is the Arabidopsis ATG6/BECLIN1 gene, which, when overexpressed in the tapetum, induces autophagic cell death and leads to the complete failure of pollen development. The expression of BECLIN1 under the control of the TGTA-TBPm3 system results in abnormal tapetal growth, delayed degeneration, and the accumulation of cellular debris in the anther, leading to the production of non-viable, deformed pollen grains. In the absence of COP1L105A, pollen viability is reduced to less than 1%, and seed set is completely abolished. However, when the female parent is crossed with a male parent expressing COP1L105A, the expression of BECLIN1 is completely abolished, tapetal development proceeds normally, pollen grains are fully viable and morphologically intact, and seed set is restored to levels indistinguishable from wild-type plants. The system exhibits no pleiotropic effects on plant growth, flowering time, leaf morphology, or seed weight, and the fertility restoration is stable across multiple generations. The invention further includes methods for the production of hybrid seeds, the maintenance of male sterile female lines, and the propagation of fertility-restoring male lines, all of which are compatible with conventional breeding practices and do not require the use of chemical inducers, temperature control, or selectable markers in the final hybrid seed. The system is scalable, genetically stable, and applicable to a wide range of dicotyledonous crops, making it a transformative tool for the commercial production of hybrid seeds.

### EXAMPLES

The invention is further illustrated by the following detailed examples, which describe the construction, characterization, and application of the two-component genetic system for male sterility and fertility restoration in Nicotiana tabacum. In the first example, a female parent line was generated by integrating a single T-DNA cassette into the tobacco genome, wherein the cassette contained the TGTA-mutated version of the tapetum-specific promoter TA29, driving the expression of the β-glucuronidase (GUS) reporter gene, and a separate expression unit encoding the HFR1NT131-TBPm3 fusion protein under the control of the constitutive Pcec promoter. Transgenic tobacco lines harboring this cassette, designated as line 1370, exhibited high levels of GUS expression specifically in the tapetum of anthers at developmental stages 2 and 3, with no detectable expression in leaves, stems, roots, or other floral tissues. Fluorimetric assays revealed GUS activity levels ranging from 10.45 to 47.57 nmoles mg−1 min−1, with an average of 21.22 ± 5.45 nmoles mg−1 min−1, confirming the high-level and stringent nature of the expression system. Histochemical staining confirmed that GUS activity was confined exclusively to the tapetal layer, with no background signal in adjacent tissues. The transgenic plants exhibited normal growth, flowering, and seed set, demonstrating that the expression of the fusion protein and the reporter gene did not impair plant fitness. In the second example, a male parent line was generated by integrating a separate T-DNA cassette into the tobacco genome, wherein the cassette contained the Arabidopsis tapetum-specific promoter A9 driving the expression of the wild-type COP1 gene. Transgenic lines expressing COP1 under this promoter, designated as line 1372, exhibited high levels of COP1 transcript accumulation in stage 1 anthers, as confirmed by quantitative RT-PCR, with expression declining sharply in later stages. When line 1370 was crossed with line 1372, the resulting F1 progeny exhibited a four- to six-fold reduction in GUS activity compared to the female parent, indicating partial transcriptional repression. However, GUS expression was still detectable, and histochemical staining revealed residual activity in the tapetum, suggesting that the nuclear concentration of wild-type COP1 was insufficient to completely degrade all HFR1NT131-TBPm3 molecules. This partial repression was attributed to the known cytoplasmic-nuclear shuttling behavior of COP1, which limits its steady-state nuclear concentration. In the third example, a modified version of the male parent cassette was constructed, wherein the COP1 gene was mutated to introduce the L105A substitution, replacing leucine at position 105 with alanine. This mutation was introduced via site-directed mutagenesis using PCR-based methods and confirmed by DNA sequencing. The resulting construct, designated as line 1373, was transformed into tobacco, and transgenic lines were selected based on high expression levels of COP1L105A in stage 1 anthers. When line 1370 was crossed with line 1373, the resulting F1 progeny exhibited a complete absence of GUS activity, with fluorimetric assays showing levels indistinguishable from negative controls. Histochemical staining confirmed the total absence of blue staining in the tapetum, and RT-PCR analysis revealed no detectable transcripts of the GUS gene, demonstrating that transcription had been completely abolished. In the fourth example, the cytotoxic gene BECLIN1 was substituted for GUS in the female parent cassette, generating line 1371. Transgenic plants expressing BECLIN1 under the TGTA-TBPm3 system exhibited complete male sterility, with pollen viability reduced to 0.76 ± 0.78%, pollen germination at 0.76 ± 0.73%, and zero seed set. Transmission electron microscopy revealed that tapetal cells in these plants underwent abnormal growth, failed to degenerate at the appropriate developmental stage, and accumulated cellular debris, leading to the collapse of microspore development. In contrast, the F1 progeny generated by crossing line 1371 with line 1373 exhibited pollen viability of 74.58 ± 1.2%, pollen germination of 69 ± 4.6%, and seed set of 71.9 ± 6.1 mg/pod, all of which were statistically indistinguishable from wild-type controls. Scanning electron microscopy confirmed that pollen grains in the F1 progeny were morphologically identical to those of wild-type plants, with normal exine and intine structures. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis of BECLIN1 transcript levels in the F1 progeny showed complete abolition of expression, with no detectable transcripts above background levels. The fertility restoration was stable across three generations, with no segregation distortion or loss of function. In the fifth example, the male sterile female line (1371) was maintained by crossing heterozygous plants (BECLIN1/−) with wild-type plants (−/−), resulting in a 1:1 segregation ratio of sterile to fertile progeny, demonstrating that the sterility phenotype is genetically stable and can be propagated without the need for chemical selection. The fertility-restoring male line (1373) was propagated by self-pollination, and all progeny retained full fertility and high expression of COP1L105A. The invention was further tested in multiple genetic backgrounds, including different tobacco cultivars, and the results were consistent across all lines, demonstrating the robustness and broad applicability of the system. The invention thus provides a fully functional, scalable, and commercially viable system for hybrid seed production that operates through the complete transcriptional silencing of a cytotoxic gene in the F1 hybrid, achieved by the targeted degradation of an essential transcription factor, and represents a significant advancement over all prior art systems.
