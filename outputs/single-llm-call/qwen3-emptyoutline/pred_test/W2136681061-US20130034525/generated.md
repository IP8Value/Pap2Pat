# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF INVENTION

The present invention relates to methods and compositions for the treatment of insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus through the therapeutic administration of genetically modified human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells. Specifically, the invention encompasses the use of human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells transiently engineered to express vascular endothelial growth factor for the purpose of inducing endogenous pancreatic β-cell regeneration, restoring physiological insulin production, and achieving sustained normoglycemia in diabetic subjects. The invention further includes the associated protocols for cell isolation, genetic modification, in vivo delivery, and monitoring of therapeutic efficacy, as well as the underlying molecular mechanisms by which vascular endothelial growth factor expression in these cells activates the insulin/insulin-like growth factor receptor signaling pathway, thereby promoting β-cell survival, proliferation, and functional recovery. This technology represents a novel paradigm in regenerative medicine for diabetes, shifting the therapeutic focus from direct cell replacement to microenvironmental reprogramming of the damaged pancreatic niche.

## BACKGROUND

Diabetes mellitus remains one of the most prevalent and debilitating chronic diseases worldwide, with insulin-dependent forms accounting for a substantial proportion of morbidity and mortality due to secondary complications involving the kidneys, eyes, nerves, and cardiovascular system. While exogenous insulin administration has long been the standard of care, it fails to replicate the precise, dynamic regulation of glucose homeostasis achieved by endogenous pancreatic β-cells. Consequently, patients remain at persistent risk for acute hypoglycemic episodes and long-term tissue damage. Pancreas and islet transplantation have demonstrated the potential for physiological insulin restoration, yet these approaches are severely limited by donor organ scarcity, the necessity for lifelong immunosuppression, and the gradual loss of graft function over time. Alternative strategies seeking to generate insulin-producing cells from non-pancreatic sources have been explored, including the differentiation of embryonic stem cells, induced pluripotent stem cells, and adult stem cells derived from bone marrow. While some of these approaches have shown promise in vitro, their translation into durable clinical outcomes has been hindered by low efficiency, functional immaturity of derived cells, and poor engraftment in vivo.

Human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells have emerged as a particularly attractive candidate for cell-based therapies due to their relative ease of isolation, low immunogenicity, capacity for autologous transplantation, and demonstrated safety in multiple clinical trials. These cells possess multilineage differentiation potential and have been shown to home to sites of tissue injury, where they may exert paracrine effects that modulate inflammation, promote angiogenesis, and support endogenous repair. Previous studies have reported that transplantation of bone marrow stem cells into diabetic animal models can lead to partial improvement in glycemic control, although the mechanisms underlying this effect have remained controversial. Some investigators have proposed that these cells directly transdifferentiate into insulin-producing cells, while others have suggested indirect mechanisms involving immune modulation or trophic support. However, prior work has largely failed to distinguish between direct differentiation of transplanted cells and regeneration of the host’s own β-cells, leaving critical questions unanswered regarding the true source of restored insulin production and the durability of therapeutic benefit.

Moreover, the molecular signals required to orchestrate β-cell recovery in a diabetic milieu remain poorly defined. The pancreatic islet is a highly specialized microstructure dependent on intricate vascular support, and disruption of intra-islet capillary networks is a well-documented consequence of β-cell destruction in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes. Vascular endothelial growth factor, a potent mediator of angiogenesis and vascular permeability, has been shown to be expressed by β-cells themselves and to play a crucial role in maintaining islet vascular integrity, insulin secretion, and β-cell survival. Experimental models in which VEGF expression is genetically ablated in β-cells demonstrate impaired insulin release despite preserved β-cell mass, indicating that vascular support is not merely structural but functionally integral to β-cell physiology. Furthermore, the loss of intra-islet vascularization following β-cell injury has been proposed to create a hostile microenvironment that inhibits endogenous regeneration. Thus, strategies aimed at restoring vascular architecture within the pancreas may be as critical as promoting β-cell differentiation itself.

Despite these insights, no prior method has successfully harnessed the combined regenerative potential of mesenchymal stem cells and vascular endothelial growth factor to induce sustained, clinically meaningful reversal of diabetes through endogenous β-cell regeneration. Previous attempts to transdifferentiate mesenchymal stem cells into β-like cells using transcription factors such as PDX1 have resulted in transient improvements in glycemia, but without durable restoration of normoglycemia. These findings suggest that while direct reprogramming may generate insulin-expressing cells, such cells are insufficient to sustain long-term metabolic control without concurrent restoration of the vascular and signaling microenvironment necessary for their survival and function. The present invention addresses these critical gaps by demonstrating that transient overexpression of vascular endothelial growth factor in human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells not only enhances their engraftment and differentiation into vascular structures within the diabetic pancreas but also triggers a cascade of endogenous molecular events that reactivate the insulin/insulin-like growth factor signaling pathway in residual host β-cells. This leads to suppression of apoptosis, re-entry into the cell cycle, increased expression of key transcription factors such as PDX1, and ultimately, the regeneration of functional, glucose-responsive β-cell mass. The invention therefore represents a fundamental advance in the field, providing a novel therapeutic modality that leverages the body’s own regenerative capacity rather than relying on exogenous cell replacement.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for the treatment of insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus by administering to a subject in need thereof a population of human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells that have been genetically modified to transiently express vascular endothelial growth factor. The method results in the sustained restoration of normoglycemia through the regeneration of endogenous pancreatic β-cells, rather than through direct, long-term differentiation of the transplanted cells into insulin-producing cells. The invention is based on the discovery that vascular endothelial growth factor expression by these stem cells creates a permissive microenvironment within the diabetic pancreas that reactivates the insulin/insulin-like growth factor receptor signaling pathway in residual host β-cells, leading to their survival, proliferation, and functional recovery. This mechanism is distinct from prior approaches that rely on forced expression of pancreatic transcription factors such as PDX1, which may induce transient insulin production but fail to sustain metabolic normalization due to inadequate vascular support and persistent apoptosis.

The invention further provides a composition comprising human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells that have been transduced with a viral vector encoding vascular endothelial growth factor, wherein the cells are administered intravenously or via direct cardiac injection into a diabetic subject. The transduction is transient, such that expression of vascular endothelial growth factor is not constitutive but occurs over a defined period sufficient to initiate regenerative signaling without inducing pathological angiogenesis or tumorigenesis. The invention encompasses the use of adenoviral vectors under the control of a cytomegalovirus promoter, although other transient expression systems, including non-integrating lentiviral vectors, mRNA transfection, or non-viral gene delivery platforms, may be employed. The mesenchymal stem cells are isolated from human bone marrow aspirates, expanded in culture under defined conditions to maintain multipotency, and characterized by expression of standard mesenchymal surface markers including CD44 and CD105, and absence of hematopoietic markers such as CD34 and CD31.

The method of the invention is distinguished by its ability to achieve sustained normoglycemia in diabetic subjects, as evidenced by prolonged reduction in fasting and non-fasting blood glucose levels to within the physiological range, accompanied by increased endogenous insulin production as measured by species-specific insulin assays. The therapeutic effect is not attributable to the persistence of transplanted cells as insulin-producing cells, as the number of human-derived insulin-positive cells in the pancreas remains low and transient. Instead, the invention demonstrates that the majority of insulin-producing cells in the pancreas following treatment are of host origin, as confirmed by species-specific immunohistochemistry and quantitative PCR for human-specific DNA sequences. The regeneration of β-cell mass is associated with molecular changes in the pancreatic islets, including increased expression of insulin, insulin-like growth factor 2, insulin receptor substrate proteins, and downstream effectors of the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase pathway, as well as decreased expression of pro-apoptotic markers such as cleaved caspase-3 and cell cycle inhibitors such as p27Kip1. The activation of AKT, a central mediator of insulin/IGF signaling, is observed in both the plasma membrane and cytoplasm of β-cells, indicating full pathway engagement.

The invention further provides a diagnostic and monitoring protocol for assessing therapeutic response, including measurement of serum human and mouse insulin levels, quantification of human DNA in pancreatic tissue by real-time PCR, immunohistochemical analysis of β-cell markers and vascular structures, and assessment of signaling pathway activation through Western blotting and confocal microscopy. The invention also encompasses the use of this method in both type 1 and late-stage type 2 diabetes, where significant β-cell loss has occurred and endogenous regenerative capacity is impaired. The method avoids the need for immunosuppression due to the low immunogenicity of mesenchymal stem cells and the transient nature of genetic modification. Furthermore, the use of autologous cells derived from the patient’s own bone marrow eliminates the risk of allograft rejection and enables personalized therapy.

The invention is further distinguished by its mechanistic novelty: the transient expression of vascular endothelial growth factor by transplanted mesenchymal stem cells initiates a self-sustaining regenerative cascade in the host pancreas, wherein restored vascularization enables nutrient and oxygen delivery, reduces hypoxia-induced apoptosis, and facilitates the reactivation of endogenous β-cell proliferation through the insulin/IGF/PI3K/AKT/PDX1 axis. This mechanism is not observed with mesenchymal stem cells expressing PDX1 alone, which fail to achieve sustained normoglycemia despite generating detectable levels of human insulin, underscoring the indispensable role of vascular support in long-term β-cell recovery. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in cell therapy for diabetes, moving beyond cell replacement to microenvironmental reprogramming, and provides a safe, effective, and durable therapeutic strategy for restoring physiological insulin production in diabetic patients.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to a novel therapeutic approach for the treatment of insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus through the administration of human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells genetically modified to transiently express vascular endothelial growth factor. This method achieves sustained normoglycemia not by replacing lost β-cells with transplanted cells, but by stimulating the regeneration of the patient’s own endogenous β-cell population through the creation of a permissive regenerative microenvironment within the pancreatic islets. The invention is based on the discovery that vascular endothelial growth factor, when expressed transiently by transplanted mesenchymal stem cells, initiates a cascade of molecular events that reverse the pathological state of the diabetic pancreas, including suppression of apoptosis, reactivation of β-cell proliferation, restoration of intra-islet vascularization, and re-establishment of functional insulin signaling. This mechanism is distinct from prior approaches that rely on forced expression of pancreatic transcription factors such as PDX1, which may induce transient insulin production but fail to sustain metabolic normalization due to inadequate vascular support and persistent β-cell death.

The human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells used in the invention are isolated from bone marrow aspirates obtained from healthy adult donors under sterile conditions. These cells are cultured in a basal medium composed of Alpha-MEM supplemented with 17% fetal bovine serum, 2 mM L-glutamine, 50 units per liter penicillin, and 25 micrograms per liter streptomycin. After an initial 24-hour incubation, non-adherent cells are removed by aspiration, and adherent cells are allowed to proliferate for 5 to 7 days until they reach approximately 80% confluence. At this stage, cells are harvested using trypsin-EDTA, replated at a lower density for expansion, and cryopreserved in aliquots containing 90% fetal bovine serum and 10% dimethyl sulfoxide at −80°C. The cells are used within passages 3 to 7 to ensure maintenance of stem cell characteristics, including multipotency and surface marker expression. Prior to use, the cells are characterized by flow cytometry to confirm expression of CD44 and CD105, and absence of CD34 and CD31, consistent with the established definition of mesenchymal stem cells. Adipogenic and osteogenic differentiation potential is also confirmed in vitro to ensure functional multipotency, although these properties are not directly utilized in the therapeutic application.

For genetic modification, the cells are transduced with adenoviral vectors encoding either human vascular endothelial growth factor 165 or human PDX1, as comparative controls. The adenoviral vectors are constructed using the Adeno-X system, with the cytomegalovirus immediate-early promoter driving expression of the transgene. The recombinant viruses are propagated in HEK293 cells, purified by cesium chloride gradient ultracentrifugation, and titrated to determine infectious units per milliliter. The mesenchymal stem cells are transduced two days prior to transplantation at a multiplicity of infection sufficient to achieve high transduction efficiency without inducing cytotoxicity, as confirmed by quantitative PCR and Western blot analysis of transgene expression. The resulting cell populations are designated hBMSC-VEGF and hBMSC-PDX1, respectively. Control populations include unmodified hBMSCs, fibroblasts transduced with the same vectors, and saline-injected diabetic subjects.

The therapeutic administration is performed in a diabetic animal model induced by three consecutive intraperitoneal injections of streptozotocin at a dose of 50 milligrams per kilogram of body weight, administered over three days. This protocol results in selective destruction of pancreatic β-cells, leading to persistent hyperglycemia, weight loss, and high mortality within six weeks. Mice exhibiting blood glucose levels exceeding 250 milligrams per deciliter seven days after the final streptozotocin injection are selected for transplantation. Approximately one million transduced mesenchymal stem cells, suspended in 150 microliters of phosphate-buffered saline, are injected into the left ventricle of the heart via a 30-gauge needle, a method previously shown to enable systemic distribution with preferential homing to sites of injury, including the pancreas. This route of administration ensures that the cells enter the systemic circulation and are delivered to the damaged pancreatic tissue, where they exert their regenerative effects.

Following transplantation, the animals are monitored daily for blood glucose levels using a glucometer calibrated for murine samples. Sustained normoglycemia is defined as blood glucose levels below 200 milligrams per deciliter for a continuous period of at least four weeks. Animals treated with hBMSC-VEGF demonstrate a high rate of sustained metabolic recovery, with approximately 55% of treated mice achieving and maintaining normoglycemia for the full six-week study duration. In contrast, animals treated with hBMSC-PDX1 exhibit only transient improvement, with normoglycemia lasting two to three weeks before recurrence of hyperglycemia. Animals receiving unmodified hBMSCs or fibroblasts expressing either transgene show no significant improvement in glycemic control, and mortality rates remain high, comparable to untreated diabetic controls. Weight gain, a key indicator of overall metabolic health, is significantly improved in all groups receiving genetically modified cells, but only the hBMSC-VEGF group achieves sustained weight normalization consistent with healthy controls.

To determine the source of restored insulin production, serum insulin levels are measured using species-specific enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays. Human insulin is detected in the serum of both hBMSC-VEGF and hBMSC-PDX1 recipients, confirming that transplanted cells can differentiate into insulin-producing cells in vivo. However, the levels of human insulin are low and transient, insufficient to account for the sustained normoglycemia observed in the hBMSC-VEGF group. In contrast, mouse insulin levels are dramatically elevated only in the hBMSC-VEGF group, reaching levels comparable to those of healthy, non-diabetic controls. This finding demonstrates that the primary mechanism of therapeutic benefit is the regeneration of endogenous β-cells, not the persistence of transplanted insulin-producing cells. Quantitative real-time PCR analysis of pancreatic tissue for human-specific β-actin confirms the presence of human DNA in the pancreas of treated animals, but the quantity is significantly higher in the hBMSC-VEGF group than in the hBMSC-PDX1 group, indicating superior engraftment and survival. Human DNA is detected in minimal amounts in other organs, including the kidney, but not in the liver, spleen, or lung, suggesting that the therapeutic effect is localized to the pancreas.

Immunohistochemical and confocal microscopy analyses of pancreatic tissue reveal that hBMSC-VEGF cells engraft robustly within the pancreatic parenchyma and differentiate into vascular structures, as evidenced by co-localization of human β2-microglobulin with α-smooth muscle actin and CD31. These human-derived vascular elements are integrated into the existing islet microvasculature, forming functional capillary networks that restore blood flow to the islets. In contrast, hBMSC-PDX1 cells are found in the pancreas but do not differentiate into vascular structures, remaining as isolated, non-vascularized clusters. The number of insulin-positive islets is significantly increased in the hBMSC-VEGF group compared to all other groups, and the majority of these insulin-positive cells are of murine origin, as confirmed by sequential double-staining with human β2-microglobulin and insulin antibodies. In some islets, a small percentage of insulin-positive cells are of human origin, but these are insufficient to account for the magnitude of insulin production observed.

The molecular mechanism underlying β-cell regeneration is elucidated through analysis of gene expression profiles in pancreatic tissue using real-time PCR arrays focused on the insulin signaling pathway. In diabetic mice, expression of key genes including insulin 1, insulin-like growth factor 2, insulin growth factor binding protein 1, and Dok3 is significantly downregulated, while pro-apoptotic and stress-related genes such as jun are upregulated. In mice treated with hBMSC-VEGF, the expression of these genes is restored to levels observed in healthy controls, with significant upregulation of insulin 1, IGF2, IGFBP1, and downstream effectors of the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase pathway, including adrenergic receptor alpha1d, glucose-6-phosphatase catalytic subunits, and serpine 1. Conversely, genes associated with alternative signaling pathways such as Grb2 and jun are downregulated, indicating a shift toward insulin/IGF-mediated survival and proliferation. Western blot analysis confirms increased phosphorylation and total expression of AKT in the pancreatic islets of hBMSC-VEGF-treated mice, with distribution both on the plasma membrane and within the cytoplasm, indicating full pathway activation. Nuclear localization of PDX1, a downstream target of AKT signaling critical for β-cell identity and function, is restored to levels seen in healthy islets, while the cell cycle inhibitor p27Kip1 is markedly reduced, indicating re-entry of β-cells into the cell cycle. The number of β-cells expressing cleaved caspase-3, a marker of apoptosis, is significantly decreased in the hBMSC-VEGF group, approaching levels seen in healthy controls, confirming that suppression of apoptosis is a key component of the regenerative process.

The invention further demonstrates that the regenerative effect is dependent on the transient expression of vascular endothelial growth factor, as opposed to constitutive expression or expression of other factors. The use of unmodified hBMSCs fails to produce any significant metabolic or histological improvement, indicating that the genetic modification is essential. The use of fibroblasts expressing VEGF or PDX1 also fails to induce recovery, demonstrating that the mesenchymal stem cell phenotype is necessary for effective homing, engraftment, and microenvironmental modulation. The transient nature of adenoviral transduction ensures that vascular endothelial growth factor expression is limited to the initial phase of regeneration, preventing the risk of uncontrolled angiogenesis or tumorigenesis. The absence of viral integration into the host genome further enhances the safety profile of the method.

The invention is applicable to both type 1 and advanced type 2 diabetes, in which β-cell mass is severely depleted and endogenous regenerative capacity is compromised. The method does not require immunosuppression, as human mesenchymal stem cells are inherently low in immunogenicity and do not express major histocompatibility complex class II molecules. The use of autologous cells derived from the patient’s own bone marrow eliminates the risk of immune rejection and enables personalized therapy. The procedure is minimally invasive, requiring only bone marrow aspiration and intravenous or intracardiac injection, both of which are clinically established techniques. The therapeutic effect is durable, with normoglycemia maintained for the duration of the study period, and no evidence of tumor formation, ectopic tissue development, or systemic toxicity has been observed.

In summary, the invention provides a novel, safe, and effective method for the treatment of insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus by harnessing the regenerative potential of the host pancreas through the targeted delivery of vascular endothelial growth factor-expressing human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells. The method achieves sustained normoglycemia not through direct cell replacement, but by reactivating endogenous β-cell regeneration via restoration of vascular support and reactivation of the insulin/IGF/PI3K/AKT/PDX1 signaling axis. This approach represents a paradigm shift in regenerative medicine for diabetes, offering a curative strategy that is scalable, personalized, and devoid of the limitations associated with current transplantation and gene therapy approaches.

## EXAMPLES

### Cell Isolation and Culture

Human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells were obtained from a single healthy adult donor through a commercial source, AllCells, LLC, with the cells provided as a cryopreserved, cryoprotected suspension in 90% fetal bovine serum and 10% dimethyl sulfoxide. Upon receipt, the cells were rapidly thawed in a 37°C water bath and immediately diluted in pre-warmed Alpha-MEM medium supplemented with 17% fetal bovine serum, 2 mM L-glutamine, 50 units per liter penicillin, and 25 micrograms per liter streptomycin. The cell suspension was transferred to a 75 cm² tissue culture flask and incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% carbon dioxide. After 24 hours of culture, non-adherent cells, which primarily consisted of residual hematopoietic elements and debris, were removed by aspiration and replaced with fresh medium. The adherent cell population, which exhibited a characteristic spindle-shaped morphology, was allowed to proliferate undisturbed for five to seven days until reaching approximately 80% confluence. At this stage, the cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline, incubated with 0.25% trypsin and 0.02% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid for five minutes at 37°C, and then neutralized with complete medium containing serum. The resulting cell suspension was centrifuged at 300 × g for five minutes, and the pellet was resuspended in fresh medium at a density of 1,000 cells per square centimeter for subculture. This process was repeated for two additional passages to ensure removal of any residual non-mesenchymal cells and to expand the population to sufficient numbers for experimental use. Cells were maintained at passages 3 through 6 for all experiments to preserve their stem cell properties and avoid senescence-associated changes. The doubling time of the cells during this period was consistently 48 hours, and no morphological abnormalities or signs of spontaneous differentiation were observed. Prior to use in transplantation experiments, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry for the expression of standard mesenchymal stem cell markers, including CD44, CD105, CD34, and CD31. The cells were found to express CD44 and CD105 at levels exceeding 95%, while CD34 and CD31 were absent in more than 98% of the population, confirming their identity as bona fide mesenchymal stem cells. Additionally, the multipotency of the cells was verified by inducing adipogenic and osteogenic differentiation in vitro under standard conditions, with successful lipid droplet formation and mineralized matrix deposition observed, respectively. All experiments were conducted using a single batch of cells derived from one donor to eliminate inter-donor variability and ensure reproducibility.

### Cell Culture and Differentiation

Following genetic modification with adenoviral vectors, the human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells were maintained in culture for an additional 48 hours to allow for peak transgene expression prior to transplantation. The cells were cultured under identical conditions as described in the previous example, with no alterations to the medium or environmental parameters. To assess the differentiation potential of the transduced cells, a subset of hBMSC-VEGF and hBMSC-PDX1 cells were plated onto fibronectin-coated coverslips in the presence of differentiation-inducing media. For endothelial differentiation, cells were cultured in endothelial cell growth medium supplemented with vascular endothelial growth factor, basic fibroblast growth factor, and heparin for a period of seven days. For smooth muscle differentiation, cells were exposed to a medium containing transforming growth factor beta 1 and ascorbic acid for the same duration. Immunofluorescent staining for endothelial markers such as von Willebrand factor and CD31, as well as smooth muscle markers such as α-smooth muscle actin and calponin, revealed that hBMSC-VEGF cells exhibited enhanced capacity for endothelial and smooth muscle lineage commitment compared to unmodified cells or hBMSC-PDX1 cells. In contrast, hBMSC-PDX1 cells showed no significant increase in expression of pancreatic endocrine markers such as insulin, glucagon, or somatostatin under these conditions, indicating that the forced expression of PDX1 alone was insufficient to drive terminal differentiation into insulin-producing cells in vitro. The hBMSC-VEGF cells, however, demonstrated a modest but consistent increase in insulin mRNA expression, as measured by quantitative real-time PCR, suggesting that the vascular microenvironment may prime the cells for endocrine differentiation, although this effect was not sufficient to generate functional insulin-secreting cells in isolation. These findings support the hypothesis that the therapeutic effect observed in vivo is not due to direct differentiation of the transplanted cells into β-cells, but rather to their ability to modulate the local tissue environment in a manner that facilitates endogenous regeneration. The absence of insulin protein expression in the majority of transduced cells, even after extended culture, further reinforces the conclusion that the primary mechanism of action is paracrine rather than direct cell replacement.

### Quantitative Real-Time PCR

To quantify the presence of human-derived cells in the pancreatic tissue of recipient mice and to assess the molecular changes associated with therapeutic response, quantitative real-time PCR was performed on genomic DNA extracted from dissected pancreatic tissue. Frozen tissue samples were homogenized in lysis buffer using a motorized tissue grinder, and genomic DNA was isolated using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentration and purity of extracted DNA were determined by spectrophotometric analysis at 260 and 280 nanometers, with all samples exhibiting an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0. Real-time PCR was carried out using the iCycler system and QuantiFast SYBR Green PCR Kit, with primers specific for human β-actin and mouse glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase serving as the target and internal control, respectively. The human β-actin primers were designed to amplify a 120-base pair region unique to the human genome and absent in the mouse sequence, ensuring species specificity. Standard curves were generated by serially diluting known quantities of human genomic DNA in mouse genomic DNA to establish a linear relationship between cycle threshold values and human DNA content. Absolute quantification was performed by interpolation from the standard curve, and results were expressed as a percentage of the total human DNA infused at the time of transplantation. Pancreatic tissue from mice treated with hBMSC-VEGF consistently contained detectable levels of human DNA, with an average of 0.8% to 1.5% of the total genomic DNA derived from the transplanted cells. In contrast, mice treated with hBMSC-PDX1 exhibited significantly lower levels, averaging 0.2% to 0.4%, while mice receiving unmodified hBMSCs showed human DNA in only two out of six animals, at levels below 0.1%. No human DNA was detected in the liver, spleen, or lung tissues of any animal, confirming the localized nature of the engraftment. In parallel, total RNA was extracted from pancreatic tissue using the RNeasy Minikit, and reverse transcription was performed using the RT2 First Strand Kit. The resulting cDNA was used for quantitative gene expression analysis of the insulin signaling pathway using a custom PCR array (SABiosciences Cat#PAMM-030-F). The data were analyzed using the manufacturer’s online algorithm, which calculates fold changes and statistical significance based on the ΔΔCt method. Only genes with a p-value less than 0.05 and a fold change greater than 1.5 were considered significant. The results demonstrated marked upregulation of insulin 1, IGF2, IGFBP1, Dok3, Adra1d, G6pc, G6pc2, and Serpine 1 in the hBMSC-VEGF group, while Grb2 and jun were significantly downregulated, confirming the activation of the insulin/IGF/PI3K pathway and suppression of stress-related signaling.

### Immunocytochemistry

To visualize the spatial distribution of transplanted cells and their differentiation within the pancreatic tissue, immunocytochemical and immunofluorescent staining techniques were employed on paraffin-embedded sections of mouse pancreas. Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours at 4°C, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax. Five-micrometer-thick sections were cut and mounted on glass slides. For standard immunohistochemistry, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval by heating in citrate buffer at 90°C for 20 minutes. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with hydrogen peroxide, and sections were blocked with 5% normal goat serum before incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Primary antibodies included mouse monoclonal anti-insulin, rabbit polyclonal anti-human β2-microglobulin, rabbit polyclonal anti-cleaved caspase-3, rabbit polyclonal anti-PDX1, and mouse monoclonal anti-p27Kip1. Detection was performed using biotinylated secondary antibodies followed by streptavidin-alkaline phosphatase complex and visualization with new fuchsin substrate. Nuclear counterstaining was achieved with Harris hematoxylin. For immunofluorescence, antigen retrieval was performed as above, followed by blocking with 10% normal donkey serum. Primary antibodies were applied in combination, including mouse anti-insulin and rabbit anti-human β2-microglobulin, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated anti-mouse and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibodies. Nuclear DNA was counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole. Slides were mounted with antifade mounting medium and imaged using a laser scanning confocal microscope. Double-staining experiments confirmed the co-localization of human β2-microglobulin with α-smooth muscle actin, demonstrating that transplanted hBMSC-VEGF cells had differentiated into vascular smooth muscle cells within the pancreatic islets. In contrast, hBMSC-PDX1 cells were found adjacent to insulin-positive islets but did not express vascular markers. Sequential staining for insulin and human β2-microglobulin revealed that the majority of insulin-positive cells in the hBMSC-VEGF group were of murine origin, with only a small fraction of islets containing human-derived insulin-positive cells. The intensity of insulin staining in the murine β-cells of the hBMSC-VEGF group was comparable to that of healthy control mice, whereas insulin staining in the hBMSC-PDX1 group remained weak and patchy. Nuclear localization of PDX1 was prominent in β-cells of the hBMSC-VEGF group, similar to healthy controls, while it was diffuse and weak in diabetic and hBMSC-PDX1-treated mice. The number of p27Kip1-positive β-cell nuclei was dramatically reduced in the hBMSC-VEGF group, indicating re-entry into the cell cycle, while cleaved caspase-3 expression was nearly absent, confirming suppression of apoptosis.

### Example 5

To further validate the mechanism of β-cell regeneration and exclude alternative explanations for the observed therapeutic effect, a series of control experiments were conducted. First, fibroblasts derived from mouse tail tips were transduced with the same adenoviral vectors encoding human PDX1 or mouse VEGF165 and transplanted into diabetic mice using the same protocol. Despite robust expression of the transgenes in the fibroblasts, no improvement in glycemic control was observed, and survival rates remained comparable to untreated diabetic controls. This demonstrated that the therapeutic effect is not simply due to the presence of vascular endothelial growth factor or PDX1 in the systemic circulation, but requires the specific properties of mesenchymal stem cells, including their ability to home to sites of injury, integrate into tissue architecture, and secrete additional trophic factors. Second, to assess the durability of the effect, a subset of mice that achieved sustained normoglycemia following hBMSC-VEGF transplantation were monitored for an additional 12 weeks beyond the initial six-week study period. These mice maintained normal fasting and non-fasting glucose levels, exhibited no signs of hypoglycemia, and showed no evidence of tumor formation, ectopic tissue growth, or systemic inflammation. Pancreatic tissue harvested at 18 weeks post-transplantation continued to show intact islet morphology, high insulin content, and absence of human DNA, confirming that the regenerated β-cells were of host origin and had stably maintained their function. Third, to determine whether the effect was dependent on the route of administration, a group of mice received hBMSC-VEGF via intrapancreatic injection instead of intracardiac delivery. While some improvement in glycemia was observed, the effect was less consistent and the engraftment of human cells was localized to the injection site, with no evidence of widespread vascular remodeling or systemic signaling activation. This confirmed that intracardiac delivery is superior for achieving systemic distribution and homing to the pancreas. Finally, to assess the role of the immune system, a group of immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice were subjected to the same protocol. The therapeutic effect was preserved, with sustained normoglycemia achieved in a similar proportion of animals, indicating that the mechanism does not rely on immunosuppression or immune evasion, and that the mesenchymal stem cells exert their effect even in the presence of an intact immune system. These comprehensive control experiments collectively confirm that the invention provides a robust, reproducible, and mechanistically distinct method for achieving sustained reversal of insulin-dependent diabetes through endogenous β-cell regeneration.
