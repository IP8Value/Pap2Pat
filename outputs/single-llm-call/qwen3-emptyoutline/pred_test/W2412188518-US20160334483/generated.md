# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has long been a cornerstone of noninvasive medical diagnostics, offering unparalleled soft-tissue contrast and three-dimensional anatomical detail without the use of ionizing radiation. Among the various MRI techniques, ultrashort echo time (UTE) and zero echo time (ZTE) sequences have emerged as critical tools for visualizing tissues with extremely short transverse relaxation times, such as cortical bone, tendons, ligaments, lung parenchyma, and the inner ear structures. These tissues, characterized by T2* values on the order of microseconds to a few hundred microseconds, decay too rapidly to be captured by conventional MRI sequences, which typically require echo times exceeding one millisecond. As a result, these structures appear signal void or severely degraded in standard imaging protocols, limiting diagnostic utility in neurology, otolaryngology, orthopedics, and pulmonary medicine.

The ZTE technique, in particular, has gained prominence due to its ability to initiate signal acquisition immediately following a hard radiofrequency (RF) excitation pulse, effectively achieving an echo time approaching zero. This is accomplished by applying magnetic field gradients prior to the RF pulse, ensuring that the k-space trajectory begins to evolve before the signal decays appreciably. However, this advantage comes with significant practical constraints. The fundamental design of ZTE necessitates that the excitation bandwidth and readout bandwidth be identical, since the same gradient waveform is used both to spatially encode the signal and to excite the spins. Consequently, any attempt to increase the readout bandwidth—thereby accelerating the rate of k-space traversal and reducing the temporal window during which signal decay occurs—inevitably increases the excitation bandwidth as well. This dual dependency imposes a severe limitation on the clinical applicability of ZTE, particularly at high magnetic field strengths such as 7 Tesla, where the benefits of increased signal-to-noise ratio are counterbalanced by heightened susceptibility to off-resonance effects and accelerated T2* decay.

An increase in excitation bandwidth directly correlates with a reduction in the duration of the RF pulse required to achieve a given flip angle. Shorter RF pulses demand higher peak power and result in elevated specific absorption rate (SAR), a critical safety parameter regulated by international standards to prevent tissue heating. At 7 Tesla, SAR limits are particularly stringent, often constraining the maximum allowable flip angle and pulse duration to levels that compromise image quality. Moreover, the rapid switching between transmit and receive (T/R) modes required in ZTE sequences presents another formidable challenge. Most clinical MRI scanners are equipped with T/R switches that exhibit switching delays on the order of tens of microseconds or longer. During this delay, the receiver gate remains closed, and no signal is acquired. In ZTE, the k-space center—the region most critical for image contrast and resolution—is sampled immediately following the RF pulse, meaning that this switching delay results in the irreversible loss of the central k-space data. The number of missing k-space points is proportional to the product of the T/R switching delay and the excitation bandwidth, so increasing the bandwidth to mitigate signal decay inadvertently exacerbates the size of the missing central region.

To address this issue, the PETRA (Pointwise-encoding time reduction with radial acquisition) technique was developed as a hybrid extension of ZTE and single-point imaging (SPI). PETRA retains the radial k-space sampling of ZTE but introduces a separate, low-bandwidth SPI acquisition to recover the central k-space points lost during the T/R switching delay. In SPI, the gradient amplitude is stepped down incrementally, allowing each k-space point to be sampled individually at progressively lower gradient strengths. This enables the acquisition of the central k-space region without requiring a fast T/R switch, thereby circumventing the primary hardware limitation of conventional ZTE. However, while PETRA successfully recovers the central k-space data, it does not resolve the fundamental trade-off between excitation bandwidth and signal decay. When higher readout bandwidths are employed to improve temporal resolution and reduce blurring from off-resonance and T2* decay, the number of k-space points lost during the T/R delay increases proportionally. Consequently, the SPI acquisition must cover a larger region of k-space, requiring more individual gradient steps and significantly increasing the total scan time. This elongation of acquisition duration introduces motion artifacts, reduces patient throughput, and diminishes the clinical utility of the technique, particularly in settings where rapid imaging is essential.

Furthermore, the chemical shift between water and fat protons becomes more pronounced at higher field strengths, leading to spatial misregistration and ghosting artifacts in regions with heterogeneous fat distribution. These off-resonance effects manifest as duplicated edges, smearing, and loss of fine anatomical detail, particularly at tissue-air interfaces such as those found in the paranasal sinuses, middle ear, and cochlea. The severity of these artifacts scales with the bandwidth, as the spatial displacement of fat relative to water is inversely proportional to the readout bandwidth. Thus, while increasing the readout bandwidth improves the temporal fidelity of signal sampling, it simultaneously worsens the spatial distortion caused by off-resonance. Conventional approaches to mitigate off-resonance artifacts—such as frequency-selective saturation, fat suppression, or post-processing correction algorithms—often fail in the context of ultrashort T2* tissues, where the signal is already attenuated beyond the detection threshold, rendering suppression techniques ineffective or counterproductive.

Existing solutions to these challenges have been largely constrained by hardware limitations and the rigid coupling of excitation and readout parameters in ZTE. Attempts to use frequency-modulated or phase-modulated RF pulses to achieve broader excitation profiles with lower peak power have introduced new complications, including nonlinear phase distortions that require complex iterative reconstruction methods to correct. These methods, while theoretically promising, are computationally intensive, time-consuming, and often incompatible with real-time clinical workflows. Moreover, they do not address the fundamental issue of T/R switching delays or the escalating SAR burden associated with high-bandwidth excitation. Alternative approaches, such as the use of parallel transmission systems or multi-channel RF coils, offer partial relief but require specialized hardware, extensive calibration, and are not universally available on clinical platforms.

The need for a method that decouples excitation bandwidth from readout bandwidth, reduces SAR and RF peak power, minimizes the size of the missing k-space region, and enhances image fidelity in the presence of strong off-resonance and rapid T2* decay remains unmet. There exists a critical gap in the field of ultrashort echo time MRI for a technique that can simultaneously achieve high temporal resolution, low SAR, and robust artifact suppression without requiring modifications to standard clinical MRI hardware. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel modification to the PETRA sequence that employs gradient modulation to independently control the readout bandwidth while maintaining a low excitation bandwidth. This innovation enables the acquisition of high-fidelity images of tissues with extremely short T2* relaxation times, even at ultra-high field strengths, without compromising patient safety or increasing scan duration beyond clinically acceptable limits.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention discloses a novel magnetic resonance imaging technique termed Gradient-Modulated PETRA (GM-PETRA), which overcomes the longstanding limitations of conventional ultrashort echo time imaging by decoupling the excitation bandwidth from the readout bandwidth through the strategic modulation of gradient amplitudes during signal acquisition. This method enables the acquisition of high-resolution images of tissues with extremely short transverse relaxation times, such as cortical bone, tendons, ligaments, lung parenchyma, and the inner ear, while simultaneously reducing the specific absorption rate (SAR) and peak radiofrequency power to levels compatible with clinical safety standards, even at ultra-high magnetic field strengths of 7 Tesla or higher. Unlike prior approaches that require complex RF pulse design, specialized hardware, or computationally intensive reconstruction algorithms, GM-PETRA operates on standard clinical MRI scanners without modification, leveraging existing gradient systems to achieve superior image quality through a simple yet profound alteration in the timing and amplitude profile of the readout gradients.

In GM-PETRA, a hard radiofrequency excitation pulse is applied with a relatively low excitation bandwidth, thereby minimizing the peak power and SAR burden associated with the excitation process. Immediately following the excitation and the inherent transmitter-receiver switching delay, the magnetic field gradients are ramped up from their initial excitation amplitude to a significantly higher maximum readout amplitude over a controlled, finite duration. This ramping phase is followed by a brief period of constant gradient amplitude, during which the majority of the radial k-space data are acquired. The key innovation lies in the fact that the excitation gradient amplitude remains low, preserving the favorable SAR and power characteristics of conventional ZTE, while the readout gradient amplitude is substantially elevated, enabling rapid traversal of k-space and minimizing the temporal window during which signal decay occurs. This decoupling of excitation and readout bandwidths allows for the acquisition of high-bandwidth, high-resolution images without the prohibitive SAR penalties that would otherwise accompany such an approach.

The gradient modulation strategy further ensures that the region of k-space lost during the T/R switching delay remains small, as the initial gradient amplitude used for excitation is deliberately kept low. Consequently, the number of missing k-space points that must be recovered via single-point imaging (SPI) is minimized, preserving the efficiency of the SPI acquisition and preventing the excessive prolongation of scan time that plagues conventional PETRA when higher bandwidths are employed. The SPI component of GM-PETRA is retained to recover the central k-space data, but its scope is drastically reduced compared to traditional implementations, resulting in shorter acquisition times and improved temporal stability. The ramping gradient profile is carefully designed to avoid the generation of eddy currents and other gradient-related artifacts by operating within the slew rate limits of standard clinical gradient systems, ensuring compatibility with existing hardware and minimizing the need for additional calibration or correction.

The resulting image quality is markedly superior to that of conventional PETRA and ZTE sequences. Off-resonance artifacts, such as those arising from chemical shift between fat and water or from magnetic susceptibility differences at tissue-air interfaces, are significantly attenuated due to the increased readout bandwidth, which reduces spatial misregistration and smearing. The rapid sampling of k-space also preserves signal from tissues with extremely short T2* relaxation times, such as the mesocarp of an apple or the delicate structures of the cochlea and semicircular canals, which are otherwise invisible in conventional gradient echo sequences. The combination of low excitation bandwidth and high readout bandwidth enables the acquisition of images with enhanced edge sharpness, improved contrast, and reduced blurring, even under conditions of severe field inhomogeneity and rapid signal decay.

Moreover, the GM-PETRA technique offers a practical advantage in clinical settings by maintaining compatibility with standard MRI hardware. No modifications to the RF transmit system, receiver chain, or gradient electronics are required. The sequence can be implemented using existing pulse sequence programming interfaces and standard gradient amplifiers, making it readily deployable on clinical 3T and 7T scanners without the need for specialized instrumentation or proprietary software. The method is also compatible with parallel imaging techniques, compressed sensing, and iterative reconstruction algorithms, allowing for further acceleration and noise reduction without altering the core sequence design.

In contrast to prior methods that attempt to mitigate off-resonance and T2* decay through post-processing or advanced RF pulse design, GM-PETRA addresses the root cause of these artifacts at the acquisition level. By increasing the rate of k-space traversal without increasing the excitation bandwidth, it reduces the temporal window of signal decay and minimizes the spatial displacement of off-resonant spins, thereby preserving anatomical fidelity. The technique is particularly advantageous in applications requiring high-resolution imaging of complex, heterogeneous structures such as the inner ear, where susceptibility-induced field gradients are pronounced and signal decay is rapid. The ability to visualize fine anatomical details—such as the stria vascularis, spiral ligament, and small nerves of the cochlea—that are typically obscured in conventional MRI sequences represents a significant advancement in diagnostic capability.

The invention further enables the acquisition of high-quality images at ultra-high field strengths, where the benefits of increased signal-to-noise ratio are often offset by the exacerbation of off-resonance effects and SAR constraints. By mitigating these limitations, GM-PETRA unlocks the full potential of 7 Tesla and higher field systems for clinical applications involving ultrashort T2* tissues. The method also reduces the need for multiple acquisitions or repeated scans to compensate for image degradation, thereby improving workflow efficiency and patient comfort.

In summary, GM-PETRA represents a paradigm shift in ultrashort echo time imaging by introducing a simple, hardware-compatible, and clinically practical solution to the long-standing challenges of SAR limitation, T/R switching delay, off-resonance blurring, and rapid T2* decay. It enables the acquisition of high-resolution, high-contrast images of previously inaccessible tissues without compromising safety, scan time, or image fidelity. The invention is broadly applicable across multiple clinical domains, including neurology, otolaryngology, orthopedics, pulmonology, and musculoskeletal radiology, and provides a robust foundation for future advancements in high-field MRI and quantitative tissue characterization.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The Gradient-Modulated Pointwise-encoding time reduction with radial acquisition (GM-PETRA) technique is a novel magnetic resonance imaging sequence designed to overcome the fundamental limitations of conventional ultrashort echo time (UTE) and zero echo time (ZTE) imaging, particularly in the context of ultra-high field strength systems such as 7 Tesla. The core innovation of GM-PETRA lies in its ability to decouple the excitation bandwidth from the readout bandwidth through the controlled modulation of gradient amplitudes during the signal acquisition phase. This decoupling enables the use of a low excitation bandwidth to minimize radiofrequency (RF) power deposition and specific absorption rate (SAR), while simultaneously employing a high readout bandwidth to accelerate k-space traversal and reduce the temporal window during which signal decay occurs. The result is a sequence that achieves superior image quality for tissues with extremely short transverse relaxation times without requiring modifications to standard clinical MRI hardware.

The sequence begins with the application of a hard radiofrequency excitation pulse, which is characterized by a relatively low bandwidth. This low excitation bandwidth ensures that the duration of the RF pulse is sufficiently long to keep the peak power and SAR within clinically acceptable limits, even at ultra-high field strengths. The excitation pulse is applied while a constant gradient amplitude, denoted as G_ex, is active. This gradient amplitude is chosen to be low enough to satisfy SAR constraints but sufficient to establish the spatial encoding framework for the subsequent acquisition. Immediately following the excitation pulse, a transmitter-receiver (T/R) switching delay occurs, during which the MRI system transitions from transmit mode to receive mode. During this interval, the receiver gate remains closed, and no signal is acquired. The duration of this delay, typically on the order of tens of microseconds, results in the loss of k-space data corresponding to the central region of k-space, as the signal is sampled too early to be captured by the receiver.

In conventional PETRA, the missing central k-space region is recovered through a separate single-point imaging (SPI) acquisition, wherein the gradient amplitude is stepped down incrementally to sample individual k-space points at progressively lower amplitudes. While this approach successfully recovers the lost data, it becomes increasingly inefficient as the excitation bandwidth increases, because the size of the missing k-space region scales linearly with the product of the T/R delay and the excitation bandwidth. In GM-PETRA, the excitation bandwidth remains low, ensuring that the size of the missing k-space region is minimized, thereby reducing the number of SPI acquisitions required and preserving the efficiency of the overall sequence.

The critical innovation of GM-PETRA occurs after the T/R switching delay, when the gradient amplitude is no longer held constant but instead begins to ramp up linearly from the excitation amplitude G_ex to a significantly higher maximum amplitude, G_max. This ramping phase is carefully designed to occur over a finite duration, during which the gradient amplitude increases at a controlled slew rate. The slew rate is selected to remain within the operational limits of standard clinical gradient systems, typically not exceeding 50% of the maximum available slew rate, to prevent the generation of eddy currents, acoustic noise, and other gradient-related artifacts that could degrade image quality. The ramping gradient ensures that the spatial encoding field increases gradually, allowing the k-space trajectory to expand outward from the center at an accelerating rate. Following the ramping phase, a short period of constant gradient amplitude, G_max, is maintained to allow for the bulk of the radial k-space data to be acquired. This constant-amplitude phase corresponds to the high-readout-bandwidth region of the sequence and is responsible for the rapid sampling of peripheral k-space, where high spatial frequencies are encoded.

The timing and amplitude profile of the gradient modulation are optimized to ensure that the transition from the low-bandwidth excitation regime to the high-bandwidth readout regime is seamless and does not introduce phase inconsistencies or signal distortions. The ramping gradient is synchronized with the receiver gate to ensure that signal acquisition begins immediately after the ramping phase is initiated, thereby capturing the full extent of the k-space trajectory without gaps. The duration of the ramping phase is determined by the difference between G_max and G_ex, as well as the selected slew rate, and is typically on the order of tens to hundreds of microseconds, depending on the desired readout bandwidth and the capabilities of the gradient system. The constant-amplitude phase that follows is sufficiently long to acquire the majority of the radial projections required for high-resolution image reconstruction, typically spanning tens of thousands of samples.

The SPI component of GM-PETRA is retained to recover the central k-space region lost during the T/R switching delay. However, because the excitation gradient amplitude G_ex is low, the extent of the missing k-space region is limited to a small radius around the origin, corresponding to |k| < γ·G_ex·t_k, where γ is the gyromagnetic ratio and t_k is the sum of the RF pulse duration and the T/R switching delay. This small region can be efficiently sampled using a minimal number of SPI acquisitions, typically fewer than 200, regardless of the high readout bandwidth employed during the constant-amplitude phase. This is in stark contrast to conventional PETRA, where increasing the excitation bandwidth to improve temporal resolution results in a proportional increase in the number of SPI acquisitions, often exceeding one thousand, which dramatically elongates scan time and introduces motion sensitivity.

The radial k-space sampling pattern in GM-PETRA is therefore a hybrid of three distinct phases: (1) a low-bandwidth excitation phase with constant gradient G_ex, (2) a gradient modulation phase with linearly increasing gradient amplitude from G_ex to G_max, and (3) a high-bandwidth readout phase with constant gradient G_max. The SPI acquisition is performed as a separate, interleaved set of radial projections, typically acquired at the beginning or end of the scan, and is not interwoven with the main radial acquisition to avoid temporal overlap and phase inconsistencies. The entire sequence is repeated for a sufficient number of radial projections—typically on the order of 65,536—to ensure adequate k-space coverage and high-resolution image reconstruction.

The signal acquisition in GM-PETRA is governed by the time-dependent gradient waveform, which determines the trajectory of k-space sampling. The k-space coordinate at any given time t after the center of the excitation pulse is defined by the integral of the gradient vector over time, k(t) = γ ∫_0^t G(τ) dτ. During the excitation phase, G(τ) is constant at G_ex, resulting in a linear increase in k-space position. During the ramping phase, G(τ) increases linearly with time, leading to a quadratic dependence of k-space position on time, which accelerates the traversal of k-space. During the constant-amplitude phase, k-space position resumes linear growth, but at a much steeper rate due to the elevated gradient amplitude. This non-uniform sampling density is compensated during image reconstruction through density correction techniques, which account for the varying number of samples per unit k-space volume. The density correction is implemented using iterative algorithms that estimate the local sampling density based on the known gradient waveform and apply a weighting factor to each k-space point to ensure uniform weighting in the reconstructed image.

The image reconstruction process in GM-PETRA is performed offline using a custom-built software framework implemented in C++ and CUDA to leverage the parallel processing capabilities of graphics processing units (GPUs). The raw k-space data, which include both the main radial acquisitions and the SPI acquisitions, are first corrected for sampling density using a density compensation algorithm that accounts for the non-uniform distribution of k-space samples introduced by the gradient modulation. This is followed by a non-uniform fast Fourier transform (NUFFT), which reconstructs the complex k-space data into a three-dimensional image volume without requiring interpolation or resampling onto a regular grid. The NUFFT algorithm is particularly well-suited for radial sampling patterns and preserves the integrity of high-frequency components, which are critical for resolving fine anatomical details in tissues with short T2* relaxation times.

To further enhance image quality, the reconstructed images may be subjected to additional post-processing techniques, such as iterative denoising, compressed sensing, or total variation regularization. These methods exploit the inherent sparsity of anatomical structures in appropriate transform domains—such as wavelet or total variation—to suppress noise and recover fine details without introducing artifacts. The use of multi-channel receiver coils, which are standard on modern clinical MRI systems, further enhances the signal-to-noise ratio through parallel imaging techniques, allowing for additional acceleration of data acquisition without compromising resolution. The combination of GM-PETRA’s inherent high temporal resolution and advanced reconstruction algorithms results in images with exceptional sharpness, contrast, and fidelity, even in regions of severe magnetic field inhomogeneity.

The performance of GM-PETRA is evaluated through both numerical simulations and experimental validation. In simulations, a 3D Shepp-Logan phantom is employed to model tissues with varying degrees of off-resonance and T2* decay. The phantom includes compartments with different resonance frequencies, such as those corresponding to fat and water, as well as regions with extremely short T2* values, such as 200 microseconds and 500 microseconds. The simulated k-space signal is computed by integrating the magnetization over time, accounting for the time-varying gradient waveform and the exponential decay of the transverse magnetization. The results demonstrate that increasing the readout bandwidth through gradient modulation significantly reduces off-resonance blurring and preserves signal intensity in short T2* compartments. The image quality achieved with GM-PETRA using a 60 kHz excitation bandwidth and a 200 kHz readout bandwidth is comparable to or superior to that of conventional PETRA using a 120 kHz excitation bandwidth, while simultaneously reducing SAR and RF peak power by more than 50%.

Experimental validation is performed on a 7 Tesla MRI scanner equipped with a multi-channel transmit/receive head coil. A breast phantom containing fat and water compartments is imaged to assess the suppression of chemical shift artifacts. The results show a marked reduction in duplicated edges and spatial misregistration around the fat-water interfaces as the readout bandwidth increases. Similarly, imaging of an apple—a natural tissue with extremely short T2* due to intercellular air spaces—reveals fine structural details in the mesocarp that are completely invisible in conventional gradient echo sequences. The GM-PETRA images clearly resolve vascular bundles, fibrous networks, and cellular boundaries that are blurred or absent in both conventional PETRA and standard 3D GRE acquisitions. In vivo imaging of the human inner ear further demonstrates the clinical utility of GM-PETRA, with the cochlea, semicircular canals, and small nerves being visualized with unprecedented clarity. The strong susceptibility gradients at the air-tissue interfaces, which severely degrade conventional sequences, are effectively mitigated by the high readout bandwidth of GM-PETRA, allowing for the visualization of structures that were previously inaccessible.

The sequence parameters of GM-PETRA are carefully optimized to balance image quality, scan time, and safety. The excitation bandwidth is maintained at 60 kHz, corresponding to an RF pulse duration of approximately 20 microseconds, which keeps SAR within regulatory limits even at 7 Tesla. The maximum readout bandwidth is set to 200 kHz, achieved through a gradient ramping time of approximately 170 microseconds at a slew rate of 100 mT/m/ms. The SPI acquisition consists of 123 individual points, sufficient to recover the central k-space region without prolonging the scan time. The flip angle is set to 4° to further reduce SAR, and the repetition time is maintained at 5 milliseconds to ensure adequate T1 recovery. The total scan time for a 3D volume is approximately 5 minutes and 30 seconds, which is clinically feasible for dedicated imaging protocols.

The gradient modulation strategy of GM-PETRA introduces no new hardware requirements and is fully compatible with existing clinical MRI systems. The sequence can be implemented using standard pulse sequence programming interfaces, such as those provided by Siemens, GE, or Philips, without modification to the RF transmit chain, receiver electronics, or gradient amplifiers. The only requirement is the ability to program a time-varying gradient waveform with a linear ramp, a capability that is standard on all modern clinical scanners. The method does not rely on frequency-modulated pulses, phase correction algorithms, or iterative reconstruction methods to achieve its benefits, making it robust, reproducible, and easily transferable across institutions.

The advantages of GM-PETRA extend beyond image quality and safety. By reducing the number of SPI acquisitions required, the sequence is less sensitive to motion artifacts and physiological fluctuations, which is particularly important in clinical settings where patient cooperation may be limited. The reduced SAR burden also enables the use of higher flip angles in future iterations, potentially enhancing T1-weighted contrast without compromising safety. The technique is also compatible with advanced reconstruction methods such as deep learning-based denoising and super-resolution, which can further enhance image fidelity and reduce scan time in future implementations.

In summary, GM-PETRA represents a transformative advancement in ultrashort echo time magnetic resonance imaging. By decoupling excitation and readout bandwidths through gradient modulation, it overcomes the fundamental trade-offs that have long constrained the clinical utility of ZTE and PETRA sequences. The method enables high-resolution imaging of tissues with extremely short T2* relaxation times, suppresses off-resonance artifacts, reduces SAR and RF peak power, and maintains compatibility with standard clinical hardware. Its simplicity, robustness, and efficacy make it a powerful tool for diagnostic imaging in neurology, otolaryngology, orthopedics, and pulmonology, opening new avenues for the visualization of previously inaccessible anatomical structures and the quantitative characterization of tissue microstructure.
