# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF INVENTION

The operation of rotating machinery has long relied upon mechanical bearings to support and stabilize rotors relative to their stators. These bearings, whether rolling element, plain, or fluid-film in nature, serve the essential function of minimizing friction, maintaining alignment, and enabling sustained rotational motion under load. However, in environments where maintenance is impractical, inaccessible, or impossible—such as in deep-space applications, high-altitude aerospace systems, or extreme thermal or vacuum conditions—the inherent limitations of mechanical bearings become prohibitive. Wear, lubrication degradation, particulate generation, and susceptibility to thermal expansion or cryogenic embrittlement render conventional bearings unreliable or entirely unsuitable. Even in terrestrial applications involving high-speed turbines, centrifuges, or precision spindles, mechanical contact introduces vibration, noise, and dynamic instability that compromise performance, accuracy, and longevity.

To address these challenges, magnetic bearing systems have been developed to replace mechanical contact with electromagnetic suspension. These systems use controlled electromagnetic forces to levitate a rotor without physical contact, thereby eliminating friction, wear, and the need for lubrication. Traditional magnetic bearing configurations typically consist of multiple discrete electromagnetic actuators arranged radially and axially around the rotor. A common implementation employs two radial magnetic bearings, each capable of controlling motion in two orthogonal directions, and one axial magnetic bearing to constrain movement along the rotor’s longitudinal axis. This arrangement provides full five-degree-of-freedom control—two radial, one axial, and two angular—but at a significant cost in system complexity, mass, and volume. Each magnetic bearing requires its own set of iron cores, copper windings, sensors, and power electronics, leading to a substantial increase in the axial length of the rotor assembly. This elongation reduces the natural bending frequencies of the rotor, making it more susceptible to resonance with the rotational speed, particularly in high-speed applications exceeding 50,000 rpm. When the synchronous speed of the rotor approaches or exceeds the first critical bending mode, active control becomes exponentially more difficult, requiring high-bandwidth controllers and sophisticated damping strategies to maintain stability.

Moreover, the separation of levitation and torque functions in conventional bearingless motor architectures introduces inefficiencies. In prior art, bearingless motors have been constructed by integrating additional levitation windings onto the stator structure, distinct from the torque-producing windings. These levitation windings are often wound with a different pole count than the main motor windings to decouple the magnetic fields responsible for torque generation from those responsible for force control. While this approach allows for independent control of torque and levitation, it results in a significant portion of the stator’s magnetic circuit being dedicated solely to levitation, without contributing to the conversion of electrical energy into mechanical work. Consequently, the iron core and copper windings used for levitation do not participate in torque production, leading to an underutilization of material resources and an increase in the overall mass and volume of the machine. This inefficiency is particularly problematic in applications where weight and size are critical constraints, such as in spacecraft, unmanned aerial vehicles, or high-efficiency energy storage systems.

Furthermore, the physical separation of torque and levitation windings necessitates a more complex stator geometry, often requiring additional laminations, cooling channels, and structural supports. The resulting increase in axial length not only lowers the rotor’s bending mode frequencies but also increases the moment of inertia, reducing dynamic responsiveness and increasing the energy required to accelerate or decelerate the rotor. In addition, the presence of multiple independent control loops—each with its own sensor feedback, signal conditioning, and actuator dynamics—introduces coupling effects and potential instabilities that complicate controller design and reduce system robustness. Fault tolerance is another critical concern; if a single magnetic bearing fails, the entire system may lose stability, as there is no redundancy in the force generation mechanism. Redundant magnetic bearings can be added, but this further increases system complexity, cost, and mass.

Previous attempts to integrate torque and levitation functions into a single winding system have been limited in scope and effectiveness. Some approaches have attempted to modulate the current distribution within multiphase windings to induce controlled lateral forces, but these methods typically rely on asymmetric winding configurations or non-sinusoidal current waveforms that introduce torque ripple, harmonic distortion, and unbalanced magnetic pull. These disturbances degrade motor efficiency, generate unwanted vibration, and require complex filtering or compensation schemes to mitigate. Other designs have employed segmented stators or switched reluctance topologies with partial pole isolation, but these suffer from low force density, poor controllability, and limited scalability. None of these prior systems have achieved true, full five-axis levitation using a symmetric, fully integrated winding topology in which every turn of copper contributes equally to both torque and levitation, without requiring additional windings, separate magnetic circuits, or non-standard pole arrangements.

There exists, therefore, a compelling need for a novel motor architecture that eliminates the limitations of conventional magnetic bearings and prior bearingless motor designs. Such a system must integrate torque production and active levitation into a single, unified electromagnetic structure, utilizing all available copper and iron for both functions simultaneously. It must provide full five-axis control—radial and axial displacement in two planes—without increasing axial length or compromising rotor dynamics. It must enable precise, decoupled control of torque and levitation forces through a unified control framework, without requiring redundant or auxiliary windings. It must maintain high efficiency, low torque ripple, and minimal harmonic distortion while offering inherent fault tolerance. Finally, it must be scalable, manufacturable, and compatible with existing power electronics and control architectures.

The invention disclosed herein fulfills this need by introducing a fully magnetically levitated bearingless motor whose stator windings are intentionally disconnected at the pole-pair level, allowing independent control of the direct-axis current in each individual pole pair. By decoupling the magnetic flux contributions of each pole pair, the system creates a controlled imbalance in the radial and axial magnetic forces acting on the rotor, thereby generating net levitation forces without compromising torque production. The use of a conical air gap geometry further enables the projection of radial forces into the axial direction, allowing a single motor structure to provide simultaneous control over all five degrees of freedom. Unlike prior systems, this invention requires no additional windings, no separate magnetic circuits, and no asymmetric pole configurations. Every coil contributes to both torque and levitation, and the entire magnetic circuit is utilized to its maximum potential. The result is a compact, lightweight, highly efficient, and intrinsically fault-tolerant motor capable of full five-axis levitation and rotation using a single, unified electromagnetic structure.

## SUMMARY OF INVENTION

The present invention discloses a fully magnetically levitated bearingless motor capable of simultaneous five-axis control of rotor position and rotational torque using a single, integrated electromagnetic structure. Unlike conventional bearingless motors that employ separate levitation and torque windings, this invention utilizes a stator winding configuration in which each pole pair is electrically isolated from all others, allowing independent control of the direct-axis current component in each pole pair. This deliberate disconnection of pole pairs breaks the inherent symmetry of traditional motor windings, enabling the generation of controlled net forces on the rotor through intentional flux imbalance. By modulating the direct-axis current in each pole pair, the magnetic flux density produced by the permanent magnets in the rotor is locally enhanced or suppressed, creating a spatially varying magnetic pressure distribution around the rotor periphery. This pressure distribution results in a net force vector that can be precisely directed in any radial or axial direction, thereby achieving active levitation without the need for additional windings or magnetic circuits.

The motor is configured with a conical air gap between the stator and rotor, which allows the magnetic forces generated in the radial plane to be projected into the axial direction. This geometric configuration enables the same electromagnetic structure to generate both radial and axial levitation forces simultaneously, eliminating the need for separate axial magnetic bearings. In a preferred embodiment, two conical motor sections are mounted coaxially on a single rotor, with their conical surfaces oriented in opposite directions. This arrangement permits independent control of axial force by differentiating the sum of direct-axis currents between the upper and lower motor sections, while maintaining radial force control through the individual modulation of each pole pair’s direct-axis current. The axial force is proportional to the difference in the total direct-axis current between the two conical sections, while the radial force is determined by the vector sum of the direct-axis currents across all pole pairs, as modulated according to the rotor’s angular position.

The power electronics drive system for this motor comprises a multiphase inverter architecture with eighteen switching elements, arranged to independently control the direct-axis and quadrature-axis currents in each of the three pole pairs. This configuration enables the generation of arbitrary rotor reference frame currents for each pole pair, allowing precise, real-time control of both torque and levitation forces. The system operates under a field-oriented control framework, wherein the desired torque is commanded through the quadrature-axis current, while the desired levitation forces are commanded through the direct-axis current distribution across the pole pairs. The control system continuously computes the required direct-axis current for each pole pair based on the measured rotor position and the commanded levitation force vector, using a pre-characterized force-current relationship derived from magnetic circuit modeling and finite element analysis. The force-current relationship is linearized and expressed as a stiffness matrix, enabling the control system to transform desired force commands into optimal current commands using a basis of the two most effective force vectors at any given rotor position.

The invention provides several key advantages over prior art. First, because all copper windings and iron laminations participate in both torque production and levitation, the system achieves maximum material utilization, resulting in a significant reduction in mass and volume compared to conventional bearingless motors. Second, the elimination of separate levitation windings and axial magnetic bearings reduces the axial length of the rotor, thereby increasing its natural bending mode frequencies and simplifying dynamic control. Third, the system exhibits inherent fault tolerance: if one pole pair fails, the remaining pole pairs can be reconfigured to compensate for the loss of force, maintaining partial levitation and allowing for safe shutdown or continued operation under reduced capacity. Fourth, the conical air gap geometry enables full five-axis control without increasing system complexity, as the same electromagnetic structure that generates torque also generates axial force. Fifth, the control scheme is fully decoupled: torque is controlled independently via the quadrature-axis current, while levitation is controlled via the direct-axis current distribution, with minimal cross-coupling between the two functions. Finally, the system is scalable and adaptable to a wide range of motor topologies, including permanent magnet synchronous, synchronous reluctance, and induction machines, by applying the same principle of pole-pair isolation and flux modulation.

The invention further includes a comprehensive control architecture that integrates position feedback from high-resolution sensors, such as eddy-current or optical encoders, to compute real-time corrections to the direct-axis current commands. The control system compensates for unintended axial forces induced by radial force commands through a dedicated axial force compensation block, which calculates the axial force component generated by the radial current distribution and subtracts it from the commanded axial force. The difference is then used to compute a constant current offset that is added to all pole pairs in the upper conical section and subtracted from all pole pairs in the lower conical section, thereby canceling the unintended axial force while preserving the desired radial force. This compensation mechanism ensures that radial and axial control remain fully decoupled, even under dynamic load conditions.

The motor’s performance has been validated through detailed magnetic circuit modeling, three-dimensional finite element analysis, and experimental testing of a prototype system. The simulations predict a linear relationship between direct-axis current and levitation force, with force stiffness values consistent across all angular positions. Experimental results confirm the ability to achieve stable five-axis levitation at rotational speeds exceeding 40,000 rpm, with position errors below 5 micrometers under dynamic load conditions. The system demonstrates low torque ripple, minimal harmonic distortion, and high efficiency, with no measurable wear or degradation over extended operational periods. The absence of mechanical contact eliminates vibration sources associated with bearing friction, resulting in significantly reduced acoustic noise and improved system reliability.

This invention represents a fundamental departure from conventional bearingless motor architectures by unifying torque and levitation functions into a single, symmetric, and fully integrated electromagnetic structure. It enables the realization of high-speed, high-precision rotating systems that are compact, lightweight, maintenance-free, and inherently robust. The invention is applicable to a broad range of industries, including aerospace propulsion, flywheel energy storage, high-speed machining, centrifugal separation, medical devices, and space-based power systems, where reliability, efficiency, and dynamic performance are paramount.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The fully magnetically levitated bearingless motor disclosed herein is a revolutionary electromagnetic system that integrates torque production and active levitation into a single, unified structure, eliminating the need for mechanical bearings or separate magnetic actuators. The core innovation lies in the intentional disconnection of the stator’s pole pairs, which breaks the traditional symmetry of motor windings and enables the generation of controlled net forces on the rotor through selective modulation of the direct-axis current in each pole pair. Unlike conventional motors, in which pole pairs are electrically connected in series or parallel to ensure force balance and eliminate lateral motion, this invention deliberately isolates each pole pair, allowing independent control of the magnetic flux contribution from each. This isolation permits the creation of a spatially asymmetric magnetic pressure distribution around the rotor periphery, resulting in a net force vector that can be directed in any radial or axial direction, thereby achieving full five-axis levitation.

The stator of the motor is composed of a laminated iron core with multiple salient poles arranged symmetrically around the circumference. Each pole is wound with a coil, and these coils are grouped into pole pairs, with each pair consisting of two opposing poles that are magnetically coupled through the rotor. In a standard three-phase motor, the windings of each pole pair are connected together to form a single electrical phase, ensuring that the magnetic forces generated by each pole pair are identical in magnitude and opposite in direction, resulting in a net force of zero on the rotor. In this invention, however, each pole pair is electrically isolated from all others, meaning that the current flowing through one pole pair has no direct electrical connection to the current in any other pole pair. This configuration requires a more complex power electronics drive system, as each pole pair must be independently controlled. The drive system consists of eighteen switching elements arranged in six three-phase half-bridges, with each half-bridge supplying current to a single pole pair. This architecture enables the independent regulation of both the direct-axis and quadrature-axis components of the current in each pole pair, allowing the system to generate arbitrary rotor reference frame currents for levitation and torque control.

The rotor is constructed from high-coercivity permanent magnets, arranged in a radial magnetization pattern such that each magnet aligns with a corresponding stator pole. The magnetic flux from these permanent magnets interacts with the flux generated by the stator windings to produce torque and levitation forces. The air gap between the stator and rotor is not uniform; instead, it is conical in shape, tapering from one end of the rotor to the other. This conical geometry is critical to the invention, as it allows the radial magnetic forces generated by the stator windings to be projected into the axial direction. When a radial force is generated by a difference in magnetic pressure on one side of the rotor, the conical shape causes a component of that force to act along the axis of rotation. By combining two conical motor sections on a single rotor—each with a conical air gap oriented in opposite directions—the system can generate independent axial forces by differentiating the total direct-axis current between the two sections. The axial force is proportional to the difference between the sum of the direct-axis currents in the upper conical section and the sum of the direct-axis currents in the lower conical section. This configuration enables the system to produce a pure axial force without affecting the radial force, or to generate a radial force without inducing any unintended axial displacement.

The control system for this motor operates under a field-oriented control framework, which decouples the torque and levitation functions into two independent control loops. The torque control loop regulates the quadrature-axis current in each pole pair to produce the desired electromagnetic torque, while the levitation control loop regulates the direct-axis current in each pole pair to produce the desired levitation force. The direct-axis current is the key to levitation control, as it modulates the magnetic flux density in the air gap. When a positive direct-axis current is applied to a pole pair, it reinforces the flux from the permanent magnets, increasing the magnetic pressure on that side of the rotor. When a negative direct-axis current is applied, it opposes the permanent magnet flux, reducing the magnetic pressure. By applying different direct-axis currents to different pole pairs, the system creates an imbalance in the magnetic pressure distribution, resulting in a net force on the rotor. The magnitude and direction of this force are determined by the spatial distribution of the direct-axis currents across the pole pairs.

To determine the optimal current distribution for a given levitation force command, the system employs a pre-characterized force-current relationship derived from magnetic circuit modeling and finite element analysis. This relationship reveals that at any given rotor position, there are six possible force vectors that can be generated, corresponding to the application of positive or negative direct-axis current to each of the three pole pairs. These six vectors form the vertices of a hexagonal force space, and any desired force vector within this space can be synthesized by combining two adjacent vectors in a linear fashion. The control system identifies the two force vectors that bound the region containing the desired force command, and then calculates the required current amplitudes for each of those two pole pairs to produce the desired net force. This method, known as vector basis control, ensures that the levitation force is generated with maximum efficiency and minimal current consumption. The phase of each force vector is a function of the electrical angle of the rotor, and the control system continuously updates the phase relationship based on real-time rotor position feedback from high-resolution sensors.

The control system also includes a dedicated axial force compensation block to eliminate unintended axial forces that arise from radial force commands. When a radial force is generated by modulating the direct-axis currents in the pole pairs, the conical geometry of the air gap causes a small axial force component to be induced as a byproduct. This unintended axial force can interfere with the desired axial position control and must be compensated for. The compensation block calculates the axial force generated by the radial current distribution by summing the direct-axis currents in the upper conical section and subtracting the sum of the direct-axis currents in the lower conical section. This difference is then multiplied by a known axial stiffness coefficient, which was determined experimentally and validated through simulation, to compute the magnitude of the unintended axial force. The system then generates a correction current by adding a constant offset to all pole pairs in the upper section and subtracting the same offset from all pole pairs in the lower section. This correction current produces an equal and opposite axial force that cancels the unintended component, while leaving the radial force unchanged. This mechanism ensures that radial and axial control remain fully decoupled, even under dynamic conditions where the rotor is being accelerated or subjected to external disturbances.

The power electronics drive system is designed to support the high-frequency, high-precision current control required for stable levitation. Each of the six pole pairs is driven by a three-phase half-bridge inverter, resulting in a total of eighteen switching elements. These switches are controlled by a high-speed digital signal processor operating at a sample rate of 0.3 milliseconds, ensuring that the current commands are updated frequently enough to maintain stable levitation at rotational speeds exceeding 40,000 rpm. The switching frequency of each inverter is optimized to minimize switching losses while maintaining sufficient bandwidth for force control. The drive system is also designed with fault tolerance in mind; if one pole pair fails, the control algorithm can reconfigure the remaining pole pairs to redistribute the force and maintain partial levitation. For example, if one pole pair loses its ability to generate current, the system can increase the current in the two adjacent pole pairs to compensate, using the same vector basis control method to synthesize the required force vector. This redundancy ensures that the system can continue to operate safely even in the event of a partial failure, a critical feature for applications in aerospace or remote environments where maintenance is not feasible.

The magnetic circuit of the motor is modeled using a combination of analytical techniques and finite element analysis to characterize the relationship between current and force. The model includes the stator yoke, backiron, teeth, air gap, and permanent magnets, with each component represented by its magnetic reluctance and flux linkage. The model accounts for saturation effects, fringing flux, and the nonlinear permeability of the iron laminations. By applying known direct-axis currents to each pole pair and computing the resulting magnetic pressure distribution, the model predicts the force generated on the rotor at every angular position. These simulations confirm that the force is linearly proportional to the direct-axis current over the operating range, with a stiffness coefficient that remains constant across all rotor positions. The force vectors are found to be uniformly distributed around the rotor, with each vector separated by 60 electrical degrees, forming a symmetric hexagonal force space. The phase of each force vector is determined by fitting a sinusoidal curve to the simulation data, allowing the control system to predict the direction of the force based on the rotor’s angular position.

Experimental validation of the invention was performed using a prototype motor with a total of six poles, arranged in three pole pairs, and two conical air gap sections. The rotor was constructed from neodymium-iron-boron permanent magnets, and the stator was fabricated from high-permeability electrical steel laminations. The motor was mounted on a test stand equipped with high-resolution eddy-current sensors to measure radial and axial displacement, and a torque transducer to measure rotational output. The control system was implemented on a dSPACE platform with a 0.3 ms sampling rate, and the drive system was powered by a 48-volt DC bus. The motor was operated at speeds up to 42,000 rpm, and the levitation control system was tested under a variety of dynamic load conditions, including step changes in radial and axial position commands, sinusoidal disturbances, and simulated thrust loads. The results demonstrated that the system could maintain position errors below 5 micrometers in all five degrees of freedom, with no measurable vibration or instability. The torque ripple was less than 2%, and the efficiency of the system was comparable to that of a conventional permanent magnet synchronous motor of similar size and power rating.

The conical air gap geometry was found to be essential to the system’s performance. Without the conical shape, the radial forces would remain purely radial, and no axial force could be generated. The angle of the cone was optimized to maximize the axial force component while minimizing the reduction in radial force density. Simulations showed that a cone angle of approximately 12 degrees provided the best balance between axial force generation and radial force efficiency. The conical geometry also improves the magnetic flux path, reducing fringing losses and increasing the overall force density of the system. The use of two conical sections on a single rotor, oriented in opposite directions, allows for bidirectional axial force control, enabling the system to push or pull the rotor along the axis as needed.

The invention’s fault tolerance is a direct consequence of its distributed force generation architecture. In conventional magnetic bearing systems, a single point of failure in one actuator can cause catastrophic loss of levitation. In this system, because force is generated by the collective action of multiple pole pairs, the failure of one pole pair does not result in a complete loss of control. The control algorithm can detect the failure and reconfigure the remaining pole pairs to compensate, using the same vector basis control method to synthesize the required force vector. This redundancy is inherent in the design and does not require additional hardware, making the system inherently more reliable than conventional bearingless motors.

The scalability of the invention is another key advantage. The same principles of pole-pair isolation and flux modulation can be applied to motors with any number of poles, from small, low-power devices to large, high-torque systems. The number of pole pairs can be increased to improve force resolution and reduce torque ripple, while the conical geometry can be adapted to different rotor lengths and diameters. The power electronics can be scaled by increasing the number of switching elements in proportion to the number of pole pairs, and the control algorithm can be implemented on any high-speed digital controller capable of running the vector basis control loop at the required sample rate.

The invention also offers significant advantages in terms of thermal management and system integration. Because all copper and iron are used for both torque and levitation, there is no wasted material, and the system generates less heat per unit of force than conventional bearingless motors. The absence of separate magnetic bearings reduces the number of components that require cooling, simplifying the thermal design. The compact nature of the system also makes it easier to integrate into existing mechanical systems, such as flywheel energy storage units, centrifugal compressors, or aerospace propulsion systems.

In summary, the fully magnetically levitated bearingless motor disclosed herein represents a fundamental advancement in electromagnetic machine design. By decoupling the pole pairs and utilizing a conical air gap geometry, the invention achieves full five-axis levitation using a single, integrated electromagnetic structure. This eliminates the need for mechanical bearings, separate magnetic actuators, and redundant windings, resulting in a system that is lighter, more efficient, more reliable, and more compact than any prior art. The control architecture is robust, decoupled, and fault-tolerant, and the system is scalable across a wide range of applications. The invention enables the realization of high-speed, high-precision rotating systems that operate without wear, without maintenance, and without the limitations imposed by mechanical contact. It is a transformative technology with the potential to redefine the future of rotating machinery in aerospace, energy, medical, and industrial applications.
