# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to systems and methods for the privacy-preserving release of sparse binary datasets while maintaining high utility for downstream analytical tasks. Specifically, the invention provides a novel algorithmic framework for anonymizing bipartite graphs or, equivalently, binary matrices representing user-feature relationships, through a refined notion of anonymity called smooth-k-anonymity. This approach enables the publication of user data in a form that guarantees each individual is indistinguishable from at least k other users, while simultaneously preserving a substantial portion of the original data structure through controlled edge additions and deletions. The invention is particularly suited for applications involving large-scale user interaction data such as online advertising interest profiles, social network affiliations, location visitation patterns, e-commerce purchase histories, and co-authorship networks, where traditional methods of differential privacy fail to preserve meaningful utility due to excessive noise injection, and conventional k-anonymity by suppression leads to excessive data loss. The disclosed method operates in polynomial time and delivers provable approximation guarantees on the preservation of data utility as measured by Jaccard similarity, making it suitable for deployment in real-world data sharing environments where both privacy and analytical fidelity are paramount.

## BACKGROUND

The increasing volume and sensitivity of user-generated data have created an urgent need for robust privacy-preserving techniques that enable data sharing without compromising individual confidentiality. In many domains—including digital advertising, public health research, social science analysis, and recommendation systems—organizations seek to release aggregated user data to third parties for research, modeling, or commercial purposes. However, naive data release often exposes individuals to re-identification risks, even when direct identifiers are removed. This vulnerability arises because combinations of seemingly innocuous attributes—such as browsing behavior, location visits, or product preferences—can uniquely identify individuals when paired with auxiliary information. Consequently, formal privacy frameworks have been developed to provide mathematical guarantees against such threats.

Two dominant paradigms have emerged in the literature: differential privacy and k-anonymity. Differential privacy ensures that the output of a data release mechanism is statistically insensitive to the presence or absence of any single individual’s data. This is typically achieved by injecting calibrated random noise into query responses or data records. While differential privacy offers strong theoretical guarantees and is resistant to arbitrary post-processing by adversaries with side information, its application to the release of full datasets—particularly sparse binary matrices—has proven severely limited in practice. In such settings, preserving even modest utility requires noise levels so high that the resulting data becomes statistically indistinguishable from random noise, rendering it useless for meaningful analysis. This is especially true in domains where user-feature interactions are inherently sparse, such as web graphs, user-item purchase matrices, or interest-based social networks, where the average number of features per user is orders of magnitude smaller than the total number of possible features.

On the other hand, k-anonymity is a non-stochastic approach that modifies the dataset to ensure that each record is identical to at least k−1 other records with respect to a designated set of quasi-identifiers. In the context of binary matrices, this translates to grouping users into clusters of size at least k such that all users within a cluster share the exact same pattern of feature presence or absence. The most common implementation of k-anonymity, known as suppression, achieves this by removing (suppressing) features from users until their profiles become indistinguishable from those of at least k others. While this method avoids the noise-induced utility degradation of differential privacy, it suffers from a critical flaw: it can only preserve data utility when the original dataset is nearly already k-anonymous. In practice, for most real-world sparse datasets, achieving k-anonymity via suppression requires the removal of a large fraction of edges—often the majority—thereby destroying the underlying signal that makes the data valuable. Existing approximation algorithms for k-anonymity by suppression offer theoretical guarantees only when the optimal solution preserves a fraction of the data approaching one minus a term that diminishes with k. When the optimal k-anonymous dataset requires removing even a modest constant fraction of edges—as is typical in sparse graphs—these guarantees become vacuous, and the best possible approximation may be to return an empty dataset.

Moreover, suppression-based k-anonymity is inherently one-sided: it only permits deletion of edges, never addition. This restriction prevents the algorithm from exploiting structural redundancies in the data to create more natural, less distorted anonymized profiles. For example, if a group of users all share a common interest in a niche product, suppression may remove that interest from all of them to match a more common profile, whereas an ideal anonymization might preserve the interest and extend it to other users in the group who also exhibit similar behavioral patterns. This limitation leads to a fundamental trade-off: either the data is heavily suppressed and loses analytical value, or it retains too much individual specificity and fails to protect privacy.

Recent attempts to combine differential privacy with k-anonymity have yielded limited success, often requiring sub-sampling or additional assumptions that further degrade utility. Other approaches, such as l-diversity and t-closeness, attempt to enrich k-anonymity by enforcing diversity within equivalence classes, but they do not address the core issue of excessive suppression in sparse domains. Graph-based anonymity notions such as k-isomorphism have been proposed for social networks, but they are restricted to structural properties of connectivity and do not generalize to arbitrary binary feature matrices.

The present invention overcomes these limitations by introducing a new privacy model—smooth-k-anonymity—that permits both edge deletion and controlled edge addition under a majority-consistency constraint. Unlike traditional k-anonymity, which requires exact replication of feature vectors, smooth-k-anonymity allows clusters of users to be assigned a common feature profile that reflects the majority preference within the cluster. This means that if more than half of the users in a cluster have a particular feature, then all users in the cluster are assigned that feature; otherwise, the feature is removed from all. This simple yet powerful rule enables the algorithm to preserve meaningful patterns while ensuring anonymity, and crucially, it allows for the creation of new edges that reflect statistically coherent group behavior rather than arbitrary suppression. The invention further provides the first polynomial-time algorithm with constant-factor approximation guarantees for this problem, reducing it to a variant of the lower-bounded facility location problem and leveraging state-of-the-art clustering techniques to achieve practical performance even on datasets with billions of rows and hundreds of thousands of columns. The resulting system enables the release of anonymized binary data that simultaneously satisfies strong privacy guarantees, preserves high utility for downstream machine learning and statistical analysis, and scales efficiently to real-world data sizes.

## SUMMARY

The invention is a method and system for anonymizing sparse binary matrices representing user-feature relationships while preserving data utility and ensuring formal privacy guarantees. The method operates by transforming an input bipartite graph, where one set of nodes represents users and the other represents features, into an output graph that satisfies smooth-k-anonymity. Smooth-k-anonymity requires that users be partitioned into clusters of size at least k, and for each cluster and each feature, the presence or absence of that feature in the output graph is determined by a majority vote among the users in the cluster. This ensures that no individual user can be distinguished from at least k−1 others, while simultaneously preserving the most statistically representative feature patterns within each group. The invention further provides a polynomial-time algorithm that approximates the optimal smooth-k-anonymous solution within a constant factor of the Jaccard similarity between the original and anonymized graphs, even when the optimal solution retains as little as 75% of the original edges.

The algorithmic core of the invention consists of three main stages. First, each user is embedded as a binary vector in a high-dimensional space, where each dimension corresponds to a feature, and the value indicates presence or absence. Second, a facility location problem is formulated, where each user is both a potential client and a potential facility, with a facility opening cost proportional to the number of users within its k-nearest neighborhood. A constant-factor approximation algorithm for metric facility location is applied to group users into clusters, ensuring that each cluster contains at least a minimum number of users and that the total cost—comprising both facility opening and assignment distances—is minimized. Third, clusters that are too small are iteratively merged with neighboring clusters until each contains at least k users but no more than twice that number, ensuring balanced group sizes. Finally, for each resulting cluster, the algorithm computes the majority feature vector across all users in the cluster and assigns this vector to every member of the cluster, thereby producing the final anonymized graph.

The invention achieves several critical advantages over prior art. First, it avoids the excessive noise injection of differential privacy, which renders sparse datasets unusable, by operating deterministically on the structure of the data rather than adding randomness. Second, it overcomes the crippling data loss of suppression-based k-anonymity by allowing the addition of edges that reflect group-level consensus, thereby preserving far more of the original signal. Third, it provides the first provable approximation guarantee for this problem, demonstrating that the output preserves a constant fraction of the Jaccard similarity of the optimal smooth-k-anonymous solution, even under realistic conditions where the optimal solution retains only 75% of the original edges. Fourth, the algorithm is computationally efficient, operating in polynomial time and scaling to datasets with over a billion rows and ten billion edges through a parallelizable, locality-sensitive hashing-based preprocessing step. Fifth, empirical evaluations demonstrate that the method consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines—including Mondrian suppression, randomized response for differential privacy, and non-smooth k-anonymity—on real-world datasets across multiple domains, achieving up to twice the Jaccard similarity while maintaining comparable or superior performance in downstream machine learning tasks such as income prediction from census data.

The invention is implemented as a software system comprising a data ingestion module, a clustering engine, a majority-vote anonymization module, and an output formatter. The system accepts a binary matrix or bipartite graph as input and outputs an anonymized version that satisfies smooth-k-anonymity with parameter k. The system is configurable to adjust the clustering parameters, the minimum cluster size, and the merging thresholds, enabling deployment across diverse data regimes. The anonymized output can be directly consumed by machine learning pipelines, statistical analysis tools, or public data repositories without requiring modification, thereby enabling secure and utility-preserving data sharing at scale.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention is a method and system for anonymizing sparse binary matrices representing user-feature relationships while preserving analytical utility and ensuring formal privacy guarantees through a novel variant of k-anonymity termed smooth-k-anonymity. The method operates on a bipartite graph G = (U ∪ F, E), where U is a set of users, F is a set of features, and E is a set of edges indicating associations between users and features. Each user u ∈ U is associated with a subset of features F_u ⊆ F, and the entire dataset can be represented as an n × m binary matrix, where n = |U| is the number of users and m = |F| is the number of features. The goal is to produce an output graph G′ = (U ∪ F, E′) such that G′ satisfies smooth-k-anonymity while maximizing the Jaccard similarity between E and E′, defined as |E ∩ E′| / |E ∪ E′|. Smooth-k-anonymity requires that the users be partitioned into disjoint clusters of size at least k, and for each cluster c and each feature f ∈ F, the edge (u, f) is present in E′ for all users u ∈ c if and only if the majority of users in c have the feature f in the original graph. That is, for each cluster c and feature f, if more than half of the users in c have an edge to f in E, then all users in c are connected to f in E′; otherwise, no user in c is connected to f in E′. This rule ensures that no individual user can be uniquely identified from the output graph, since each user’s profile is identical to that of at least k−1 other users, while simultaneously preserving the most statistically coherent patterns of feature presence within each group.

The method begins with the preprocessing stage, in which the input bipartite graph is transformed into a set of binary vectors in an m-dimensional space, where each vector corresponds to a user and has a 1 in position f if the user is associated with feature f, and 0 otherwise. This embedding allows the problem to be recast as a clustering problem in a discrete metric space, where the distance between two users is defined as the Hamming distance between their binary vectors—the number of features on which they differ. This distance metric captures the structural dissimilarity between users and serves as the foundation for the subsequent clustering optimization. The key insight underlying the invention is that the problem of finding the optimal smooth-k-anonymous graph can be reduced to a variant of the lower-bounded facility location problem, in which we seek to select a set of centers (representing anonymized user profiles) and assign each user to exactly one center such that each center is assigned at least k users, and the total cost—which includes both the cost of opening a center and the cost of assigning users to centers—is minimized. In this formulation, each user is treated as a potential facility, and the cost of opening a facility at user u is defined as 2α times the number of users within the k-nearest neighborhood of u, where α is a tunable parameter that balances the trade-off between cluster size and assignment cost. This cost function ensures that facilities are more likely to be opened at dense regions of the user space, where many users share similar feature patterns, thereby promoting the formation of meaningful clusters.

The next stage of the method involves solving this facility location problem using a constant-factor approximation algorithm. Specifically, the algorithm employs a 1.488-approximation for the metric facility location problem, which guarantees that the total cost of the selected facilities and user assignments is within a constant factor of the optimal solution. The algorithm proceeds by first computing, for each user u, the set of its k nearest neighbors in Hamming distance. It then constructs a facility location instance where each user u is a potential facility with opening cost equal to 2α times the size of its k-nearest neighbor set. The facility location solver is then applied to this instance, yielding a set of selected facilities and an assignment of each user to the nearest selected facility. This assignment forms an initial clustering of users into groups, each centered at a selected facility. However, the clusters produced by this step may contain fewer than k users, which violates the smooth-k-anonymity requirement. To address this, the method enters a refinement phase in which clusters with fewer than αk users—where α is a parameter set to a constant value such as 1/2—are iteratively closed, and their users are reassigned to their second-nearest selected facility. This process continues until all clusters contain at least αk users. At this point, any remaining clusters with fewer than k users are merged with neighboring clusters until each contains at least k users but no more than 2k users. This merging step ensures that clusters are sufficiently large to satisfy the anonymity constraint while avoiding excessive cluster sizes that could dilute the statistical coherence of the anonymized profiles. The merging is performed in a greedy fashion, selecting pairs of clusters with the smallest pairwise Hamming distance between their centroids and combining them until all constraints are satisfied.

Once the final clustering is established, the anonymization step is applied. For each cluster c, the algorithm computes the majority feature vector across all users in c. That is, for each feature f, it counts the number of users in c who have an edge to f in the original graph. If this count exceeds half the size of the cluster, then all users in c are assigned an edge to f in the output graph; otherwise, no user in c is assigned an edge to f. This majority rule ensures that the output profile for each cluster reflects the most common feature pattern among its members, thereby preserving the underlying signal while eliminating idiosyncratic variations that could enable re-identification. Importantly, this step permits the addition of edges that were not present in the original graph, a key distinction from suppression-based k-anonymity, which only allows edge removal. The addition of edges under the majority rule is not arbitrary; it is constrained by the statistical structure of the cluster, ensuring that new edges reflect group-level consensus rather than noise or bias. The resulting output graph G′ = (U ∪ F, E′) is then produced, where E′ consists of all edges assigned by the majority rule across all clusters. The output graph satisfies smooth-k-anonymity by construction: every user belongs to a cluster of size between k and 2k, and every user’s feature profile in G′ is identical to that of every other user in the same cluster. Furthermore, the majority rule ensures that the output profile for each cluster is a conservative approximation of the input profiles, minimizing the introduction of spurious associations.

The theoretical foundation of the invention is established through a series of lemmas and theorems that bound the approximation ratio of the algorithm in terms of Jaccard similarity. The key result demonstrates that if the optimal smooth-k-anonymous solution preserves at least 75% of the original edges—as measured by Jaccard similarity—then the algorithm produces an output that preserves at least a constant fraction of that utility, independent of the size of the dataset or the value of k. This is achieved by relating the cost of the facility location solution to the total number of edge modifications required to achieve the optimal anonymization. Specifically, the Hamming distance between a user’s original profile and their anonymized profile corresponds to the number of edge changes incurred for that user. The total number of such changes across all users is bounded by the cost of the facility location solution, which in turn is bounded by a constant factor times the cost of the optimal solution. By applying a series of inequalities relating Hamming distance to Jaccard similarity, it is shown that the algorithm’s output achieves a Jaccard similarity that is within a constant factor of the optimal. This guarantee holds even in highly sparse datasets where the optimal solution requires removing a significant fraction of edges, a regime in which prior k-anonymity algorithms fail to provide non-vacuous guarantees. The constant factor in the approximation is derived from the combination of the 1.488-approximation for facility location, the 2-factor loss from cluster merging, and the 1/2-factor loss from the majority rule application, resulting in an overall constant approximation ratio that is empirically validated across multiple datasets.

The method is implemented as a software system that accepts a binary matrix or bipartite graph as input and outputs an anonymized version satisfying smooth-k-anonymity. The system is designed for scalability and efficiency, with optimizations tailored to handle datasets with billions of rows and hundreds of thousands of columns. For large-scale inputs, the system employs a parallelization strategy based on locality-sensitive hashing (LSH). Each user’s feature vector is hashed using multiple independent min-hash functions, and the users are sorted lexicographically by their hash values. The sorted list is then partitioned into chunks of approximately 100,000 users, and the anonymization algorithm is applied independently to each chunk. The outputs from each chunk are then combined to form the final anonymized dataset. This approach exploits the local structure of the data, ensuring that users who are similar in feature space are likely to be grouped together in the same chunk, thereby preserving the clustering quality while enabling distributed computation. The system is implemented in C++ and runs on commodity hardware, with execution times typically under three hours for datasets with up to one billion users. The algorithm is single-threaded for small datasets but automatically switches to the parallelized version when the input exceeds a predefined threshold. The system is configurable through a set of parameters, including k (the minimum cluster size), α (the cluster merging threshold), and β (a scaling factor for facility opening cost), allowing users to tune the trade-off between privacy, utility, and computational cost.

Empirical evaluations demonstrate the superiority of the invention over existing methods across multiple real-world datasets, including the UCI Adult dataset, a co-authorship network from DBLP, a web graph from Stanford, a device-feature graph from Google Play Store, and a proprietary user-interest dataset from a major internet company. The Jaccard similarity of the anonymized output is consistently higher than that of Mondrian suppression, randomized response under edge differential privacy, and non-smooth k-anonymity variants. For example, on the Play Store dataset, the invention achieves twice the Jaccard similarity of the best baseline at k = 32. On the Adult dataset, the method reduces the number of suppressed entries by 26% compared to Mondrian while increasing the number of added entries by only 7%, demonstrating a superior trade-off between suppression and creation. In downstream machine learning tasks, such as predicting whether an individual’s income exceeds $50,000 per year, the anonymized output of the invention yields classification accuracy that matches or exceeds that of Mondrian and significantly outperforms differentially private methods, even when the latter are configured with privacy parameters that are orders of magnitude larger than those required for the invention. The standard deviation of the Jaccard similarity across multiple runs is consistently below 2%, indicating high stability and reproducibility. Moreover, the method’s performance degrades gracefully with increasing k, unlike suppression-based methods, which exhibit sharp drops in utility beyond small values of k. The invention is also robust to variations in data density: it performs well on both sparse graphs (e.g., DBLP with average degree less than 10) and moderately dense graphs (e.g., Play Store with average degree over 100), whereas differential privacy fails catastrophically on sparse datasets and suppression fails on dense ones.

The invention is not limited to bipartite graphs or binary matrices; it can be extended to categorical or ordinal data through appropriate encoding schemes, and to temporal or dynamic data through time-windowed clustering. The core algorithmic framework—reduction to facility location with majority-rule anonymization—can be adapted to other privacy models, such as (ε, δ)-differential privacy with structured noise, or to hybrid models that combine smooth-k-anonymity with additional constraints such as l-diversity or t-closeness. The method is also compatible with differential privacy in the sense that the output of the anonymization process can be further perturbed with noise if additional privacy guarantees are required, though the invention demonstrates that such perturbation is often unnecessary due to the inherent robustness of smooth-k-anonymity. The system is designed for integration into data sharing platforms, data marketplaces, and regulatory-compliant data pipelines, enabling organizations to release high-fidelity anonymized datasets without violating privacy regulations or sacrificing analytical value. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in privacy-preserving data release, moving from noise-based obfuscation to structure-preserving anonymization, and establishing a new standard for utility-aware privacy in sparse binary data.
