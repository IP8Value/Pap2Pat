# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to a medical device and associated method for the repositioning of an endoscopic nasobiliary drainage catheter following endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography, specifically designed to facilitate the safe, efficient, and reliable transfer of the drainage catheter from the oral cavity to the nasal cavity without requiring direct manual manipulation of the oropharyngeal structures or the catheter tip. The invention is particularly useful in clinical settings where patients are under moderate sedation and where rapid, minimally invasive procedural completion is essential to reduce procedural time, minimize patient discomfort, and eliminate risks of iatrogenic injury to both the patient and the operator. The device is engineered to overcome the anatomical and ergonomic limitations inherent in conventional repositioning techniques, especially in patients with challenging oropharyngeal anatomy, such as those classified with higher Mallampati scores, and is compatible with standard endoscopic equipment and fluoroscopic guidance systems commonly used in gastrointestinal endoscopy suites. The invention constitutes a novel, single-use, integrated system comprising a pre-shaped, rigid yet flexible curved wire loop and a calibrated plastic guide tube with standardized depth markings, which together enable a streamlined, tactile-guided transfer mechanism that eliminates the need for visual confirmation of the catheter tip within the oral cavity, thereby rendering the procedure independent of line-of-sight access and operator dexterity in confined anatomical spaces.

## BACKGROUND ART

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography is a widely utilized diagnostic and therapeutic intervention for the management of biliary and pancreatic ductal pathologies, including choledocholithiasis, malignant biliary strictures, and postoperative bile leaks. In a significant subset of patients presenting with acute cholangitis, obstructive jaundice, or suspected biliary infection, post-procedural endoscopic nasobiliary drainage is routinely indicated to ensure adequate biliary decompression, facilitate bile drainage, and enable subsequent sampling for microbiological or cytological analysis. The standard protocol for establishing endoscopic nasobiliary drainage involves the initial placement of a drainage catheter through the working channel of the endoscope, positioning it across the papilla and into the common bile duct, followed by externalization of the distal end of the catheter through the patient’s mouth. Subsequently, the catheter must be repositioned from the oral cavity to the nasal cavity to allow for secure external fixation and to prevent accidental dislodgement during patient mobilization or post-procedural care. 

Historically, this repositioning step has been accomplished using a manual technique that requires the operator to first withdraw the endoscope from the patient’s mouth, remove the oral airway device, and manually open the patient’s oral cavity—often while the patient remains sedated but not fully awake. The operator then inserts a flexible plastic guide tube through one of the patient’s nasal passages, advances it toward the oropharynx, and attempts to grasp the exposed distal tip of the drainage catheter using either fingers or forceps inserted into the oral cavity. Once the catheter tip is secured, the plastic tube is pulled retrograde through the oropharynx and connected to the drainage catheter, after which the entire assembly is withdrawn through the nasal passage to complete the procedure. This conventional method is fraught with multiple inherent challenges. First, the requirement to manually open the patient’s mouth while sedated carries a substantial risk of accidental biting injury to the operator’s fingers, particularly in patients who are not fully anesthetized or who exhibit involuntary reflexes. Second, the visualization of the catheter tip within the oral cavity is frequently compromised due to limited access, poor lighting, excessive salivary secretions, or anatomical constraints such as a high Mallampati score, a short neck, or a narrow oropharyngeal space. These factors often lead to prolonged procedural times, repeated attempts, and increased likelihood of mucosal trauma, dental injury, or pharyngeal bleeding. Third, the manual grasping of the catheter tip using forceps introduces additional risks of catheter kinking, tearing, or misalignment, which may compromise the integrity of the drainage system and necessitate complete reinsertion. 

Several alternative techniques have been proposed in the literature to mitigate these drawbacks. One such method involves the use of a crossed guidewire loop, wherein two guidewires are manipulated to form a loop in the oral cavity that can be used to capture the catheter tip. However, this technique requires precise coordination, additional fluoroscopic or endoscopic visualization, and is highly dependent on operator experience. The guidewires are prone to slippage, lack standardized dimensions, and are difficult to control in patients with limited oral access. Other modifications have included the use of specialized retrieval nets, suction-assisted tip capture devices, or pre-attached guidewire extensions; however, these approaches remain cumbersome, require additional instrumentation, and have not been widely adopted due to their complexity, cost, or lack of reproducibility. Moreover, none of the prior art solutions address the fundamental issue of dependency on direct visual access to the oropharynx or the necessity of manual contact with the patient’s oral structures. As a result, the repositioning step continues to represent one of the most time-consuming, hazardous, and unpredictable phases of the overall ERCP procedure, particularly in high-risk populations such as elderly patients, those with limited mouth opening, or those with prior head and neck surgeries. There remains a critical and unmet need in the field for a device and method that enables rapid, safe, and reliable repositioning of the ENBD catheter without requiring removal of the oral airway, without manual manipulation of the catheter tip, and without reliance on direct visualization of the oropharyngeal anatomy. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel, self-contained, tactile-guided system that decouples the repositioning process from visual constraints and manual dexterity, thereby transforming a traditionally high-risk, variable step into a standardized, predictable, and efficient maneuver.

## DISCLOSURE OF THE INVENTION

### Technical Problem

The primary technical problem addressed by the present invention is the inefficiency, inherent risk, and procedural variability associated with the conventional method of repositioning an endoscopic nasobiliary drainage catheter from the oral cavity to the nasal cavity following endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography. Specifically, the conventional technique necessitates the removal of the oral airway device, manual opening of the patient’s mouth, and direct tactile or instrumental manipulation of the catheter tip within the oral cavity, all of which are performed while the patient remains under moderate sedation. This sequence introduces multiple sources of procedural delay, operator injury, and patient trauma. The requirement for direct visualization of the catheter tip renders the procedure highly susceptible to anatomical limitations, particularly in patients with a high Mallampati score, limited mouth opening, or a short, thick neck, where the oropharyngeal structures are not adequately visualized even under optimal endoscopic lighting. Furthermore, the manual grasping of the catheter tip using fingers or forceps exposes the operator to the risk of accidental biting injuries, which are not uncommon in sedated patients who exhibit involuntary jaw closure. The use of forceps increases the likelihood of mucosal laceration, tooth damage, or unintended catheter dislodgement due to the narrow working space and lack of tactile feedback. Additionally, the conventional technique requires the patient to remain partially awake or to be reawakened to facilitate mouth opening, thereby prolonging the overall procedural time and increasing patient discomfort. In many cases, the inability to securely grasp the catheter tip results in repeated attempts, extended fluoroscopic exposure, or even abandonment of the ENBD procedure in favor of alternative drainage methods, which may be less optimal for the patient’s clinical condition. The absence of a standardized, reliable, and operator-independent method for catheter repositioning has led to significant variability in procedural outcomes across institutions and practitioners, with repositioning times ranging from over two minutes to more than five minutes in difficult cases. The technical problem, therefore, is not merely one of efficiency, but of safety, reproducibility, and accessibility—particularly in high-risk populations where conventional methods are most likely to fail. There exists a clear and urgent need for a device and method that eliminates the necessity of direct oral cavity manipulation, removes the dependency on visual access to the catheter tip, prevents operator injury, reduces procedural time irrespective of anatomical variation, and enables seamless completion of the repositioning step while the patient remains fully sedated and the oral airway remains in place.

### Technical Solution

The technical solution provided by the present invention is a novel, integrated medical device system comprising a pre-formed, rigid yet pliable curved wire loop, referred to as the J-Loop, and a calibrated, transparent, flexible plastic guide tube with standardized depth markings. The J-Loop is constructed from a single continuous segment of medical-grade stainless steel or nitinol wire, bent into a J-shaped configuration with a rounded, blunt terminal end and a straight, ergonomically contoured handle portion. The curved portion of the loop is dimensioned to conform to the natural curvature of the oropharynx, with a radius of curvature designed to facilitate smooth passage from the oral cavity to the hypopharynx without exerting excessive pressure on the soft tissues. The handle is sized to be comfortably gripped by the operator’s dominant hand and is textured to prevent slippage during manipulation. The plastic guide tube is constructed from a biocompatible, radiolucent polymer such as polyurethane or polyethylene, with a lumen diameter sufficient to accommodate the ENBD catheter and marked at predetermined intervals—typically at 5 cm, 10 cm, 15 cm, and 20 cm—from the distal tip. These markings serve as depth indicators to ensure consistent and reproducible insertion of the tube into the nasal cavity without over-insertion or under-insertion. The distal end of the plastic tube is beveled to reduce resistance during insertion and is designed to passively engage with the J-Loop when both are aligned within the oropharyngeal space. The operational method involves the following sequence: after completion of the ERCP and externalization of the ENBD catheter through the mouth, the endoscope is withdrawn, and the oral airway is left in place. The J-Loop is then inserted into the oral cavity through the side of the airway, advanced gently along the posterior pharyngeal wall until its curved tip is positioned just proximal to the epiglottis. Simultaneously, the plastic guide tube is inserted into one of the patient’s nasal passages and advanced to a predetermined depth indicated by the markings—typically 15 to 20 cm—such that its distal end resides in the nasopharynx. The operator then applies gentle, continuous traction to the handle of the J-Loop, causing the curved portion of the loop to engage the plastic tube within the oropharynx. The design of the J-Loop ensures that the plastic tube is captured securely within the loop’s curve without requiring direct visualization or manual grasping. As the J-Loop is withdrawn through the oral cavity, it pulls the plastic tube along with it, creating a continuous pathway from the nasal cavity to the oral cavity. The distal end of the plastic tube is then connected to the externalized ENBD catheter using a standard luer-lock or snap-fit coupling, and the entire assembly is pulled retrograde through the nasal passage to complete the repositioning. The entire process is performed without removing the oral airway, without direct contact with the patient’s oral structures, and without the need for fluoroscopic or endoscopic guidance beyond the initial catheter placement. The device is single-use, sterile, and compatible with standard endoscopic equipment, making it readily integrable into existing clinical workflows. The technical solution thus eliminates the need for manual manipulation of the catheter tip, removes the dependency on visual access to the oropharynx, and provides a tactile, geometry-based mechanism for catheter transfer that is independent of patient anatomy or operator experience.

### Advantageous Effects

The implementation of the present invention yields a multitude of advantageous effects that collectively transform the ENBD repositioning procedure from a high-risk, time-intensive, and operator-dependent maneuver into a rapid, safe, and universally reproducible step. The most significant advantage is the dramatic reduction in procedural time. Clinical data demonstrate that the average time required for repositioning using the conventional technique exceeds three minutes, whereas the time required using the J-Loop system is reduced to less than one minute, representing a reduction of approximately 77%. This time savings is not merely a matter of efficiency; it directly contributes to reduced sedation duration, decreased risk of respiratory depression, and improved patient throughput in high-volume endoscopy units. A second major advantage is the elimination of operator injury risk. By removing the necessity for manual insertion of fingers or forceps into the patient’s oral cavity, the invention completely obviates the risk of accidental biting injuries, which are a well-documented occupational hazard in gastrointestinal endoscopy. This not only enhances operator safety but also reduces anxiety and improves procedural confidence, particularly among trainees or less experienced practitioners. A third advantage is the prevention of iatrogenic patient trauma. The conventional technique frequently results in mucosal abrasions, dental chipping, or pharyngeal bleeding due to the mechanical forces exerted during grasping and pulling maneuvers. The J-Loop system, by contrast, operates through passive capture and smooth traction, minimizing tissue contact and eliminating the need for forceful manipulation. No cases of oral mucosal injury, tooth damage, or bleeding were observed in the clinical cohort using the new technique, whereas such complications occurred in multiple cases with the conventional method. A fourth advantage lies in the device’s ability to function effectively regardless of the patient’s Mallampati score or oropharyngeal anatomy. While the conventional technique demonstrated significantly prolonged repositioning times in patients with Mallampati scores of 3 or 4, the J-Loop system exhibited no statistically significant difference in performance across all anatomical classifications, indicating that the device is universally applicable and not limited by anatomical constraints. This is a critical advancement, as patients with high Mallampati scores are often the most challenging to manage and are at the highest risk for procedural failure using conventional methods. A fifth advantage is the ability to perform the repositioning while the patient remains fully sedated and the oral airway remains in place, eliminating the need to awaken the patient or reposition the airway device. This enhances patient comfort, reduces procedural stress, and allows for seamless integration into the ERCP workflow without interruption. Additionally, the device is radiolucent and compatible with fluoroscopic imaging, allowing for confirmation of catheter position if needed, without introducing metallic artifacts or interference. The J-Loop system is also cost-effective, as it is a simple, single-use device that does not require specialized training or additional instrumentation beyond standard endoscopic equipment. Its design is intuitive, requiring minimal instruction for adoption, and its standardized depth markings ensure consistency across operators and institutions. The invention thus provides a comprehensive solution that simultaneously improves safety, efficiency, reproducibility, and patient outcomes, while reducing the cognitive and physical burden on the endoscopist. These advantages collectively represent a paradigm shift in the management of ENBD catheter repositioning, transforming a historically problematic step into a routine, reliable, and nearly effortless component of endoscopic biliary intervention.

## MODE FOR CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION

The mode for carrying out the invention involves the precise and sequential deployment of the J-Loop device system in conjunction with standard endoscopic equipment and procedural protocols for endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography and subsequent endoscopic nasobiliary drainage. The device system consists of two primary components: the J-Loop, a pre-formed curved wire element with a rigid handle, and a calibrated plastic guide tube with standardized depth markings. The J-Loop is manufactured from a single strand of medical-grade stainless steel or nitinol alloy, with a diameter of approximately 0.8 to 1.2 millimeters, chosen to provide sufficient rigidity for controlled manipulation while maintaining flexibility to conform to the natural curvature of the oropharynx without causing tissue trauma. The curved portion of the J-Loop is formed into a J-shaped arc with a radius of curvature between 25 and 35 millimeters, optimized to align with the anatomical contour of the posterior pharyngeal wall from the base of the tongue to the level of the epiglottis. The terminal end of the curved segment is rounded and blunted to prevent puncture or laceration of soft tissues, and the entire surface is polished to a smooth finish to reduce friction during passage. The handle portion extends approximately 8 to 12 centimeters from the base of the curve and is contoured to fit comfortably within the operator’s grip, with a textured, non-slip surface composed of a medical-grade silicone or thermoplastic elastomer to ensure secure handling even in the presence of moisture or lubricant. The handle is designed to be held between the thumb and index finger, allowing for controlled, linear traction without rotational torque, which is critical to prevent unintended dislodgement of the plastic tube. The plastic guide tube is fabricated from a radiolucent, biocompatible polymer such as polyurethane or low-density polyethylene, with an outer diameter of 3.5 to 4.5 millimeters and an internal lumen of approximately 2.5 to 3.0 millimeters, sufficient to allow the ENBD catheter to pass through without resistance. The tube is transparent to permit visual confirmation of patency and to allow for the detection of any blood or secretions within the lumen. Along its length, at intervals of 5 centimeters, starting from the distal tip, there are circumferential, laser-etched or ink-printed markings in alternating colors—typically black and white—to enable precise depth estimation during insertion. The distal end of the tube is beveled at an angle of approximately 30 degrees to facilitate smooth passage through the nasal vestibule and into the nasopharynx. The proximal end of the tube is flared to prevent accidental withdrawal into the nasal passage and to provide a stable surface for connection to the ENBD catheter. The device is supplied sterile, individually packaged in a sealed, peel-open pouch, and is intended for single-use only to prevent cross-contamination and ensure consistent performance.

The procedure for repositioning the ENBD catheter begins immediately after the completion of the endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography and the successful placement of the drainage catheter within the biliary tree. The endoscope is carefully withdrawn from the patient’s mouth, leaving the oral airway device—typically a bite block or oropharyngeal airway—in situ. The ENBD catheter is then externalized through the mouth and secured temporarily to the patient’s cheek or dental arch using a sterile adhesive strip to prevent accidental retraction. The J-Loop device is removed from its sterile packaging using standard aseptic technique, and the operator, standing at the patient’s head, grasps the handle of the J-Loop between the thumb and index finger of the dominant hand. The curved portion of the J-Loop is introduced into the oral cavity through the lateral opening of the airway device, avoiding direct contact with the teeth or tongue. The device is advanced gently along the posterior pharyngeal wall, following the natural curvature of the oropharynx, until the curved tip of the J-Loop is positioned just proximal to the epiglottis, approximately 1 to 2 centimeters above the vocal cords. This position is confirmed by tactile feedback and the resistance encountered as the curve engages the soft tissues of the hypopharynx. Simultaneously, the plastic guide tube is inserted into one of the patient’s nasal passages—preferably the right side for right-handed operators—using gentle, steady pressure. The tube is advanced until the distal tip reaches the predetermined depth, as indicated by the markings on the tube. The optimal depth is typically 15 to 20 centimeters, measured from the nasal vestibule to the point where the distal tip of the tube resides in the nasopharynx, adjacent to the posterior nares. This depth is selected to ensure that the distal end of the tube is positioned sufficiently close to the J-Loop within the oropharynx to permit engagement without over-insertion, which could cause nasal trauma or displacement of the tube into the trachea. Once both components are in position, the operator applies slow, continuous, and linear traction to the handle of the J-Loop, pulling it retrograde toward the oral cavity. As the J-Loop is withdrawn, the curved portion of the device captures the plastic guide tube within its arc, creating a mechanical linkage between the two components. The capture is passive and does not require visual confirmation; the operator relies on the tactile sensation of resistance and the smooth, continuous movement of the plastic tube as it is drawn through the oropharynx. The plastic tube is pulled through the oral cavity until its proximal end emerges from the mouth, at which point it is connected to the externalized ENBD catheter using a standard luer-lock connector or a snap-fit coupling, ensuring a leak-proof seal. The connection is verified by gentle aspiration or flushing with saline to confirm patency. Once the connection is secure, the operator applies steady traction to the plastic tube, pulling the entire assembly—ENBD catheter, plastic tube, and J-Loop—through the nasal passage. The J-Loop is discarded after the procedure, as it is no longer needed, and the ENBD catheter is secured to the patient’s face using adhesive tape or a fixation device. The entire process, from the withdrawal of the endoscope to the secure fixation of the ENBD catheter in the nasal cavity, typically requires between 30 and 60 seconds, with the majority of the time spent on the initial positioning of the plastic tube. The procedure is completed without the need for additional instrumentation, without removal of the oral airway, and without any direct contact between the operator and the patient’s oral structures. The device may be used in conjunction with fluoroscopic guidance if desired, as both the J-Loop and the plastic tube are radiolucent and do not interfere with imaging. The ENBD catheter itself may be visualized under fluoroscopy during initial placement, and the final position of the catheter may be confirmed post-procedure using plain radiography if clinical indication exists. The J-Loop system is compatible with all standard ENBD catheters, regardless of manufacturer or diameter, and may be used in adult and pediatric populations with appropriate sizing adjustments. The device is not intended for use in patients with known nasal obstruction, recent nasal surgery, or severe coagulopathy, and should be avoided in patients with a history of severe oropharyngeal trauma or anatomical anomalies that would preclude safe passage of the device. The invention may be deployed in any clinical setting where ERCP and ENBD are performed, including inpatient, outpatient, and emergency department endoscopy suites. The simplicity of the device, the lack of need for specialized training, and the absence of dependency on visual access make it particularly suitable for use in low-resource environments or by less experienced practitioners. The device may be manufactured in multiple sizes to accommodate variations in patient anatomy, including pediatric, small-framed adult, and large-framed adult configurations, each with proportionally adjusted loop curvature and tube length. The manufacturing process involves precision wire bending, heat treatment for shape memory retention, surface polishing, and sterilization via ethylene oxide or gamma irradiation. The plastic tube is extruded, marked, and sterilized separately and then assembled with the J-Loop in a cleanroom environment. Each device is individually packaged and labeled with a unique lot number and expiration date. The invention may be used in conjunction with standard sedation protocols, including propofol, midazolam, or fentanyl, and does not require general anesthesia or endotracheal intubation. The device may also be adapted for use in other endoscopic drainage procedures requiring catheter repositioning, such as endoscopic nasopancreatic drainage or transnasal biliary stent placement, by modifying the dimensions of the loop and tube to accommodate different catheter sizes and anatomical pathways. The invention represents a significant advancement in endoscopic procedural technology by providing a simple, safe, and universally applicable solution to a long-standing clinical challenge, thereby improving patient outcomes, enhancing operator safety, and increasing procedural efficiency across diverse clinical settings.
