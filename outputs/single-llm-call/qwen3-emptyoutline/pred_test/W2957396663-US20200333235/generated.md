# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to systems and methods for the rapid, label-free assessment of cancer cell viability in response to targeted therapeutic agents, particularly those conjugated to antibodies directed against activated matriptase, a membrane-bound protease overexpressed on the surface of various epithelial cancers. More specifically, the invention encompasses a microfluidic impedance cytometry platform integrated with a machine learning classifier that simultaneously measures and analyzes the electrical impedance response of individual cells across multiple frequencies to determine their viability status without the need for chemical staining, fluorescent labeling, or optical imaging. The system enables real-time, high-throughput evaluation of tumor cell sensitivity to antibody-drug conjugates, thereby facilitating personalized therapeutic decision-making at the point of care. The invention further extends to the use of such systems in clinical settings for screening patient-derived tumor cells following biopsy dissociation, enabling clinicians to predict the efficacy of targeted therapies prior to systemic administration. The technology is particularly suited for use in oncology diagnostics, drug development pipelines, and precision medicine applications where rapid, accurate, and non-destructive assessment of cellular response to targeted biologics is critical.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Cancer remains a leading cause of mortality globally, with conventional treatments such as chemotherapy and radiation often resulting in significant systemic toxicity due to their non-specific mechanisms of action. While targeted therapies—particularly those employing monoclonal antibodies conjugated to cytotoxic payloads—have improved therapeutic specificity by exploiting molecular markers uniquely expressed on tumor cells, their clinical success remains highly variable across patients. This variability stems largely from heterogeneity in target antigen expression, intracellular drug processing, and intrinsic cellular resistance mechanisms. Consequently, there exists an urgent clinical need for diagnostic tools capable of rapidly determining whether a patient’s tumor cells are susceptible to a given targeted agent before initiating treatment. Such tools would enable clinicians to avoid ineffective therapies, reduce unnecessary toxicity, and tailor treatment regimens to individual patients, thereby improving outcomes and reducing healthcare costs.

Current gold-standard methods for assessing cell viability, such as the trypan blue dye exclusion assay performed on automated platforms like the Vi-Cell® system, rely on membrane integrity as a proxy for viability. These methods, while widely adopted, suffer from several critical limitations. First, they require the addition of exogenous dyes that irreversibly alter the biological state of the cells, precluding downstream molecular analyses such as genomic sequencing, proteomic profiling, or single-cell RNA expression studies. Second, these assays demand relatively large sample volumes (typically 0.5–1 mL), which is problematic when dealing with limited clinical specimens such as fine-needle aspirates or small tumor biopsies. Third, the reliance on optical detection necessitates bulky, expensive instrumentation that is incompatible with point-of-care deployment in clinics, community hospitals, or resource-limited settings. Furthermore, the time required for staining, incubation, and image acquisition introduces delays that hinder rapid clinical decision-making.

Alternative methods have been explored, including flow cytometry with Annexin V/propidium iodide staining, luminescent ATP-based viability assays, and metabolic assays such as MTS or MTT. However, these approaches similarly require labeling, are not single-cell resolved in real time, and often involve multi-step protocols that increase variability and reduce reproducibility. Microfluidic technologies have emerged as promising platforms for reducing reagent consumption and enabling high-throughput analysis, yet most existing microfluidic systems for drug screening continue to depend on optical readouts, such as fluorescence or absorbance, which reintroduce the same limitations associated with labeling and instrumentation complexity.

Electrical impedance spectroscopy has long been recognized as a label-free technique capable of probing the dielectric properties of biological cells. When cells are suspended in a conductive medium and subjected to an alternating electric field, their membranes and intracellular components interact with the field in a frequency-dependent manner, producing measurable changes in impedance. At low frequencies, current flows primarily around the cell due to the insulating nature of the plasma membrane, whereas at high frequencies, current penetrates the membrane and interacts with intracellular components. This frequency-dependent behavior provides a rich, multi-dimensional signature of cellular state that can distinguish live cells from dead ones based on differences in membrane integrity, cytoplasmic conductivity, and cellular morphology.

Despite these advantages, prior implementations of impedance cytometry have been constrained by their reliance on single-frequency measurements, which provide insufficient discriminatory power to reliably classify complex biological states such as early apoptosis, necrosis, or partial membrane disruption. Moreover, existing systems lack the computational sophistication to extract and interpret the subtle, multi-parameter patterns inherent in multifrequency impedance data. While some studies have employed impedance measurements to assess viability in bacterial or erythrocyte models, none have demonstrated the capability to classify cancer cell viability with high accuracy in the context of targeted therapy response, nor have they integrated machine learning algorithms to optimize classification performance using multi-frequency features derived from both amplitude and phase responses.

Furthermore, no prior system has combined the following critical elements: (1) a microfluidic channel with integrated gold electrodes designed for stable, low-noise impedance measurements; (2) simultaneous acquisition of impedance data across multiple discrete frequencies ranging from hundreds of kilohertz to tens of megahertz; (3) extraction of two distinct, physiologically meaningful features—amplitude change and phase shift—for each individual cell event; (4) training of a supervised machine learning classifier, specifically a support vector machine with a Gaussian kernel, on labeled data derived from known live and dead cell populations; and (5) validation of the classifier’s predictive accuracy against a gold-standard viability assay using samples with known, graded viability percentages. The absence of such an integrated system has left a critical gap in the field: the inability to rapidly, accurately, and non-destructively determine whether a patient’s tumor cells will respond to a targeted antibody-drug conjugate prior to treatment initiation.

The present invention addresses these limitations by introducing a novel, fully integrated diagnostic platform that leverages multifrequency impedance cytometry and machine learning to deliver a label-free, high-accuracy, point-of-care capable method for assessing cancer cell viability in response to targeted therapeutics. The invention is not merely an incremental improvement over existing technologies but represents a paradigm shift in how therapeutic efficacy is evaluated, moving from static, endpoint, dye-dependent assays to dynamic, real-time, electrical signature-based classification that preserves cell integrity for further analysis. This innovation enables the translation of personalized oncology from a conceptual framework into a practical, clinically deployable diagnostic tool.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a system and method for the label-free, rapid, and highly accurate assessment of cancer cell viability in response to targeted therapeutic agents, particularly antibody-drug conjugates directed against activated matriptase. The system comprises a microfluidic device fabricated on a glass substrate with integrated gold electrodes, a multifrequency alternating current impedance measurement system, and a machine learning classifier trained to distinguish between live and dead cells based on their multifrequency impedance signatures. The method involves exposing cancer cells to a targeted therapeutic agent, introducing the treated cells into the microfluidic channel, applying a series of alternating electrical signals across multiple discrete frequencies simultaneously, measuring the resulting changes in amplitude and phase of the impedance signal for each individual cell as it transits the sensing region, extracting these features for each event, and classifying the viability status of each cell using a trained support vector machine classifier with a Gaussian kernel.

The microfluidic device is constructed using standard microfabrication techniques, including photolithography to pattern gold electrodes on a fused silica wafer, followed by bonding of a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) channel to the electrode substrate via oxygen plasma treatment. The electrodes are arranged in a pair with a defined width and spacing to create a sensing region through which cells flow under gravity-driven flow, eliminating the need for external pumps and minimizing electronic interference. The system operates by applying a low-amplitude, multi-frequency excitation signal to the electrodes and measuring the resulting current response using a lock-in amplifier, which enables high-sensitivity detection of impedance perturbations caused by individual cells passing through the channel.

For each cell event, two key features are extracted: the amplitude change, which reflects the magnitude of impedance perturbation due to the cell’s dielectric properties, and the phase change, which reflects the angular shift in the current relative to the applied voltage, indicative of the capacitive and resistive components of the cell’s interaction with the electric field. These features are measured simultaneously at four discrete frequencies, including a fixed reference frequency of 500 kHz and three higher frequencies selected from the range of 20 MHz to 30 MHz, which are known to probe intracellular properties. The extracted feature set for each cell is then fed into a support vector machine classifier that has been previously trained on a dataset of labeled events derived from pure populations of live and dead cancer cells, with labels confirmed by an independent, gold-standard viability assay.

The classifier, optimized using a Gaussian radial basis function kernel, maps the input feature space into a higher-dimensional space to enable nonlinear separation between live and dead cell populations. The system achieves classification accuracies exceeding 95% when both amplitude and phase features are used in combination, with true positive and true negative rates surpassing 95% and 97%, respectively. Importantly, the system maintains high accuracy even when tested on samples with mixed viability percentages (e.g., 50%, 82%, or 90% live cells), demonstrating its robustness in clinically relevant scenarios where cell populations are heterogeneous. The output of the classifier is a percentage of live cells in the sample, which directly correlates with the likelihood that the targeted therapy will be effective in vivo.

The invention eliminates the need for any chemical staining, fluorescent labeling, or optical imaging, thereby preserving cell integrity for downstream molecular analyses such as genomic sequencing, protein expression profiling, or functional assays. It requires only microliter-scale sample volumes, making it suitable for use with small biopsy specimens. The system is compact, low-cost, and operates without complex instrumentation, rendering it ideal for deployment in clinical laboratories, oncology clinics, and point-of-care settings. The machine learning model can be retrained using patient-specific cell populations—such as untreated tumor cells and heat-killed controls—to account for inter-patient heterogeneity, ensuring personalized diagnostic accuracy. The invention thus enables a new paradigm in precision oncology: the real-time, label-free prediction of therapeutic efficacy prior to treatment initiation, thereby optimizing clinical decision-making and improving patient outcomes.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### A. Methods and Systems for Classifying Biological Particles

The invention encompasses a novel method and system for classifying biological particles, specifically cancer cells, based on their multifrequency electrical impedance signatures in response to targeted therapeutic agents. The system is designed to determine the viability status of individual cells without the use of dyes, labels, or optical components, thereby preserving their biological integrity for subsequent analyses. The method begins with the preparation of a suspension of cancer cells that have been exposed to a targeted therapeutic agent, typically an antibody conjugated to a cytotoxic payload and directed against activated matriptase, a membrane-bound serine protease overexpressed on the surface of various epithelial cancers including breast, ovarian, lung, and hematological malignancies. The cells are washed and resuspended in a physiologically compatible buffer, such as phosphate-buffered saline, to ensure consistent ionic strength and minimize non-specific impedance artifacts.

The prepared cell suspension is introduced into a microfluidic channel fabricated on a glass substrate that contains a pair of interdigitated gold electrodes. The channel is constructed from polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) using soft lithography techniques, with a cross-sectional dimension of approximately 100 micrometers in width and 30 micrometers in height, optimized to ensure single-file transit of cells through the sensing region defined by the electrodes. The electrodes, each 20 micrometers in width and separated by a 25-micrometer gap, are patterned onto a fused silica wafer using electron beam evaporation of a 10-nanometer chromium adhesion layer followed by a 100-nanometer gold layer, with liftoff processing to achieve precise electrode geometry. The PDMS channel is permanently bonded to the electrode substrate via oxygen plasma treatment, creating a sealed, hydrophilic microfluidic environment that facilitates stable, gravity-driven flow without the need for external pumps, which could introduce mechanical or electrical noise.

Once the cell suspension is introduced into the inlet of the microfluidic channel, the cells migrate through the sensing region under the influence of capillary forces generated by a slight height differential between the inlet and outlet reservoirs. As each cell passes between the electrodes, it perturbs the electric field generated by a multifrequency alternating current excitation signal applied across the electrodes. This excitation signal is generated by a lock-in amplifier capable of simultaneously applying and measuring signals at four discrete frequencies: 500 kHz, 20 MHz, 25 MHz, and 30 MHz. The 500 kHz frequency serves as a reference for membrane-associated dielectric behavior, while the higher frequencies (20–30 MHz) penetrate the cell membrane and interact with intracellular components, enabling discrimination between intact and compromised cellular structures.

The lock-in amplifier measures the complex impedance response of the system in real time, capturing both the amplitude and phase of the current relative to the applied voltage for each frequency. The raw data stream is processed using a custom algorithm that first detrends the signal to remove low-frequency drift caused by temperature fluctuations or electrode polarization effects, followed by noise filtering using a digital bandpass filter to eliminate high-frequency electronic interference. The algorithm then identifies individual cell events by detecting transient peaks in the impedance signal that exceed a predefined threshold, corresponding to the passage of a single cell through the sensing region. For each identified event, the system calculates two key features: the amplitude change, defined as the difference between the baseline impedance magnitude and the peak impedance magnitude during the cell transit, and the phase change, defined as the angular displacement of the current waveform relative to the excitation voltage, derived from the real and imaginary components of the complex impedance.

These features are extracted independently for each of the four frequencies, resulting in an eight-dimensional feature vector for each cell event: four amplitude values and four phase values. The feature vectors are normalized using z-score standardization to ensure equal weighting across all dimensions and to mitigate variations due to differences in cell size, flow velocity, or buffer conductivity. The normalized feature vectors are then input into a trained support vector machine classifier that has been previously optimized using a Gaussian radial basis function kernel. The classifier was trained on a dataset comprising over one thousand labeled events derived from pure populations of live and dead cancer cells, where viability status was confirmed using an independent, gold-standard assay such as trypan blue exclusion or flow cytometry with annexin V/propidium iodide staining. Live cells were labeled as “1” and dead cells as “0,” and the classifier learned the nonlinear decision boundary that best separates these two classes in the high-dimensional feature space.

The classifier outputs a probability score for each cell event, which is converted into a binary classification (live or dead) using a threshold determined by maximizing the Youden index on the training dataset. The overall viability percentage of the sample is calculated as the proportion of events classified as live, providing a quantitative measure of therapeutic efficacy. The system is capable of processing hundreds of cells per minute, enabling high-throughput analysis with minimal sample consumption. The entire process, from sample introduction to viability prediction, can be completed in less than ten minutes, making it suitable for rapid clinical decision-making.

The system further includes a user interface that displays the real-time impedance traces, the classification results, and the calculated viability percentage, along with a confidence metric derived from the classifier’s margin distances. The interface also allows for retraining of the classifier using new patient-specific data, such as untreated tumor cells and heat-killed control cells, to account for inter-patient heterogeneity in cell morphology, membrane composition, or drug sensitivity. This adaptive capability ensures that the diagnostic system remains accurate across diverse patient populations and tumor types. The hardware is housed in a compact, shielded enclosure to minimize electromagnetic interference, and the system can be powered by a standard laptop or portable computing device, making it ideal for deployment in clinical laboratories, oncology clinics, or even remote settings.

The invention further includes a method for validating the classifier’s performance using independent test sets with known viability percentages, such as 50%, 82%, and 90% live cells, generated by varying the duration of drug exposure. The classifier consistently predicts viability percentages within ±3% of the ground truth, demonstrating exceptional accuracy and robustness. The system’s performance is further validated using confusion matrices, receiver operating characteristic curves, and cross-validation techniques, all of which confirm high sensitivity, specificity, and generalizability. Unlike prior systems that rely on single-frequency measurements or optical detection, this invention leverages the synergistic information contained in multifrequency amplitude and phase responses, combined with the nonlinear classification power of machine learning, to achieve unprecedented accuracy in label-free cell viability assessment.

### B. Definitions

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms are defined to ensure consistent interpretation and legal clarity. The term “activated matriptase” refers to the proteolytically cleaved, enzymatically active form of the membrane-bound serine protease matriptase, which is overexpressed on the surface of various epithelial cancer cells, including but not limited to breast, ovarian, prostate, lung, and hematological malignancies such as B-cell lymphoma and multiple myeloma. This form of matriptase is distinct from its zymogen precursor and is recognized as a biomarker for tumor aggressiveness and poor prognosis. The term “targeted therapeutic agent” refers to a biologically active molecule, typically a cytotoxic drug such as a microtubule inhibitor, DNA alkylator, or topoisomerase inhibitor, covalently conjugated to a monoclonal antibody or antibody fragment that specifically binds to activated matriptase expressed on the surface of tumor cells. The conjugation is achieved through chemical linkers that remain stable in circulation but release the cytotoxic payload upon internalization into the target cell.

The term “label-free” denotes a method or system that does not require the addition of exogenous dyes, fluorescent tags, radioactive isotopes, enzymatic substrates, or other molecular markers to detect or quantify biological properties. In the context of this invention, label-free assessment of cell viability is achieved solely through the measurement of intrinsic electrical properties of cells as they interact with an applied alternating electric field, without any chemical modification of the cells prior to or during analysis. The term “impedance cytometry” refers to the quantitative measurement of the electrical impedance of individual cells as they pass through a microfluidic channel containing electrodes, wherein changes in impedance are correlated with the dielectric properties of the cells, including membrane capacitance, cytoplasmic conductivity, and cellular morphology.

The term “multifrequency impedance spectroscopy” refers to the simultaneous application and measurement of alternating current signals at multiple discrete frequencies, typically ranging from hundreds of kilohertz to tens of megahertz, to probe the frequency-dependent dielectric behavior of biological particles. In this invention, four specific frequencies—500 kHz, 20 MHz, 25 MHz, and 30 MHz—are used to capture both membrane-level and intracellular-level responses, enabling enhanced discrimination between live and dead cells. The term “amplitude change” refers to the magnitude of the change in the impedance signal’s envelope (i.e., the peak-to-baseline difference in voltage or current amplitude) that occurs when a cell transits the sensing region of the microfluidic channel. This parameter is sensitive to the cell’s size, shape, and dielectric contrast with the surrounding medium.

The term “phase change” refers to the angular shift in the phase of the current waveform relative to the applied voltage excitation, measured in degrees or radians, that occurs during the transit of a cell through the sensing region. This parameter reflects the capacitive and resistive components of the cell’s interaction with the electric field and is particularly sensitive to changes in membrane integrity and intracellular composition. The term “microfluidic channel” refers to a narrow, enclosed flow path fabricated in a polymer or elastomeric material, such as polydimethylsiloxane, and bonded to a substrate containing electrodes, designed to guide individual cells through a defined sensing region where impedance measurements are performed. The channel dimensions are optimized to ensure single-file transit of cells and to minimize hydrodynamic dispersion and wall effects.

The term “gold electrodes” refers to conductive structures made of gold, deposited on a glass or silicon substrate using physical vapor deposition techniques, with a chromium adhesion layer beneath to ensure mechanical stability. Gold is selected for its electrochemical inertness, resistance to corrosion in physiological buffers, and low noise characteristics under alternating current excitation. The term “lock-in amplifier” refers to a precision electronic instrument that detects and measures small AC signals in the presence of high levels of noise by using a reference signal to extract the amplitude and phase of the signal at a specific frequency. In this invention, the lock-in amplifier is configured to simultaneously measure multiple frequencies, enabling real-time, multi-parameter impedance acquisition.

The term “support vector machine” refers to a supervised machine learning algorithm that constructs a hyperplane or set of hyperplanes in a high-dimensional feature space to separate data points into distinct classes. In this invention, a support vector machine with a Gaussian radial basis function kernel is employed to classify cell viability based on the eight-dimensional feature vector derived from multifrequency amplitude and phase measurements. The term “Gaussian kernel” refers to a nonlinear mapping function that computes the similarity between two feature vectors based on the Euclidean distance between them, effectively transforming the input space into a higher-dimensional space where linear separation becomes possible. This kernel is selected for its ability to handle complex, non-linear relationships between impedance features and cell viability status.

The term “viability percentage” refers to the proportion of cells in a sample that are determined to be alive, as calculated by the classifier based on the fraction of events classified as live. This metric is used to infer the efficacy of a targeted therapeutic agent, with a higher percentage of live cells indicating resistance to the drug and a lower percentage indicating sensitivity. The term “point-of-care diagnostic” refers to a medical device or system that can be operated at or near the site of patient care, such as a clinic, hospital ward, or laboratory, without requiring centralized instrumentation or specialized personnel. The system of this invention is designed to meet the criteria of a point-of-care diagnostic by being compact, rapid, low-cost, and requiring minimal sample preparation.

The term “downstream molecular analysis” refers to any subsequent biological or biochemical characterization of cells that have been analyzed by the system, including but not limited to genomic sequencing, transcriptomic profiling, proteomic analysis, flow cytometry, immunofluorescence, or functional assays. The label-free nature of this invention ensures that cells remain unaltered and viable after impedance measurement, enabling such analyses to proceed without interference from dyes or fixation agents. The term “training dataset” refers to a collection of labeled impedance events derived from known populations of live and dead cells, used to optimize the parameters of the machine learning classifier. The term “test dataset” refers to a separate collection of unlabeled or independently labeled impedance events used to evaluate the performance and generalizability of the trained classifier.

The term “gravity-driven flow” refers to the movement of fluid through the microfluidic channel induced by a height differential between the inlet and outlet reservoirs, without the use of external pumps, syringes, or pressure sources. This method minimizes mechanical vibration and electronic noise, which could interfere with sensitive impedance measurements. The term “oxygen plasma treatment” refers to a surface modification process in which the PDMS channel and glass substrate are exposed to a low-pressure oxygen plasma to render their surfaces hydrophilic, thereby enabling stable bonding and preventing air bubble formation during fluid flow. The term “cell event” refers to a single, transient impedance perturbation detected during the transit of one cell through the sensing region, which is analyzed as an independent data point for classification purposes. The term “classifier accuracy” refers to the proportion of correctly classified cell events out of the total number of events tested, as determined by comparison to a gold-standard viability assay.
