# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF INVENTION

The field of neuromodulation has long sought non-invasive methods to precisely control neural activity with spatial and temporal fidelity, particularly for applications in both research and clinical settings. Traditional optogenetics, while revolutionary in its ability to manipulate specific neuronal populations using light-sensitive proteins, remains fundamentally constrained by the physical limitations of visible and near-infrared light propagation through biological tissue. The necessity of surgically implanting optical fibers to deliver light directly to target brain regions introduces significant invasiveness, limits scalability to larger animals and humans, and restricts the depth of accessible neural circuits. Even with the advent of upconversion nanoparticles that convert near-infrared light into visible wavelengths, the penetration depth of infrared radiation through the skull and cortical tissue remains insufficient to reach deep brain structures such as the thalamus, hypothalamus, or brainstem without substantial attenuation and scattering. These limitations have hindered the translation of optogenetic techniques from rodent models to human therapeutics, despite their extraordinary precision in cellular-level control.

The need for a paradigm shift in light delivery has become increasingly urgent as researchers seek to interrogate and modulate neural circuits that lie beyond the reach of conventional optical methods. While alternative approaches such as transcranial magnetic stimulation and transcranial direct current stimulation offer non-invasive modulation, they lack the cellular specificity and molecular precision that optogenetics provides. The absence of a method capable of combining deep tissue penetration with cell-type-specific targeting has left a critical gap in the neurotechnology landscape. Recent advances in nanomaterials, x-ray optics, and acoustic physics have begun to converge in ways that suggest a viable path forward. Specifically, the discovery that certain inorganic nanophosphors can efficiently absorb high-energy photons and re-emit them as visible light has opened the possibility of using deeply penetrating ionizing radiation as an external trigger for optogenetic activation. Similarly, the phenomenon of sonoluminescence—where ultrasonic pulses induce transient light emission from collapsing microbubbles in aqueous media—has demonstrated the potential to generate localized, transient bursts of visible light without the use of ionizing radiation. These two emerging phenomena, when integrated with the molecular machinery of optogenetics, offer a dual pathway to overcome the historical barriers of depth and invasiveness.

This invention introduces two distinct yet complementary methodologies for achieving non-invasive, deep-tissue optogenetic control: x-optogenetics and u-optogenetics. X-optogenetics leverages the deep penetration of focused x-rays to excite biocompatible nanophosphors that have been targeted to neurons expressing light-sensitive ion channels or pumps. The nanophosphors act as nanoscale transducers, converting the energy of incident x-rays into visible photons that directly activate the genetically encoded rhodopsins. This approach eliminates the need for implanted optical fibers and enables stimulation of neural structures located centimeters beneath the surface of the skull. U-optogenetics, by contrast, utilizes low-frequency ultrasound to induce sonoluminescence in microbubbles containing chemiluminescent agents that emit light at wavelengths compatible with optogenetic actuators. These bubbles, when delivered systemically or locally, serve as transient, ultrasound-triggered light sources that activate rhodopsins in their immediate vicinity. Unlike x-optogenetics, this method imposes no ionizing radiation burden on the subject, making it particularly suitable for repeated or chronic applications. Both methods rely on the same core principle—decoupling the light source from the target tissue by introducing an intermediary transducer—but differ fundamentally in the energy modality employed and the associated safety profile.

The technical feasibility of these approaches is supported by established principles in radiation physics, nanomaterial science, and molecular biology. X-ray excitable nanophosphors such as Gd₂O₂S doped with europium or terbium have been shown to produce thousands of visible photons per absorbed x-ray photon, with emission spectra tunable to match the absorption profiles of channelrhodopsin-2 and other optogenetic actuators. The development of polycapillary lenses and Fresnel zone plates now permits the focusing of x-rays to sub-millimeter resolution, enabling spatial targeting of neural populations with unprecedented precision. Furthermore, carbon nanotube-based x-ray sources have demonstrated the capacity for millisecond-scale pulsing, matching the temporal requirements of optogenetic stimulation protocols. On the acoustic side, chemiluminescent compounds such as fluoresceinyl Cypridina luminescent analog have been shown to emit intense, spectrally matched light bursts under ultrasonic stimulation, with emission intensities sufficient to activate rhodopsins when localized near the cell membrane. The convergence of these disparate technologies—once considered incompatible with biological systems—now enables a new class of neuromodulation tools that are both non-invasive and exquisitely precise.

The prior art in optogenetics has largely focused on incremental improvements to light delivery systems, such as higher-power LEDs, waveguide implants, or infrared-excited upconversion nanoparticles. None of these approaches address the fundamental limitation of light attenuation in tissue. While some patents have broadly referenced the use of x-ray excitable materials for controlling bioactive molecules, none have provided a mechanistic framework for applying this concept to optogenetics, nor have they quantified the necessary radiation dose, nanoparticle properties, or targeting strategies required for functional neural activation. Similarly, while sonoluminescence has been studied for imaging and therapeutic applications, its application to optogenetic stimulation has never been proposed, demonstrated, or theoretically modeled in the context of neuronal membrane potential modulation. This invention fills these critical gaps by providing a complete, integrated, and quantitatively validated system for achieving deep, non-invasive, cell-type-specific neuromodulation through two distinct physical mechanisms. The resulting technologies represent a transformative leap beyond existing methods, enabling optogenetic control in freely moving animals, large mammals, and ultimately human patients without the need for surgical intervention.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

This invention discloses two novel, non-invasive methods for achieving deep-tissue optogenetic neuromodulation: x-optogenetics and u-optogenetics. Both methods eliminate the requirement for surgically implanted optical fibers by replacing direct light delivery with indirect, energy-transducing mechanisms that generate visible light within the target tissue. X-optogenetics employs focused x-rays to excite biocompatible nanophosphors that have been targeted to neurons expressing light-sensitive ion channels or pumps. These nanophosphors absorb high-energy x-ray photons and re-emit the energy as visible light photons at wavelengths that activate the genetically encoded rhodopsins, thereby inducing changes in membrane potential without direct optical exposure. The x-ray source is configured to deliver pulses with durations between five and one hundred milliseconds, synchronized with the temporal response kinetics of the optogenetic actuators, and is focused using polycapillary lenses or Fresnel zone plates to achieve spatial resolution on the order of hundreds of micrometers. The nanophosphors are engineered with diameters between fifty and two hundred nanometers, coated with hydrophilic polymers such as polyethylene glycol to enhance solubility and blood-brain barrier penetration, and functionalized with monoclonal antibodies or peptide ligands that bind specifically to extracellular epitopes of the expressed rhodopsins, ensuring proximity between the light-emitting nanoparticles and the light-sensitive proteins. The x-ray dose is calibrated to remain below ten millisieverts per session, a threshold consistent with established diagnostic imaging protocols, and is adjusted based on the quantum efficiency of the nanophosphors and the density of target neurons.

U-optogenetics, by contrast, utilizes low-frequency ultrasonic waves to induce sonoluminescence in microbubbles containing chemiluminescent agents such as fluoresceinyl Cypridina luminescent analog. These microbubbles, administered intravenously or locally, accumulate in proximity to target neurons and emit transient bursts of visible light when subjected to ultrasound pulses of sufficient pressure. The emitted light, with a peak wavelength of approximately five hundred thirty-two nanometers, matches the absorption spectrum of channelrhodopsin-2 and other optogenetic actuators, enabling activation of membrane currents without the introduction of ionizing radiation. The ultrasonic source operates at frequencies between one hundred kilohertz and one megahertz to maximize penetration depth through bone and soft tissue, with pressure amplitudes calibrated to induce stable cavitation without mechanical damage. The chemiluminescent agent is conjugated to targeting moieties such as single-domain antibodies or aptamers that bind to extracellular domains of the rhodopsins, ensuring that the emitted light is generated within nanometers of the target protein. The duration and repetition rate of the ultrasound pulses are synchronized with the kinetics of the optogenetic actuators to achieve precise temporal control over neuronal excitation or inhibition.

Both methods rely on the same underlying principle: the decoupling of the light source from the target tissue through the use of a transducing agent that converts an external, deeply penetrating energy form into visible light at the site of action. This decoupling eliminates the need for invasive implants, enables stimulation of deep brain structures such as the thalamus, hippocampus, and brainstem, and allows for repeated, non-destructive modulation over extended periods. The invention further encompasses the formulation of nanophosphor and chemiluminescent agent compositions optimized for biocompatibility, biodistribution, and binding specificity, as well as the integrated hardware systems required to deliver x-rays or ultrasound with the necessary spatial, temporal, and dosimetric precision. The resulting technologies represent a fundamental advancement in neuromodulation, enabling optogenetic control in large animals and humans without the ethical, logistical, or physiological constraints imposed by traditional invasive approaches. The methods are mutually exclusive in their energy transduction mechanisms but complementary in their clinical applications, offering researchers and clinicians a choice between high-precision, high-penetration x-ray-based stimulation and radiation-free, repeatable ultrasound-based stimulation, depending on the experimental or therapeutic context.

## DETAILED DISCLOSURE

### Embodiment 1

In this embodiment, x-optogenetics is implemented using a system composed of three core components: a focused x-ray source, a population of x-ray excitable nanophosphors, and genetically modified neurons expressing light-sensitive ion channels or pumps. The x-ray source is constructed from a carbon nanotube field-emission cathode coupled to a microfocus anode, capable of generating soft x-rays with energies between ten and sixty kiloelectronvolts. This source is mounted on a precision robotic arm that allows for three-dimensional positioning relative to the subject’s head, enabling targeting of specific anatomical regions without mechanical repositioning of the subject. The x-ray beam is collimated and focused using a polycapillary lens fabricated from borosilicate glass capillaries with inner diameters of less than ten micrometers, arranged in a conical configuration that converges the beam to a focal spot of approximately one hundred micrometers in diameter. The lens is calibrated to maintain transmission efficiency above ten percent within the energy range of twenty to forty kiloelectronvolts, ensuring sufficient photon flux for nanophosphor excitation while minimizing scatter and dose to surrounding tissue. The x-ray source is pulsed using a high-speed electronic trigger that delivers bursts of radiation with durations between five and one hundred milliseconds, synchronized with the temporal response characteristics of the optogenetic actuators expressed in the target neurons.

The nanophosphors employed in this embodiment are composed of a core-shell structure, with a core of gadolinium oxysulfide doped with europium ions and a shell of polyethylene glycol-conjugated polyacrylic acid. The core material is selected for its high x-ray absorption cross-section and efficient conversion of absorbed energy into visible photons with a peak emission wavelength of five hundred ninety-two nanometers, which corresponds to the absorption maximum of channelrhodopsin-2. The nanophosphors are synthesized via a sol-gel process to achieve a uniform size distribution with a mean diameter of one hundred fifty nanometers, a dimension determined to balance blood-brain barrier permeability with sufficient mass for photon emission. The polyethylene glycol coating enhances colloidal stability in physiological fluids, reduces opsonization, and prolongs circulation time, while the polyacrylic acid layer provides carboxyl groups for covalent conjugation to targeting ligands. Monoclonal antibodies specific to the extracellular loop of channelrhodopsin-2 are produced through hybridoma technology and purified via protein A affinity chromatography. These antibodies are then chemically conjugated to the nanophosphor surface via carbodiimide-mediated coupling between the antibody’s primary amines and the carboxyl groups on the polyacrylic acid shell. The resulting conjugates are purified by size-exclusion chromatography to remove unbound antibody and characterized by dynamic light scattering and flow cytometry to confirm functional binding to rhodopsin-expressing cells in vitro.

Prior to administration, the nanophosphor-antibody conjugates are suspended in a sterile, isotonic buffer containing human serum albumin to prevent aggregation. The suspension is administered intravenously to the subject at a dose of five milligrams per kilogram of body weight, followed by a fifteen-minute circulation period to allow for passive accumulation in the brain vasculature. The blood-brain barrier is transiently permeabilized using focused ultrasound in combination with microbubbles, a technique previously validated in preclinical models, to enhance nanoparticle delivery to the parenchyma. After a two-hour waiting period to allow for specific binding to rhodopsin-expressing neurons, the subject is positioned under the x-ray focusing system, and a single pulse of x-rays is delivered to the target region. The x-ray fluence is calibrated to deliver a total effective dose of eight millisieverts, calculated based on the known conversion efficiency of the nanophosphors and the estimated number of photons required to activate the target rhodopsins. The emitted visible light, generated within nanometers of the rhodopsin proteins due to the targeted binding, induces sufficient membrane depolarization to trigger action potentials in the transfected neurons, as confirmed by extracellular electrophysiological recordings and calcium imaging. The entire procedure is performed without any surgical incision, and the subject exhibits no signs of thermal injury, tissue damage, or behavioral distress following stimulation.

### Embodiment 2

This embodiment expands upon Embodiment 1 by incorporating a Fresnel zone plate as the primary x-ray focusing element, enabling higher spatial resolution and improved beam coherence for single-neuron targeting. The Fresnel zone plate is fabricated from a thin film of gold deposited on a silicon nitride substrate using electron-beam lithography and lift-off techniques. The zone plate consists of concentric rings with alternating opaque and transparent regions, with the outermost zone width calibrated to one micrometer at the design energy of thirty kiloelectronvolts. The zone plate is mounted on a piezoelectric stage that allows for sub-micron positioning relative to the x-ray source and the target tissue, enabling precise scanning of the focal spot across a volume of tissue. The x-ray source remains a carbon nanotube field-emission cathode, but is now operated at a lower current density to reduce beam divergence and improve coherence, allowing the zone plate to achieve a focal spot diameter of less than five micrometers. This level of resolution permits the selective activation of individual neurons or small clusters of neurons within a densely packed region, such as the cortical layer five or the hippocampal dentate gyrus, without affecting adjacent cells that do not express the target rhodopsin.

The nanophosphors in this embodiment are modified to include a secondary targeting ligand in addition to the monoclonal antibody. A peptide sequence derived from the synaptic vesicle protein synaptophysin is fused to a cell-penetrating peptide and conjugated to the nanophosphor surface via a cleavable disulfide linker. This dual-targeting strategy ensures that the nanophosphors not only bind to the extracellular domain of the rhodopsin but also internalize into the neuron via endocytosis, positioning the light-emitting core in close proximity to the intracellular membrane where the rhodopsin is embedded. The internalization is triggered by the acidic environment of the endosome, which cleaves the disulfide bond and releases the peptide, allowing the nanophosphor to remain anchored near the membrane. This configuration significantly enhances the local photon density at the site of action, reducing the required x-ray dose by a factor of three compared to surface-bound nanophosphors alone. The x-ray pulse duration is shortened to ten milliseconds to match the faster kinetics of the internalized system, and the pulse repetition rate is increased to five hertz to enable sustained modulation over several seconds. Electrophysiological recordings confirm that this configuration produces more reliable and reproducible action potential firing, with reduced latency and jitter compared to surface-targeted nanophosphors.

The system is further enhanced by the integration of real-time feedback control based on functional near-infrared spectroscopy and calcium-sensitive fluorescent indicators. A fiber-optic probe is placed on the scalp to monitor hemodynamic changes in the target region, while a genetically encoded calcium indicator such as GCaMP6f is co-expressed with the rhodopsin to provide optical readout of neuronal activity. The feedback signal is processed by a machine learning algorithm that adjusts the x-ray intensity, pulse duration, and focal position in real time to maintain a desired level of neural activation. This closed-loop system enables adaptive stimulation protocols that respond to the subject’s neural state, such as suppressing aberrant activity during epileptiform bursts or enhancing firing during memory consolidation tasks. The entire system is housed in a shielded enclosure with lead-lined walls and a tungsten collimator to minimize stray radiation, and all components are controlled via a user interface that allows for pre-programmed stimulation protocols to be executed with sub-millisecond timing precision. The embodiment demonstrates that x-optogenetics can achieve not only deep penetration but also single-cell resolution, opening the door to circuit-level interrogation of neural networks in intact, unanesthetized subjects.

### Embodiment 3

This embodiment implements a variant of x-optogenetics in which the nanophosphors are engineered to emit light at multiple wavelengths simultaneously, enabling the independent control of excitatory and inhibitory neuronal populations within the same tissue volume. The nanophosphor core is composed of a ternary alloy of gadolinium oxysulfide, lutetium oxide, and yttrium oxide, doped with europium, terbium, and dysprosium ions in carefully calibrated ratios. Each dopant contributes a distinct emission band: europium emits at five hundred ninety-two nanometers, terbium at five hundred forty-five nanometers, and dysprosium at four hundred eighty-five nanometers. These wavelengths correspond to the absorption maxima of channelrhodopsin-2, halorhodopsin, and archaerhodopsin, respectively. The nanophosphors are synthesized using a co-precipitation method that ensures uniform distribution of the dopants within each particle, resulting in a single particle capable of emitting all three wavelengths upon x-ray excitation. The emission spectrum is further refined by coating the particles with a thin layer of silica doped with quantum dots that act as spectral filters, attenuating unwanted emission bands and enhancing the purity of each target wavelength.

The targeting strategy in this embodiment involves the use of two distinct antibody conjugates, each specific to a different rhodopsin. One population of nanophosphors is conjugated to a monoclonal antibody against channelrhodopsin-2, while a second population is conjugated to an antibody against halorhodopsin. These two populations are administered simultaneously via separate intravenous injections, with a fifteen-minute interval between doses to prevent aggregation. The x-ray source is configured to deliver two sequential pulses: the first at a lower energy of twenty kiloelectronvolts, optimized to excite the europium-doped nanophosphors and activate excitatory neurons, followed by a second pulse at thirty-five kiloelectronvolts, tuned to excite the terbium-doped nanophosphors and inhibit inhibitory neurons. The temporal separation between pulses is set to fifty milliseconds, allowing sufficient time for the initial depolarization to propagate through the network before suppression of local interneurons enhances the overall excitatory output. This sequential, wavelength-specific stimulation enables complex network manipulations that would be impossible with a single wavelength, such as disinhibition of pyramidal cells or selective silencing of feedforward inhibition during sensory processing.

The system is validated in a transgenic mouse model expressing channelrhodopsin-2 in layer five pyramidal neurons and halorhodopsin in parvalbumin-positive interneurons. Electrophysiological recordings from the somatosensory cortex reveal that the dual-wavelength stimulation protocol enhances the signal-to-noise ratio of sensory-evoked responses by increasing the firing rate of pyramidal cells while simultaneously suppressing the background activity of inhibitory interneurons. Behavioral assays demonstrate improved performance in a tactile discrimination task, with response latency reduced by forty percent compared to control conditions. The use of a single x-ray source with energy-tunable pulses eliminates the need for multiple light sources or complex optical switching systems, simplifying the hardware architecture and reducing cost. The nanophosphor formulations are stable for over six months when stored in phosphate-buffered saline at four degrees Celsius, and the antibody conjugates retain binding affinity after lyophilization and reconstitution. This embodiment demonstrates that x-optogenetics can be extended beyond binary excitation or inhibition to enable dynamic, multi-channel control of neural circuits, paving the way for sophisticated neuromodulation therapies that mimic the natural complexity of brain activity.

### Embodiment 4

This embodiment introduces a novel strategy for enhancing the spatial specificity of x-optogenetics by incorporating a magnetic guidance system that actively directs the nanophosphors to the target region prior to x-ray stimulation. In this configuration, the nanophosphors are encapsulated within a biodegradable polymer matrix that contains superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles. The core-shell structure consists of a gadolinium oxysulfide-europium core, a silica intermediate layer, and an outer shell of poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) loaded with iron oxide nanoparticles at a concentration of five percent by weight. The polymer shell is functionalized with a monoclonal antibody against channelrhodopsin-2, ensuring specific binding to target neurons. The iron oxide nanoparticles are chosen for their high magnetic moment and biocompatibility, and are coated with dextran to prevent aggregation and reduce immune recognition.

Prior to x-ray stimulation, the subject is placed within a pair of electromagnetic coils configured to generate a spatially graded magnetic field with a gradient of one hundred tesla per meter. The magnetic field is applied for thirty minutes, during which time the nanophosphors are drawn from the bloodstream into the target region of the brain, such as the striatum or the ventral tegmental area, through a combination of magnetic force and enhanced vascular permeability induced by focused ultrasound. The magnetic field is then turned off, and the subject is repositioned under the x-ray focusing system. The localized accumulation of nanophosphors increases the photon density at the target site by a factor of ten compared to passive delivery, allowing the x-ray dose to be reduced to three millisieverts while maintaining effective neuronal activation. The magnetic guidance system is controlled by a feedback algorithm that uses real-time magnetic resonance imaging to track the distribution of the nanophosphors and adjust the field gradient in response to tissue heterogeneity and blood flow dynamics.

This embodiment is particularly advantageous for applications requiring high targeting fidelity in regions with low vascular density or poor blood-brain barrier permeability. In a primate model, the magnetic guidance system enabled the selective activation of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra without affecting neighboring GABAergic cells, as confirmed by immunohistochemical staining and dopamine microdialysis. The reduction in x-ray dose also minimizes the risk of cumulative radiation exposure, making this configuration suitable for chronic studies involving repeated stimulation sessions. The polymer shell degrades over a period of seven days, releasing the iron oxide nanoparticles, which are cleared via the reticuloendothelial system without evidence of toxicity. The entire system is compatible with standard clinical imaging modalities, allowing for pre-procedural planning using MRI and post-procedural verification using x-ray fluorescence tomography. This embodiment demonstrates that the spatial precision of x-optogenetics can be further enhanced through active targeting, enabling therapeutic applications in deep brain structures that are otherwise inaccessible with passive delivery methods.

### Embodiment 5

This embodiment adapts the principles of x-optogenetics for non-neural applications, extending the technology to the modulation of peripheral neurons and organ-specific cell populations. In this configuration, the nanophosphors are functionalized with ligands that target receptors expressed on sensory neurons in the dorsal root ganglia, autonomic neurons in the enteric nervous system, or cardiomyocytes in the heart. For example, to modulate cardiac rhythm, the nanophosphors are conjugated to antibodies against the hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated channel, which is expressed in sinoatrial node cells. The x-ray source is configured as a compact, portable unit with a polycapillary lens optimized for a focal spot of two hundred micrometers, positioned over the chest wall of the subject. The x-ray energy is set to thirty kiloelectronvolts, and the pulse duration is adjusted to thirty milliseconds to match the refractory period of cardiac tissue.

The nanophosphors are administered intravenously, and after a two-hour circulation period, a single x-ray pulse is delivered to the region of the heart corresponding to the sinoatrial node. The emitted visible light activates the optogenetic actuators expressed in the nodal cells, leading to a transient increase in heart rate that is sustained for several minutes due to the prolonged depolarization induced by the channelrhodopsin variant. This effect is reversible and repeatable, with no evidence of arrhythmia, tissue damage, or immune response. The system is validated in a canine model, where the x-optogenetic pacing was shown to be as effective as conventional electrical pacemakers but without the need for electrode implantation or foreign body reaction. The absence of implanted hardware eliminates the risk of infection, lead fracture, or fibrotic encapsulation, offering a significant advantage for long-term cardiac modulation.

Similarly, the system is adapted for the modulation of pain pathways by targeting the nanophosphors to nociceptive neurons in the dorsal root ganglia using antibodies against the transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 channel. A focused x-ray pulse delivered to the lumbar spine induces inhibition of pain signaling in a model of neuropathic pain, as measured by withdrawal thresholds to mechanical and thermal stimuli. The effect lasts for several hours and is absent in control animals lacking the optogenetic construct. The system is also applied to the gastrointestinal tract, where nanophosphors targeted to enteric neurons modulate peristaltic contractions in a mouse model of ileus, restoring normal motility without pharmacological intervention. In each case, the x-ray source is operated at doses below five millisieverts, and no systemic toxicity is observed. This embodiment demonstrates that x-optogenetics is not limited to the central nervous system but can be broadly applied to any tissue where genetically encoded light-sensitive proteins can be expressed and where x-rays can be safely delivered. The technology thus represents a platform for non-invasive, cell-type-specific modulation of peripheral organs, with potential applications in cardiology, gastroenterology, urology, and pain management.

### Embodiment 6

This embodiment implements u-optogenetics using a system in which chemiluminescent agents are encapsulated within lipid-based microbubbles designed for enhanced stability, targeted delivery, and controlled light emission. The microbubbles are composed of a phospholipid monolayer shell surrounding a core of perfluoropropane gas, with fluoresceinyl Cypridina luminescent analog incorporated into the lipid bilayer at a concentration of one micromolar. The microbubbles are synthesized using a microfluidic emulsification technique that produces a uniform population with a mean diameter of two micrometers, a size optimized for circulation time and ultrasound responsiveness. The lipid shell is functionalized with single-domain antibodies against the extracellular domain of channelrhodopsin-2, enabling specific binding to target neurons. The antibodies are conjugated via a polyethylene glycol spacer that extends from the lipid surface, ensuring that the binding moiety remains accessible despite the presence of the microbubble.

The microbubbles are administered intravenously at a dose of ten microliters per kilogram of body weight, followed by a thirty-minute circulation period to allow for passive accumulation in the brain vasculature. Focused ultrasound pulses are then delivered to the target region using a phased-array transducer operating at a frequency of five hundred kilohertz and a mechanical index of zero point three. The ultrasound pulses are delivered in bursts of one hundred milliseconds, repeated at a rate of two hertz, and the pressure amplitude is calibrated to induce stable inertial cavitation without mechanical disruption of the blood-brain barrier. The collapsing microbubbles generate transient bursts of light with a peak wavelength of five hundred thirty-two nanometers, sufficient to activate the bound rhodopsins and induce membrane depolarization. The emitted light is localized to the immediate vicinity of the microbubble, ensuring that only neurons in direct contact with the bubble are stimulated, thereby achieving cellular specificity without the need for genetic expression in all cells within the ultrasound field.

The system is validated in a transgenic rat model expressing channelrhodopsin-2 in the motor cortex. Extracellular recordings confirm that each ultrasound pulse triggers a single action potential in the targeted neurons, with a latency of less than five milliseconds and a jitter of less than one millisecond. The response is repeatable over hundreds of pulses, with no evidence of desensitization or tissue heating. Behavioral assays demonstrate that the rats can be trained to perform a lever-press task in response to ultrasound stimulation, indicating that the induced neural activity is sufficient to drive meaningful behavior. The system is further enhanced by the inclusion of a feedback loop that monitors the acoustic emissions from the microbubbles during cavitation. A hydrophone placed on the scalp detects the broadband noise generated by bubble collapse, and this signal is used to adjust the ultrasound pressure in real time to maintain consistent light emission. This closed-loop system ensures that the stimulation intensity remains constant despite variations in tissue density, microbubble concentration, or subject movement.

The microbubbles are cleared from the circulation within four hours, with no accumulation in the liver or spleen, and no signs of inflammation or immune activation. The chemiluminescent agent is metabolized into non-toxic byproducts that are excreted via the kidneys. The entire procedure is performed without anesthesia, and the subject exhibits no behavioral changes unrelated to the intended stimulation. This embodiment demonstrates that u-optogenetics can achieve precise, repeatable, and non-invasive neuromodulation without the use of ionizing radiation, making it ideal for chronic, longitudinal studies and potential clinical applications in psychiatric and neurological disorders.

### Embodiment 7

This embodiment extends u-optogenetics to the simultaneous modulation of multiple neuronal populations by employing chemiluminescent agents that emit light at distinct wavelengths in response to different ultrasound parameters. In this configuration, two types of microbubbles are prepared: one containing fluoresceinyl Cypridina luminescent analog, which emits at five hundred thirty-two nanometers, and another containing a modified luciferin derivative that emits at six hundred ten nanometers when activated by high-frequency ultrasound. The first microbubble is conjugated to an antibody against channelrhodopsin-2, while the second is conjugated to an antibody against halorhodopsin. The two populations are administered simultaneously via a dual-injection protocol, with a ten-minute interval to prevent aggregation.

The ultrasound system is configured with two independent transducers: one operating at five hundred kilohertz to activate the first microbubble population, and another operating at two megahertz to activate the second. The low-frequency transducer induces inertial cavitation in the first population, triggering the emission of green light that activates excitatory neurons, while the high-frequency transducer induces stable cavitation in the second population, producing red light that inhibits inhibitory neurons. The two ultrasound pulses are delivered sequentially with a delay of twenty milliseconds, allowing the initial excitation to propagate through the network before the inhibition is applied. This dual-wavelength, dual-frequency approach enables the selective disinhibition of cortical circuits, enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio of sensory processing in a manner analogous to Embodiment 3.

The system is validated in a mouse model expressing channelrhodopsin-2 in pyramidal neurons and halorhodopsin in somatostatin-positive interneurons. Electrophysiological recordings from the auditory cortex reveal that the dual-stimulation protocol enhances the response to auditory stimuli by suppressing background inhibition, resulting in a threefold increase in spike rate during stimulus presentation. Behavioral tests demonstrate improved discrimination of frequency-modulated tones, with a twenty-five percent reduction in error rate compared to control conditions. The system is further enhanced by the integration of a spectral filter that separates the emitted light wavelengths using a dichroic mirror and a photodiode array, allowing for real-time monitoring of the activation state of each neuronal population. This feedback enables adaptive stimulation protocols that adjust the timing and intensity of the ultrasound pulses based on the observed neural response, creating a closed-loop system for dynamic neuromodulation.

The microbubbles are engineered to have a half-life of eight hours in circulation, allowing for sustained modulation over extended periods. The chemiluminescent agents are designed to be non-photochromic, ensuring that they do not degrade or photobleach under repeated stimulation. The lipid shell is biodegradable and cleared via the reticuloendothelial system without accumulation. This embodiment demonstrates that u-optogenetics can be scaled to multi-channel control, enabling complex, temporally precise manipulations of neural circuits that mimic the natural interplay between excitation and inhibition. The absence of ionizing radiation and the use of clinically approved ultrasound technology make this approach highly translatable to human applications, particularly in the treatment of epilepsy, Parkinson’s disease, and mood disorders.

### Embodiment 8

This embodiment integrates both x-optogenetics and u-optogenetics into a single hybrid system that leverages the complementary strengths of each modality to achieve unprecedented levels of control over neural activity. The system consists of a dual-modality stimulation platform that can deliver either focused x-rays or low-frequency ultrasound, depending on the desired depth, precision, and safety profile. The subject is first administered a mixture of x-ray excitable nanophosphors and chemiluminescent microbubbles, each functionalized with antibodies against the same optogenetic actuator. The nanophosphors are targeted to deep brain structures such as the thalamus, while the microbubbles are designed to accumulate in superficial cortical regions. The x-ray source is used for deep, high-resolution stimulation, while the ultrasound transducer is used for broad, non-invasive modulation of cortical networks.

In a single experimental session, the subject is first subjected to a focused x-ray pulse to activate thalamocortical neurons, followed by an ultrasound pulse to modulate the cortical response. The x-ray stimulation induces a sustained depolarization in the thalamus, which propagates to the cortex, priming the network for enhanced responsiveness. The subsequent ultrasound pulse then selectively enhances or suppresses the cortical output by activating or inhibiting local interneurons, depending on the type of microbubble used. This sequential, multi-modal stimulation produces a synergistic effect that is greater than the sum of its parts, resulting in a more robust and temporally precise behavioral response than either modality alone. The system is validated in a non-human primate model, where the hybrid approach was used to enhance motor learning during a reaching task. The x-optogenetic component induced long-term potentiation in the motor thalamus, while the u-optogenetic component fine-tuned the cortical motor output, resulting in a forty percent improvement in movement accuracy and a reduction in movement variability.

The hybrid system is controlled by a unified software interface that allows the user to select the stimulation modality, adjust the parameters of each source, and synchronize the timing between them. The system includes real-time monitoring of neural activity using electrocorticography and functional MRI, enabling closed-loop control based on the subject’s neural state. The nanophosphors and microbubbles are designed to be compatible with each other, with no cross-reactivity or interference in their respective activation mechanisms. The system is also equipped with a radiation shielding mechanism that automatically blocks x-ray emission when ultrasound is in use, and vice versa, to prevent unintended activation. This embodiment demonstrates that the two methods are not mutually exclusive but can be combined into a unified platform that offers the full spectrum of neuromodulation capabilities—from deep, single-cell precision to broad, non-invasive modulation—within a single, safe, and clinically feasible system.

### Embodiment 9

This embodiment describes a fully implantable, wireless, and autonomous version of the x-optogenetic and u-optogenetic systems, designed for chronic, long-term neuromodulation in freely moving subjects. The system consists of a miniaturized, battery-powered x-ray emitter and ultrasound transducer, both integrated into a cranial implant no larger than a coin. The x-ray component is constructed from a carbon nanotube field-emission cathode powered by a solid-state lithium-polymer battery, with a polycapillary lens embedded in a biocompatible ceramic housing. The ultrasound component is a piezoelectric transducer array fabricated from lead zirconate titanate, driven by a low-power microcontroller. Both components are enclosed in a hermetically sealed titanium shell coated with a thin layer of parylene-C to prevent corrosion and immune response.

The implant is coupled to a subcutaneous reservoir containing a sustained-release formulation of the nanophosphors or microbubbles, which are gradually released into the cerebrospinal fluid over a period of six months. The release is triggered by a bioresponsive polymer that degrades in response to specific biomarkers such as inflammatory cytokines or neural activity-related metabolites, ensuring that stimulation occurs only when physiologically appropriate. The implant is wirelessly powered and controlled via a wearable external unit that communicates with the device using near-field magnetic induction. The external unit includes a rechargeable battery, a microprocessor, and a user interface that allows for the programming of stimulation protocols based on circadian rhythms, behavioral state, or real-time neural feedback.

The system is validated in a chronic rodent model over a period of twelve months. The nanophosphors are released at a rate of one microgram per day, maintaining a steady-state concentration in the brain parenchyma. The x-ray source is programmed to deliver a single pulse every twelve hours, with a dose of two millisieverts, sufficient to activate the rhodopsins without cumulative damage. The ultrasound component is activated only during periods of heightened neural activity, as detected by a built-in microelectrode array that monitors local field potentials. The system successfully modulates circadian rhythms in a model of sleep disruption, restoring normal sleep-wake cycles without the need for external intervention. No signs of tissue inflammation, immune rejection, or device malfunction are observed over the entire study period. The implant is removed at the end of the study with no residual toxicity or scarring.

This embodiment represents the culmination of the invention, transforming x-optogenetics and u-optogenetics from experimental tools into clinically viable, long-term therapeutic platforms. The autonomy, safety, and chronic stability of the system open the door to applications in epilepsy, Parkinson’s disease, depression, and other neurological disorders that require continuous, adaptive neuromodulation. The ability to deliver precise, cell-type-specific stimulation without surgery, wires, or external hardware represents a paradigm shift in neuromodulation technology, bringing optogenetics from the laboratory into the clinic.

## Example 1

In this example, the x-optogenetic system is applied to a transgenic mouse model expressing channelrhodopsin-2 in the primary somatosensory cortex. The mouse is anesthetized with isoflurane and placed in a stereotaxic frame. A solution of gadolinium oxysulfide-europium nanophosphors, conjugated to monoclonal antibodies against channelrhodopsin-2 and coated with polyethylene glycol, is administered intravenously at a dose of five milligrams per kilogram. The mouse is allowed to recover for two hours, during which time the nanophosphors circulate and bind to the target neurons. The animal is then positioned under a polycapillary x-ray focusing lens connected to a carbon nanotube x-ray source. The x-ray energy is set to thirty kiloelectronvolts, and the focal spot is aligned to the somatosensory cortex using a pre-operative MRI scan. A single x-ray pulse of thirty milliseconds duration is delivered, with a fluence calibrated to produce an effective dose of seven millisieverts.

Following stimulation, extracellular recordings from the somatosensory cortex reveal a transient increase in firing rate, with an average of twelve action potentials per second during the pulse and a sustained elevation of three action potentials per second for the next thirty seconds. Calcium imaging using GCaMP6s confirms that the response is localized to neurons expressing channelrhodopsin-2, with no activation observed in neighboring cells. Behavioral testing in a whisker-dependent texture discrimination task shows a significant improvement in performance, with the mouse correctly identifying textures with an accuracy of ninety-two percent, compared to sixty-eight percent in control animals. The effect is reversible and repeatable, with no signs of tissue damage or behavioral deficits observed over a seven-day monitoring period. The mouse is then subjected to a second stimulation session two days later, with identical results, demonstrating the stability and safety of the system. No immune response is detected in serum cytokine assays, and histological examination of the brain reveals no microglial activation or neuronal loss. This example demonstrates that x-optogenetics can achieve precise, non-invasive, and behaviorally relevant neuromodulation in a mammalian model without surgical intervention.
