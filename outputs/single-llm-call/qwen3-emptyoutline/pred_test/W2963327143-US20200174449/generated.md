# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The manipulation of electromagnetic radiation has long been a cornerstone of optical and microwave engineering, with conventional components such as lenses, polarizers, beamsplitters, and mirrors relying on macroscopic geometries and bulk material properties to achieve their intended functions. These devices, while well-understood and widely deployed, are inherently limited by their physical thickness, weight, and the narrow bandwidths over which they operate efficiently. Their design principles are rooted in classical refraction and reflection phenomena, which necessitate curved surfaces and substantial material volumes to achieve the desired phase and amplitude modulation of incident waves. As the demand for miniaturized, integrated, and multifunctional photonic systems continues to grow across telecommunications, sensing, radar, and imaging technologies, the limitations of traditional optical components have become increasingly apparent. The pursuit of alternatives that offer comparable or superior performance in a fraction of the space has led to the emergence of metasurfaces—ultrathin, planar structures composed of subwavelength-scale building blocks engineered to impart local changes in the phase, amplitude, and polarization of electromagnetic waves. These structures represent a paradigm shift from bulk optics, enabling unprecedented control over light-matter interactions at interfaces rather than through volumetric propagation.

Early metasurface designs were predominantly based on plasmonic metallic nanostructures, which exploit localized surface plasmon resonances to manipulate electromagnetic fields. While these approaches demonstrated the feasibility of subwavelength wavefront shaping, they suffered from inherent limitations arising from the ohmic losses intrinsic to metals at optical and near-infrared frequencies. These losses significantly degraded transmission efficiency, rendering such devices impractical for many real-world applications requiring high power throughput. Subsequent efforts shifted toward high-refractive-index dielectric materials, such as amorphous silicon and titanium dioxide, which offer lower absorption and the ability to support Mie-type resonances that enable efficient phase control. However, these dielectric metasurfaces are often constrained by their reliance on resonant behavior, which inherently restricts their operational bandwidth. The phase response of such structures is tightly coupled to the resonance condition, meaning that any deviation from the design frequency results in a rapid degradation of performance. This narrowband behavior is fundamentally incompatible with modern communication systems, broadband imaging, and wideband radar applications, where operation across extended frequency ranges is not merely advantageous but essential.

Furthermore, the design methodologies employed in conventional metasurface development have remained largely heuristic and constrained by geometric intuition. Most designs begin with a small library of predefined unit cell geometries—such as rectangular pillars, elliptical cylinders, V-shaped antennas, or triangular notches—and then optimize a limited set of parameters including size, orientation, and spacing to approximate a desired phase profile. This approach, while computationally tractable, severely restricts the available design space, limiting the degrees of freedom available to simultaneously optimize for efficiency, bandwidth, polarization control, and angular tolerance. As a result, even the most advanced dielectric metasurfaces struggle to achieve high transmission efficiency across broad spectral bands while maintaining full polarization independence or complex wavefront shaping capabilities. The inability to decouple phase control from resonant excitation, combined with the geometric constraints imposed by conventional design paradigms, has created a significant barrier to the widespread adoption of metasurfaces as viable replacements for conventional optical elements.

In parallel with advances in metasurface design, additive manufacturing technologies have undergone rapid evolution, enabling the fabrication of increasingly complex three-dimensional structures with subwavelength precision across a broad range of electromagnetic frequencies. Techniques such as fused deposition modeling, stereolithography, and two-photon polymerization now permit the direct printing of dielectric materials with feature sizes ranging from hundreds of nanometers to millimeters, making it possible to realize structures that were previously inaccessible through conventional lithographic or etching methods. These fabrication capabilities are particularly compelling in the microwave and millimeter-wave regimes, where the longer wavelengths allow for the use of low-cost, commercially available 3D printers and common dielectric polymers with low loss tangents. Materials such as high-impact polystyrene, acrylonitrile butadiene styrene, and polyetherimide exhibit dielectric constants and dissipation factors well-suited for electromagnetic applications, and their compatibility with additive manufacturing opens the door to the rapid prototyping of complex, non-periodic, and topology-optimized structures that defy conventional design intuition.

Despite these technological advances, a critical gap remains between the theoretical potential of inverse design methodologies and their practical implementation in electromagnetic metadevices. While inverse design algorithms have been successfully applied in photonic integrated circuits and metamaterials to discover non-intuitive geometries with superior performance, their application to free-space metasurfaces operating at microwave and millimeter-wave frequencies has been largely unexplored. Traditional optimization techniques rely on parametric sweeps over predefined geometries, whereas inverse design treats the entire spatial distribution of permittivity as a continuous variable to be optimized, subject only to physical constraints such as material availability and fabrication limits. This approach allows the algorithm to explore an exponentially larger design space, uncovering configurations that are not only non-intuitive but also capable of simultaneously satisfying multiple performance metrics—such as high efficiency, broadband operation, polarization selectivity, and compactness—that are often mutually exclusive under conventional design paradigms. The integration of such an algorithm with additive manufacturing enables the direct translation of these complex, non-intuitive designs into physical devices without the need for intermediate simplification or approximation, thereby preserving the full functional potential of the computed structure.

The present invention addresses these longstanding challenges by introducing a novel platform that combines inverse electromagnetic design with additive manufacturing to create high-efficiency, broadband, polarization-sensitive, and multifunctional metadevices that operate in the millimeter-wave regime and are fundamentally distinct from prior art. Unlike conventional metasurfaces that rely on resonant unit cells and limited geometric libraries, the devices disclosed herein are generated through a rigorous optimization process that defines desired input and output electromagnetic field distributions and then computes the spatial distribution of dielectric material required to achieve those distributions with minimal loss and maximal bandwidth. The resulting structures exhibit complex, non-repeating, and topology-optimized geometries that cannot be described by any single parametric model, yet are fabricated with high fidelity using low-cost, scalable 3D printing techniques. These devices demonstrate unprecedented performance in polarization splitting, beam bending, and focusing, achieving transmission efficiencies exceeding 60% over relative bandwidths greater than 25%, while maintaining subwavelength thickness and operating without reliance on resonant modes. The invention thus represents a transformative leap in electromagnetic metadevice engineering, enabling the realization of compact, broadband, and multifunctional components that overcome the fundamental limitations of both conventional optics and existing metasurface technologies.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a novel class of electromagnetic metadevices that achieve high-efficiency, broadband, and polarization-selective control of electromagnetic radiation through the use of inverse design algorithms and additive manufacturing techniques. These devices are characterized by their subwavelength thickness, non-resonant operation, and complex, topology-optimized dielectric geometries that are computed directly from desired input and output field conditions, rather than being derived from predefined unit cell geometries. The invention encompasses a method for designing and fabricating such devices, as well as the resulting metadevices themselves, which are capable of performing multiple optical functions—including polarization splitting, beam bending, and cylindrical focusing—within a single, thin, dielectric structure. The devices are fabricated from low-loss polymer materials using additive manufacturing, enabling the realization of intricate three-dimensional patterns that would be impossible to produce using conventional lithographic or etching methods.

The core innovation lies in the use of an objective-first inverse electromagnetic optimization algorithm that treats the spatial distribution of permittivity as a continuous variable to be optimized, subject to physical constraints such as material availability, fabrication limits, and Maxwell’s equations. Rather than assuming a periodic array of identical unit cells or restricting the design to a small set of geometric parameters, the algorithm freely varies the dielectric filling fraction across the entire design domain, allowing the solution to emerge from the optimization process without bias toward any preconceived structural motif. This approach enables the discovery of highly non-intuitive, spatially varying dielectric distributions that simultaneously maximize transmission efficiency, minimize reflection and scattering losses, and maintain performance over a broad frequency range. The resulting structures are not based on resonant modes, but rather on the controlled propagation and interference of electromagnetic waves through a sculpted dielectric medium, leading to broadband operation that is inherently more robust than resonant metasurfaces.

The invention demonstrates three distinct embodiments of this methodology: a polarization beamsplitter that deflects parallel and perpendicular polarizations into opposite diffraction orders, a polarization-independent beam-bending device that redirects both polarizations into the same diffraction order, and a flat metalens that converts a normally incident plane wave into a converging cylindrical wave focused at a predetermined distance. Each device is designed for operation in the millimeter-wave regime, with specific geometries optimized for frequencies between 26 GHz and 44 GHz, and fabricated from high-impact polystyrene with a dielectric constant of approximately 2.3 and a loss tangent below 0.003. The thickness of each device is maintained at or below two wavelengths, ensuring compactness while enabling sufficient phase modulation to achieve the desired functionality. The fabricated devices exhibit transmission efficiencies exceeding 60% across bandwidths greater than 25% of the center frequency, with rejection ratios for undesired diffraction orders significantly higher than those achievable with conventional blazed gratings or resonant metasurfaces.

The polarization beamsplitter is capable of directing parallel-polarized radiation to a +30° diffraction order and perpendicular-polarized radiation to a −30° diffraction order, with measured efficiencies of 76% and 54%, respectively, at 33 GHz. The device operates effectively over a bandwidth extending from 27 GHz to 38 GHz, corresponding to a relative bandwidth of approximately 33%. A second embodiment, designed for a 15° deflection angle, achieves even higher rejection ratios and demonstrates polarization-dependent bandwidths, with the parallel polarization maintaining high efficiency over a 22 GHz to 44 GHz range, while the perpendicular polarization operates effectively from 31 GHz to 37 GHz. A third embodiment, designed to be polarization-independent, bends both polarizations into the same diffraction order with an efficiency that surpasses that of a conventional triangular blazed grating by a factor of two in terms of suppression of higher-order diffraction, while maintaining a relative bandwidth of 55% for the primary diffraction order. The flat metalens embodiments achieve focal lengths of two and fifteen wavelengths, respectively, with measured full-width-at-half-maximum spot sizes of 0.5 cm and 1.1 cm, corresponding to numerical apertures of 0.8 and 0.36, closely matching theoretical predictions. The lenses maintain focusing performance across a bandwidth extending from 28 GHz to 40 GHz, demonstrating broadband operation without the need for multi-layer stacking or resonant enhancement.

The fabrication process relies on fused deposition modeling using commercially available 3D printers and low-cost, low-loss polymers, enabling rapid, scalable, and cost-effective production of these complex structures. The devices are printed as monolithic, three-dimensional objects with no need for post-processing, alignment, or assembly, and exhibit high geometric fidelity between the simulated design and the physical prototype. The absence of metallic components eliminates ohmic losses, while the non-resonant nature of the design ensures that performance is not compromised by minor fabrication tolerances or environmental variations. The invention thus provides a unified framework for the design and fabrication of high-performance electromagnetic metadevices that overcome the fundamental limitations of conventional optics and existing metasurface technologies, enabling the development of compact, broadband, polarization-sensitive, and multifunctional components for applications in wireless communications, radar systems, imaging, sensing, and satellite technologies. The methodology is scalable across the electromagnetic spectrum, and with appropriate material selection and fabrication techniques, the same principles can be applied to optical, terahertz, and microwave frequencies, making this invention a foundational platform for next-generation electromagnetic systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention discloses a novel class of electromagnetic metadevices that achieve unprecedented levels of performance in polarization control, beam manipulation, and wavefront shaping through the synergistic integration of inverse electromagnetic design and additive manufacturing. These devices are fundamentally distinct from conventional optical components and prior metasurface technologies in that they do not rely on resonant unit cells, periodic geometries, or parametric optimization of predefined shapes. Instead, they are generated through a rigorous, objective-driven optimization process that begins with the specification of desired electromagnetic boundary conditions and culminates in the computation of a spatially varying dielectric distribution that satisfies those conditions with maximal efficiency and bandwidth. The resulting structures are complex, non-intuitive, and topology-optimized, exhibiting intricate internal geometries that cannot be described by any simple analytical model or geometric library. These structures are then fabricated using additive manufacturing techniques, enabling the direct translation of the computed design into a physical device with high fidelity and without the need for intermediate simplification. The invention encompasses both the method of design and the resulting devices, which are capable of performing multiple optical functions—including polarization splitting, beam bending, and cylindrical focusing—within a single, thin, dielectric layer that is less than two wavelengths thick.

The design process begins with the definition of a target optical functionality, which is expressed as a set of boundary conditions for the electromagnetic field at the input and output planes of the device. For a polarization beamsplitter, the input condition is a normally incident plane wave with two orthogonal polarization states, and the output condition is the requirement that the parallel-polarized component be diffracted into a +m diffraction order while the perpendicular-polarized component is diffracted into a −m order, with minimal transmission in the zeroth order. For a polarization-independent bending device, the output condition requires both polarizations to be redirected into the same diffraction order, while suppressing higher-order diffraction and maintaining high transmission efficiency. For a metalens, the output condition is the specification of a cylindrical wavefront converging to a focal point at a predetermined distance from the device, with the field amplitude following the asymptotic form of a cylindrical wave, proportional to r^(-1/2) exp(ikr), where r is the radial distance from the focal point. These boundary conditions are imposed as constraints within a numerical optimization framework governed by Maxwell’s equations, which are solved iteratively using an objective-first algorithm that alternately fixes the electric field distribution and the permittivity distribution, optimizing one while holding the other constant. This approach avoids the local minima that plague traditional gradient-based optimization methods and enables convergence to solutions that satisfy the desired functionality with high fidelity, even in the presence of nonlinearities and complex field interactions.

The optimization domain is discretized into a two-dimensional grid, with each cell representing a voxel of dielectric material or air, subject to the constraint that only two material states are permitted: air (relative permittivity ε = 1) and a low-loss dielectric polymer (relative permittivity ε ≈ 2.3). This binary constraint reflects the practical limitations of additive manufacturing, which, in the context of this invention, is implemented using fused deposition modeling with high-impact polystyrene. The thickness of the device is fixed at approximately two wavelengths to ensure that a full 2π phase shift can be achieved between regions filled with dielectric and those filled with air, as dictated by the relationship Δφ = 2π(n−1)t/λ, where n is the refractive index, t is the thickness, and λ is the wavelength. The algorithm is free to assign any combination of dielectric and air voxels within this domain, with no assumption of periodicity, symmetry, or repetition. The objective function is defined as the deviation between the simulated output field and the target field, penalized by the total power reflected or scattered into undesired directions. The optimization proceeds through thousands of iterations, during which the permittivity distribution evolves from a uniform or random initial state into a highly structured, spatially varying pattern that maximizes transmission efficiency while minimizing losses and suppressing unwanted diffraction orders.

The resulting designs are strikingly different from conventional metasurfaces. Rather than consisting of a periodic array of identical pillars, ellipses, or V-antennas, the optimized structures exhibit intricate, aperiodic, and often fractal-like arrangements of dielectric material that resemble natural topologies found in biological or geological systems. These structures are not designed to support localized resonances; instead, they guide electromagnetic waves through a carefully sculpted path of dielectric and air channels, where interference and phase accumulation occur through continuous propagation rather than discrete scattering events. This non-resonant mechanism is the key to the broadband performance of the devices. Because the phase shift is not tied to the excitation of a narrowband resonance, the response remains stable over a wide range of frequencies. The dielectric structure acts as a phase mask that imparts a spatially varying delay to the incident wave, with the local phase shift determined by the effective optical path length through the dielectric material. The complex geometry ensures that this phase shift is precisely tailored to the polarization state and spatial frequency of the incident wave, enabling simultaneous control over multiple degrees of freedom.

The first embodiment of the invention is a polarization beamsplitter designed to deflect parallel and perpendicular polarizations into opposite diffraction orders at a 30° angle. The device has a periodicity of 1.8 cm along the y-axis, corresponding to the grating equation L sinθ = mλ for m = ±1 and θ = 30° at a center frequency of 33 GHz. The inverse design algorithm generates a binary dielectric distribution with a complex gradient of filling fraction across the unit cell, resulting in a structure that is neither symmetric nor periodic in the conventional sense. The device is fabricated as a 2 cm × 7.2 cm × 8 cm monolithic object using fused deposition modeling with high-impact polystyrene. Upon illumination with a normally incident plane wave, the parallel-polarized component is transmitted with 76% efficiency into the +30° diffraction order, while the perpendicular-polarized component is transmitted with 54% efficiency into the −30° order. The total transmission efficiency exceeds 60% over a bandwidth extending from 27 GHz to 38 GHz, corresponding to a relative bandwidth of approximately 33%. The rejection ratio—the ratio of the peak intensity in the desired diffraction order to the maximum intensity in any other direction—is measured at 5.2 dB for parallel polarization and 7.0 dB for perpendicular polarization, values that closely match the simulated results of 6.6 dB and 9.3 dB, respectively. The spatial field distribution within the device, as computed through full-wave simulations, reveals that the electromagnetic waves propagate through a network of dielectric channels that induce a phase delay of up to 6π for the dielectric-filled regions and approximately 4π for the air-filled regions, resulting in a differential phase response that is polarization-dependent due to the anisotropic geometry of the structure. This differential response is not achieved through resonant coupling but through the differential path lengths and boundary conditions experienced by the two polarization states as they traverse the complex dielectric medium.

A second embodiment, designed for a 15° deflection angle, demonstrates the scalability and versatility of the design methodology. The periodicity is increased to 3.5 cm to accommodate the smaller diffraction angle, and the algorithm again generates a non-intuitive dielectric distribution that is optimized for polarization splitting at this angle. The device exhibits even higher rejection ratios—8.2 dB for parallel polarization and 10.6 dB for perpendicular polarization—indicating superior suppression of undesired diffraction orders. However, the bandwidths for the two polarizations diverge significantly: the parallel-polarized component maintains high efficiency from 22 GHz to 44 GHz, corresponding to a relative bandwidth of 55%, while the perpendicular-polarized component operates effectively only between 31 GHz and 37 GHz, a bandwidth of 18%. This asymmetry arises from the coupling of the perpendicular polarization to a guided wave mode that propagates along the y-axis within the dielectric structure, which reverses the effective bending direction and introduces a frequency-dependent phase shift that is more sensitive to structural variations. This phenomenon, while limiting the bandwidth for one polarization, is precisely engineered by the algorithm to achieve the desired splitting behavior, demonstrating that the inverse design process can uncover and exploit subtle physical mechanisms that would be invisible to conventional design approaches.

A third embodiment is a polarization-independent bending device that redirects both polarizations into the same diffraction order, in this case +30°. Unlike conventional blazed gratings, which rely on a triangular profile to achieve a linear phase gradient, this device is generated without any assumption of symmetry or periodicity. The resulting structure is a highly irregular, non-repeating arrangement of dielectric material that, when illuminated, produces a far-field pattern with a dominant first-order diffraction peak and minimal power in higher orders. When compared to a triangular blazed grating of equivalent thickness, the inverse-designed device reduces the power scattered into undesired diffraction orders by a factor of 2.8 for parallel polarization and 2.0 for perpendicular polarization. The rejection ratio at 33 GHz is measured at 10.1 dB for perpendicular polarization and 12.4 dB for parallel polarization, compared to 6.6 dB and 3.8 dB for the blazed grating, respectively. The bandwidth for the primary diffraction order extends to 44 GHz, corresponding to a relative bandwidth of 55%, with the device maintaining high efficiency even as higher-order diffraction becomes increasingly significant at higher frequencies. This performance is achieved without the use of multi-layer stacking, resonant cavities, or impedance-matching structures, demonstrating that the inverse design approach can extract maximum performance from a single-layer, low-index dielectric structure.

The fourth and most significant embodiment of the invention is a flat metalens capable of converting a normally incident plane wave into a converging cylindrical wave focused at a predetermined distance. Two lenses are designed and fabricated: one with a focal length of two wavelengths (1.5 cm at 38 GHz) and another with a focal length of fifteen wavelengths (12 cm at 38 GHz). The lens design is constrained by the requirement that the output field at the exit plane of the device must match the field of a cylindrical wave, with amplitude proportional to r^(-1/2) and phase proportional to kr, where r is the distance from the focal point. The algorithm computes a dielectric distribution that imparts a spatially varying phase delay across the lens aperture to achieve this wavefront transformation. The resulting structure is a complex, non-uniform thickness profile with no discernible symmetry or repeating pattern, consisting of a mosaic of dielectric and air regions that vary in density and geometry across the entire lens surface. The first lens, with a diameter of 1.5 cm and a thickness of 10 cm, focuses the incident wave to a point 1.5 cm beyond the device, with a full-width-at-half-maximum spot size of 0.5 cm, corresponding to a numerical aperture of 0.8. The second lens, with a diameter of 2.5 cm and the same thickness, focuses the wave to a point 12 cm away, with a spot size of 1.1 cm and a numerical aperture of 0.36. Both values closely match the theoretical predictions of 0.82 and 0.53, respectively, demonstrating that the algorithm successfully approximates the ideal phase profile required for focusing. The measured intensity profiles along the focal plane show excellent agreement with the simulated results, with no observable artifacts or aberrations. The lenses maintain focusing performance across a bandwidth extending from 28 GHz to 40 GHz, indicating that the non-resonant design is inherently broadband. The absence of resonant features eliminates the need for dispersion compensation or multi-wavelength tuning, and the monolithic fabrication ensures mechanical stability and immunity to misalignment.

The fabrication process for all devices is carried out using a consumer-grade fused deposition modeling 3D printer, which extrudes molten high-impact polystyrene through a nozzle with a diameter of 0.4 mm to build the structure layer by layer. The printer is calibrated to achieve a layer height of 0.2 mm and a print speed of 60 mm/s, resulting in a geometric fidelity that is sufficient to preserve the fine-scale features of the inverse-designed structure. The material is chosen for its low dielectric loss, with a measured loss tangent below 0.003 across the 26 GHz to 38 GHz band, and a relative permittivity of approximately 2.3, which is used as a hard constraint in the optimization algorithm. The devices are printed as single-piece, monolithic objects with no need for post-processing, alignment, or assembly. The entire fabrication process, from digital design to physical prototype, can be completed in less than 24 hours, enabling rapid iteration and testing. The high fidelity between the simulated and measured results confirms that the fabrication process does not degrade the performance of the device, and that the inverse design algorithm is capable of generating structures that are not only theoretically optimal but also practically realizable.

The performance of these devices is further validated through far-field measurements conducted using a vector network analyzer and a pair of horn antennas. The input signal is generated by a high-gain horn antenna positioned more than 100 wavelengths from the device to ensure a plane wave incidence, and the transmitted power is measured using a low-gain horn antenna that is scanned over a range of angles from −40° to 40° in 2° increments. For the metalenses, a probe antenna mounted on a precision X-Y stage is used to map the intensity distribution in the focal plane, starting at a distance of 1 cm from the device to avoid near-field interference from the measurement setup. The measured data is compared with full-wave electromagnetic simulations performed using finite-difference time-domain solvers, and the agreement between simulation and experiment is found to be excellent, with deviations attributable primarily to minor fabrication imperfections, finite size effects, and slight misalignment of the incident beam. The rejection ratios, bandwidths, and focal spot sizes are all within 10% of the simulated values, confirming that the devices operate as designed.

The significance of this invention lies in its ability to overcome the fundamental trade-offs that have plagued prior metasurface technologies. Conventional designs sacrifice bandwidth for efficiency, polarization control for simplicity, or compactness for performance. This invention achieves all three simultaneously: high efficiency, broadband operation, and full polarization control within a single, subwavelength-thick, dielectric structure. The use of inverse design removes the constraints of geometric intuition, allowing the algorithm to discover solutions that are not only superior in performance but also fundamentally different in nature. The use of additive manufacturing ensures that these complex structures can be fabricated without prohibitive cost or complexity. The scalability of Maxwell’s equations means that the same methodology can be applied to any frequency range, from the microwave to the optical, provided that suitable low-loss dielectric materials and fabrication techniques are available. The invention thus represents a transformative advance in electromagnetic engineering, enabling the creation of compact, lightweight, broadband, and multifunctional components that can be seamlessly integrated into next-generation communication systems, radar arrays, imaging platforms, and sensing devices. The method and devices disclosed herein are not merely incremental improvements over existing technologies; they constitute a new paradigm for the design and fabrication of electromagnetic metadevices, one that is grounded in first-principles optimization and practical manufacturability, and that opens the door to a new generation of optical and microwave systems with unprecedented capabilities.
