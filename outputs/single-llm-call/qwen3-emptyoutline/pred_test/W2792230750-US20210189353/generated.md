# DESCRIPTION

## DESCRIPTION

### STATE OF THE ART

Enzymes capable of catalyzing the cleavage of Amadori products—covalent adducts formed between reducing sugars and amino groups of amino acids, peptides, or proteins—have long been recognized for their potential in both diagnostic and therapeutic applications. These enzymes, historically classified as Fructosyl Amino Acid Oxidases (FAOX), operate through a highly specific oxidative mechanism that targets the C–N bond linking the amino acid moiety to the fructosyl group, thereby releasing the parent amine, glucosone, and hydrogen peroxide. The enzymatic reaction is strictly dependent on the presence of a flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) cofactor, which undergoes reduction during substrate oxidation and is subsequently reoxidized by molecular oxygen, generating hydrogen peroxide as a stoichiometric byproduct. This reaction pathway has been exploited in clinical diagnostics for the quantification of glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), a well-established biomarker for long-term glycemic control in diabetic patients. In such assays, proteolytic digestion of hemoglobin liberates glycated N-terminal valine residues, which serve as the specific substrate for Fructosyl Peptide Oxidase (FPOX), a subclass of Amadoriase enzymes. The hydrogen peroxide generated is then detected via a coupled peroxidase reaction with a chromogenic substrate, enabling colorimetric quantification with high sensitivity and specificity.

Beyond diagnostic applications, the therapeutic potential of Amadoriase enzymes has been proposed for the mitigation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), which accumulate in tissues over time as a consequence of prolonged hyperglycemia and contribute to the pathogenesis of diabetic complications including nephropathy, retinopathy, neuropathy, and vascular stiffening. The irreversible nature of glycation reactions renders them particularly insidious, as accumulated AGEs alter protein structure, impair function, and trigger inflammatory cascades through interaction with the receptor for AGEs (RAGE). While endogenous repair mechanisms exist, they are insufficient to counteract the rate of AGE formation under chronic hyperglycemic conditions. Thus, exogenous enzymatic deglycation represents a promising strategy to reverse or slow the progression of glycation-induced damage. However, native Amadoriase enzymes exhibit negligible activity toward intact proteins due to steric constraints imposed by the narrow, deeply buried catalytic tunnel that restricts access to larger substrates. Furthermore, the catalytic efficiency of these enzymes is inherently limited by their susceptibility to thermal denaturation, which compromises their utility in both industrial and clinical contexts.

In the food industry, the Maillard reaction—the non-enzymatic condensation of reducing sugars with amino acids, particularly asparagine—under thermal processing conditions leads to the formation of desirable flavor and aroma compounds. Unfortunately, this same reaction pathway also generates acrylamide, a probable human carcinogen identified in a wide range of thermally processed foods including fried potatoes, baked goods, coffee, and cereal-based products. Regulatory agencies worldwide have issued guidelines to reduce acrylamide levels in food, yet current mitigation strategies—such as modifying processing parameters, using alternative ingredients, or applying enzymatic treatments with asparaginase—have limited efficacy or negatively impact sensory and nutritional qualities. The application of Amadoriase enzymes to hydrolyze early-stage Amadori products before they dehydrate and rearrange into acrylamide precursors offers a mechanistically rational approach to curbing acrylamide formation without altering the organoleptic profile of the final product. However, the thermal stability of wild-type Amadoriase is insufficient to withstand the high temperatures employed in frying (177–191 °C), roasting (≥204 °C), or baking (up to 250 °C), rendering it incompatible with most food processing conditions.

The stabilization of enzymes for industrial and biomedical use has traditionally been approached through either directed evolution or rational design. Directed evolution, which relies on iterative rounds of random mutagenesis and high-throughput screening, has proven effective in enhancing thermostability but is resource-intensive, time-consuming, and often yields unpredictable outcomes. Rational design, by contrast, seeks to introduce specific mutations based on structural and biophysical insights, such as the formation of additional salt bridges, hydrogen bonds, or disulfide linkages. Disulfide bonds, in particular, have been widely employed to rigidify protein conformations and reduce the entropy of the unfolded state, thereby increasing the free energy barrier to denaturation. However, the introduction of disulfide bonds is fraught with risk: the geometric constraints imposed by the covalent linkage between cysteine side chains can distort local folding, perturb catalytic residues, or induce global conformational changes that impair function. Moreover, computational tools for predicting the stabilizing effect of mutations remain imperfect, frequently generating false positives—mutations that appear favorable in silico but lead to misfolding, aggregation, or loss of activity upon experimental expression.

Prior attempts to stabilize Amadoriase enzymes have yielded modest improvements in thermal resistance, typically on the order of 3–5 °C in T50 (the temperature at which 50% of enzymatic activity is lost), achieved through multi-site mutagenesis and screening. No prior study has successfully employed a computational framework based on molecular dynamics simulations to prospectively screen a library of disulfide bond candidates for enhanced thermostability while preserving catalytic function. Furthermore, no Amadoriase variant has been reported that retains significant residual activity after exposure to temperatures exceeding 80 °C, a threshold necessary for practical application in food processing or long-term storage of biosensors. The absence of a robust, predictive methodology for stabilizing Amadoriase enzymes has thus hindered their translation from laboratory curiosities into viable tools for clinical diagnostics, therapeutic intervention, or industrial biocatalysis.

### SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel, thermostable variant of Amadoriase I, designated as SS17, which exhibits a substantial increase in thermal stability relative to the wild-type enzyme while retaining full catalytic activity toward its natural substrate, fructosyl-lysine. This variant is characterized by the substitution of aspartic acid at position 295 and lysine at position 303 with cysteine residues, resulting in the formation of a structurally stabilizing disulfide bond between these two residues. The engineered enzyme demonstrates a T50 of 60.6 °C, representing an increase of more than 8 °C compared to the wild-type enzyme, and retains detectable enzymatic activity even after incubation at 95 °C for 10 minutes. The disulfide bond introduced in the SS17 variant is confirmed by X-ray crystallography to be properly formed and geometrically constrained, with no significant alteration to the architecture of the catalytic pocket or the FAD-binding site. The enzyme maintains its kinetic parameters, including Michaelis-Menten constant (Km) and catalytic turnover number (kcat), within a range comparable to the wild-type enzyme, indicating that the mutation does not impair substrate binding or the chemical mechanism of catalysis.

The invention further encompasses a second thermostable variant, SS03, which features cysteine substitutions at positions 67 and 121, forming a second stabilizing disulfide bond. This variant exhibits a T50 of 55.3 °C, an increase of approximately 3 °C over the wild-type enzyme, and retains full catalytic efficiency. Both SS03 and SS17 variants are produced in recombinant Escherichia coli with high solubility and yield, and exhibit the characteristic absorption spectrum of FAD-bound enzymes, with distinct peaks at 368 nm and 454 nm, confirming proper cofactor incorporation. The enzymatic activity of both variants is abolished upon reduction of the disulfide bonds with dithiothreitol (DTT), demonstrating that the enhanced thermal stability is directly attributable to the covalent cross-linking introduced by the engineered disulfide bridges.

The invention also includes a method for the rational design and identification of thermostable enzyme variants through a high-throughput computational screening protocol based on molecular dynamics simulations. This method involves the generation of a library of potential disulfide bond candidates using conformational and energetic constraints derived from the crystal structure of the target enzyme. Each candidate variant is subjected to molecular dynamics simulations at multiple temperatures, and the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) of the protein backbone is calculated over the trajectory. The slope of the linear regression of average RMSF as a function of temperature is used as a quantitative metric to rank the thermal sensitivity of each variant. Mutants exhibiting a slope lower than that of the wild-type enzyme are identified as candidates with enhanced thermal stability, while those with higher slopes are discarded. This computational filter enables the rapid prioritization of a small subset of promising candidates for experimental validation, drastically reducing the number of variants requiring laborious biochemical characterization.

The invention further provides a method for the production of the thermostable Amadoriase variants using standard recombinant expression techniques in Escherichia coli, followed by purification via nickel-affinity chromatography and size-exclusion chromatography. The purified enzymes are stable for extended periods under ambient storage conditions and retain activity after multiple freeze-thaw cycles. The invention further encompasses the use of these stabilized enzymes in diagnostic devices for the detection of glycated biomarkers, including glycated hemoglobin and glycated albumin, where enhanced shelf-life and resistance to thermal degradation during transport and storage significantly improve assay reliability. The invention further encompasses the use of these enzymes in food processing systems to reduce the formation of acrylamide during thermal treatments such as frying, baking, roasting, and toasting, by hydrolyzing Amadori products formed between asparagine and reducing sugars prior to their conversion into acrylamide. The invention further encompasses the use of these enzymes in therapeutic formulations for the prevention or reduction of protein glycation in vivo, where the increased thermal stability serves as a foundational platform for subsequent engineering efforts aimed at broadening substrate specificity to include intact glycated proteins.

The invention further encompasses a crystalline form of the SS17 variant of Amadoriase I, as determined by X-ray crystallography at a resolution of 2.85 Å, with the disulfide bond between Cys295 and Cys303 clearly resolved in the electron density map. The crystal structure reveals that the overall fold of the enzyme is preserved, with a root mean square deviation (RMSD) of 0.804 Å relative to the wild-type enzyme, and that the catalytic pocket remains unaltered. The disulfide bond is located in a surface-exposed loop region distant from the active site, thereby minimizing the risk of perturbing substrate access or catalytic residues. The crystal structure further reveals a localized conformational shift in a loop spanning residues 63 to 69, which is displaced by approximately 2.7 Å relative to the wild-type enzyme, yet remains more ordered than the corresponding region in the related enzyme Amadoriase II, suggesting that the disulfide bond in SS17 confers rigidity to a region that is otherwise highly flexible.

The invention further encompasses a method for the preparation of a stabilized enzyme formulation comprising the SS17 or SS03 variant of Amadoriase I, optionally in combination with stabilizing excipients such as glycerol, sucrose, trehalose, polyethylene glycol, or albumin, for use in diagnostic kits, biosensors, or food processing applications. The formulation may be lyophilized, spray-dried, or encapsulated in polymer matrices to further enhance stability during long-term storage. The invention further encompasses a biosensor device comprising a solid support coated with the SS17 or SS03 variant of Amadoriase I, coupled to a hydrogen peroxide detection system such as horseradish peroxidase and a chromogenic substrate, for the quantitative measurement of glycated biomarkers in biological fluids. The biosensor exhibits an extended shelf-life of at least six months at ambient temperature and maintains a coefficient of variation below 5% in repeated measurements over a period of 90 days.

The invention further encompasses a food processing system comprising a reactor vessel in which the SS17 or SS03 variant of Amadoriase I is immobilized on a solid support or suspended in a reaction medium, and through which a food stream containing reducing sugars and amino acids is passed prior to or during thermal treatment, thereby reducing the concentration of Amadori products and consequently suppressing acrylamide formation. The system is operable at temperatures up to 90 °C, and the enzyme retains at least 50% of its initial activity after continuous operation for 24 hours under these conditions.

The invention further encompasses a therapeutic composition comprising the SS17 or SS03 variant of Amadoriase I, formulated for parenteral, topical, or oral administration, for the reduction of glycated proteins in biological tissues. The composition may be co-administered with agents that enhance tissue penetration, such as cell-penetrating peptides, liposomes, or nanoparticles, and may be delivered via sustained-release formulations to prolong enzymatic activity in vivo. The invention further encompasses a method for treating or preventing diabetic complications by administering the stabilized Amadoriase variant to a subject suffering from or at risk of developing diabetic nephropathy, retinopathy, neuropathy, or cardiovascular disease, wherein the administration results in a measurable reduction in circulating levels of glycated albumin, glycated collagen, or glycated low-density lipoprotein.

The invention further encompasses a diagnostic kit comprising a container holding the SS17 or SS03 variant of Amadoriase I in lyophilized form, a buffer solution for reconstitution, a chromogenic substrate for hydrogen peroxide detection, and instructions for use in the measurement of glycated biomarkers in serum, plasma, or urine. The kit is stable for at least 12 months when stored at 4 °C and for at least 3 months when stored at room temperature, without the need for refrigeration.

The invention further encompasses a method for the identification of additional thermostable variants of Amadoriase I or related enzymes by applying the computational screening protocol described herein to other enzymes whose stability is limited by conformational flexibility, thereby enabling the rapid development of robust biocatalysts for industrial, medical, and environmental applications.

### DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a novel class of thermostable Amadoriase I enzymes, engineered through rational design to possess enhanced resistance to thermal denaturation while preserving their catalytic function. These enzymes are derived from the wild-type Amadoriase I originally isolated from Aspergillus fumigatus and are characterized by the introduction of specific disulfide bonds that rigidify the protein scaffold without compromising the geometry of the active site or the binding affinity for the FAD cofactor. The invention provides not only the engineered enzyme variants themselves but also the methods for their production, characterization, and application in diagnostic, therapeutic, and industrial contexts. The structural and functional properties of these variants are the result of a novel computational screening methodology that enables the prospective identification of stabilizing mutations through molecular dynamics simulations, thereby circumventing the need for exhaustive experimental mutagenesis and screening.

The first embodiment of the invention is the SS17 variant of Amadoriase I, which contains two amino acid substitutions: aspartic acid at position 295 is replaced by cysteine, and lysine at position 303 is replaced by cysteine. These substitutions result in the formation of a disulfide bond between the sulfur atoms of the two cysteine side chains, creating a covalent cross-link that constrains the conformational flexibility of a surface loop region located near the C-terminal domain of the enzyme. The disulfide bond is not located in the vicinity of the catalytic pocket or the FAD-binding site, which are situated in a central cleft of the protein, and therefore does not interfere with substrate access or the catalytic mechanism. The crystal structure of the SS17 variant, determined at a resolution of 2.85 Å, confirms the presence of the disulfide bond with clear electron density connecting the Cβ atoms of residues 295 and 303. The bond length is 2.03 Å, and the dihedral angles χ1 and χ2 are consistent with a right-handed spiral conformation, indicative of a low-energy, stable geometry. The overall fold of the enzyme is preserved, with a root mean square deviation (RMSD) of 0.804 Å for all Cα atoms when superimposed on the wild-type structure. The catalytic residues, including the conserved histidine that acts as a general base and the tyrosine that stabilizes the transition state, remain in their native positions, with deviations less than 0.5 Å. The FAD cofactor is bound in an identical orientation, with hydrogen bonding interactions between the isoalloxazine ring and surrounding residues unchanged. The disulfide bond introduces a localized conformational shift in a flexible loop spanning residues 63 to 69, which is displaced by approximately 2.7 Å relative to the wild-type enzyme. This displacement, while measurable, does not obstruct the substrate access tunnel and, in fact, appears to stabilize a region that is intrinsically disordered in the wild-type enzyme and in the related Amadoriase II isoform. The loop in the SS17 variant exhibits lower B-factors in the crystal structure, indicating reduced thermal motion, and is resolved in the electron density map, whereas the corresponding region in Amadoriase II could not be modeled due to its high flexibility.

The second embodiment of the invention is the SS03 variant of Amadoriase I, which contains two amino acid substitutions: serine at position 67 is replaced by cysteine, and proline at position 121 is replaced by cysteine. These substitutions form a second disulfide bond that connects two residues located in a β-sheet region near the N-terminal domain of the enzyme. The disulfide bond is positioned such that it links two strands of the β-sheet, effectively stapling them together and reducing the conformational entropy of the unfolded state. The crystal structure of the SS03 variant, determined at a resolution of 2.15 Å, confirms the formation of the disulfide bond with a bond length of 2.05 Å and dihedral angles consistent with a stable conformation. The RMSD between the SS03 variant and the wild-type enzyme is only 0.268 Å, indicating an almost perfect structural superposition. The catalytic pocket, the FAD-binding site, and the substrate access tunnel are all preserved with sub-angstrom precision. The disulfide bond in SS03 does not induce any detectable conformational change in neighboring regions, and the B-factors for the surrounding residues are comparable to those in the wild-type enzyme. The SS03 variant exhibits a T50 of 55.3 °C, representing a 2.85 °C increase over the wild-type enzyme, and retains full catalytic efficiency as measured by the kcat/Km ratio for fructosyl-lysine.

The invention further encompasses a double mutant variant, designated SSDM, which combines the cysteine substitutions from both SS03 and SS17. This variant was produced to assess whether the two stabilizing disulfide bonds act synergistically to further enhance thermal stability. However, biochemical characterization of the SSDM variant revealed a T50 of 55.8 °C, which is nearly identical to that of the SS03 variant and significantly lower than the additive effect expected from the combination of two independent stabilizing mutations. This observation suggests that the presence of both disulfide bonds may interfere with the folding pathway of the enzyme, potentially leading to the preferential formation of one disulfide bond over the other, or inducing a conformational strain that counteracts the stabilizing effect of the second bond. The inability to obtain high-quality crystals of the SSDM variant precluded a definitive structural analysis, but the enzymatic data strongly indicate that the SS17 mutation does not confer additional stability when combined with SS03. This finding underscores the importance of evaluating mutations in isolation before attempting combinatorial approaches, and highlights the non-additive nature of protein stabilization through disulfide bond engineering.

The production of the SS03 and SS17 variants was achieved through recombinant expression in Escherichia coli BL21(DE3)pLysS cells, using a pET-3a expression vector containing the gene encoding Amadoriase I. The wild-type gene was modified by site-directed mutagenesis to introduce the desired cysteine substitutions, and the resulting plasmids were verified by DNA sequencing. Expression was induced with isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at a concentration of 0.5 mM, and cultures were grown overnight at 25 °C to promote soluble protein expression and minimize inclusion body formation. After cell lysis by sonication, the soluble fraction was purified using nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) affinity chromatography, exploiting the N-terminal hexahistidine tag engineered into the expression construct. The eluted protein was further purified by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 200 column to remove aggregates and ensure homogeneity. The purified enzymes were concentrated to approximately 10 mg/mL and stored at −80 °C in a buffer containing 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0. The yield of purified enzyme was approximately 30 mg per liter of culture for both SS03 and SS17, comparable to the yield obtained for the wild-type enzyme, indicating that the mutations do not impair folding or solubility. In contrast, the SS11 variant, which contained cysteine substitutions at positions 233 and 404, exhibited extremely low expression levels and no detectable FAD binding, suggesting that these residues are critical for the structural integrity of the FAD-binding domain. This observation further validates the computational screening approach, as SS11 was correctly identified as a false positive and excluded from further analysis.

The enzymatic activity of the SS03 and SS17 variants was assessed using a continuous spectrophotometric assay that monitors the formation of glucosone, the primary product of Amadoriase catalysis, at a wavelength of 322 nm. The assay was performed at 25 °C using fructosyl-lysine as the substrate, and the kinetic parameters were determined by fitting the initial reaction rates to the Michaelis-Menten equation. The Km values for SS03 and SS17 were 0.34 mM and 0.68 mM, respectively, compared to 0.51 mM for the wild-type enzyme, indicating that the mutations do not significantly alter substrate affinity. The kcat values were 21.9 s−1 for SS03, 22.9 s−1 for SS17, and 21.6 s−1 for the wild-type enzyme, demonstrating that the catalytic turnover rate is unaffected. The catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km) for SS03 was 64.2 s−1 mM−1, for SS17 was 33.3 s−1 mM−1, and for the wild-type enzyme was 41.7 s−1 mM−1. The slight reduction in catalytic efficiency observed for SS17 is attributed to a minor narrowing of the substrate access tunnel, as confirmed by CAVER 3.0.2 analysis, which revealed a reduction in the bottleneck diameter from 1.83 Å in the wild-type enzyme to 1.57 Å in the SS17 variant. This narrowing does not prevent substrate entry but may slightly slow the rate of diffusion into the active site. Importantly, this effect is not observed in the SS03 variant, which maintains a tunnel diameter identical to that of the wild-type enzyme, and thus exhibits a catalytic efficiency higher than that of the wild-type.

The thermal stability of the variants was evaluated by incubating the enzymes at temperatures ranging from 25 °C to 100 °C for 10 minutes, followed by cooling to 4 °C and measurement of residual activity. The T50 values, defined as the temperature at which 50% of the initial activity is lost, were determined by fitting the data to a Boltzmann sigmoidal curve. The wild-type enzyme exhibited a T50 of 52.4 °C, while the SS03 variant showed a T50 of 55.3 °C, representing a 2.85 °C increase. The SS17 variant demonstrated a T50 of 60.6 °C, an increase of 8.22 °C over the wild-type enzyme. Notably, the SS17 variant retained 50% of its activity even after exposure to 60 °C, and measurable activity was detected even after incubation at 95 °C, a temperature at which the wild-type enzyme and all other variants were completely inactivated. The addition of 100 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), a reducing agent that cleaves disulfide bonds, abolished the enhanced thermal stability of both SS03 and SS17, causing their T50 values to revert to levels indistinguishable from that of the wild-type enzyme. This result provides definitive evidence that the increased thermostability is directly attributable to the formation of the engineered disulfide bonds and not to other effects such as changes in surface charge or hydrophobic packing.

The pH dependence of enzymatic activity was also evaluated, as many diagnostic and food processing applications require stable performance across a range of pH values. The activity profiles of the wild-type, SS03, SS17, and SSDM variants were nearly identical, with a sharp increase in activity above pH 8.0 and a plateau between pH 8.5 and 11.0. Below pH 8.0, activity declined sharply, consistent with the known pH sensitivity of the catalytic histidine residue. The lack of change in pH profile indicates that the introduced mutations do not alter the ionization state of key catalytic residues or the electrostatic environment of the active site. This finding is critical for applications in biological fluids, which typically have a pH between 7.3 and 7.5, as it ensures that the enzyme will function optimally under physiological conditions.

The UV-visible absorption spectra of the purified enzymes confirmed the presence of the FAD cofactor in both SS03 and SS17 variants, with characteristic peaks at 368 nm and 454 nm corresponding to the π–π* transitions of the isoalloxazine ring. The absorbance ratio at 454 nm to 280 nm was consistent with a 1:1 stoichiometry of FAD to enzyme, indicating complete cofactor incorporation. In contrast, the SS11 variant lacked both absorption peaks, confirming the absence of bound FAD and supporting the conclusion that this variant is misfolded. The absence of spectral shifts in the SS03 and SS17 variants further confirms that the electronic environment of the FAD cofactor is unchanged, and that the disulfide bonds do not perturb the redox properties of the enzyme.

The crystal structures of the SS03 and SS17 variants were solved using X-ray crystallography, with data collected at the Swiss Light Source. The SS03 variant crystallized in the same space group (P212121) as the wild-type enzyme, with unit cell dimensions of 70.15 Å, 83.08 Å, and 117.59 Å, and a resolution of 2.15 Å. The SS17 variant crystallized in a different space group (P21), with unit cell dimensions of 68.85 Å, 90.46 Å, and 81.30 Å, and a resolution of 2.85 Å. The difference in crystal packing is attributed to the conformational rearrangement induced by the disulfide bond in SS17, which alters the surface topology and intermolecular contacts. Despite this difference, the refinement statistics for both structures were excellent, with Rwork/Rfree values of 13.92%/18.07% for SS03 and 17.18%/24.40% for SS17. The Ramachandran plot for both structures showed that over 97% of the residues were in favored or allowed regions, with no residues in disallowed conformations, confirming the high quality of the models. The electron density maps for the disulfide bonds were unambiguous, with clear connectivity between the sulfur atoms of the mutated cysteines. The B-factors for the disulfide-bonded residues were among the lowest in the structure, indicating high rigidity, while the surrounding loops exhibited B-factors comparable to those in the wild-type enzyme, confirming that the stabilization is localized and does not propagate globally.

The molecular dynamics simulations that underpinned the design of these variants were performed using the NAMD and ACEMD software packages, with the AMBER99SBildn force field for the protein and GAFF for the FAD cofactor. The systems were solvated in a box of approximately 50,000 atoms, with explicit water molecules and physiological salt concentration. Simulations were conducted at three temperatures—273 K, 300 K, and 340 K—to capture the thermal response of the protein. The root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) of the Cα atoms was calculated over the last 4 ns of each trajectory, excluding the initial equilibration period. The average RMSF at each temperature was plotted, and the slope (λ) of the linear regression was used as a metric for thermal sensitivity. The wild-type enzyme exhibited a slope of 0.00151, while the SS03 and SS17 variants exhibited slopes of 0.00151 and 0.00118, respectively, indicating that these variants are less sensitive to thermal fluctuations. The SS07 variant, which was also selected for experimental testing, exhibited a slope of 0.00115, suggesting it should be stabilizing, yet biochemical characterization revealed a 4-fold reduction in catalytic efficiency and a T50 lower than that of the wild-type enzyme. This discrepancy was resolved by structural analysis, which showed that the disulfide bond in SS07 (between H106 and G150) narrows the substrate access tunnel, thereby impairing substrate diffusion. This finding demonstrates that while the computational screening method is highly effective at identifying mutations that reduce conformational flexibility, it cannot predict steric or dynamic effects on substrate access, and thus must be complemented by experimental validation.
