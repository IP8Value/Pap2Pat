# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a patient safety system for radiotherapy treatment delivery, specifically designed to provide continuous, real-time, and independent verification of patient positioning relative to the planned isocenter during external beam radiation therapy. The system is applicable across all anatomical treatment sites, including but not limited to head and neck, thoracic, abdominal, pelvic, and extremity regions, and is compatible with a wide range of linear accelerators, image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) modalities, and immobilization devices. It operates without the need for ionizing radiation, does not rely on internal anatomical landmarks, and is functionally independent of the treatment machine’s control system, couch design, or the specific IGRT technology employed. The invention integrates passive optical tracking of a single infrared reflective marker, synchronized with the treatment record and verify system, to automatically initiate position verification upon beam selection and to continuously monitor spatial deviations in three dimensions throughout the treatment session. This system is intended to serve as a fail-safe mechanism that detects gross positioning errors—such as incorrect couch shifts, misaligned immobilization devices, erroneous override of interlocks, or misregistration due to anatomical similarity—that may escape detection by conventional IGRT systems due to workflow limitations, human error, or technical constraints. By providing an independent, non-ionizing, and automated layer of verification that operates in parallel with existing clinical protocols, the invention significantly reduces the risk of geometric miss and ensures that treatment is delivered only when the patient’s external reference point is confirmed to be within predefined tolerances relative to the planned treatment position.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Radiotherapy is a highly complex, multi-step clinical process involving numerous personnel, sophisticated equipment, and precise spatial coordination between treatment planning, patient immobilization, imaging verification, and beam delivery. Despite significant technological advancements in intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT), and image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT), the incidence of severe treatment errors—some resulting in permanent morbidity or fatality—remains a persistent and alarming concern in clinical practice. A substantial proportion of these errors are attributable to geometric miss, which occurs when the intended target volume is not accurately aligned with the radiation beam due to incorrect patient setup. Such errors are frequently caused by human factors, including misinterpretation of imaging data, failure to recognize subtle anatomical similarities in bony structures, inadvertent movement of the treatment couch after imaging, or the incorrect override of safety interlocks. These errors are particularly dangerous in hypofractionated regimens such as stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), where small positional deviations can lead to catastrophic underdosing of the tumor or severe overdosing of critical organs.

While IGRT systems such as cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), orthogonal kV imaging, and optical surface tracking systems have been widely adopted to improve setup accuracy, they are not infallible and are subject to inherent limitations. CBCT, for example, provides only a snapshot of patient position at the time of imaging and cannot detect motion or displacement that occurs between imaging and beam delivery. Furthermore, CBCT is often incompatible with noncoplanar beam arrangements, and its use may be precluded by mechanical collisions between the imaging components and the patient or treatment couch. Optical tracking systems such as AlignRT, C-Rad Sentinel, and ExacTrac offer continuous monitoring capabilities, but their clinical utility is often restricted to specific anatomical regions—such as the head and neck or breast—due to the need for multiple reflective markers, specialized immobilization devices, or complex calibration procedures. Moreover, these systems typically require manual intervention by therapists to select the correct patient, treatment site, and couch angle for each beam, introducing opportunities for human error and disrupting the efficiency of the clinical workflow. In many institutions, therapists are required to switch between multiple IGRT systems depending on the treatment site, increasing cognitive load and the likelihood of misconfiguration.

Even in facilities with state-of-the-art IGRT infrastructure, the process of patient setup remains fundamentally manual and subject to procedural variability. The placement of skin tattoos, the alignment of immobilization devices, and the interpretation of imaging data are all dependent on human judgment and attention, which can be compromised by fatigue, time pressure, or unfamiliarity with a particular patient’s history. In cases where a patient has undergone multiple courses of radiotherapy, the presence of multiple tattoos or inconsistent immobilization practices can further complicate setup accuracy. Additionally, when treatment is delivered on a different linear accelerator than originally planned—due to machine downtime or scheduling constraints—couch parameters may be manually overridden without adequate double-checking, leading to substantial positional discrepancies. These scenarios are not rare; they occur under unusual or high-stress conditions where standard safety checks are bypassed or overlooked, precisely when the risk of error is highest.

Current safety protocols, including pre-treatment imaging, physics checks, and independent verification systems, are designed primarily to ensure accuracy under routine conditions. However, they are often insufficient to detect errors that arise from non-routine circumstances, such as the accidental movement of the couch after CBCT imaging, the misplacement of an indexing bar, or the incorrect selection of a treatment beam with mismatched couch parameters. These errors frequently go undetected until after treatment has been delivered, or they are only caught through post-treatment review, by which time the damage may already be done. There is a critical unmet need for a patient safety system that is fully automated, universally applicable across all treatment sites, independent of the treatment machine or imaging modality, and capable of providing continuous, real-time verification without adding significant burden to the clinical workflow. Such a system must be capable of detecting gross positioning errors exceeding 1 cm, which are most likely to result in severe clinical consequences, while minimizing false alarms caused by physiological motion or minor setup variations. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel, streamlined, and robust solution that leverages passive optical tracking of a single infrared reflective marker, synchronized with the treatment record and verify system, to deliver an independent, radiation-free, and fully automated layer of positional verification that operates seamlessly in the background of every treatment session.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is a fully automated patient safety system designed to prevent gross setup errors in external beam radiotherapy by providing continuous, real-time, and independent verification of patient position relative to the planned isocenter. The system operates without the use of ionizing radiation and is functionally independent of the treatment machine, couch design, immobilization device, or internal anatomy of the patient. It consists of a pair of high-resolution charge-coupled device (CCD) cameras mounted on the ceiling of the treatment room, a single infrared reflective marker affixed to either the patient’s skin or, more preferably, to a patient-specific immobilization device, and a proprietary software platform that communicates with the treatment record and verify system to automatically retrieve and synchronize treatment-specific reference data. Upon the selection of a treatment beam in the record and verify system, the software automatically initiates tracking of the infrared reflective marker, calculates its three-dimensional position in the room’s coordinate system using triangulation, and compares it in real time to the pre-established reference position derived from the treatment plan. Any deviation exceeding a clinically defined threshold triggers an immediate visual and audible alarm on monitors located both in the treatment room and in the control area, allowing the therapist to halt treatment and correct the error before beam delivery begins.

The infrared reflective marker is designed as a flat, disposable, low-profile device that is easily affixed using double-sided adhesive tape and is optimized for visibility under the camera’s field of view. For patients treated with immobilization devices such as aquaplastic face masks or custom body molds, the marker is permanently mounted on the device during the simulation phase, replacing a computed tomography (CT) contrast marker that was used for initial localization. This eliminates the need for daily skin tattoo identification and marker placement, thereby streamlining the workflow and minimizing the potential for human error. For patients without immobilization devices, such as those receiving palliative treatments or those with abdominal-pelvic disease where immobilization is not feasible, the marker is temporarily placed on the skin over a pre-existing tattoo during the first treatment session, and then permanently transferred to the immobilization device if one is subsequently fabricated. The system’s software automatically associates each treatment beam with its corresponding reference position based on the beam label retrieved from the record and verify system, ensuring that no manual input is required from the therapist at any point during the treatment process.

A key innovation of the invention is its seamless integration with the existing clinical workflow through direct communication with the record and verify system via a standardized file transfer protocol. The treatment plan, including the coordinates of the treatment isocenter and the reference marker position, is automatically exported from the treatment planning system and imported into the patient safety system’s database without manual intervention. The system further incorporates a calibration procedure that transforms the native coordinate system of the cameras into the absolute room coordinate system using a calibration jig with five known infrared markers, whose positions are determined via CT imaging and solved using a singular value decomposition algorithm to compute the optimal rotation and translation matrices. This calibration ensures that the system maintains submillimeter positional accuracy over time and is unaffected by changes in the treatment room layout or equipment configuration.

The system is further equipped with a daily quality assurance protocol that utilizes a dedicated device with a permanently mounted infrared marker, aligned to the room isocenter using the same CBCT system employed for patient setup. This allows for the automated verification of system stability and calibration integrity on a daily basis, without requiring additional personnel time or specialized equipment beyond what is already in routine use. The system’s accuracy has been validated through phantom studies, long-term stability testing, and retrospective analysis of clinical data from hundreds of treatment sessions across multiple anatomical sites. Results demonstrate that the system can detect couch shifts as small as 0.5 mm with a maximum observed deviation of less than 2.0 mm under static conditions and less than 3.0 mm under noncoplanar couch rotations. In clinical practice, the system has consistently detected gross setup errors exceeding 1 cm—including misaligned indexing bars, incorrect couch parameter overrides, and post-imaging couch displacements—that would have otherwise gone undetected until after treatment delivery.

The invention is not intended to replace existing IGRT systems but to serve as an independent, fail-safe layer of verification that operates in parallel with them. It does not rely on image fusion, anatomical registration, or internal landmarks, making it universally applicable regardless of the patient’s anatomy, the treatment technique, or the machine configuration. Its fully automated workflow ensures that no additional steps are required by therapists during routine treatment, and its passive, non-ionizing nature eliminates any additional radiation exposure to the patient. By providing continuous, real-time, and independent confirmation that the patient’s position matches the planned position, the system effectively mitigates the most dangerous and insidious sources of geometric miss in radiotherapy, thereby significantly enhancing patient safety and reducing the risk of catastrophic treatment errors.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The patient safety system described herein is a comprehensive, automated, and clinically integrated solution for the continuous, real-time verification of patient positioning during external beam radiotherapy. It is designed to operate independently of the treatment machine, the imaging modality, the couch design, the immobilization device, and the anatomical region being treated. The system’s architecture is composed of three primary functional components: an optical tracking subsystem, a data synchronization and automation subsystem, and a calibration and quality assurance subsystem. Each component is engineered to work in seamless concert with existing clinical workflows, ensuring that the system enhances safety without introducing complexity, delay, or additional burden to the clinical staff.

The optical tracking subsystem consists of a pair of high-resolution, near-infrared charge-coupled device (CCD) cameras mounted on the ceiling of the radiotherapy treatment room. These cameras are positioned at opposing angles relative to the isocenter of the treatment machine, ensuring that the infrared reflective marker affixed to the patient or immobilization device remains within the field of view of at least one camera regardless of the gantry angle or couch orientation. The cameras are connected via a proprietary data cable and a standard serial communication interface to an internal processing unit housed within the treatment room. This unit continuously captures the two-dimensional image coordinates of the infrared reflective marker at a rate of approximately fifteen frames per second. Through a process of triangulation, the system calculates the three-dimensional spatial coordinates of the marker in the native coordinate system of the cameras. The triangulation algorithm is implemented in real time and is robust to minor occlusions, ambient lighting variations, and small movements of the marker that may occur due to respiration or patient motion. The system is capable of tracking up to fifty infrared markers simultaneously, but in its intended clinical configuration, only a single marker is used per patient to simplify workflow, reduce cost, and eliminate the potential for marker misidentification.

The infrared reflective marker is a custom-designed, flat-surface, disposable device fabricated from a round, double-sided adhesive tape measuring six millimeters in diameter and one millimeter in thickness. One side of the tape is coated with a high-reflectivity infrared material, while the other side is protected by a peelable backing that allows for easy and reproducible placement on either the patient’s skin or an immobilization device. The marker is intentionally designed to be low-profile and non-intrusive, minimizing the risk of dislodgement during patient movement or interaction with the treatment couch. Its flat geometry ensures that it can be mounted flush against curved surfaces, such as the chin region of a face mask or the anterior abdominal wall of a body mold, while still maintaining sufficient reflectivity for reliable detection by the cameras. The marker’s small size and passive nature make it invisible to the patient and unobtrusive to the clinical environment, and its disposability ensures that no cross-contamination occurs between patients. In cases where the marker is affixed to a patient’s skin, it is placed over a pre-existing skin tattoo that was created during the simulation phase to mark the location of a computed tomography (CT) contrast marker. In cases where the patient is immobilized using a custom device, the marker is permanently mounted on the device during the simulation phase, replacing the CT marker after the scan is completed. This approach eliminates the need for daily tattoo identification and marker placement, thereby removing a significant source of potential human error and streamlining the workflow for both the therapist and the patient.

The data synchronization and automation subsystem is the cornerstone of the system’s clinical utility and its ability to operate without manual intervention. The system is designed to integrate directly with the treatment record and verify (R&V) system used in the radiotherapy department, which is responsible for managing patient treatment schedules, beam delivery parameters, and machine configuration settings. When a treatment beam is selected at the treatment console, the R&V system generates an external file containing the patient’s identification number and the label of the beam being loaded. This file is automatically transmitted via a secure network connection to the patient safety system’s software platform, which parses the beam label and retrieves the corresponding reference position for the infrared reflective marker from its internal database. The reference position is derived from the treatment planning system (TPS), where the coordinates of the treatment isocenter and the position of the CT marker (later replaced by the infrared marker) are recorded during the simulation phase. The TPS exports this data in a standardized radiation treatment plan (RTP) format to a designated network folder, where the patient safety system’s import module automatically retrieves and associates the marker position with the appropriate treatment site and beam label. This entire process is fully automated and requires no manual input from the therapist. Once the beam label is received, the system immediately begins tracking the infrared marker and continuously compares its real-time position to the reference position. If the deviation exceeds a clinically defined threshold—typically set at 5 millimeters for routine treatments and 10 millimeters for patients with significant respiratory motion—an alarm is triggered on both the treatment room monitor and the control room monitor, alerting the therapist to halt treatment and investigate the discrepancy.

The system is further designed to handle noncoplanar treatment geometries, which are common in stereotactic treatments and certain complex pelvic or thoracic cases. In such scenarios, the couch is rotated to a non-zero angle to achieve the desired beam orientation. The system accounts for this rotation by applying a mathematical transformation to the reference position of the infrared marker. Specifically, the vector connecting the treatment isocenter to the marker is rotated around the vertical axis of the treatment couch by the same angle and in the same direction as the couch rotation. This transformation is computed in real time using a rotation matrix derived from the couch’s angular position as reported by the R&V system. The system does not require the therapist to manually input the couch angle or select a different reference position for each beam; all transformations are performed automatically based on the beam’s metadata. This capability ensures that the system remains accurate and reliable regardless of the treatment geometry, and it eliminates a common source of error in other optical tracking systems that require manual configuration of couch angles for each beam.

The calibration and quality assurance subsystem ensures the long-term accuracy and reliability of the system. A calibration jig consisting of five commercially available infrared reflective markers is used to establish the relationship between the native coordinate system of the cameras and the absolute room coordinate system. The jig is placed on the treatment couch during the initial system installation, and its position is aligned with the imaging isocenter using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT). The coordinates of the five markers on the jig, as determined from a prior CT scan of the jig, are stored in a reference matrix. The same five markers are then imaged by the cameras, and their coordinates are recorded in a second matrix. Using a singular value decomposition (SVD) algorithm, the system computes the optimal rotation matrix and translation vector that minimizes the difference between the two sets of marker coordinates. This mathematical solution, derived from established methods in computer vision and robotics, provides a robust and repeatable transformation that maps the camera’s native coordinates to the room’s absolute coordinates with submillimeter precision. The calibration is performed only once during system installation and does not need to be repeated unless the cameras are physically relocated or the treatment room is significantly modified. The system’s long-term stability is verified on a weekly basis using the same calibration jig, which is aligned to the isocenter with CBCT and tracked by the system. The positional variation observed over time is recorded and analyzed to ensure that the system remains within acceptable tolerance limits. Any deviation exceeding 2 millimeters triggers an automatic alert to the physics team, prompting a recalibration.

In addition to the weekly calibration check, the system incorporates a daily quality assurance (QA) procedure that is fully integrated with the existing daily QA protocol used in the department. A dedicated QA device, identical in form to the daily QA3 device used for machine output and mechanical accuracy checks, is fitted with a permanently mounted infrared reflective marker. This device is aligned to the room isocenter using the same CBCT system used for patient setup, and its reference position is stored in the system’s database under a dedicated “daily QA beam” label. Each morning, when the daily QA beam is selected on the treatment console, the system automatically begins tracking the marker on the QA device and reports its positional deviation from the stored reference. This provides a daily, automated verification of the system’s calibration and stability, without requiring any additional time or effort from the clinical staff. The results of the daily QA are logged automatically and archived for audit purposes, ensuring compliance with regulatory and accreditation standards.

The system’s software platform is built on a modular, scalable architecture that allows for seamless integration with any commercial R&V system, regardless of vendor. The data import module is written in a scripting language compatible with the standard file transfer protocol (FTP) used by most treatment planning systems, ensuring that no proprietary interfaces or custom hardware are required. The software runs on a dedicated, secure computer located within the treatment room and is isolated from the hospital’s general network to ensure data privacy and system integrity. The user interface is designed for minimal interaction: it displays the real-time position of the infrared marker in three dimensions, the deviation from the reference position, and the status of the system (e.g., “Tracking,” “Alarm,” “Calibration Required”). The alarm is both visual and audible, with a distinct tone that cannot be mistaken for other treatment room alerts. The alarm persists until the therapist manually acknowledges and resets it, ensuring that no error is overlooked. The system also includes a comprehensive audit trail that logs every beam selection, every positional reading, every alarm event, and every calibration performed, providing a complete and tamper-proof record of system activity for quality assurance and incident investigation purposes.

The clinical workflow of the system is designed to be as frictionless as possible. For patients treated with immobilization devices such as aquaplastic face masks or custom body molds, the workflow begins during the simulation phase. A CT contrast marker is placed on the immobilization device at a location that is visible in the CT scan and will remain visible to the cameras during treatment. After the scan is completed, the CT marker is removed, and the infrared reflective marker is permanently affixed to the same location on the device. The coordinates of the marker relative to the treatment isocenter are recorded in the TPS and automatically exported to the patient safety system’s database. On the day of treatment, the therapist simply places the immobilization device on the patient, and the system begins tracking the marker the moment the first beam is selected. No tattoo identification, no marker placement, and no manual data entry are required. For patients without immobilization devices, such as those receiving palliative treatments or those with abdominal-pelvic disease, the CT marker is placed on the patient’s skin during simulation, and a skin tattoo is made to mark its location. During the first treatment session, the therapist locates the tattoo, places the infrared marker over it, and confirms its position using the system. The system then prompts the therapist to select a suitable location on the patient’s immobilization device (if one is to be fabricated) or to confirm that the marker will remain on the skin for subsequent sessions. If a device is fabricated, the marker is transferred to it, and the new position is recorded as the reference for all future treatments. From that point forward, the workflow becomes fully automated, identical to that of patients with immobilization devices.

The system’s accuracy has been rigorously validated through a series of phantom studies, long-term stability tests, and clinical evaluations. In phantom studies using an anthropomorphic head phantom, the system demonstrated a mean positional deviation of less than 1.5 millimeters across all three axes, with a maximum deviation of 2.3 millimeters. When couch shifts of up to 10 centimeters were introduced along each axis, the system accurately detected the shift with a maximum error of 1.5 millimeters. Even under noncoplanar conditions, with a 60-degree couch rotation, the maximum deviation remained below 2.8 millimeters. In clinical evaluations involving over 200 treatment sessions across multiple anatomical sites, the system consistently detected gross setup errors exceeding 1 centimeter that would have otherwise gone undetected. These included cases of misaligned indexing bars, incorrect couch parameter overrides, and post-imaging couch displacements. In three documented near-miss incidents, the system prevented the delivery of treatment to an incorrect anatomical site by triggering an alarm before beam delivery, thereby averting potentially catastrophic consequences. The system’s false alarm rate was found to be negligible, with no clinically significant alarms triggered by normal physiological motion, breathing, or minor setup variations. For patients with significant respiratory motion, the system’s action level was adjusted to 15 millimeters along the longitudinal axis, which effectively eliminated false alarms while preserving the ability to detect gross errors.

The system’s independence from the treatment machine, imaging modality, and internal anatomy is its most significant clinical advantage. Unlike CBCT, which relies on image fusion and anatomical registration, the system tracks an external reference point that is fixed relative to the patient’s surface. This means that it is unaffected by changes in patient anatomy, such as weight loss, tumor shrinkage, or organ motion. Unlike optical surface tracking systems that require multiple markers and are limited to specific anatomical regions, the system requires only a single marker and is applicable to all treatment sites. Unlike systems that require manual configuration of couch angles or patient selection, the system is fully automated and integrated with the R&V system, eliminating the potential for human error in data entry. The system does not require any special training beyond basic familiarity with the treatment console, and it imposes no additional time burden on the clinical staff. In fact, in clinical practice, therapists report increased confidence in patient setup and reduced anxiety about potential errors, particularly during unusual or high-stress situations such as machine changes, emergency treatments, or the use of nonstandard immobilization techniques.

The system’s design also addresses the most common sources of human error in radiotherapy. It eliminates the need for daily tattoo identification, which is a frequent source of misplacement, particularly in patients with multiple tattoos from prior treatments. It prevents the accidental override of couch parameters by requiring that any positional deviation exceeding the threshold trigger an alarm, regardless of whether the override was intentional or inadvertent. It detects errors that occur after imaging, such as the accidental movement of the couch during a clearance check or the misalignment of a treatment couch due to a mechanical fault. It is immune to the anatomical similarities that can lead to misregistration of spinal or pelvic structures in CBCT or orthogonal imaging. And it provides an independent verification that cannot be bypassed by workflow shortcuts or procedural deviations. In this way, the system does not merely supplement existing safety measures—it creates a new, fail-safe layer of protection that operates continuously and automatically, ensuring that no treatment is delivered unless the patient’s position has been independently and reliably confirmed.

In summary, the patient safety system described herein represents a paradigm shift in the approach to patient positioning verification in radiotherapy. It is not a tool for guiding setup, nor is it a replacement for IGRT. Rather, it is a fail-safe mechanism that ensures that the patient’s position is correct before treatment begins, regardless of how the setup was performed or what errors may have occurred during the process. Its fully automated workflow, universal applicability, independence from anatomy and machine configuration, and seamless integration with existing clinical systems make it uniquely suited for deployment in busy, high-volume radiotherapy departments. By providing continuous, real-time, and independent verification of patient position, the system significantly reduces the risk of geometric miss and ensures that radiotherapy is delivered with the highest possible degree of safety and precision.
