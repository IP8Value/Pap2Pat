# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Breast cancer remains one of the most prevalent malignancies affecting women globally, with ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) representing a significant proportion of screen-detected lesions. DCIS is characterized by the proliferation of malignant epithelial cells confined within the mammary ductal lumen, without invasion into the surrounding stromal tissue. Although classified as a non-invasive precursor lesion, DCIS carries a substantial risk of progression to invasive breast cancer, with longitudinal studies indicating that between 20% and 53% of untreated cases will develop invasive disease over a period exceeding ten years. This unpredictable biological behavior has led to a clinical dilemma: the current standard of care involves aggressive local intervention—typically surgical excision, often accompanied by adjuvant radiotherapy and, in select cases, endocrine therapy—in an effort to prevent progression, despite the fact that a large fraction of these lesions may never progress beyond their in situ state. The consequence of this conservative, one-size-fits-all approach is widespread overtreatment, exposing many patients to the physical, psychological, and financial burdens of therapies that may have been unnecessary. Conversely, under-treatment of high-risk lesions can lead to preventable recurrences, including those that manifest as invasive carcinoma, which carry significantly worse prognoses.

Current clinical tools for risk stratification in DCIS are largely based on histopathological features such as tumor grade, nuclear atypia, presence of comedo necrosis, tumor size, and resection margin width. The Van Nuys Prognostic Index (VNPI) and the Memorial Sloan Kettering DCIS nomogram represent two of the most widely adopted models, integrating these parameters into composite scoring systems intended to estimate recurrence risk. However, these tools have demonstrated inconsistent reproducibility across diverse patient populations, limited predictive power in multivariate analyses, and an inability to account for the underlying molecular and cellular heterogeneity that drives tumor behavior. Furthermore, they fail to capture dynamic, subcellular alterations that occur early in the neoplastic cascade, relying instead on static, macroscopic observations that may not reflect the true biological potential of the lesion. The advent of molecular assays such as the Oncotype DX Breast DCIS Score has introduced a genomic dimension to risk assessment, but its applicability is constrained by narrow validation cohorts, limited generalizability across histological subtypes, and an overreliance on proliferation-related gene expression signatures that may not fully encapsulate the spectrum of pathogenic mechanisms driving recurrence.

A growing body of evidence implicates centrosome amplification as a fundamental and early event in breast carcinogenesis. Centrosomes, the primary microtubule-organizing centers of the cell, are essential for the fidelity of mitotic spindle formation and chromosome segregation. In normal diploid cells, centrosome number is tightly regulated, with exactly one centrosome present during G1 phase and precisely two following DNA replication in S phase. Cancer cells, however, frequently exhibit centrosome amplification (CA), a condition defined by the presence of more than two centrosomes per cell (numerical amplification) or by the presence of abnormally enlarged centrosomes with increased volume (structural amplification). This phenomenon is not merely a bystander effect of genomic instability but is increasingly recognized as a causal driver of chromosomal instability (CIN), a hallmark of aggressive malignancies. Centrosome amplification has been documented in a wide array of solid tumors, including breast cancer, and is observed even in pre-invasive lesions such as DCIS, suggesting its role in the transition from in situ to invasive disease. The degree of centrosome amplification correlates with higher histological grade, larger tumor size, increased proliferation rates, and poorer clinical outcomes in invasive breast cancer. Despite this compelling association, the prognostic utility of centrosome amplification in DCIS has remained unexplored in clinical practice, primarily due to the absence of a standardized, quantitative, and reproducible methodology capable of measuring both numerical and structural aberrations in routine formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue specimens.

Traditional methods for assessing centrosome number and morphology have relied on subjective, semi-quantitative visual scoring under conventional fluorescence microscopy, which suffers from poor resolution, limited depth perception, and high inter-observer variability. These approaches are ill-suited to accurately capture the three-dimensional architecture of centrosomes, particularly when distinguishing between tightly clustered supernumerary centrosomes and structurally enlarged, single centrosomes. Moreover, prior studies have failed to differentiate between the contributions of frequency (i.e., the proportion of cells exhibiting amplification) and severity (i.e., the magnitude of deviation from normal centrosome number or volume) to overall risk. Without a robust, algorithm-driven framework to quantify these distinct phenotypes, it has been impossible to determine whether numerical amplification, structural amplification, or their combination constitutes the most biologically and clinically relevant metric. Consequently, despite the clear biological plausibility and preliminary evidence linking centrosome amplification to DCIS progression, no clinically validated biomarker based on centrosomal phenotyping has emerged to guide therapeutic decision-making.

The need for a more precise, objective, and mechanistically grounded prognostic tool in DCIS is therefore urgent. The development of such a tool would not only address the critical limitations of existing clinicopathological models but would also enable a paradigm shift from population-based risk estimation to individualized, biology-driven risk stratification. Such a method must be applicable to archived clinical specimens, compatible with standard histopathological workflows, and capable of generating a continuous, quantifiable score that reflects the cumulative burden of centrosomal dysfunction. It must further demonstrate superior predictive performance compared to established indices and provide actionable insights that can distinguish patients who may safely avoid adjuvant radiotherapy from those who stand to benefit significantly from its inclusion. The invention described herein fulfills this unmet clinical need by introducing a novel, algorithm-based centrosomal phenotyping platform that quantifies both numerical and structural centrosome amplification in DCIS tissue, computes a composite Centrosome Amplification Score (CAS), and demonstrates, across two independent, well-characterized clinical cohorts, its ability to stratify patients with unprecedented accuracy and prognostic power.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a novel, algorithm-driven method for predicting the risk of local recurrence in patients diagnosed with ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) of the breast through the quantitative assessment of centrosome amplification in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue specimens. This method introduces a composite biomarker known as the Centrosome Amplification Score (CAS), which integrates two distinct phenotypic dimensions of centrosomal aberration—numerical amplification and structural amplification—into a single, continuous, and clinically interpretable metric. The invention provides a systematic, reproducible, and objective framework for identifying patients at high risk of developing ipsilateral local recurrence, whether as recurrent DCIS or as invasive breast cancer, following breast-conserving surgery or mastectomy, thereby enabling more precise and individualized therapeutic decision-making.

The method comprises the immunofluorescent staining of tissue sections with an antibody targeting γ-tubulin, a core component of the pericentriolar material that marks centrosomes, in combination with a nuclear counterstain such as Hoechst. High-resolution three-dimensional confocal imaging is performed using standardized acquisition parameters across all samples to ensure consistency and minimize technical variability. The resulting image stacks are processed using specialized 3D volume rendering software to delineate and measure the volume of each centrosome associated with individual nuclei. Centrosomes are then categorized into two mutually exclusive classes: individually distinguishable centrosomes (iCTRs), which exhibit volumes within the physiologically normal range established from adjacent non-neoplastic breast tissue and reduction mammoplasty specimens, and megacentrosomes (mCTRs), which are defined as centrosomes whose volume exceeds the upper limit of this normal range, thereby representing structurally amplified entities. The number and volume of iCTRs and mCTRs are recorded for each nucleus within multiple regions of interest sampled across the tumor area.

An algorithm is employed to compute two sub-scores: CASi, which quantifies numerical amplification based on the frequency and severity of cells containing more than two iCTRs, and CASm, which quantifies structural amplification based on the frequency and severity of mCTRs. The frequency component of CASi reflects the proportion of tumor cells exhibiting numerical amplification, while the severity component measures the average excess number of iCTRs per cell beyond the baseline of two. Similarly, the frequency component of CASm reflects the percentage of cells containing at least one mCTR, and the severity component is derived from the z-scored deviation of each mCTR’s volume from the upper limit of normal, weighted by the number of mCTRs per nucleus and normalized by a scaling factor. The two sub-scores are then combined into a composite Centrosome Amplification Score (CAStotal), calculated as the sum of CASi and CASm, with each component weighted to contribute equally to the final metric. This score is continuous and can be applied uniformly across all tissue samples, regardless of histological grade, tumor size, or other clinicopathological variables.

The invention further includes the determination of a clinically validated threshold value for CAStotal, derived from a discovery cohort of DCIS patients with long-term follow-up, which optimally discriminates between patients at high and low risk of local recurrence within a ten-year period. This threshold is then applied prospectively to an independent validation cohort, demonstrating consistent and statistically significant stratification of recurrence risk. Patients classified as high-risk by CAStotal exhibit markedly shorter recurrence-free survival compared to those classified as low-risk, with hazard ratios substantially exceeding those of traditional prognostic indicators such as the Van Nuys Prognostic Index, tumor grade, comedo necrosis, and age. Importantly, CAStotal retains its independent prognostic significance in multivariable analyses that adjust for all known clinical and pathological confounders, including adjuvant radiotherapy, receptor status, and treatment modality. The method further demonstrates superior concordance with clinical outcomes compared to existing models, as measured by Harrell’s concordance index, and exhibits high sensitivity and negative predictive value, making it particularly useful for identifying patients who can be safely managed with surgery alone, thereby avoiding the unnecessary administration of radiotherapy.

The invention also reveals that CAStotal is capable of refining risk stratification within subgroups defined by traditional clinicopathological features. For instance, among patients with comedo necrosis—a known high-risk feature—CAStotal effectively segregates those with exceptionally high recurrence risk from those with low risk, a level of granularity unattainable by any existing clinical tool. Similarly, the score differentiates patients who benefit from adjuvant radiotherapy from those who do not, with high-risk patients treated with surgery alone showing dramatically elevated recurrence rates, whereas high-risk patients receiving radiotherapy exhibit significantly reduced recurrence, suggesting that CAStotal can guide the selective application of radiotherapy to those most likely to benefit. The method is fully compatible with standard histopathology workflows, requires no specialized equipment beyond a confocal microscope and standard immunofluorescence reagents, and can be implemented in any clinical pathology laboratory with appropriate imaging and analytical capabilities. The Centrosome Amplification Score, as defined herein, represents a paradigm-shifting biomarker that translates a fundamental cellular phenotype—centrosome amplification—into a clinically actionable metric, enabling precision oncology in DCIS for the first time.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention described herein is a method for predicting the risk of local recurrence in patients diagnosed with ductal carcinoma in situ of the breast through the quantitative assessment of centrosome amplification in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue specimens. The method is based on the discovery that the extent and nature of centrosomal aberrations in DCIS lesions are strongly predictive of subsequent disease progression, and that these aberrations can be objectively measured, quantified, and integrated into a single, reliable biomarker known as the Centrosome Amplification Score. The invention encompasses a multi-step analytical pipeline that begins with the preparation and staining of clinical tissue sections, proceeds through high-resolution three-dimensional imaging, and culminates in algorithm-based computational analysis that generates a continuous, prognostically informative score. This score enables clinicians to stratify patients into distinct risk categories with high accuracy, facilitating personalized therapeutic decisions regarding the necessity of adjuvant radiotherapy following surgical excision.

The process commences with the selection of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue blocks from patients diagnosed with pure ductal carcinoma in situ, confirmed by dual independent pathological review to exclude any microinvasive or invasive components. The tissue sections are cut at a thickness of five micrometers and mounted on glass slides. Immunofluorescent staining is performed using a monoclonal antibody specific for γ-tubulin, a conserved protein component of the pericentriolar matrix that is essential for centrosome integrity and microtubule nucleation. The antibody is conjugated to a fluorophore emitting in the red spectrum, allowing for clear visualization of centrosomal structures. Concurrently, nuclei are counterstained with Hoechst 33342, a cell-permeable DNA-binding dye that emits in the blue spectrum and enables precise spatial association of centrosomes with individual cell nuclei. The staining protocol is optimized to ensure uniform signal intensity across all samples, with blocking steps, antibody concentrations, and incubation times standardized to minimize batch-to-batch variability. Following staining, slides are coverslipped with an antifade mounting medium to preserve fluorescence integrity during imaging.

The stained tissue sections are imaged using a laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with a 63x oil immersion objective lens and a numerical aperture of 1.4 to achieve optimal spatial resolution. All imaging parameters—including laser power, detector gain, offset, pinhole size, scan speed, and z-step interval—are held constant across the entire cohort to eliminate technical artifacts and ensure comparability. The imaging protocol is designed to capture the full three-dimensional volume of centrosomes, with optical sections acquired at intervals of 0.3 micrometers to ensure complete sampling of the z-axis. At least ten distinct regions of interest are selected per sample, each encompassing a minimum of twenty to thirty tumor nuclei with associated centrosomes. These regions are chosen to represent the most representative areas of the tumor, avoiding areas of necrosis, tissue folding, or excessive autofluorescence. The selection of regions is performed without knowledge of patient outcome or clinicopathological features to prevent observer bias. The resulting image stacks are stored in a standardized format and transferred to a dedicated computational platform for analysis.

The image analysis phase employs specialized 3D volume rendering software capable of segmenting and quantifying fluorescent objects in three dimensions. A background subtraction algorithm is applied uniformly to all images to remove non-specific fluorescence and reduce noise. The volume of each γ-tubulin-positive structure is calculated by integrating pixel intensities across all optical sections within a defined threshold, with boundaries determined by the natural gradient of fluorescence intensity surrounding each centrosome. The volume of each centrosome is then compared against a reference range established from normal breast epithelial cells. This reference range is derived from the analysis of centrosomes in adjacent non-neoplastic tissue from cancer patients and in reduction mammoplasty specimens from disease-free individuals. The mean volume of centrosomes in these normal tissues is found to range between 0.2 and 0.74 cubic micrometers, with the upper limit of 0.74 µm³ serving as the critical threshold for defining structural amplification. Centrosomes falling within this range are classified as individually distinguishable centrosomes (iCTRs), while those exceeding this volume are classified as megacentrosomes (mCTRs). This distinction is critical, as it separates numerical amplification—defined by an increased number of centrosomes—from structural amplification—defined by an increase in the volume of individual centrosomes—thereby enabling the independent quantification of two distinct biological phenomena.

The algorithm proceeds to assign each iCTR and mCTR to its associated nucleus based on spatial proximity, ensuring that centrosomes are counted only once per cell. For each nucleus, the total number of iCTRs is recorded, and if this number exceeds two, the cell is considered to exhibit numerical amplification. The severity of numerical amplification is calculated as the average number of excess iCTRs per cell beyond the physiological baseline of two. The frequency of numerical amplification is calculated as the percentage of tumor cells exhibiting more than two iCTRs. Similarly, for structural amplification, the algorithm records the number of mCTRs per nucleus and calculates the frequency as the percentage of cells containing at least one mCTR. The severity of structural amplification is computed by determining the z-score for each mCTR, defined as the difference between its volume and the upper limit of normal (0.74 µm³), divided by the standard deviation of mCTR volumes observed across the entire cohort. This z-score is then multiplied by the number of mCTRs present in the nucleus, and the resulting values are averaged across all cells to yield a severity score for structural amplification. This approach ensures that both the magnitude of volume deviation and the abundance of structurally abnormal centrosomes are captured in the severity metric.

The two sub-scores, CASi and CASm, are then computed using the following formulas: CASi equals the product of the frequency of numerical amplification and a scaling factor βi, added to the product of the severity of numerical amplification and the same scaling factor. CASm is similarly computed as the sum of the product of the frequency of structural amplification and a scaling factor βm, and the product of the severity of structural amplification and βm. The scaling factors βi and βm are empirically determined to ensure that both numerical and structural components contribute equally to the final composite score, thereby preventing one dimension from dominating the other. The final Centrosome Amplification Score, CAStotal, is obtained by summing CASi and CASm, resulting in a continuous, dimensionless value that reflects the cumulative burden of centrosomal aberration in the tumor. This score is not categorical but rather exists on a spectrum, allowing for fine-grained risk stratification and statistical modeling without arbitrary binning.

To establish a clinically meaningful threshold for risk stratification, the CAStotal values from a discovery cohort of patients with long-term follow-up are analyzed using a series of log-rank tests. Each possible CAStotal value within the cohort is tested as a potential cutoff point to dichotomize patients into high-risk and low-risk groups, and the value that yields the most statistically significant difference in recurrence-free survival is selected as the optimal threshold. This threshold is then applied prospectively to an independent validation cohort, where its ability to stratify recurrence risk is confirmed. The resulting risk categories are validated using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, Cox proportional hazards modeling, and concordance index calculations. The method demonstrates that patients with a CAStotal above the threshold exhibit a hazard ratio for local recurrence that is substantially higher than that conferred by any traditional clinicopathological variable, including tumor grade, comedo necrosis, age, and resection margin width. Importantly, this association remains statistically significant even after adjusting for adjuvant radiotherapy, hormone receptor status, and other confounders in multivariable models, establishing CAStotal as an independent predictor of outcome.

The invention further demonstrates that CAStotal is not merely a surrogate for known prognostic factors but provides additive and superior prognostic information. When compared directly to the Van Nuys Prognostic Index, CAStotal achieves significantly higher hazard ratios and greater concordance with clinical outcomes. Moreover, within subgroups of patients defined by traditional risk factors—such as those with comedo necrosis or high nuclear grade—CAStotal is able to further stratify recurrence risk into distinct subcategories, identifying a subset of patients within these high-risk groups who have a remarkably low probability of recurrence and may therefore be candidates for de-escalated therapy. Conversely, it identifies a subset of patients with low-grade or non-comedo DCIS who exhibit exceptionally high CAStotal values and correspondingly high recurrence risk, suggesting that these individuals may benefit from more aggressive intervention. This ability to refine risk prediction beyond existing clinical parameters represents a major advancement in the management of DCIS.

The method is fully compatible with standard clinical pathology workflows and requires no specialized reagents beyond those commonly available in immunohistochemistry laboratories. The staining protocol can be completed within a single day, and the imaging and analysis pipeline can be automated to process multiple samples simultaneously. The algorithm is implemented in commercially available software with customizable parameters, allowing for adaptation to different imaging platforms. The CAStotal score can be easily integrated into electronic health records and pathology reports, providing clinicians with a quantitative, objective metric to guide treatment decisions. The invention further enables the use of core biopsies for risk assessment, potentially reducing the need for repeat surgeries in cases with close or positive margins. The method has been validated across two independent, geographically and demographically distinct cohorts, demonstrating robustness and generalizability. The biological underpinning of the score—centrosome amplification as a driver of chromosomal instability and tumor evolution—provides a mechanistic rationale for its predictive power, distinguishing it from purely statistical models based on correlative features.

In clinical practice, the implementation of this method would involve the routine acquisition of tissue from DCIS patients undergoing surgical excision. A representative section would be stained for γ-tubulin and Hoechst, imaged using a confocal microscope, and analyzed using the algorithm to generate a CAStotal score. Based on the predefined threshold, the patient would be classified as high-risk or low-risk for local recurrence. Those classified as low-risk could be managed with surgery alone, avoiding the morbidity and cost of adjuvant radiotherapy. Those classified as high-risk would be recommended for the addition of radiotherapy, with the understanding that this intervention significantly reduces their recurrence risk. The score may also be used to guide enrollment in clinical trials evaluating novel therapeutic strategies for high-risk DCIS, such as targeted agents aimed at centrosome regulation or chromosomal stability. The invention thus represents a transformative advance in the precision management of ductal carcinoma in situ, offering a pathway to reduce overtreatment while ensuring that those at greatest risk receive the most appropriate and effective therapy.

### DISCUSSION

The invention described herein represents a paradigm shift in the clinical management of ductal carcinoma in situ by introducing the first quantitative, mechanism-based biomarker capable of predicting the risk of local recurrence with a level of precision and biological plausibility unmatched by any existing clinical tool. The Centrosome Amplification Score, or CAS, is not merely another statistical model derived from correlative clinicopathological variables; it is a direct readout of a fundamental cellular phenotype—centrosome amplification—that has been mechanistically linked to chromosomal instability, tumor evolution, and disease progression in breast cancer. The derivation of CAS from the quantification of two distinct forms of centrosomal aberration—numerical and structural—provides a multidimensional view of a process that is inherently complex and heterogeneous. By integrating these dimensions into a single, continuous score, the method captures the cumulative burden of centrosomal dysfunction in a way that reflects the underlying biology of the tumor, rather than abstracting it into discrete categories based on subjective histological features.

The clinical utility of CAS is demonstrated by its ability to outperform the Van Nuys Prognostic Index, a tool that has been in use for decades and is widely regarded as the standard for DCIS risk stratification. The VNPI, despite its widespread adoption, suffers from inherent limitations: it relies on a combination of tumor size, margin width, nuclear grade, and patient age, all of which are subject to inter-observer variability and lack biological specificity. In contrast, CAS is derived from a direct, objective measurement of a subcellular structure that is under tight molecular control and whose dysregulation is causally linked to mitotic errors and genomic instability. The fact that CAS retains its prognostic power even after adjusting for all components of the VNPI—and in fact, demonstrates a significantly higher hazard ratio—suggests that it captures a dimension of biological risk that is orthogonal to, and more fundamental than, the features upon which traditional indices are based. This is further reinforced by the observation that CAS stratifies patients with equal or greater accuracy even within subgroups defined by VNPI categories, indicating that it provides additive, not redundant, information.

The ability of CAS to refine risk stratification within traditionally high-risk subgroups is perhaps its most compelling clinical feature. For example, patients with comedo necrosis—a feature historically associated with aggressive behavior—are often recommended for radiotherapy regardless of other factors. However, CAS reveals that within this group, a subset of patients exhibits low centrosomal amplification and correspondingly low recurrence risk, suggesting that they may safely forgo radiotherapy. Conversely, patients without comedo necrosis but with high CAS values exhibit recurrence rates comparable to or exceeding those of high-grade, necrotic lesions, indicating that they may benefit from intensified therapy. This level of granularity is unprecedented and has the potential to transform clinical decision-making from a binary, population-based approach to a nuanced, individualized strategy. The same principle applies to tumor grade: high-grade DCIS is routinely treated aggressively, yet CAS shows that not all high-grade lesions are equally dangerous, and some low-grade lesions are biologically more aggressive than their histology suggests. This challenges the assumption that histological grade alone is a sufficient proxy for biological potential and underscores the need for molecular and cellular phenotyping in risk assessment.

The biological rationale for CAS as a predictor of recurrence is deeply rooted in the role of centrosomes in maintaining genomic fidelity. Centrosome amplification leads to multipolar spindles, merotelic attachments, and lagging chromosomes during mitosis, all of which contribute to chromosomal instability. This instability, in turn, fuels intratumoral heterogeneity, allowing subclones with diverse phenotypes—including those capable of invasion—to emerge and expand. The observation that both numerical and structural amplification are predictive of recurrence suggests that the tumor’s capacity to generate genetic diversity is not merely a function of how many extra centrosomes are present, but also how structurally abnormal they are. Structurally amplified centrosomes, or megacentrosomes, may represent a more advanced state of dysfunction, potentially associated with altered microtubule dynamics, impaired cell polarity, and enhanced migratory capacity—all of which are prerequisites for invasion. The fact that these two forms of amplification are independently predictive, yet synergistic when combined, suggests that they may represent complementary mechanisms of tumor progression: numerical amplification drives clonal diversity, while structural amplification enhances invasive potential. This dual mechanism provides a compelling explanation for why CAS is such a powerful predictor of both DCIS and invasive recurrence.

The method’s compatibility with formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue is a critical advantage that ensures its immediate translatability to clinical practice. Unlike genomic assays that require fresh or frozen tissue, or complex sequencing workflows, CAS can be implemented in any pathology laboratory with access to a confocal microscope and standard immunofluorescence equipment. The staining protocol is simple, robust, and does not require specialized reagents or extensive training. The imaging and analysis pipeline, while computationally intensive, can be automated and scaled to process hundreds of samples per week. The algorithm is transparent, reproducible, and based on well-defined physical measurements—volume, number, and spatial distribution—that are not subject to interpretation or bias. This contrasts sharply with subjective grading systems, where inter-observer variability can be as high as 30%, and with genomic signatures that are proprietary, expensive, and limited in applicability. The fact that CAS can be derived from core biopsies further enhances its utility, as it may allow for preoperative risk stratification, guiding the extent of surgical excision and reducing the need for re-excisions due to positive margins.

The implications of this invention extend beyond DCIS. Centrosome amplification is a hallmark of many aggressive cancers, including triple-negative breast cancer, ovarian cancer, and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. The methodology described herein could be readily adapted to these malignancies by establishing tissue-specific normal ranges for centrosome volume and applying the same algorithmic framework. This opens the door to a new class of phenotypic biomarkers that are not based on gene expression or mutation status, but on the structural and organizational integrity of subcellular organelles. Such biomarkers may prove particularly valuable in cancers where genomic heterogeneity is high and mutational signatures are poorly predictive, offering a more direct window into the functional state of the tumor cell.

The limitations of the current study are acknowledged and addressed in the design of the method. The cohorts used for validation were retrospective and lacked uniform treatment with endocrine therapy, which may limit generalizability to populations where hormonal modulation is standard. However, the fact that CAS remained predictive even in the absence of such therapy suggests that its biological underpinnings are independent of estrogen receptor status. The imbalance in high-grade cases between the discovery and validation cohorts was intentional, designed to test the robustness of the score across heterogeneous populations, and the consistent performance across both cohorts confirms its reliability. Future prospective studies will be required to validate the clinical utility of CAS in real-time decision-making, but the statistical power, biological plausibility, and technical feasibility of the method provide a strong foundation for such trials.

In conclusion, the Centrosome Amplification Score represents a transformative innovation in the field of breast oncology. It moves beyond the descriptive, correlative models of the past and introduces a quantitative, mechanistic, and actionable biomarker that reflects the true biological aggressiveness of ductal carcinoma in situ. By enabling clinicians to distinguish between patients who can be safely treated with surgery alone and those who require adjuvant radiotherapy, CAS has the potential to reduce overtreatment, improve quality of life, and ensure that therapeutic resources are directed toward those who stand to benefit the most. This invention does not merely improve upon existing tools—it redefines the paradigm of risk assessment in pre-invasive disease, offering a new standard of care grounded in cellular biology rather than histological appearance.
