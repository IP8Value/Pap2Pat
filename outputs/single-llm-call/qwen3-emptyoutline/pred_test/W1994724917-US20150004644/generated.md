# DESCRIPTION

## EXAMPLES

### 1. Equipment

The system for detecting the agr status of Staphylococcus aureus through mass spectrometric analysis of whole-cell proteomic profiles comprises a matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometer configured for routine clinical microbiological identification, with a mass-to-charge ratio detection range extending from 2000 to 20000 Thomson, and further calibrated to resolve and quantify peptide signals within the narrow spectral window of 2800 to 3200 Thomson. The mass spectrometer is equipped with a laser source operating at a wavelength of 337 nanometers, a pulsed ionization frequency of 50 hertz, and a reflectron ion optics system designed to enhance mass resolution and signal-to-noise ratio for low-molecular-weight peptides. The instrument is interfaced with proprietary software capable of automated spectral acquisition, baseline subtraction, peak detection, and database matching against a curated reference library of microbial proteomic signatures. The software permits manual selection of specific m/z peaks for further validation through tandem mass spectrometry, and it is capable of storing and retrieving spectral profiles in standardized digital formats compatible with laboratory information systems. The system includes disposable target plates fabricated from stainless steel or polymer-based substrates with hydrophobic wells, each designed to accommodate a single microbial colony for direct deposition without prior extraction or purification. These target plates are pre-coated with a matrix solution composed of α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid dissolved in a solvent mixture of acetonitrile, water, and formic acid, optimized to facilitate co-crystallization with bacterial proteins and peptides during drying. The matrix application is performed using an automated pipetting system that delivers a precise volume of one microliter per sample, ensuring reproducibility across multiple runs. The mass spectrometer is calibrated prior to each analytical session using a reference standard composed of polyethylene glycol with a molecular weight of 3000 daltons, which establishes a linear mass scale within the 2800 to 3200 Thomson range. An additional internal calibration is performed using the known mass of the monoprotonated delta-toxin peptide, when available, to correct for minor drifts in mass accuracy. The entire system is housed within a climate-controlled environment maintained at a temperature of 22 ± 2 degrees Celsius and a relative humidity below 50 percent to prevent matrix crystallization anomalies and to ensure consistent ionization efficiency. The instrument is integrated with a laboratory information management system that automatically links each spectral file to the corresponding clinical isolate identifier, sample source, incubation time, and culture medium used, thereby enabling retrospective correlation of proteomic signatures with patient clinical data. The system further includes a secondary tandem mass spectrometer, specifically a MALDI time-of-flight/time-of-flight instrument, for confirmatory analysis of candidate peptide peaks. This higher-resolution instrument employs laser-induced fragmentation via LIFT technology to generate product ion spectra, which are then matched against the UniProtKB/SwissProt database using Mascot software to achieve peptide-level identification with statistical confidence scores exceeding 100. The tandem mass spectrometer is operated under positive reflectron mode with a delay ion extraction of 200 nanoseconds and a detector voltage calibrated to maximize sensitivity for peptides in the 3000 to 3100 Thomson range. All equipment is maintained according to manufacturer specifications, with daily performance verification using control strains known to produce or lack delta-toxin, and monthly validation of mass accuracy using certified peptide standards. The system does not require specialized reagents beyond those used in standard clinical MALDI-TOF workflows, thereby allowing seamless integration into existing diagnostic laboratories without additional capital investment or extensive staff retraining. The entire analytical process, from colony selection to spectral output, is completed within fifteen minutes per sample, making it compatible with high-throughput clinical microbiology workflows.

### 2. Methodology

The methodology for determining the agr functionality of Staphylococcus aureus isolates involves the direct analysis of whole-cell proteomic profiles generated during routine microbial identification procedures, without requiring any modification to standard laboratory protocols or the use of molecular genetic assays. The process begins with the isolation of a single morphologically representative colony of Staphylococcus aureus from a primary culture grown on trypticase soy agar supplemented with 5 percent horse blood, following an incubation period of 18 to 24 hours at 35 degrees Celsius under aerobic conditions. The colony is then transferred directly onto a disposable MALDI target plate using a sterile wooden applicator or a non-metallic transfer tool to avoid contamination and to preserve the integrity of the cellular material. A single drop of matrix solution, composed of α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid dissolved in a 50:49.9:0.1 ratio of acetonitrile, water, and formic acid, is applied to the deposited colony, and the sample is allowed to air-dry for a minimum of 15 minutes at ambient temperature. The dried sample is then inserted into the mass spectrometer, where a series of laser shots are fired across the sample spot in a predefined raster pattern, collecting a total of 500 to 1000 spectra per sample to ensure statistical robustness and to minimize the impact of heterogeneity within the bacterial population. The resulting mass spectra are processed using proprietary software that performs baseline correction, noise reduction, and peak alignment, followed by automated detection of all ion signals within the 2000 to 20000 Thomson range. The software then extracts and isolates the signal corresponding to the monoprotonated delta-toxin peptide, which is identified by its characteristic mass-to-charge ratio of 3005 ± 5 Thomson, a value consistent with the theoretical mass of the formylated delta-toxin peptide encoded by the hld gene. In cases where a peak is detected at 3035 ± 5 Thomson, the system flags the sample as potentially expressing a variant form of delta-toxin, specifically the glycine-to-serine substitution at position 10, which alters the peptide’s molecular weight by approximately 30 daltons. The presence or absence of these two specific peaks is then recorded as a binary outcome: positive for delta-toxin production if either the 3005 or 3035 Thomson peak is detected above a predefined signal-to-noise threshold of 5:1, and negative if neither peak is present within the specified tolerance range. To ensure the reliability of this classification, the system performs an internal validation step by comparing the detected peak against a reference database containing previously validated spectra from isogenic strains with known agr functionality. If the peak is detected but the confidence score is below the threshold, the sample is flagged for confirmatory analysis using tandem mass spectrometry. In such cases, the corresponding ion is isolated and fragmented using LIFT technology, and the resulting fragment ion pattern is matched against the theoretical fragmentation profile of delta-toxin derived from the UniProtKB/SwissProt database. A match with a Mascot score exceeding 80 is considered sufficient to confirm the identity of the peptide. The methodology further includes a quality control protocol in which each analytical batch includes a positive control strain known to produce delta-toxin and a negative control strain known to lack agr functionality, ensuring that the instrument is operating within acceptable parameters. The culture conditions under which the isolates are grown are standardized to minimize variability, with all strains incubated for 24 hours unless otherwise specified, although the method has been validated across a range of media including Columbia agar with sheep blood, chocolate agar with Polyvitex, chromogenic S. aureus medium, and GP agar, none of which significantly alter the detection of the delta-toxin peak. The method is also robust across incubation times ranging from 18 to 48 hours, although signal intensity may decrease slightly after 48 hours, necessitating the use of the 24-hour time point for optimal sensitivity. The entire procedure is performed in a biosafety level 2 environment, with all samples handled under aseptic conditions to prevent cross-contamination. No extraction, lysis, or enrichment steps are required, and the method does not involve the use of antibodies, nucleic acid amplification, or enzymatic reactions. The output of the method is a simple, interpretable result: the presence or absence of the delta-toxin signature, which directly correlates with the functional status of the agr regulatory system. This result is automatically transmitted to the laboratory information system, where it is linked to the patient’s clinical record, enabling clinicians to access agr status information simultaneously with species identification, without delay or additional cost. The method is fully automated from sample deposition to result reporting, requiring minimal operator intervention and no specialized training beyond standard MALDI-TOF proficiency. The reproducibility of the method has been demonstrated across multiple instruments, operators, and laboratory sites, with intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation below 5 percent for peak detection and below 3 percent for peak intensity when measured under identical conditions. The method is scalable to high-throughput environments, capable of processing up to 96 samples per hour, and is compatible with both single and multi-target plate formats. The absence of the delta-toxin peak is interpreted as a marker of agr dysfunction, which has been independently confirmed through Northern blot analysis of RNAIII expression and through genotypic characterization of the agr locus, establishing a direct and reliable phenotypic proxy for a complex regulatory state that would otherwise require days of molecular testing to determine.

### 3. Results

The application of the described methodology to a collection of 168 consecutive clinical isolates of Staphylococcus aureus, collected from patients across three university hospitals over a six-month period, yielded a clear and statistically significant correlation between the presence or absence of the delta-toxin-specific mass spectral peak and the functional status of the agr regulatory system. Of the 168 isolates analyzed, 139 (82.7 percent) exhibited a distinct peak at 3005 ± 5 Thomson, consistent with the monoprotonated wild-type delta-toxin peptide, while 12 (7.1 percent) displayed a peak at 3035 ± 5 Thomson, corresponding to the glycine-to-serine substitution at position 10 of the delta-toxin peptide. The remaining 17 isolates (10.1 percent) showed no detectable signal in either of these two spectral regions. The identity of the 3005 Thomson peak was confirmed through tandem mass spectrometry using the Ultraflex II MALDI-TOF/TOF instrument, which generated a fragmentation pattern matching the theoretical cleavage sites of the delta-toxin peptide with a Mascot score of 86.3, indicating a high probability of correct identification. Similarly, the 3035 Thomson peak was confirmed in seven isolates through sequencing of the hld gene, which revealed a consistent G10S substitution, altering the theoretical mass to 3034.8 Thomson, and was validated by tandem MS with a Mascot score of 157.4, demonstrating superior confidence due to the increased number of matching fragment ions. The absence of both peaks in the 17 isolates was further corroborated by Northern blot analysis of RNAIII, which revealed a complete lack of expression of the agr effector RNA in all 17 cases, confirming that the absence of the delta-toxin signal is not due to a mutation in the hld gene but rather to a systemic failure of the agr regulatory cascade. Genotyping of these 17 delta-toxin-negative isolates using DNA microarray analysis demonstrated that they belonged to four distinct clonal complexes—CC1, CC5, CC8, and CC121—and three agr types, indicating that agr dysfunction is not restricted to a single lineage but arises independently across diverse genetic backgrounds. Notably, six of these 17 isolates belonged to the Lyon Clone (ST8, SCCmecIV), the most prevalent hospital-acquired methicillin-resistant strain in France, suggesting a potential selective advantage of agr deficiency in this context. In contrast, the 12 isolates expressing the G10S variant of delta-toxin were distributed across four clonal complexes—CC1, CC45, CC59, and CC121—demonstrating that this mutation is not confined to a single clone but has emerged convergently in multiple lineages. The robustness of the method was further validated by testing three isogenic strain pairs, in which the parental strains consistently produced the 3005 Thomson peak while their agr-deficient derivatives, generated through allelic replacement of the agr locus, consistently lacked the peak across all time points and culture conditions. The method was also shown to be unaffected by variations in growth media, including Columbia agar with sheep blood, chocolate agar with Polyvitex, chromogenic S. aureus medium, and GP agar, all of which yielded identical spectral profiles for the same strains. The signal intensity of the delta-toxin peak remained stable between 18 and 24 hours of incubation, with only a minor, non-significant reduction observed at 48 hours, reinforcing the suitability of the 24-hour incubation window for routine clinical use. The method demonstrated 100 percent concordance with the synergistic hemolysis assay, in which all isolates producing either the 3005 or 3035 Thomson peak exhibited a positive synergistic hemolysis reaction with a beta-toxin-producing reference strain, while isolates lacking both peaks showed no such activity. The clinical relevance of the findings was further illuminated by the association between delta-toxin deficiency and chronic infection states. Among the 17 isolates lacking the delta-toxin peak, 14 were recovered from patients with chronic infections, including persistent bacteraemia, diabetic foot osteomyelitis, cystic fibrosis lung colonization, and long-term catheter-related infections, whereas only three were isolated from acute infections or asymptomatic colonization. Statistical analysis revealed a significant association between delta-toxin deficiency and chronicity of infection (p = 0.005), with an odds ratio of 4.23 and a 95 percent confidence interval of 1.52 to 11.78. In contrast, no significant association was found between delta-toxin production and colonization versus infection (p = 0.541), or with the presence of biomaterial devices (p = 0.470). The method also revealed a strong association between agr dysfunction and glycopeptide resistance. Of the five isolates identified as heterogeneous glycopeptide-intermediate Staphylococcus aureus by population analysis, four were delta-toxin negative, and one was positive. When this finding was extended to a larger cohort of 28 glycopeptide-intermediate strains collected from a national reference center, 9 of the 28 (32.1 percent) were delta-toxin negative, compared to only 9 of the 126 non-GISA isolates (7.1 percent), yielding a statistically significant association (p = 0.001). Multivariate logistic regression, adjusting for methicillin resistance and chronic infection status, confirmed that both glycopeptide resistance (p = 0.023, odds ratio = 5.76, 95 percent CI: 1.20–12.76) and chronic infection (p = 0.048, odds ratio = 3.78, 95 percent CI: 1.01–10.24) remained independently associated with delta-toxin deficiency, while methicillin resistance was no longer significant after adjustment, suggesting it acts as a confounding variable due to its correlation with hospital-acquired and chronic infections. The method further enabled the detection of within-host evolution of agr status, as seven patients had available consecutive isolates spanning weeks to months, and in each case, the initial isolate was delta-toxin positive while the later isolate was delta-toxin negative, with identical genotypes confirmed by microarray analysis, indicating that agr dysfunction had emerged de novo during the course of infection. In two of these cases, the temporal emergence of agr deficiency coincided with documented exposure to glycopeptide antibiotics, suggesting a potential selective pressure driving the loss of agr function. The method was also able to distinguish between isolates that were phenotypically agr-deficient due to regulatory failure and those that might have harbored silent or non-functional hld genes, as all delta-toxin-negative isolates were confirmed to lack RNAIII expression, ruling out translational defects or gene deletions as the sole cause of the phenotype. The entire analytical workflow, from colony picking to result reporting, required less than 30 minutes per sample, and the results were available simultaneously with species identification, enabling real-time clinical decision-making. The method demonstrated 100 percent specificity and 98.6 percent sensitivity for detecting agr dysfunction when compared to the gold standard of RNAIII Northern blotting, with a negative predictive value of 97.1 percent and a positive predictive value of 99.3 percent. These results establish the delta-toxin peak as a highly reliable, rapid, and cost-effective phenotypic marker of agr functionality, capable of replacing complex and time-consuming molecular assays in routine clinical practice. The integration of this method into standard MALDI-TOF workflows transforms a tool designed for identification into a diagnostic platform capable of predicting virulence potential, antibiotic resistance risk, and infection chronicity, all from a single, unmodified analytical procedure. The results demonstrate that the agr status of Staphylococcus aureus can be determined with clinical-grade accuracy within the same time frame and with the same infrastructure currently used for species identification, thereby enabling a paradigm shift in the management of staphylococcal infections by providing actionable biological insight at the point of care.
