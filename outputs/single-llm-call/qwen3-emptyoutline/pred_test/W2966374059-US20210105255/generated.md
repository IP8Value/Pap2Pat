# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to cryptographic systems and authentication protocols designed for secure and efficient verification of digital identities in distributed computing environments, particularly in scenarios involving resource-constrained devices and untrusted servers. More specifically, the invention encompasses a novel class of cryptosystems termed verifiable encryption, which enables the computation of distance metrics between plaintexts directly on their encrypted representations without requiring decryption, thereby preserving confidentiality while permitting authenticated comparison. This class of systems is particularly suited for applications in Internet of Things (IoT) networks, cloud-based authentication services, smart access control systems, and ubiquitous computing environments where computational resources are limited, network latency must be minimized, and the integrity of sensitive biometric or personal data must be maintained without exposing it to intermediate servers. The invention further provides a deterministic authentication protocol leveraging verifiable encryption to enable secure unlocking of local devices via untrusted networked servers, eliminating the need for public key infrastructure, certificate authorities, or interactive challenge-response mechanisms. The method achieves sub-millisecond verification times even for high-dimensional data such as 8192-bit biometric templates, making it uniquely suited for real-time, high-frequency authentication scenarios such as door access, payment terminals, and wearable device unlocking, where traditional cryptographic methods impose prohibitive computational or latency overheads.

## BACKGROUND ART

In contemporary digital ecosystems, the authentication of individuals relies heavily on the secure handling of digital identities—data elements derived from biometrics, passwords, tokens, or certificates that uniquely associate a user with a digital persona. These identities are frequently stored on remote servers, transmitted across public networks, and verified through cryptographic protocols designed to prevent impersonation, eavesdropping, and data leakage. Conventional authentication frameworks, such as those based on public key infrastructure (PKI), rely on interactive zero-knowledge proofs like those of Fiat-Shamir and Schnorr, which require multiple rounds of message exchange, the distribution of long-term secret keys, and the involvement of trusted third parties such as certificate authorities. While these methods provide strong security guarantees under ideal conditions, they introduce significant latency, computational complexity, and infrastructure dependencies that render them unsuitable for deployment on low-power embedded devices, real-time access systems, or environments where network connectivity is intermittent or unreliable.

Moreover, the increasing prevalence of Internet of Things (IoT) devices has amplified the demand for lightweight, scalable, and privacy-preserving authentication mechanisms. Many IoT endpoints, such as smart locks, wearable health monitors, and automated retail kiosks, lack the processing power, memory, or energy budget to support computationally intensive cryptographic operations such as RSA key exchanges, elliptic curve digital signatures, or fully homomorphic encryption. Simultaneously, users expect near-instantaneous response times—often under ten milliseconds—for routine interactions such as unlocking a door or authorizing a payment. Traditional approaches that rely on transmitting plaintext biometrics or hashed credentials to remote servers create unacceptable privacy risks, as the server becomes a single point of compromise capable of reconstructing or misusing sensitive identity data. Even encrypted transmissions, such as those secured by TLS or SSL, expose metadata, timing patterns, and ciphertexts to potential traffic analysis or server-side breaches.

Alternative methods such as searchable encryption and homomorphic encryption have been proposed to enable computation on encrypted data. However, searchable encryption typically permits only keyword matching and requires precomputed indices, rendering it ineffective for continuous or analog data such as facial features, gait patterns, or fingerprint minutiae. Homomorphic encryption, while theoretically capable of supporting arithmetic operations on ciphertexts, remains impractical for real-time applications due to its massive computational overhead, large ciphertext expansion, and prohibitive memory requirements—even in optimized variants such as Paillier or BGV schemes. Furthermore, these systems generally do not support direct distance computation between two encrypted inputs, which is essential for biometric verification where similarity is measured via Hamming distance, Euclidean norm, or other metric functions.

Existing authentication protocols for local device access, such as PIN entry, pattern locks, or fingerprint scanning on smartphones, operate under the assumption of a trusted local environment and do not involve networked verification. These methods are fast and user-friendly but are inherently insecure when applied to shared or remotely managed devices, as the digital identity is stored in plaintext on the device and vulnerable to physical extraction or malware. Conversely, network-based authentication for shared devices—such as enterprise access systems or multi-user smart home hubs—requires the transmission of encrypted credentials to a central server, which then performs the verification. This architecture exposes the system to server-side attacks, man-in-the-middle interception, and dependency on continuous connectivity. No prior system has successfully combined the speed of local authentication with the security of networked verification while eliminating the need for key distribution, public key cryptography, or trusted third parties.

The prior art also fails to recognize a fundamental conceptual gap: the absence of a cryptographic framework that permits two encrypted representations of a digital identity to be combined in a manner that reveals only their relative similarity—without revealing either identity, the key, or any intermediate state. While one-time pad encryption has long been known for its information-theoretic security, its application has been restricted to point-to-point secure communication and has never been extended to enable verifiable distance computation between two independently encrypted instances of the same or similar identity data. Similarly, while block cipher modes such as ECB, CBC, and CFB are widely deployed, they are explicitly designed to prevent any meaningful operation on ciphertexts, rendering them incompatible with distance-based verification. Even the output feedback (OFB) mode, which generates a keystream independent of the plaintext, has not been previously leveraged to construct a verifiable encryption system capable of supporting deterministic authentication without key exchange.

Thus, there exists a critical unmet need for a cryptographic system that simultaneously satisfies the following requirements: (1) enables secure comparison of encrypted digital identities without decrypting them; (2) permits computation of a distance metric between two encrypted representations using only public operations; (3) requires no prior key distribution between the user, device, and server; (4) operates with minimal computational overhead, even on low-power devices; (5) is deterministic rather than probabilistic, eliminating the need for multiple verification rounds; (6) resists plaintext and chosen-ciphertext attacks even when the server is fully compromised; and (7) is compatible with analog or high-dimensional identity data such as biometrics. The present invention fulfills this need through the introduction of verifiable encryption and a novel authentication protocol built upon it, achieving unprecedented performance and security characteristics in distributed authentication environments.

## SUMMARY OF INVENTION

### Technical Problem

The technical problem addressed by the present invention arises from the inherent conflict between the need for secure, networked authentication of digital identities and the constraints imposed by real-world deployment environments. Specifically, existing authentication systems either sacrifice security for speed—by storing or transmitting plaintext credentials on local devices—or sacrifice speed and scalability for security—by relying on complex public key protocols, interactive proofs, or trusted third parties that introduce latency, infrastructure dependency, and single points of failure. This dichotomy renders current solutions inadequate for emerging use cases such as unlocking shared IoT devices via untrusted cloud servers, verifying biometric access across distributed nodes, or enabling secure authentication on resource-constrained embedded systems where milliseconds matter and cryptographic overhead is prohibitive.

A critical limitation of prior art systems is their inability to compute a meaningful similarity metric—such as Hamming distance or Euclidean norm—between two encrypted representations of a digital identity without first decrypting them. This forces systems to either transmit plaintext data to a server for comparison, exposing sensitive information to potential compromise, or to transmit encrypted data and rely on the server to perform decryption, which violates the principle of end-to-end confidentiality. Even in systems that employ homomorphic encryption, the computational cost of evaluating distance functions on ciphertexts remains prohibitively high, often requiring seconds or minutes of processing time, far exceeding the acceptable threshold for user-facing applications. Furthermore, all known interactive authentication protocols, including those based on zero-knowledge proofs, require multiple rounds of communication, synchronized state management, and persistent key storage, making them unsuitable for asynchronous, disconnected, or low-bandwidth environments.

Another unresolved challenge is the vulnerability of authentication systems to server-side compromise. In conventional architectures, the server holds either the plaintext identity, the decryption key, or the verification logic, making it a high-value target for attackers. A breach of the server can lead to mass identity theft, impersonation, or replay attacks. Even if ciphertexts are stored, the server may still be able to perform offline dictionary attacks, statistical inference, or side-channel analysis to reconstruct identity data. Moreover, the requirement for key distribution between users and servers introduces operational complexity, increases the attack surface, and undermines the scalability of the system across millions of devices and users.

The present invention recognizes that the root cause of these problems lies in the absence of a cryptographic primitive that allows two independently encrypted instances of the same identity to be combined in a way that reveals only their relative difference, while preserving the confidentiality of both the original data and the keys used for encryption. No prior system has demonstrated a method by which a server, possessing no knowledge of the plaintext or the encryption keys, can compute a value that, when processed by a trusted local device, yields a verifiable measure of similarity between two identities—without ever exposing any sensitive information to the server. This gap in the state of the art prevents the realization of a truly secure, fast, and scalable authentication paradigm for distributed environments.

### Solution to Problem

The solution to the aforementioned technical problem is provided by a novel cryptographic framework termed verifiable encryption, which comprises a pair of encryption functions operating over distinct but compatible key spaces, such that the distance between two plaintexts can be computed directly on their corresponding ciphertexts through a deterministic, non-interactive operation, without requiring decryption or knowledge of the original keys. Verifiable encryption is defined by a tuple of mathematical components: a plaintext space, a ciphertext space, a key space, two encryption functions, a combining function, and a decoding function. The encryption functions are applied independently to two instances of a digital identity, each using a distinct, ephemeral key. The combining function operates on the resulting ciphertexts to produce a derived ciphertext that encodes the metric difference between the two plaintexts. The decoding function, executed only on a trusted local device, uses the two ephemeral keys to recover the exact value of the distance metric, which is then compared against a predefined threshold to determine authenticity.

This framework is implemented in a novel authentication protocol that eliminates the need for public key infrastructure, certificate authorities, key distribution, or interactive challenge-response sequences. In the registration phase, a user transmits their digital identity to a trusted local device, which generates a unique encryption key, encrypts the identity, and transmits only the resulting ciphertext to an untrusted server for storage. In the verification phase, the user again provides their digital identity to the same trusted device, which generates a new, one-time encryption key, encrypts the input, and sends the new ciphertext to the server. The server computes a combining function on the two stored and transmitted ciphertexts, producing a derived ciphertext that encodes the distance between the registered and presented identities. This derived ciphertext is returned to the trusted device, which applies the decoding function using both the original and the one-time key to recover the exact distance value. If this value falls within an acceptable threshold, authentication is granted.

Crucially, the server never learns the plaintext identity, the encryption keys, or the distance value. The only information it ever handles are ciphertexts, and it performs no decryption, no key storage, and no decision-making. The entire verification logic resides exclusively on the trusted device, which is physically secured and resistant to tampering. The use of ephemeral keys ensures that even if a ciphertext is intercepted or stolen, it cannot be reused, replayed, or linked to any other authentication event. The combining function is constructed such that it is computationally infeasible to reverse-engineer the plaintexts or keys from the derived ciphertext, even with unlimited computational power, provided the underlying encryption scheme is secure.

This solution is instantiated using a one-time pad cryptosystem, wherein the combining function is implemented as a bitwise XOR operation between two ciphertexts, and the decoding function is a second XOR operation that cancels out the ephemeral keys, leaving only the XOR of the two plaintexts—the Hamming distance of which is directly proportional to their similarity. The system is scalable to any bit-length of identity data, from 128 bits to 8192 bits, and achieves consistent processing times across all lengths due to the linear, bitwise nature of the operations involved. The protocol is deterministic, requiring exactly one round of communication between the device and server, and completes in less than one millisecond even for the largest data sizes, making it uniquely suited for real-time, high-frequency authentication scenarios.

### Advantageous Effects

The advantageous effects of the present invention are profound and multifaceted, spanning security, performance, scalability, and operational simplicity. First and foremost, the invention provides a level of security that is unattainable by any prior authentication system: the server, which performs the core computational task of distance derivation, is rendered completely oblivious to the plaintext identities, the encryption keys, and the final decision outcome. Even if the server is fully compromised, an attacker gains access only to ciphertexts that are computationally useless without the corresponding ephemeral keys, which are never transmitted and are destroyed immediately after each authentication event. This eliminates the risk of mass data breaches, credential theft, or server-side impersonation attacks that plague conventional systems.

Second, the invention achieves unprecedented speed and efficiency. All cryptographic operations—encryption, combining, and decoding—are implemented using bitwise XOR and simple arithmetic comparisons, which are among the fastest operations available in modern processors. The system exhibits constant-time complexity regardless of the size of the digital identity, processing 128-bit and 8192-bit templates in identical timeframes of approximately 0.95 microseconds per operation. This performance characteristic is orders of magnitude faster than public key cryptography, homomorphic encryption, or interactive zero-knowledge proofs, which scale poorly with data size and often require milliseconds to seconds per verification. The result is a system capable of supporting hundreds of authentications per second on a single low-power microcontroller, enabling deployment on battery-operated IoT devices, embedded access control systems, and wearable technology without requiring additional hardware acceleration.

Third, the invention eliminates the need for any form of key distribution, certificate authority, or trusted third party. Registration and verification occur entirely through the interaction of the user, the trusted device, and the untrusted server, with no prior coordination or shared secrets required. This dramatically reduces deployment complexity, lowers operational costs, and removes the single point of failure associated with centralized key management systems. The use of ephemeral keys ensures perfect forward secrecy: even if a user’s identity is later compromised, past authentication records cannot be retroactively verified or forged.

Fourth, the invention is inherently compatible with analog and high-dimensional identity data such as biometrics. By employing a threshold-based comparison on the recovered distance metric, the system can tolerate minor variations in fingerprint scans, facial recognition vectors, or voiceprints without compromising security. This flexibility allows the same protocol to be applied across diverse authentication modalities, from PIN codes to iris patterns, without modification to the underlying cryptographic structure.

Fifth, the deterministic nature of the protocol eliminates the probabilistic uncertainty inherent in interactive proofs, ensuring consistent and predictable behavior under all conditions. There is no need for multiple rounds, retransmissions, or fallback mechanisms, which improves reliability in low-bandwidth or high-latency networks. The system operates seamlessly even in offline or intermittent connectivity scenarios, as the server’s role is purely passive—storing and returning ciphertexts without requiring real-time processing or state synchronization.

Finally, the invention provides a clear and enforceable security boundary: the trusted device is the sole entity capable of making the authentication decision, and it is physically isolated from the network. This architecture aligns with modern security best practices, such as the principle of least privilege and zero-trust design, by ensuring that sensitive operations are never delegated to untrusted entities. The server is treated as a mere storage and relay mechanism, incapable of influencing the outcome of authentication, thereby minimizing its attack surface and liability. These combined advantages render the present invention uniquely suited for the next generation of secure, scalable, and user-centric authentication systems.

## DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

### Outline of Exemplary Embodiments of Present Disclosure

The present invention is embodied in a cryptographic authentication system comprising a trusted local device, an untrusted server, and a user interface, wherein digital identities are verified through a sequence of operations based on verifiable encryption. In a first exemplary embodiment, the system is deployed in a smart door lock system, where a resident’s biometric fingerprint is registered once and subsequently used to unlock the door via a mobile application or embedded keypad. During registration, the fingerprint template is captured by the device, encrypted using a randomly generated one-time pad key, and transmitted to a cloud server for storage. The device retains the key locally and discards the plaintext. During verification, the user presents their fingerprint again, the device generates a new one-time key, encrypts the new template, and sends it to the server. The server computes the XOR of the two ciphertexts and returns the result to the device. The device then XORs the result with the original key and the new key to recover the Hamming distance between the two templates. If the distance is below a threshold of 60 bits, the lock is released. The entire process completes in under one millisecond, and the server never learns the fingerprint or the decision.

In a second exemplary embodiment, the system is integrated into a multi-user smart home hub, such as a voice-controlled assistant or centralized access controller, that serves multiple residents. Each user registers their digital identity—whether a voiceprint, facial recognition vector, or gesture sequence—individually through their personal smartphone, which acts as the trusted device. The hub, which is owned by the property manager and connected to the internet, stores only encrypted representations of these identities. When a user attempts to interact with the hub, their smartphone encrypts the presented identity and sends it to the hub, which forwards it to the server. The server computes the distance between the presented and registered ciphertexts and returns the result. The smartphone decodes the distance and sends an authorization signal to the hub only if the match is valid. The hub has no knowledge of the identities, the keys, or the outcome, and cannot be used to impersonate any user.

In a third exemplary embodiment, the system is employed in a checkout-free retail environment, where customers enter the store using facial recognition. A camera at the entrance captures the customer’s face, encrypts it using a key generated by a secure enclave on their smartphone, and transmits the ciphertext to a backend server. The server compares this ciphertext with the registered template for the customer’s account and returns the derived distance. The smartphone decodes the distance and, if it falls within an acceptable similarity range, sends a signal to the store’s payment system to initiate a transaction. The store’s servers never store, process, or learn the customer’s facial data, ensuring compliance with privacy regulations such as GDPR and CCPA.

In a fourth exemplary embodiment, the system is adapted for use in healthcare monitoring devices, such as wearable heart rate sensors or glucose monitors, which transmit encrypted health data to a cloud server for analysis. The device uses verifiable encryption to authenticate the user before transmitting sensitive medical records. The server stores encrypted templates of the user’s physiological patterns, and during each transmission, the device encrypts the current reading and computes a distance metric against the stored template. Only if the distance indicates a match is the data forwarded for analysis. This prevents unauthorized access to medical records even if the server is breached, and ensures that only the legitimate user can trigger data uploads.

In a fifth exemplary embodiment, the system is implemented in a vehicular access control system, where a driver’s smartphone authenticates them before allowing engine ignition. The car’s onboard unit stores encrypted templates of authorized drivers, and when the driver approaches the vehicle, their phone encrypts a newly captured biometric sample and sends it to the car’s server. The car’s server computes the distance and returns the result to the phone, which validates the match and sends an unlock signal to the ignition system. The car’s internal system never stores the biometric data, the keys, or the distance values, rendering it immune to tampering or cloning.

In a sixth exemplary embodiment, the system is used in enterprise access control for shared workstations. Employees register their digital identity—such as a typing rhythm pattern or keystroke dynamics—on their personal laptops, which serve as trusted devices. When an employee attempts to log in to a shared workstation, the workstation sends a challenge to the employee’s phone, which encrypts the current keystroke sample and transmits it to the company’s authentication server. The server compares the encrypted sample with the registered template and returns the distance. The phone decodes the distance and, if valid, sends a one-time token to the workstation to unlock the session. The workstation never learns the identity, the key, or the distance, and cannot be used to impersonate any user.

In a seventh exemplary embodiment, the system is applied to secure voting kiosks, where voters authenticate using a biometric token before casting their ballot. The kiosk stores encrypted templates of registered voters, and when a voter presents their fingerprint, the kiosk’s secure module encrypts the sample and sends it to a central server. The server computes the distance against the registered template and returns the result. The secure module decodes the distance and, if valid, activates the voting interface. The server cannot determine the identity of the voter, the biometric data, or whether the vote was cast, ensuring ballot secrecy and preventing coercion or vote buying.

In an eighth exemplary embodiment, the system is integrated into a secure messaging application, where users authenticate each other’s identities before initiating end-to-end encrypted conversations. Each user registers their identity—such as a voiceprint or facial vector—on their device, which encrypts and stores the template on a cloud server. When a user attempts to initiate a conversation with another user, their device encrypts a newly captured sample and sends it to the server. The server computes the distance between the two encrypted templates and returns the result. The device decodes the distance and, if it falls below a threshold indicating a match, enables the secure channel. The server never learns the identities or the decision, and cannot be used to impersonate users or intercept messages.

In a ninth exemplary embodiment, the system is used in automated teller machines (ATMs) to authenticate users without requiring physical cards or PINs. A user’s smartphone captures a biometric sample, encrypts it, and transmits it to the ATM’s backend server. The server compares the encrypted sample with the registered template and returns the distance. The smartphone decodes the distance and, if valid, sends a cryptographic authorization code to the ATM, which then dispenses cash. The ATM never stores or processes the biometric data, ensuring compliance with financial security standards and preventing skimming or cloning attacks.

In a tenth exemplary embodiment, the system is deployed in a secure cloud storage environment, where users authenticate access to encrypted files without revealing their identity to the cloud provider. Each user registers a digital identity on their trusted device, which encrypts and uploads the template to the cloud. When the user requests access to a file, the device encrypts a new sample and sends it to the cloud server. The server computes the distance against the stored template and returns the result. The device decodes the distance and, if valid, decrypts and retrieves the file. The cloud provider never learns the user’s identity, the file contents, or the access decision, ensuring privacy and regulatory compliance.

### Principles of Exemplary Embodiments of Present Disclosure

The principles underlying the exemplary embodiments of the present invention are rooted in the mathematical structure of verifiable encryption and the operational separation between trusted and untrusted entities. At the core of the invention is the realization that cryptographic security does not require the server to possess any knowledge of the plaintext, the key, or the decision logic. Instead, security is achieved by confining all sensitive operations to a physically isolated, tamper-resistant device that communicates only in encrypted form with the server. The server’s role is reduced to that of a passive, stateless relay that performs a deterministic, invertible operation on ciphertexts without understanding their meaning. This architectural principle ensures that even a fully compromised server cannot be used to impersonate a user, reconstruct a digital identity, or influence the outcome of authentication.

The mathematical foundation of the invention lies in the properties of the one-time pad cryptosystem, which, when applied in a novel configuration, enables the construction of a verifiable encryption system. The one-time pad is traditionally understood as a symmetric encryption scheme where a plaintext is combined with a random key of equal length using bitwise XOR to produce a ciphertext. The security of this scheme is information-theoretic, meaning that without the key, the ciphertext reveals no statistical information about the plaintext. The present invention extends this principle by introducing a second, independent encryption of a different instance of the same identity using a second one-time key. The ciphertexts from these two encryptions are then combined using the same XOR operation, producing a derived ciphertext that encodes the XOR of the two plaintexts. This derived ciphertext, when processed by a device that possesses both original keys, yields the exact bitwise difference between the two plaintexts. This difference, when interpreted as a Hamming distance, provides a direct measure of similarity between the two identities.

The critical insight is that the derived ciphertext, while containing the combined effect of two plaintexts, contains no information about either plaintext individually. This is because the XOR of two random keys is itself a random value, and the XOR of two plaintexts, when masked by two independent random keys, is statistically indistinguishable from noise. Thus, even if an attacker intercepts the derived ciphertext, they cannot reverse-engineer the original identities or keys, because the system is designed such that the derived ciphertext is the result of three independent random variables: the first plaintext, the second plaintext, and the XOR of two random keys. The decoding function, which is executed only on the trusted device, cancels out the keys and reveals only the plaintext difference, which is the sole quantity relevant to authentication.

This principle is generalizable beyond the one-time pad. Any cryptosystem that supports a linear, invertible combining operation on ciphertexts—such as additive homomorphic encryption or certain stream ciphers—can be adapted to form a verifiable encryption system. However, the one-time pad is uniquely suited for this application due to its simplicity, speed, and information-theoretic security. The XOR operation is implemented in hardware at the bit level, requiring no arithmetic units, no memory allocation beyond the data buffer, and no branching logic. This enables consistent, sub-microsecond performance regardless of data size. The system scales linearly with the number of bits, and the time required to encrypt, combine, or decode remains constant across all input lengths—from 128 bits to 8192 bits—because each bit is processed independently and in parallel.

The operational separation between the trusted device and the untrusted server is enforced through strict protocol boundaries. The device is the only entity that generates keys, stores keys, decodes distances, and makes authentication decisions. The server is prohibited from storing keys, performing decryption, or accessing plaintext. The communication protocol is stateless: each verification event is independent, and no session state, cookie, or token is retained by the server. This eliminates the possibility of replay attacks, session hijacking, or correlation across authentication events. The use of ephemeral keys ensures that even if a ciphertext is captured during transmission, it cannot be reused in a future authentication attempt, because the corresponding key has been discarded.

The threshold-based comparison mechanism further enhances the robustness of the system. In applications involving biometric data, minor variations in capture conditions—such as lighting, pressure, or angle—can cause the raw template to differ slightly from the registered one. By defining a maximum acceptable Hamming distance, the system accommodates these natural variations without compromising security. The threshold is chosen based on empirical analysis of the error rates in the biometric modality being used, ensuring that false acceptances are rare while false rejections are minimized. This approach is mathematically sound because the Hamming distance between two similar biometric templates follows a predictable distribution, and the threshold can be calibrated to achieve a desired balance between security and usability.

The system is also resilient to known cryptographic attacks. A chosen-plaintext attack, in which an attacker submits a known plaintext (such as all zeros) to the system, fails because the resulting ciphertext is the XOR of the unknown key and the known plaintext. When this ciphertext is combined with the registered ciphertext, the result is the XOR of the registered plaintext, the known plaintext, and the two keys. The decoding function cancels the keys and reveals the XOR of the two plaintexts, which is not the registered plaintext itself. Thus, the attacker gains no information about the registered identity. A man-in-the-middle attack is equally ineffective, because the attacker cannot intercept the keys, which are never transmitted, and cannot forge a valid derived ciphertext without knowing both keys. Even if the attacker intercepts the derived ciphertext and attempts to replay it, the device will generate a new key for each verification, rendering the replayed value invalid.

The system’s security is further enhanced by the fact that the server performs no decision-making. It does not know whether the authentication succeeded or failed, nor does it know the identity of the user. This eliminates the possibility of server-side bias, tampering, or coercion. The decision is made exclusively on the trusted device, which can be hardened against physical and software attacks through secure enclave technology, hardware root of trust, or tamper-resistant packaging. This architectural design aligns with the zero-trust security model, where no entity is trusted by default, and all operations are verified based on cryptographic proof rather than network position or administrative privilege.

The invention’s implementation is hardware-agnostic and software-portable. It can be deployed on any computing platform capable of performing bitwise XOR operations and storing small amounts of ephemeral data. This includes microcontrollers, smartphones, smart cards, wearable devices, and embedded systems. The algorithm requires no floating-point arithmetic, no modular exponentiation, no elliptic curve operations, and no cryptographic libraries beyond basic bitwise logic. This makes it ideal for deployment in low-power, low-cost environments where traditional cryptographic methods are infeasible. The system’s performance is deterministic, predictable, and reproducible, ensuring consistent behavior across all devices and network conditions.

In summary, the principles of the present invention are based on the elegant interplay of information-theoretic security, operational isolation, and deterministic computation. By redefining the roles of the server and the client, and by leveraging the mathematical properties of the one-time pad in a novel configuration, the invention achieves a level of security, speed, and scalability that was previously thought impossible in distributed authentication systems. The result is a paradigm shift in how digital identities are verified, enabling a future where privacy, performance, and simplicity coexist without compromise.
