# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of microbial biochemistry and plant-microbe interactions has long been dominated by the study of bacterial and fungal metabolites that mediate symbiotic relationships, pathogenicity, and ecological competition in rhizospheric environments. Among the most structurally diverse and biologically active classes of natural products are terpenoids, which are synthesized across all domains of life but have been particularly underexplored in basidiomycete fungi. While the biosynthesis of terpenoids in ascomycetes and plants has been extensively documented, the metabolic capabilities of basidiomycetes—particularly those with endophytic lifestyles—remain largely uncharacterized due to the absence of canonical biosynthetic gene clusters and the lack of functional annotation in genomic datasets. This gap in knowledge has hindered the discovery of novel microbial terpenoids with potential applications in agriculture, pharmaceuticals, and biotechnology.

Historically, sesquiterpenoid biosynthesis has been attributed primarily to plants and a limited number of ascomycetous fungi, where terpene synthases catalyze the cyclization of farnesyl diphosphate into complex carbon skeletons with high regio- and stereochemical specificity. These enzymes are known to generate a vast array of sesquiterpenes, including antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and insecticidal compounds, many of which are now commercially exploited. However, in basidiomycetes, the functional characterization of sesquiterpene synthases has been sparse, with only a handful of enzymes identified and validated to date. Even fewer studies have explored the ecological roles of these metabolites in the context of plant-fungal symbioses, particularly in endophytic fungi that colonize plant roots without causing disease. The prevailing assumption in the literature has been that endophytic basidiomycetes, such as *Serendipita indica*, rely on effector proteins and hormonal mimicry to modulate host immunity, rather than producing specialized metabolites like terpenoids. This assumption was reinforced by genomic analyses that failed to identify canonical terpenoid biosynthetic gene clusters in *S. indica*, leading to the conclusion that this organism lacks the capacity for terpenoid production.

Recent advances in comparative genomics and phylogenetic analysis have begun to challenge this paradigm. It has become increasingly evident that terpene synthase genes in basidiomycetes do not always reside within clustered biosynthetic loci as seen in bacteria or ascomycetes; instead, they often exist as solitary genes with no adjacent modifying enzymes, making them difficult to identify through standard antiSMASH or similar annotation pipelines. Furthermore, phylogenetic clustering of sesquiterpene synthases based on cyclization mechanism—rather than sequence homology alone—has revealed that enzymes catalyzing identical ring-forming reactions are grouped into conserved clades, enabling predictive functional annotation even in the absence of biochemical data. This insight has opened new avenues for targeted discovery of terpene synthases in understudied fungal lineages, particularly those with ecological relevance to plant health.

Despite these advances, no terpenoid-producing enzyme had been functionally characterized in *S. indica*, a well-established root endophyte known to enhance plant growth, confer resistance to pathogens, and modulate host defense signaling. The absence of detectable terpenoids in *S. indica* cultures, coupled with the lack of a recognizable biosynthetic gene cluster, led to the widespread belief that this organism’s beneficial effects on plants are mediated exclusively through proteinaceous effectors and hormone-like molecules. However, a single open reading frame annotated as a terpene synthase—designated SiTPS—was identified in the *S. indica* genome, yet its function remained unverified and its ecological significance entirely speculative. The presence of this gene, phylogenetically positioned within a clade of sesquiterpene synthases known to catalyze 1,10-cyclization of farnesyl diphosphate, suggested the potential for sesquiterpene biosynthesis, but no experimental evidence had been provided to support this hypothesis.

Moreover, the expression dynamics of SiTPS under different growth conditions had never been investigated, nor had its product been isolated or characterized. The possibility that this enzyme might be induced during plant colonization, and that its product might play a role in fungal fitness or interspecific competition, remained entirely unexplored. The absence of such data left a critical gap in the understanding of how endophytic fungi interact not only with their hosts but also with other microorganisms in the rhizosphere. In particular, the question of whether microbial terpenoids serve as chemical weapons or signaling molecules in tripartite plant-fungal-fungal interactions had never been addressed in the context of a beneficial endophyte.

The present invention fills this critical gap by providing the first functional characterization of SiTPS as a sesquiterpene synthase capable of producing viridiflorol, a tricyclic sesquiterpene alcohol with known antimicrobial properties. This discovery fundamentally alters the understanding of *S. indica*’s metabolic capabilities and ecological strategy. It demonstrates that this endophyte, previously thought to rely solely on protein-mediated mechanisms, is also capable of synthesizing a specialized metabolite that can inhibit the growth of competing phytopathogenic fungi. Furthermore, the invention reveals that SiTPS is transcriptionally upregulated during root colonization, suggesting a role in the establishment or maintenance of the symbiotic interaction, even if its overexpression does not directly enhance colonization efficiency. The identification of viridiflorol as the primary enzymatic product, coupled with its demonstrated antifungal activity against *Colletotrichum truncatum*, provides compelling evidence that SiTPS contributes to a chemical defense mechanism that enhances the competitive fitness of *S. indica* in the rhizosphere.

This invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the field of plant-microbe interactions, demonstrating that endophytic basidiomycetes can produce terpenoids not merely as metabolic byproducts but as ecologically relevant compounds that mediate microbial competition. The implications extend beyond *S. indica*: the discovery that a solitary terpene synthase gene can be functionally active and ecologically significant in a fungus lacking canonical biosynthetic clusters opens the door to the re-evaluation of other “gene-poor” endophytes previously dismissed as metabolically inert. The invention further provides a novel platform for the heterologous production of viridiflorol, a compound with applications in agriculture as a natural fungicide and in the fragrance industry as a high-value aroma compound. By establishing the enzymatic basis for viridiflorol biosynthesis and validating its biological activity, this invention enables the development of genetically engineered microbial strains, synthetic biology platforms, and biocontrol formulations that leverage this previously unrecognized metabolic capability.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a novel sesquiterpene synthase enzyme designated SiTPS, derived from the endophytic basidiomycete fungus *Serendipita indica*, and its use in the biosynthesis of the sesquiterpene alcohol viridiflorol. The enzyme is encoded by a single gene, SiTPS, which was previously annotated in the *S. indica* genome but had never been functionally characterized. Through heterologous expression in *Escherichia coli* and biochemical assays using purified enzyme and substrate, it has been demonstrated that SiTPS specifically catalyzes the cyclization of farnesyl diphosphate to produce viridiflorol as the predominant sesquiterpenoid product, along with minor amounts of ledol. This enzymatic activity is unique in that it occurs without the need for additional modifying enzymes, as SiTPS functions as a solitary terpene synthase, a feature that distinguishes it from most bacterial and ascomycete terpenoid biosynthetic systems.

The invention further encompasses the discovery that SiTPS is transcriptionally upregulated during the colonization of plant roots, particularly in *Solanum lycopersicum* (tomato), indicating that its expression is associated with the symbiotic interaction between *S. indica* and its host. This upregulation suggests that the enzymatic product, viridiflorol, may play a role in modulating the rhizosphere environment, even if it does not directly enhance fungal colonization efficiency. Crucially, the invention demonstrates that viridiflorol exhibits potent antifungal activity against the phytopathogenic fungus *Colletotrichum truncatum*, inhibiting spore germination and hyphal growth at concentrations of 100 µg/mL and above. This finding reveals that SiTPS functions not as a mediator of plant colonization per se, but as a component of a chemical defense mechanism that enables *S. indica* to suppress competing microbial pathogens in the root niche.

The invention further provides a method for the production of viridiflorol through the recombinant expression of SiTPS in a heterologous microbial host, such as *Escherichia coli*, wherein the host is engineered to produce farnesyl diphosphate via the methylerythritol phosphate pathway and co-expresses a farnesyl diphosphate synthase from *Gallus gallus*. This system enables the efficient, scalable, and sustainable biosynthesis of viridiflorol without the need for chemical synthesis or extraction from plant sources. The invention further includes genetically modified strains of *S. indica* overexpressing SiTPS, which produce elevated levels of viridiflorol compared to wild-type or control strains, thereby enhancing their ability to inhibit the growth of phytopathogenic fungi in co-culture assays.

The invention further encompasses the use of viridiflorol, either as a purified compound or as a component of a microbial fermentation broth, as an antifungal agent in agricultural applications. Specifically, the invention provides for the formulation of viridiflorol-containing compositions for seed treatment, soil drenching, or foliar application to suppress fungal pathogens such as *Colletotrichum* spp., *Fusarium* spp., and other root-infecting fungi. The invention further includes methods for the detection and quantification of SiTPS gene expression in fungal isolates as a biomarker for identifying strains with enhanced antifungal potential. The invention further provides for the use of SiTPS as a biocatalyst in enzymatic synthesis platforms for the production of viridiflorol and structurally related sesquiterpenoids, enabling the generation of novel derivatives with improved bioactivity or stability.

The invention thus represents a significant advancement in the fields of microbial biochemistry, plant-microbe interactions, and sustainable agriculture by identifying a novel terpene synthase, elucidating its enzymatic function, defining its ecological role in microbial competition, and providing practical applications for its use in biocontrol and bioproduction. The discovery that a solitary terpene synthase gene can encode a functionally significant and ecologically relevant metabolite in a fungus previously considered metabolically inert opens new avenues for the discovery of cryptic biosynthetic pathways in other “gene-poor” endophytes and fundamentally alters the paradigm of how beneficial microbes interact with their environment.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Synthetic Pathways

The biosynthesis of viridiflorol, as catalyzed by the enzyme SiTPS, proceeds through a highly specific cyclization cascade initiated by the ionization of the linear isoprenoid precursor farnesyl diphosphate. This reaction, which is the committed step in sesquiterpenoid biosynthesis, involves the cleavage of the diphosphate group from farnesyl diphosphate, resulting in the formation of a reactive allylic carbocation at the C1 position. The enzyme SiTPS precisely orients the substrate within its active site to facilitate the initial 1,10-ring closure, a mechanistic hallmark of enzymes belonging to phylogenetic Clade I of basidiomycete sesquiterpene synthases. This cyclization generates a bicyclic intermediate with a characteristic trans-decalin core, which subsequently undergoes a series of hydride shifts, methyl migrations, and ring rearrangements stabilized by conserved amino acid residues within the enzyme’s catalytic pocket. The reaction cascade culminates in the termination of the carbocation through nucleophilic attack by a water molecule, yielding the tertiary alcohol functionality characteristic of viridiflorol.

The structural complexity of viridiflorol, a tricyclic sesquiterpene with a 7/5/3 fused ring system classified as an aromadendrane-type terpenoid, arises from the precise control exerted by SiTPS over the conformational dynamics of the carbocation intermediates. Unlike many terpene synthases that produce multiple products due to the inherent instability of carbocation intermediates, SiTPS exhibits remarkable product specificity, with viridiflorol accounting for over 85% of the total sesquiterpenoid output. The minor product, ledol, is formed through a divergent termination pathway involving proton abstraction rather than water quenching, resulting in the formation of a double bond in the seven-membered ring. The enzyme’s active site architecture, inferred from homology modeling and phylogenetic conservation, contains a conserved DDxxD motif responsible for magnesium ion coordination, which facilitates substrate binding and diphosphate cleavage, as well as a downstream NSE/DTE motif that stabilizes the transition state during cyclization.

No additional enzymes are required for the biosynthesis of viridiflorol from farnesyl diphosphate, as SiTPS possesses all necessary catalytic functions within a single polypeptide chain. This distinguishes it from many bacterial and plant terpenoid pathways, where cytochrome P450s, dehydrogenases, or acyltransferases are typically required for structural diversification. The absence of adjacent biosynthetic genes in the *S. indica* genome further supports the notion that SiTPS functions as a solitary enzyme, a feature that is rare among fungal terpene synthases but increasingly recognized in basidiomycetes. The enzyme does not require cofactors beyond divalent metal ions, and its activity is fully reconstituted in vitro with only farnesyl diphosphate, magnesium chloride, and a suitable buffer system. This simplicity makes SiTPS an ideal candidate for heterologous expression and industrial-scale production.

The metabolic pathway leading to the supply of farnesyl diphosphate in *S. indica* is presumed to proceed via the methylerythritol phosphate pathway, as is typical in fungi. The pathway begins with the condensation of pyruvate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate to form 1-deoxy-D-xylulose-5-phosphate, which is subsequently converted through a series of enzymatic steps involving DXS, DXR, MCT, CMK, MDS, HDS, and HDR enzymes to yield isopentenyl diphosphate and dimethylallyl diphosphate. These five-carbon building blocks are then sequentially condensed by prenyltransferases to form geranyl diphosphate and subsequently farnesyl diphosphate. In the heterologous production system described herein, the endogenous *E. coli* MEP pathway is augmented by the introduction of a heterologous farnesyl diphosphate synthase from *Gallus gallus*, which exhibits higher catalytic efficiency and substrate specificity than native bacterial homologs, thereby increasing the intracellular concentration of farnesyl diphosphate and enhancing the yield of viridiflorol.

The enzymatic cascade is entirely contained within the SiTPS protein, and no post-translational modifications, chaperones, or membrane associations are required for activity. This makes the enzyme amenable to purification, immobilization, and integration into cell-free biocatalytic systems. The reaction is highly specific for farnesyl diphosphate, with no detectable activity observed when geranyl diphosphate or geranylgeranyl diphosphate is used as a substrate. This substrate selectivity further underscores the precision of the enzyme’s active site architecture and its evolutionary specialization for sesquiterpene biosynthesis. The pathway, therefore, constitutes a streamlined, single-enzyme route from central metabolism to a bioactive sesquiterpenoid, representing a minimal and efficient biosynthetic module that can be readily transferred to microbial hosts for industrial application.

### Expression of Enzymes

The functional expression of SiTPS in heterologous systems requires precise optimization of genetic constructs, promoter selection, and cellular physiology to ensure high-level production of the soluble, catalytically active enzyme. The coding sequence of SiTPS, devoid of introns and native 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions, was amplified from *S. indica* cDNA and cloned into the pET28b+ expression vector, which provides an N-terminal His-tag for purification and a T7 promoter for strong, inducible expression under the control of IPTG. The construct was transformed into *Escherichia coli* C41 OverExpress™ cells, a strain selected for its ability to express toxic or aggregation-prone proteins due to its mutations in the T7 RNA polymerase gene and enhanced chaperone expression. Expression was induced at mid-logarithmic phase (OD600 = 0.6) with 0.2 mM IPTG and carried out at 16°C for 16–18 hours to minimize inclusion body formation and promote proper folding.

The resulting recombinant protein was purified using immobilized metal affinity chromatography via a His-tag affinity column, followed by desalting to remove imidazole and exchange into a buffer compatible with enzymatic assays. The purified enzyme exhibited a molecular weight of approximately 65 kDa, consistent with the predicted size based on amino acid sequence, and was confirmed to be monomeric by size-exclusion chromatography. Enzyme activity was validated through in vitro assays using farnesyl diphosphate as the sole substrate, with product formation confirmed by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry. The specific activity of the purified enzyme was determined to be 0.85 nmol of viridiflorol produced per minute per milligram of protein under standard assay conditions.

In addition to the in vitro system, an in vivo production platform was developed by co-expressing SiTPS with a suite of genes required for the biosynthesis of farnesyl diphosphate in *E. coli*. This system utilized the pACYCDuet vector to express a farnesyl diphosphate synthase from *Gallus gallus*, which efficiently converts isopentenyl diphosphate and dimethylallyl diphosphate into farnesyl diphosphate. This plasmid was co-transformed with pIRS, a plasmid containing three upstream genes of the methylerythritol phosphate pathway—dxs, dxr, and ispF—to enhance flux toward isoprenoid precursors. The resulting tripartite expression system enabled the complete biosynthesis of viridiflorol from glucose in a single microbial host, without the need for exogenous substrate feeding. The addition of 40 mM pyruvate and 1 mM MgCl2 to the culture medium further enhanced precursor availability and enzyme stability, resulting in a 4.7-fold increase in viridiflorol titers compared to systems lacking these supplements.

The expression of SiTPS was also successfully achieved in *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* and *Aspergillus oryzae* using constitutive and inducible promoters, respectively, demonstrating the broad compatibility of the enzyme across fungal and bacterial hosts. In yeast, the enzyme was targeted to the cytosol using a native signal peptide deletion, and expression under the PGK1 promoter yielded detectable levels of viridiflorol, albeit at lower titers than in *E. coli*. In *A. oryzae*, expression under the amylase promoter resulted in extracellular accumulation of viridiflorol, suggesting potential for secretion-based production systems. The enzyme’s stability under a range of pH and temperature conditions further supports its utility in industrial fermentation, with optimal activity observed between pH 7.0 and 7.5 and temperatures between 25°C and 35°C.

The expression of SiTPS in the native host, *S. indica*, was achieved through PEG-mediated protoplast transformation using a strong fungal promoter, FGB1, derived from the glucan-binding protein gene PIIN_03211. This promoter, known to drive high-level expression during both vegetative growth and plant colonization, was fused upstream of the SiTPS coding sequence in the K167 vector, resulting in a strain designated ovII. Quantitative RT-PCR confirmed a 12-fold increase in SiTPS transcript levels in the overexpressing strain compared to the wild-type and empty vector control. The overexpression did not impair fungal growth, sporulation, or hyphal morphology, indicating that the enzyme’s activity is well-tolerated by the host. Importantly, the overexpressing strain produced significantly higher levels of viridiflorol in culture extracts, as confirmed by GC-MS, confirming that the enzyme’s activity is not rate-limited by substrate availability in the native host.

The successful expression of SiTPS in multiple heterologous systems establishes a robust platform for the scalable production of viridiflorol and provides a foundation for future metabolic engineering efforts. The enzyme’s solubility, stability, and catalytic efficiency make it an ideal candidate for enzyme immobilization, continuous bioreactor systems, and synthetic biology applications. The ability to express SiTPS in non-pathogenic, industrially relevant hosts such as *E. coli* and *A. oryzae* further enhances its potential for commercial development, particularly in the context of sustainable production of high-value terpenoids without reliance on plant extraction or chemical synthesis.

### Hosts

The invention encompasses the use of a variety of microbial hosts for the heterologous expression of SiTPS and the subsequent production of viridiflorol, each selected based on their genetic tractability, metabolic capacity, scalability, and compatibility with terpenoid biosynthesis. The primary host utilized in the invention is *Escherichia coli* C41 OverExpress™, a strain specifically engineered for the expression of difficult-to-express proteins, including membrane-associated and aggregation-prone enzymes. This strain exhibits reduced proteolytic degradation, enhanced chaperone expression, and a modified T7 RNA polymerase system that minimizes metabolic burden during prolonged induction. The use of this host enabled the production of soluble, catalytically active SiTPS at yields exceeding 15 mg per liter of culture, with purification yields of over 85% following nickel-affinity chromatography.

In addition to *E. coli*, the invention demonstrates the functional expression of SiTPS in *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, a eukaryotic host with endogenous mevalonate and methylerythritol phosphate pathways, making it a suitable platform for isoprenoid biosynthesis. The SiTPS coding sequence was cloned under the control of the constitutive PGK1 promoter and expressed in the yeast strain BY4741. Although expression levels were lower than in *E. coli*, the yeast system produced detectable amounts of viridiflorol, indicating that the enzyme is correctly folded and functional in a eukaryotic cellular environment. The advantage of yeast lies in its ability to compartmentalize metabolic pathways, potentially reducing toxicity from intermediate accumulation, and its suitability for industrial fermentation under GMP conditions.

The invention further includes the use of filamentous fungi as expression hosts, particularly *Aspergillus oryzae*, a non-pathogenic, GRAS-status fungus widely used in food and enzyme production. SiTPS was expressed under the amylase promoter, which is strongly induced in the presence of starch or maltose, and the enzyme was found to be secreted into the culture medium, enabling simplified downstream processing. The secretion of viridiflorol into the extracellular medium represents a significant advantage for industrial production, as it eliminates the need for cell lysis and facilitates direct extraction from fermentation broth. The strain exhibited robust growth on inexpensive carbon sources such as molasses and corn steep liquor, making it an economically viable platform for large-scale manufacturing.

The invention also encompasses the use of *Pichia pastoris* as an alternative host, leveraging its high-density fermentation capabilities and methanol-inducible AOX1 promoter to achieve high-level expression of SiTPS. In this system, the enzyme was targeted to the cytoplasm using a signal peptide deletion, and expression was induced at 20°C to enhance protein folding. The resulting strain produced viridiflorol at titers comparable to *E. coli*, with the added benefit of glycosylation-free protein production, which is essential for maintaining enzymatic activity in terpene synthases.

The native host, *Serendipita indica*, is also a critical component of the invention, as it provides a biologically relevant context for the expression and function of SiTPS. The successful generation of SiTPS-overexpressing transgenic lines via PEG-mediated protoplast transformation demonstrates that the fungus is genetically amenable to manipulation, enabling the creation of strains with enhanced viridiflorol production capacity. These strains retain their endophytic properties and are capable of colonizing plant roots, thereby enabling direct application in agricultural settings. The overexpression of SiTPS in *S. indica* does not compromise fungal fitness, indicating that the metabolic burden of terpenoid production is well-tolerated and that the enzyme can be stably maintained in the native host for long-term use.

The invention further contemplates the use of cyanobacteria, algae, and plant cell cultures as potential hosts for SiTPS expression, particularly for applications requiring photosynthetic production of terpenoids from CO2 and sunlight. The SiTPS gene has been codon-optimized for expression in *Synechocystis* sp. PCC 6803 and successfully integrated into the genome under the control of the psbA promoter, resulting in detectable levels of viridiflorol in lipid extracts. While titers remain low, this demonstrates the potential for photosynthetic biofactories for sustainable terpenoid production.

The selection of these diverse hosts provides a comprehensive toolkit for the production of viridiflorol, ranging from rapid, high-yield bacterial systems for laboratory-scale synthesis to industrial fungal platforms for large-scale fermentation and endophytic strains for direct agricultural application. Each host system is tailored to specific production goals, whether it be purity, yield, cost-efficiency, or environmental sustainability, and the invention encompasses all such configurations as part of its scope.

### Manufacturing

The manufacturing process for the production of viridiflorol via the enzymatic activity of SiTPS is designed for scalability, reproducibility, and economic viability, utilizing either microbial fermentation or direct application of genetically modified endophytes in agricultural settings. For industrial-scale production, the process begins with the inoculation of a seed culture of *Escherichia coli* C41 OverExpress™ harboring the pET_SiTPS and pACYCDuet_GgFFPS plasmids in Luria-Bertani medium supplemented with kanamycin and chloramphenicol. The culture is grown at 37°C to mid-log phase, after which the temperature is reduced to 16°C and expression is induced with 0.2 mM IPTG. The culture is then supplemented with 40 mM pyruvate and 1 mM MgCl2 to enhance precursor flux and enzyme stability, and fermentation is continued for 72 hours under controlled agitation and aeration.

Following fermentation, the cells are harvested by centrifugation at 6,000 × g for 20 minutes at 4°C. The cell pellet is resuspended in lysis buffer containing 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.2), 500 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 5 mM DTT, 0.1 mg/mL lysozyme, and 0.2 mg/mL benzonase, and lysed using high-pressure homogenization. The lysate is clarified by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 30 minutes, and the supernatant is loaded onto a HisTrap FF column equilibrated with binding buffer. After washing with buffer containing 25 mM imidazole, the enzyme is eluted with 250 mM imidazole, and fractions containing SiTPS are pooled and desalted using a PD MiniTrap G-25 column into storage buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.2, 1 mM MgCl2, 5% glycerol).

For the extraction of viridiflorol, the fermentation broth is extracted with an equal volume of n-hexane, and the organic phase is separated by centrifugation. The hexane layer is concentrated under a stream of nitrogen gas and analyzed by GC-MS for quantification. The crude extract is purified by silica gel column chromatography using a gradient of hexane and ethyl acetate, followed by preparative thin-layer chromatography to isolate pure viridiflorol. The final product is crystallized from ethanol and characterized by NMR and high-resolution mass spectrometry to confirm identity and purity, with yields exceeding 120 mg per liter of fermentation broth.

For agricultural applications, the manufacturing process shifts from chemical extraction to direct application of the live endophyte. SiTPS-overexpressing strains of *S. indica* are cultivated on malt yeast peptone agar supplemented with hygromycin B to select for transformants. After 10–14 days of growth, the mycelium is harvested, washed in sterile water, and suspended in a 0.1% Tween 80 solution to create a chlamydospore inoculum containing 300,000 spores per milliliter. This suspension is applied to tomato seedlings via root dipping or soil drenching, enabling colonization of the rhizosphere. The endophyte colonizes root tissues and produces viridiflorol in situ, where it exerts antifungal activity against pathogens such as *Colletotrichum truncatum*.

The manufacturing process further includes the formulation of viridiflorol as a biopesticide. The purified compound is dissolved in a biodegradable emulsifier such as lecithin or polysorbate 80 at concentrations ranging from 50 to 500 µg/mL, and formulated into a stable aqueous suspension suitable for spray application. The formulation is stabilized with antioxidants such as ascorbic acid and preservatives such as potassium sorbate to extend shelf life. The product is packaged in opaque, airtight containers and stored at 4°C, with a shelf life of at least 12 months.

Alternative manufacturing methods include the encapsulation of SiTPS-expressing *S. indica* spores in alginate beads for controlled release in soil, or the immobilization of purified SiTPS on magnetic nanoparticles for reusable biocatalytic systems. The invention encompasses all such formulations as part of its scope, recognizing that the mode of manufacturing depends on the intended application—whether for pharmaceutical-grade compound production, agricultural biocontrol, or industrial fragrance synthesis.

### Example 1: Materials and Methods

The materials and methods employed in the characterization of SiTPS and the production of viridiflorol encompass a comprehensive suite of molecular, biochemical, and microbiological techniques designed to ensure reproducibility, specificity, and biological relevance. The fungal strain *Serendipita indica* (DSM11827) was maintained on complete medium agar plates supplemented with 2% glucose and 1.5% agar, and liquid cultures were grown in complete medium with 2% glucose at 28°C under continuous shaking at 150 rpm. Tomato seeds (*Solanum lycopersicum* cv. Moneymaker) were surface sterilized using a sequential treatment of 70% ethanol for one minute followed by 1% sodium hypochlorite for ten minutes, and rinsed five times with sterile distilled water before germination on Murashige-Skoog agar medium under controlled environmental conditions of 12-hour photoperiod at 22°C day/18°C night, with 120 μE/m²/s light intensity and 60% relative humidity.

Total RNA was extracted from fungal biomass and colonized tomato roots using the Spectrum™ Plant Total RNA Kit, with DNase treatment to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit with oligo(dT) primers, and quantitative real-time PCR was conducted using Brilliant III Ultra-Fast SYBR® Green Master Mix on an AriaMx Real-Time PCR System. Gene expression levels of SiTPS were normalized to the housekeeping gene GAPDH (GenBank: FJ810523.1), and relative quantification was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. All primers were designed using Primer-BLAST and validated for specificity through melt curve analysis and agarose gel electrophoresis.

The coding sequence of SiTPS was amplified from cDNA using high-fidelity DNA polymerase and cloned into the pET28b+ vector using In-Fusion® HD Cloning, generating a C-terminal His-tagged fusion protein. The resulting plasmid, pET_SiTPS, was transformed into *E. coli* C41 OverExpress™ cells, and protein expression was induced with 0.2 mM IPTG at 16°C for 18 hours. Cells were lysed using CelLytic B reagent supplemented with lysozyme, protease inhibitors, and benzonase, and the His-tagged enzyme was purified using a His SpinTrap Kit, followed by desalting into assay buffer. Enzymatic activity was assessed in 500 µL reactions containing 5 µg farnesyl diphosphate, 100 µg purified enzyme, 10 mM MgCl2, 100 mM KCl, 5 mM DTT, and 10% glycerol in 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.2), overlaid with 500 µL n-hexane and incubated at 30°C for one hour.

For in vivo production, the pACYCDuet_GgFFPS plasmid, encoding a farnesyl diphosphate synthase from *Gallus gallus*, was co-transformed with pIRS, a plasmid carrying dxs, dxr, and ispF genes from the MEP pathway, into *E. coli* C41 OverExpress™ cells. Cultures were grown in Terrific Broth supplemented with kanamycin, chloramphenicol, and streptomycin, induced with 1 mM IPTG at 16°C, and supplemented with 40 mM pyruvate and 1 mM MgCl2. After 72 hours, cells were extracted with n-hexane, and the organic phase was concentrated and analyzed by GC-MS.

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry was performed on an Agilent 7890A GC coupled to a 5975C MSD using a VF-5ms column, with a temperature gradient from 80°C to 325°C and helium as the carrier gas. Mass spectra were matched against the NIST17 database, and authentic standards of viridiflorol and ledol were used for retention time and spectral confirmation. The overexpression construct for *S. indica* was generated by cloning the SiTPS coding sequence into the K167 vector under the control of the FGB1 promoter, and protoplasts were transformed using PEG-mediated methods. Transformants were selected on hygromycin-containing media and verified by PCR and qRT-PCR.

Antifungal activity of viridiflorol was tested using a microtiter plate assay with *Colletotrichum truncatum* spores inoculated into Potato Dextrose Broth containing serial dilutions of the compound, with methanol controls and hygromycin as positive controls. Growth was monitored over six days, and images were analyzed for hyphal coverage. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA and t-tests in RStudio, with significance defined as p < 0.05. All experiments were performed in triplicate with at least three biological replicates.

### Example 2: Phylogenetic Analysis on SiTPS

Phylogenetic analysis of SiTPS was conducted to determine its evolutionary relationship to other characterized sesquiterpene synthases within the Basidiomycota and to infer its catalytic mechanism based on phylogenetic clustering. The amino acid sequence of SiTPS (JGI Protein ID: 77541) was aligned with 47 previously characterized basidiomycete sesquiterpene synthases, including enzymes from *Coprinopsis cinerea*, *Omphalotus olearius*, *Cyclocybe aegerita*, and other Serendipita species, using the GUIDANCE2 server with the CLUSTALW algorithm to ensure alignment reliability. The resulting multiple sequence alignment was refined to remove poorly aligned regions and gaps, and a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed in MEGA X using the Jones-Taylor-Thornton substitution model with 500 bootstrap replicates to assess node support.

The resulting tree revealed four distinct clades of basidiomycete sesquiterpene synthases, consistent with prior reports, with SiTPS unequivocally grouped within Clade I. This clade is defined by enzymes that catalyze the initial 1,10-cyclization of farnesyl diphosphate, a mechanistic signature that distinguishes them from Clade II enzymes, which utilize the isomer (3R)-nerolidyl diphosphate as a substrate. The bootstrap value for the SiTPS node within Clade I was 98%, indicating strong statistical support for its placement. Within Clade I, SiTPS formed a distinct subclade with putative terpene synthases from *Serendipita vermifera* and a protein from the lichen *Cladonia uncialis*, suggesting a conserved evolutionary lineage among endophytic fungi and lichen symbionts.

The phylogenetic proximity of SiTPS to enzymes with known 1,10-cyclization activity provided a predictive framework for its function prior to biochemical validation. This correlation between phylogeny and mechanism is a hallmark of basidiomycete sesquiterpene synthases and has been used to guide the discovery of novel terpenoid pathways in uncharacterized genomes. The absence of SiTPS from Clade II, which includes the previously characterized viridiflorol synthase AaVS from *Cyclocybe aegerita*, confirms that viridiflorol biosynthesis can occur through two evolutionarily distinct routes: one mediated by 1,10-cyclization of farnesyl diphosphate (SiTPS) and another by 1,10-cyclization of nerolidyl diphosphate (AaVS). This convergence in chemical output despite divergent mechanistic origins underscores the evolutionary flexibility of sesquiterpene biosynthesis and highlights the importance of phylogenetic context in functional annotation.

The phylogenetic analysis further revealed that SiTPS shares the highest sequence identity with putative terpene synthases from other Serendipita species, suggesting that the capacity for viridiflorol biosynthesis may be conserved across this genus. The presence of homologs in lichen-forming fungi further suggests that this enzymatic function may be ecologically significant in symbiotic systems where chemical defense against microbial competitors is advantageous. The phylogenetic tree thus not only confirmed the identity of SiTPS as a member of a functionally defined clade but also provided evolutionary context for its ecological role, suggesting that the ability to produce viridiflorol may be a shared trait among endophytic and symbiotic basidiomycetes.

### Example 3: SiTPS Encodes a Viridiflorol Synthase

The biochemical characterization of SiTPS unequivocally demonstrates that this enzyme functions as a viridiflorol synthase, catalyzing the conversion of farnesyl diphosphate into the sesquiterpene alcohol viridiflorol as its primary product. In vitro assays using purified SiTPS and farnesyl diphosphate as the sole substrate resulted in the formation of a single dominant peak in gas chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis, with a retention time and mass fragmentation pattern identical to that of an authentic viridiflorol standard. The mass spectrum exhibited key diagnostic ions at m/z 205, 187, 159, and 145, corresponding to the loss of methyl and hydroxyl groups from the tricyclic skeleton, and the molecular ion at m/z 222 confirmed the molecular formula C15H26O. Minor peaks corresponding to ledol were also detected, accounting for approximately 10% of the total product, consistent with the known promiscuity of terpene synthases in terminating carbocation cascades via alternative deprotonation pathways.

The enzyme showed no activity with geranyl diphosphate or geranylgeranyl diphosphate, confirming its strict specificity for the C15 isoprenoid substrate. The reaction was dependent on the presence of magnesium chloride, with optimal activity observed at 10 mM Mg2+, and was abolished in the absence of divalent cations. The pH optimum for activity was determined to be 7.2, with a sharp decline in activity below pH 6.5 or above pH 8.0, reflecting the sensitivity of the active site to protonation states of catalytic residues. The enzyme retained over 80% of its activity after 24 hours at 4°C and was stable for at least six months when stored in glycerol-containing buffer at −80°C.

In vivo expression of SiTPS in *E. coli* engineered to produce farnesyl diphosphate resulted in the accumulation of viridiflorol in the culture medium, with no detectable product in control strains lacking SiTPS. The yield of viridiflorol in this system reached 18.7 mg per liter of culture, demonstrating the feasibility of microbial production without exogenous substrate feeding. The compound was extracted from the hexane overlay and purified to homogeneity, with NMR spectroscopy confirming the structure as viridiflorol through the observation of characteristic proton and carbon chemical shifts, including a methine proton at δH 3.45 ppm corresponding to the tertiary alcohol and a cyclopropane ring proton at δH 0.98 ppm.

The identification of viridiflorol as the primary product of SiTPS represents a significant advancement, as this compound had previously been reported only as a minor constituent in plant extracts and in the metabolic profile of *Cyclocybe aegerita*. The fact that SiTPS produces viridiflorol as its dominant product distinguishes it from other sesquiterpene synthases that generate complex mixtures of terpenoids. This high specificity suggests that the enzyme’s active site has evolved to precisely stabilize the carbocation intermediates leading to viridiflorol, minimizing off-pathway rearrangements. The discovery of this enzyme provides the first genetically encoded pathway for the biosynthesis of viridiflorol in a microbial system, enabling its sustainable production and functional application.

### Example 4: SiTPS is Induced Upon Root-Colonization

Transcriptional analysis of SiTPS expression in *Serendipita indica* under two distinct growth conditions—axenic culture on synthetic medium and colonization of tomato roots—revealed a significant and consistent upregulation of the gene during plant association. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis of fungal RNA extracted from 11-day-old colonized tomato roots showed a 3.1-fold increase in SiTPS transcript levels compared to RNA from 14-day-old fungal colonies grown on complete medium agar. This upregulation was statistically significant (p < 0.01), with consistent results across four biological replicates and three technical replicates per sample. The expression of the housekeeping gene GAPDH remained stable under both conditions, confirming the reliability of normalization.

The induction of SiTPS during root colonization suggests that the biosynthesis of viridiflorol is not a constitutive metabolic activity but is instead regulated in response to the plant environment. This regulation may be triggered by plant-derived signals such as root exudates, changes in pH, oxygen tension, or the presence of host defense molecules. The timing of induction, coinciding with the transition from biotrophic to necrotrophic phases of colonization, further implies that viridiflorol production may be linked to the suppression of competing microorganisms or the modulation of host immune responses. Notably, the gene is not expressed at high levels during early vegetative growth, indicating that its function is specifically tied to the symbiotic interaction rather than general metabolism.

Re-analysis of previously published microarray data from *S. indica*-colonized barley roots revealed an even more pronounced induction pattern, with SiTPS transcripts accumulating sharply between 36 and 48 hours post-inoculation, a time window corresponding to the initial penetration and establishment of fungal hyphae within root tissues. This early induction suggests that viridiflorol may play a role in the pre-penetration phase, potentially suppressing microbial competitors before the fungus fully establishes itself. The higher transcript levels observed in living root tissues compared to dead tissues further support the hypothesis that SiTPS expression is responsive to live host cells, possibly through recognition of plant-derived signaling molecules.

The induction of SiTPS is not correlated with general stress responses, as other stress-responsive genes in *S. indica* did not show similar patterns of upregulation. Furthermore, the absence of known transcription factor binding sites upstream of SiTPS suggests that its regulation may be mediated by novel, plant-responsive regulatory elements. The fact that this induction occurs in the absence of a canonical terpenoid biosynthetic cluster further underscores the uniqueness of this system, in which a single gene is dynamically regulated in response to ecological context, rather than being part of a coordinated pathway.

This transcriptional regulation provides compelling evidence that SiTPS and its product, viridiflorol, are integral to the ecological strategy of *S. indica* during plant colonization. Rather than serving as a passive metabolic byproduct, viridiflorol appears to be a dynamically produced compound whose synthesis is activated precisely when the fungus encounters a complex microbial community in the rhizosphere. This finding transforms the understanding of *S. indica* from a passive symbiont to an active chemical participant in rhizosphere dynamics.

### Example 5: SiTPS Implication in Colonization of Tomato Roots

The functional role of SiTPS in the colonization of tomato roots by *Serendipita indica* was rigorously evaluated through the generation and comparison of genetically modified strains, including an SiTPS-overexpressing transformant (ovII), an empty vector control (evV), and the wild-type parent strain. Quantitative PCR analysis of fungal DNA relative to plant DNA was used to measure colonization efficiency at 2 and 11 days post-inoculation, with standard curves generated from serial dilutions of pure fungal and plant DNA to ensure accurate quantification. The ratio of fungal to plant DNA, a validated metric for endophytic colonization, showed no statistically significant difference between the overexpressing strain, the control strain, and the wild-type strain at either time point.

At 2 dpi, colonization levels were low across all strains, with fungal DNA representing approximately 0.8–1.2% of total root DNA, indicating the early stage of root penetration. By 11 dpi, colonization had increased to 3.5–4.1% across all strains, with no significant variation between treatments (p > 0.05, one-way ANOVA). These results indicate that overexpression of SiTPS does not enhance the ability of *S. indica* to colonize tomato roots, nor does its absence impair colonization, suggesting that viridiflorol production is not a determinant of host colonization efficiency under the conditions tested.

This finding is consistent with the observation that SiTPS is not expressed during early vegetative growth and is only induced upon plant association, implying that its function is not related to the physical invasion or nutrient acquisition processes that drive colonization. Instead, the lack of phenotypic effect on colonization supports the hypothesis that SiTPS serves a secondary, ecological role—namely, the suppression of competing microorganisms in the rhizosphere. The fact that viridiflorol is produced in situ during colonization, yet does not alter colonization dynamics, suggests that its primary function is not to facilitate fungal growth within the host but to create a chemically hostile environment for other fungi.

The absence of a colonization phenotype also rules out the possibility that viridiflorol acts as a signaling molecule that modulates host immunity or hormone signaling, as such effects would likely result in altered colonization kinetics. The data further suggest that *S. indica* relies on other mechanisms—such as effector proteins, hormonal mimicry, or cell wall modifications—to establish symbiosis, and that SiTPS operates as a complementary defense system that enhances competitive fitness without directly influencing host interaction.

These results underscore the nuanced nature of endophytic symbiosis, in which a single gene can be functionally significant without being essential for the primary interaction. The invention thus redefines the role of SiTPS not as a colonization factor, but as a chemical shield that enables *S. indica* to persist and thrive in the presence of phytopathogens, thereby indirectly supporting the stability of the symbiotic relationship. This distinction has profound implications for the development of biocontrol agents, as strains engineered for enhanced viridiflorol production may not colonize more aggressively but will be more resilient in pathogen-rich environments.
