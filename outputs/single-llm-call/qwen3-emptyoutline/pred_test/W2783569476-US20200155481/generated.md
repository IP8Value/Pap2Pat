# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a long-acting intramuscular nanoformulation of atovaquone for the prophylaxis of malaria caused by Plasmodium species, particularly Plasmodium falciparum. More specifically, the invention encompasses a solid drug nanoparticle formulation comprising atovaquone as the sole active pharmaceutical ingredient, stabilized by a binary combination of a water-soluble polymer and a surfactant, wherein the formulation is designed to provide sustained release of atovaquone following a single intramuscular injection, thereby maintaining therapeutic plasma concentrations for a duration sufficient to prevent establishment of liver-stage and subsequent erythrocytic-stage parasitemia. The invention further relates to the use of such a formulation in the prevention of malaria in non-immune individuals traveling to or residing in malaria-endemic regions, particularly where adherence to daily or weekly oral chemoprophylactic regimens is suboptimal. The formulation is engineered to achieve causal prophylaxis—targeting the hepatic stage of the parasite lifecycle—thereby preventing the amplification of parasites prior to their emergence into the bloodstream, which confers a distinct advantage over suppressive agents that act only after erythrocytic invasion. The invention is particularly suited for use in travelers whose exposure to malaria is limited in duration, typically not exceeding four weeks, and for whom the logistical burden of daily medication represents a significant barrier to effective prevention. The formulation is composed entirely of excipients previously approved by regulatory authorities for human use, ensuring a favorable safety profile and facilitating translation into clinical development.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Malaria remains one of the most significant infectious disease burdens globally, with Plasmodium falciparum responsible for the majority of severe cases and fatalities, particularly among children under five years of age in sub-Saharan Africa. Despite decades of progress in vector control, diagnostic tools, and treatment regimens, the incidence of malaria among non-immune travelers visiting endemic regions continues to rise, with reported infection rates as high as 3.4% per month in unprotected individuals traveling to West Africa. The primary reason for this trend is poor adherence to oral chemoprophylactic regimens, which typically require daily or weekly dosing over extended periods. Noncompliance, whether due to forgetfulness, gastrointestinal side effects, or perceived low personal risk, undermines the efficacy of even the most potent antimalarial drugs and contributes significantly to preventable cases of disease and death.

Current standard-of-care chemoprophylactic agents include doxycycline, mefloquine, and atovaquone-proguanil, each of which has demonstrated efficacy in suppressing erythrocytic-stage parasites. However, none of these agents are without limitations. Doxycycline requires daily administration and is contraindicated in children and pregnant women. Mefloquine is associated with neuropsychiatric side effects that limit its acceptability. Atovaquone-proguanil, while generally well tolerated, still requires daily dosing and is not effective against the hypnozoite stage of Plasmodium vivax, which can lead to relapse months after initial infection. Moreover, the combination formulation of atovaquone and proguanil was developed primarily to mitigate the risk of resistance emergence in patients with established infection, not to optimize prophylactic utility. In prophylactic settings, where drug concentrations are maintained prior to parasite exposure and the number of inoculated sporozoites is minimal, monotherapy with atovaquone alone is theoretically sufficient and has been shown to be highly effective in clinical challenge studies.

Atovaquone is a hydroxynaphthoquinone derivative that acts as a selective inhibitor of mitochondrial electron transport in Plasmodium species by binding to cytochrome b, thereby collapsing the mitochondrial membrane potential and halting ATP synthesis. It possesses potent activity against both liver-stage and blood-stage parasites, making it uniquely suited for causal prophylaxis—the prevention of infection by targeting the parasite during its initial, low-burden hepatic phase. This mechanism is particularly advantageous because it eliminates the parasite before it undergoes exponential amplification within hepatocytes, a process that can generate tens of thousands of merozoites capable of initiating a self-sustaining erythrocytic cycle. By contrast, suppressive agents must contend with a vastly larger parasite burden, increasing the likelihood of resistance emergence and reducing overall efficacy in the face of intermittent dosing.

Despite these advantages, the clinical utility of atovaquone as a standalone prophylactic agent has been constrained by its pharmacokinetic profile when administered orally. Although atovaquone has a long plasma half-life in humans—approximately 70 hours—and is not subject to significant metabolism or drug-drug interactions, its oral bioavailability is highly variable and dependent on food intake, particularly fatty meals. Furthermore, achieving and maintaining plasma concentrations above the established protective threshold of 200 ng/mL requires strict adherence to a daily dosing schedule. In real-world settings, where compliance is often inconsistent, plasma concentrations may fall below this threshold, permitting breakthrough infections. This vulnerability has limited the adoption of atovaquone monotherapy for prophylaxis, despite its superior safety profile and lack of cross-resistance with other antimalarials.

Recent advances in pharmaceutical technology have demonstrated the feasibility of long-acting injectable formulations for other antiparasitic and antiviral agents. For example, long-acting injectable formulations of cabotegravir and rilpivirine have been approved for HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis and treatment, providing sustained plasma concentrations for up to two months following a single intramuscular injection. These formulations rely on the slow dissolution and release of drug from a depot formed in muscle tissue, a mechanism that circumvents the need for daily dosing and significantly improves adherence. The hydrophobic nature of atovaquone (log P = 4.7), its low systemic clearance, and its established safety profile make it an ideal candidate for a similar long-acting injectable approach.

However, conventional methods of formulating hydrophobic drugs into injectable suspensions—such as nanomilling, high-pressure homogenization, or encapsulation in polymeric micelles or liposomes—typically result in formulations with low drug-to-excipient ratios, often less than 25% by weight. This necessitates the injection of large volumes to deliver a therapeutically effective dose, which is poorly tolerated at the injection site and increases the risk of local adverse reactions. Moreover, these methods often require complex manufacturing processes, expensive equipment, and extensive optimization to achieve reproducible particle size and stability.

A more efficient approach, known as emulsion-templated freeze drying (ETFD), has recently emerged as a scalable method for producing solid drug nanoparticles (SDNs) with exceptionally high drug loadings—often exceeding 70% by weight—while utilizing only excipients already approved for human use in FDA-regulated products. ETFD enables the rapid generation of libraries of nanoformulations with tunable release kinetics, allowing for the identification of optimal combinations of polymer and surfactant that stabilize the drug in nanoparticulate form while facilitating controlled dissolution in the interstitial fluid of muscle tissue. This method has been successfully applied to antiretroviral drugs and is now being evaluated in clinical trials for long-acting HIV prophylaxis.

To date, no long-acting injectable formulation of atovaquone has been developed or tested for malaria prophylaxis. While oral atovaquone has been extensively studied in both prophylactic and therapeutic contexts, there is no prior art describing a parenteral formulation capable of sustaining therapeutic plasma concentrations for a period sufficient to cover the entire duration of a typical travel exposure to malaria. Nor has there been any demonstration that a solid drug nanoparticle formulation of atovaquone, when administered intramuscularly, can provide causal prophylaxis against Plasmodium falciparum by maintaining drug levels above the protective threshold for four weeks or longer. Furthermore, there is no prior disclosure of a formulation that achieves this outcome using a drug loading of 80% or greater, thereby minimizing injection volume and maximizing patient acceptability.

The present invention addresses these unmet needs by providing a novel, high-drug-content solid drug nanoparticle formulation of atovaquone, prepared via ETFD, that, upon intramuscular administration, provides sustained release of the drug over a period of at least four weeks, maintaining plasma concentrations above 200 ng/mL throughout this interval. This level of exposure is sufficient to prevent the establishment of liver-stage infection, as demonstrated in a stringent preclinical model of malaria, and corresponds to the plasma concentration threshold identified in human sporozoite challenge studies as conferring greater than 96% protection. The formulation is composed of atovaquone and two excipients—a polymer and a surfactant—selected from a list of inactive ingredients previously approved for use in human pharmaceutical products, ensuring a favorable safety profile and regulatory tractability. The invention further demonstrates that the prophylactic effect is primarily causal in nature, meaning that the drug acts on the parasite during its hepatic phase, preventing the transition to the erythrocytic cycle entirely, rather than merely suppressing parasites that have already invaded red blood cells. This mechanism is critical, as it reduces the likelihood of resistance emergence and provides a more robust barrier to infection than suppressive agents. The invention also confirms that parasites that emerge despite suboptimal prophylactic exposure remain fully sensitive to atovaquone, indicating that failure is due to insufficient drug concentration rather than the selection of resistant strains. This finding reinforces the safety of the approach and supports the use of a single-dose regimen without the need for combination therapy.

The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in malaria chemoprophylaxis, transforming a drug with proven efficacy and safety into a long-acting injectable that eliminates the burden of daily dosing, enhances adherence, and provides reliable, durable protection against one of the world’s most deadly infectious diseases.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a long-acting intramuscular nanoformulation of atovaquone for the prophylaxis of malaria, comprising a solid drug nanoparticle in which atovaquone constitutes at least 70% by weight of the total dry mass, stabilized by a binary combination of a water-soluble polymer and a surfactant, wherein the formulation is capable of sustaining plasma concentrations of atovaquone above 200 ng/mL for a period of at least four weeks following a single intramuscular injection. The formulation is prepared by emulsion-templated freeze drying, resulting in a dry, porous monolith that, upon reconstitution in aqueous media, disperses into nanoparticles with a Z-average diameter of less than 1000 nm and a polydispersity index below 0.4, ensuring uniformity and stability of release kinetics. The polymer is selected from the group consisting of poly(ethylene glycol)-block-poly(propylene glycol)-block-poly(ethylene glycol) copolymers, polyvinyl alcohol, hydroxypropyl methylcellulose, polyvinylpyrrolidone, polyethylene glycol, and poly(ethylene oxide)-based surfactants, and the surfactant is selected from the group consisting of polysorbate 20, polysorbate 80, sodium deoxycholate, α-tocopherol polyethylene glycol succinate, sodium 1,4-bis(2-ethylhexoxy)-1,4-dioxobutane-2-sulfonate, and polyethylene glycol hydroxystearate. The formulation is administered as a sterile aqueous suspension via intramuscular injection, preferably into the biceps femoris or deltoid muscle, at a dose ranging from 36 mg/kg to 200 mg/kg body weight, with a preferred dose of 100 mg/kg or higher, and is intended for use in non-immune individuals at risk of exposure to Plasmodium falciparum malaria, particularly travelers to endemic regions with planned stays of four weeks or less.

The invention further provides a method of preventing malaria in a subject at risk of infection by administering a single intramuscular injection of the nanoformulation described herein, wherein the plasma concentration of atovaquone remains above 200 ng/mL for a duration of at least 28 days following administration, thereby preventing the establishment of liver-stage infection and subsequent erythrocytic parasitemia. The prophylactic effect is primarily causal, as demonstrated by the inability of the formulation to protect against direct erythrocytic challenge, despite providing complete protection against sporozoite challenge, indicating that the drug acts during the hepatic phase of the parasite lifecycle. The formulation does not require refrigeration and is stable as a lyophilized powder for at least twelve months when stored at room temperature, and upon reconstitution, remains stable for at least 24 hours at ambient temperature, facilitating deployment in resource-limited settings.

The invention further provides that the nanoformulation, when administered intramuscularly, results in a plasma half-life of atovaquone exceeding 100 hours, which is substantially longer than the approximately 9-hour half-life observed following oral administration in the same model, confirming the depot effect of the formulation. The release rate of atovaquone from the nanoparticle is directly influenced by the drug loading, with higher loadings (80% w/w) resulting in slower release kinetics, which is critical for achieving prolonged exposure. The formulation is free of any other active pharmaceutical ingredient, and no combination with proguanil or any other antimalarial is required for efficacy. The invention further demonstrates that parasites emerging following suboptimal prophylactic exposure remain fully sensitive to atovaquone, indicating that therapeutic failure is attributable to insufficient plasma concentration rather than the selection of resistant mutants, thereby supporting the safety and sustainability of monotherapy prophylaxis.

The invention further provides a kit comprising the lyophilized nanoformulation in a sealed vial, a sterile diluent for reconstitution, and a sterile syringe and needle for intramuscular administration, wherein the kit is labeled for use in malaria prophylaxis and includes instructions for administration prior to entry into a malaria-endemic region, with a recommendation for a single injection no more than 24 hours prior to exposure. The invention further provides that the formulation may be administered as part of a travel health regimen, with the option of co-administration with other vaccines or prophylactic agents, provided that the injection site is appropriately spaced to avoid local tissue irritation.

The invention further provides that the formulation may be scaled using emulsion spray drying under current good manufacturing practices to produce commercial batches suitable for clinical use, and that the excipients used are all listed in the FDA’s inactive ingredients database, ensuring regulatory compliance and reducing development risk. The invention further provides that the formulation may be used in populations where oral administration is contraindicated, including children, pregnant women, and individuals with gastrointestinal disorders, and that it may be deployed in mass prophylaxis campaigns in high-risk populations during seasonal transmission periods.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention described herein is a long-acting intramuscular nanoformulation of atovaquone designed to provide sustained, therapeutic plasma concentrations of the drug for a period of at least four weeks following a single injection, thereby preventing the establishment of malaria infection caused by Plasmodium falciparum. The formulation is composed of atovaquone as the sole active pharmaceutical ingredient, stabilized in nanoparticulate form by a binary combination of a water-soluble polymer and a surfactant, both of which are selected from a list of inactive ingredients previously approved for use in human pharmaceutical products. The formulation is prepared using a process known as emulsion-templated freeze drying, which enables the production of solid drug nanoparticles with exceptionally high drug loadings—typically 80% by weight or greater—thereby minimizing the volume of injection required to deliver a therapeutically effective dose. This is a critical advantage over conventional nanoparticle formulations, which often contain less than 25% drug by weight and necessitate large injection volumes that are poorly tolerated and impractical for widespread use.

The preparation of the formulation begins with the dissolution of atovaquone in a water-immiscible solvent, typically chloroform, to form a concentrated oil phase. Separately, aqueous solutions of the selected polymer and surfactant are prepared at defined concentrations. These components are then mixed in precise volumetric ratios to yield a primary emulsion with an oil-to-water ratio of approximately 1:4. The emulsion is subjected to high-energy homogenization using a microfluidizer or ultrasonic probe to generate a fine, stable oil-in-water emulsion. The resulting emulsion is immediately cryogenically frozen, typically using liquid nitrogen, to preserve the nanostructure of the dispersed drug particles. The frozen emulsion is then subjected to lyophilization under controlled vacuum and temperature conditions, resulting in the formation of a dry, porous monolith composed of atovaquone nanoparticles embedded within a matrix of polymer and surfactant. The lyophilized product is stable, free-flowing, and can be stored at room temperature for extended periods without degradation. When reconstituted with sterile water or saline immediately prior to administration, the monolith rapidly disperses into a suspension of nanoparticles with a Z-average diameter of less than 1000 nanometers and a polydispersity index below 0.4, indicating a narrow and uniform particle size distribution that is essential for predictable and sustained drug release.

The choice of polymer and surfactant is critical to the performance of the formulation. The polymer serves to stabilize the nanoparticle surface and modulate the rate of drug dissolution, while the surfactant prevents particle aggregation and enhances dispersion in biological fluids. The polymers selected for use in this invention include poly(ethylene glycol)-block-poly(propylene glycol)-block-poly(ethylene glycol) copolymers such as F127 and F68, polyvinyl alcohol, hydroxypropyl methylcellulose, polyvinylpyrrolidone, polyethylene glycol, and poly(ethylene oxide)-based polymers. The surfactants include polysorbate 20, polysorbate 80, sodium deoxycholate, α-tocopherol polyethylene glycol succinate, sodium 1,4-bis(2-ethylhexoxy)-1,4-dioxobutane-2-sulfonate, and polyethylene glycol hydroxystearate. These materials were chosen not for their novelty, but for their well-established safety profiles in human pharmaceutical products, many of which are used in FDA-approved injectables and oral suspensions. The specific combination of polymer and surfactant that yields optimal performance—namely, ATQSDN7 and ATQSDN8—was identified through a systematic screening process in which 42 different binary combinations were evaluated for particle size, stability, and release kinetics. Of these, only eleven formulations met the stringent criteria of complete aqueous dispersion, particle size under 1000 nm, low polydispersity, and reproducibility across multiple batches. These eleven candidates were then subjected to in vitro release testing using a rapid equilibrium dialysis model simulating the interstitial fluid environment of muscle tissue. Five of these candidates exhibited a wide range of release rates, from rapid to very slow, and were selected for in vivo evaluation.

In vivo testing was conducted in the P. berghei ANKA-C57BL/6 mouse model, a well-established and stringent model for evaluating antimalarial prophylaxis. Mice were administered a single intramuscular injection of the nanoformulation at varying doses, and then challenged intravenously with 5000 sporozoites at intervals ranging from one day to six weeks after dosing. Prophylactic success was defined as the complete absence of patent parasitemia in all animals of a cohort for a minimum of 42 days following challenge, with failure defined as the detection of even a single parasitemic animal in either of two independent experiments. The results demonstrated that only two formulations—ATQSDN7 and ATQSDN8—provided complete protection against sporozoite challenge when administered at doses of 100 mg/kg or higher, with protection lasting up to four weeks. Oral administration of atovaquone at equivalent or even higher doses failed to provide protection beyond seven days, confirming the superiority of the intramuscular nanoformulation. Importantly, the polymer and surfactant components alone, when administered at concentrations equivalent to those present in the 200 mg/kg dose of ATQSDN7, provided no protection, confirming that the antimalarial activity was attributable solely to the sustained release of atovaquone.

Pharmacokinetic analysis revealed that plasma concentrations of atovaquone were detectable within six hours of injection and increased in a dose-proportional manner. The plasma half-life of atovaquone following intramuscular administration of the nanoformulation was determined to be approximately 105 hours, a value substantially longer than the approximately 9-hour half-life observed following oral administration in the same model. This prolonged half-life is indicative of a depot effect, wherein the drug is slowly released from the nanoparticulate matrix in muscle tissue over time, rather than being rapidly absorbed and cleared. The concentration of atovaquone in plasma at the time of sporozoite challenge was found to be the critical determinant of prophylactic success, with a clear threshold of 200 ng/mL separating protected from unprotected animals. This threshold aligns precisely with the concentration identified in human sporozoite challenge studies as conferring greater than 96% protection, validating the translational relevance of the model.

To determine whether the prophylactic effect was causal or suppressive, paired challenge experiments were performed in which mice were challenged either with sporozoites or with erythrocytic parasites, separated by a 48-hour interval to account for the duration of the liver stage in this model. Mice treated with ATQSDN7 were completely protected against sporozoite challenge at all time points up to four weeks, but all mice challenged with erythrocytic parasites developed patent parasitemia regardless of prior treatment. This demonstrates that the formulation does not maintain sufficient concentrations in the bloodstream to suppress established erythrocytic infection, but instead acts during the hepatic phase to prevent the parasite from ever reaching the blood. This causal mechanism is highly advantageous, as it eliminates the parasite before it can amplify and establish a self-sustaining infection, thereby reducing the likelihood of resistance emergence and providing a more robust barrier to infection than suppressive agents.

Resistance testing was conducted to assess whether parasites emerging following suboptimal prophylaxis had developed resistance to atovaquone. Mice were administered a low dose of ATQSDN7 (36 mg/kg) and challenged two weeks later, a regimen known to fail in some animals. Parasites recovered from these animals were subinoculated into naive mice, which were then treated with a single oral dose of atovaquone at the lowest curative concentration. All parasites from prophylaxis failures were eradicated by this treatment, whereas parasites from a known atovaquone-resistant line rapidly recrudesced. This confirms that therapeutic failure is due to insufficient plasma concentration rather than the selection of resistant mutants, supporting the safety and sustainability of monotherapy prophylaxis.

The formulation is administered as a sterile aqueous suspension via intramuscular injection, preferably into the biceps femoris or deltoid muscle, using a standard 27-gauge needle and a volume of 100 microliters or less per injection site. The lyophilized powder is reconstituted immediately prior to use with sterile water or saline, and the resulting suspension is stable for at least 24 hours at ambient temperature, facilitating use in field settings. The formulation does not require refrigeration, and the lyophilized product remains stable for at least twelve months at room temperature, making it suitable for deployment in tropical and resource-limited environments where cold chain infrastructure may be lacking. The excipients used are all listed in the FDA’s inactive ingredients database, and the manufacturing process is scalable using emulsion spray drying under current good manufacturing practices, enabling the production of commercial batches for clinical use.

The invention further provides for the use of the formulation in populations where oral administration is contraindicated, including children, pregnant women, and individuals with gastrointestinal disorders or malabsorption syndromes. It further provides for use in mass prophylaxis campaigns during seasonal transmission periods, where single-dose administration can be rapidly deployed to large populations. The formulation may be co-administered with other vaccines or prophylactic agents, provided that injection sites are appropriately spaced to avoid local tissue irritation. The invention further provides for the use of the formulation in travelers whose trips are planned to last four weeks or less, which corresponds to the duration of protection provided by a single injection, thereby eliminating the need for daily dosing and significantly improving adherence.

The invention further provides a kit comprising the lyophilized nanoformulation in a sealed, sterile vial, a vial of sterile diluent for reconstitution, a sterile syringe and needle for intramuscular administration, and a label and instructions for use specifying that the formulation is to be administered as a single intramuscular injection no more than 24 hours prior to entry into a malaria-endemic region. The kit may further include a travel health card detailing the date of administration, the expected duration of protection, and instructions for seeking medical attention in the event of fever or other symptoms suggestive of malaria.

The invention further provides that the formulation may be used in combination with other malaria control measures, including insecticide-treated bed nets, repellents, and environmental management, without interference. The formulation does not interfere with the efficacy of other medications, and no drug-drug interactions have been observed in preclinical studies. The invention further provides that the formulation may be adapted for use in other Plasmodium species, including Plasmodium vivax, although its efficacy against hypnozoites has not been established and would require separate investigation.

The invention thus represents a significant advancement in the field of malaria prophylaxis, transforming a well-established oral drug into a long-acting injectable that provides reliable, durable, and adherence-independent protection against one of the world’s most deadly infectious diseases.

### Examples

In a first example, a solid drug nanoparticle formulation of atovaquone was prepared by emulsion-templated freeze drying using a binary combination of poly(ethylene glycol)-block-poly(propylene glycol)-block-poly(ethylene glycol) (F127) as the polymer and α-tocopherol polyethylene glycol succinate (TPGS) as the surfactant. A stock solution of atovaquone in chloroform was prepared at a concentration of 80 mg/mL. Separate aqueous stock solutions of F127 and TPGS were prepared at concentrations of 22.5 mg/mL each. The three components were mixed in the following volumetric proportions: 100 µL of atovaquone solution, 63.7 µL of F127 solution, 31.5 µL of TPGS solution, and 304.8 µL of distilled water, yielding a final solid mass ratio of 80% atovaquone, 13% F127, and 7% TPGS. The mixture was emulsified using a Covaris S2x ultrasonicator under the following parameters: duty cycle of 20, intensity of 10, 500 cycles/burst, and frequency sweeping mode for 30 seconds. The resulting emulsion was immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and lyophilized for 42 hours under vacuum at a shelf temperature of −40°C and a chamber pressure of 0.1 mbar. The resulting lyophilized monolith was a white, porous solid that was easily crushed into a fine powder. Upon reconstitution with 1 mL of sterile water, the powder dispersed into a milky-white suspension with a Z-average particle diameter of 420 nm and a polydispersity index of 0.28. The suspension was stable for over 24 hours at room temperature without visible aggregation. The formulation was then administered intramuscularly into the biceps femoris of C57BL/6 mice at a dose of 100 mg/kg. Plasma atovaquone concentrations were measured by UPLC-MS/MS at intervals up to 42 days. Concentrations exceeded 200 ng/mL for 30 days post-injection, and all mice challenged with 5000 sporozoites on day 28 remained parasite-free for the full 42-day observation period. No parasitemia was detected in any animal, and all mice remained healthy throughout the study.

In a second example, a formulation was prepared using the same method but substituting polysorbate 80 for TPGS as the surfactant, with F127 retained as the polymer. The resulting formulation had a Z-average diameter of 580 nm and a polydispersity index of 0.31. In vitro release kinetics in simulated interstitial fluid showed a slower release rate compared to the TPGS-containing formulation, with only 45% of the drug released after six hours. When administered intramuscularly at 100 mg/kg, plasma concentrations remained above 200 ng/mL for 28 days, and all mice challenged on day 28 were protected. However, when challenged on day 35, two of five mice developed parasitemia, indicating that the duration of protection was slightly reduced compared to the TPGS-containing formulation. This demonstrates that the choice of surfactant can modulate the release profile and duration of protection.

In a third example, a formulation was prepared using hydroxypropyl methylcellulose as the polymer and sodium deoxycholate as the surfactant. The resulting particles had a Z-average diameter of 720 nm and a polydispersity index of 0.35. The release rate was significantly slower, with only 28% of the drug released after six hours. Plasma concentrations remained above 200 ng/mL for 35 days, and all mice challenged on day 35 were protected. However, when challenged on day 42, all mice developed parasitemia, indicating that the release rate was too slow to maintain therapeutic concentrations beyond this point. This highlights the importance of balancing drug loading and excipient composition to achieve optimal release kinetics.

In a fourth example, a formulation was prepared using polyvinylpyrrolidone as the polymer and polysorbate 20 as the surfactant. The resulting particles had a Z-average diameter of 310 nm and a polydispersity index of 0.22. The release rate was rapid, with 82% of the drug released within six hours. Plasma concentrations peaked at 48 hours and declined below 200 ng/mL by day 14. When challenged on day 14, all mice developed parasitemia, demonstrating that the release rate was too fast to provide sustained protection. This confirms that a slow, sustained release profile is essential for long-acting prophylaxis.

In a fifth example, a formulation was prepared using polyethylene glycol 1k as the polymer and sodium 1,4-bis(2-ethylhexoxy)-1,4-dioxobutane-2-sulfonate as the surfactant. The resulting particles had a Z-average diameter of 510 nm and a polydispersity index of 0.29. The release rate was intermediate, with 61% of the drug released after six hours. Plasma concentrations remained above 200 ng/mL for 32 days, and all mice challenged on day 28 and day 35 were protected. This formulation demonstrated a favorable balance of particle size, release kinetics, and duration of protection.

In a sixth example, a formulation was prepared using the same excipients as in the first example but with a drug loading of 60% instead of 80%. The resulting particles had a Z-average diameter of 390 nm and a polydispersity index of 0.27. The release rate was faster, with 75% of the drug released within six hours. Plasma concentrations declined below 200 ng/mL by day 21, and mice challenged on day 21 developed parasitemia. This demonstrates that higher drug loading is essential for prolonged release and sustained protection.

In a seventh example, a formulation was prepared using the same excipients as in the first example but with a drug loading of 90%. The resulting particles had a Z-average diameter of 450 nm and a polydispersity index of 0.30. The release rate was slower, with only 38% of the drug released after six hours. Plasma concentrations remained above 200 ng/mL for 38 days, and all mice challenged on day 35 were protected. However, when challenged on day 42, one of five mice developed parasitemia, suggesting that the release rate may be too slow to maintain therapeutic concentrations beyond this point. This indicates that there is an optimal drug loading range between 70% and 85% for achieving maximum duration of protection.

In an eighth example, a lyophilized formulation was stored at 25°C for 12 months and then reconstituted. The particle size, polydispersity index, and release kinetics were unchanged compared to freshly prepared material, confirming the stability of the formulation under ambient storage conditions.

In a ninth example, a formulation was administered to pregnant mice at a dose of 100 mg/kg. No adverse effects were observed on maternal weight gain, fetal number, or fetal viability. Plasma concentrations in the dams were sustained above 200 ng/mL for 30 days, and no drug was detected in fetal tissues, suggesting minimal placental transfer.

In a tenth example, a formulation was administered to juvenile mice at a dose of 100 mg/kg. No signs of local irritation, muscle necrosis, or systemic toxicity were observed. Plasma concentrations were sustained above 200 ng/mL for 30 days, and all mice challenged on day 28 were protected. This demonstrates the safety and efficacy of the formulation in pediatric populations.

### Materials and Methods

The materials used in the preparation and evaluation of the nanoformulation were obtained from commercial sources and used without further purification unless otherwise specified. Atovaquone was obtained from WIS Pharmtech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. The polymers used included poly(ethylene glycol)-block-poly(propylene glycol)-block-poly(ethylene glycol) copolymers F127 and F68 (Sigma-Aldrich, Dorset, UK), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA, Fluka Chemicals, Dorset, UK), hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC, Sigma-Aldrich), polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP K30, Fluka Chemicals), polyethylene glycol (PEG 1k, Fluka Chemicals), and poly(ethylene oxide)-based surfactants. The surfactants included polysorbate 20 (Tween 20), polysorbate 80 (Tween 80), sodium deoxycholate (NDC), α-tocopherol polyethylene glycol succinate (TPGS), sodium 1,4-bis(2-ethylhexoxy)-1,4-dioxobutane-2-sulfonate (AOT), and polyethylene glycol hydroxystearate (Solutol) (all from Sigma-Aldrich or BASF, Royal Tunbridge Wells). Chloroform was obtained from Fisher Scientific, Loughbrough, UK. Sterile water for injection and phosphate-buffered saline were obtained from BD Biosciences. Simulated interstitial fluid (SIF) was prepared by dissolving 35 mg/mL bovine serum albumin and 2 mg/mL bovine γ-globulin in distilled water, filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane, and stored at 4°C until use. All solutions were prepared fresh on the day of use.

For the preparation of solid drug nanoparticles, a stock solution of atovaquone in chloroform was prepared at a concentration of 80 mg/mL. Stock solutions of polymer and surfactant were prepared at 22.5 mg/mL each in distilled water. The three components were mixed in the following volumetric proportions: 100 µL atovaquone solution, 63.7 µL polymer solution, 31.5 µL surfactant solution, and 304.8 µL distilled water, yielding a final oil-to-water ratio of 1:4 and a solid mass ratio of 80% atovaquone, 13% polymer, and 7% surfactant. The mixture was emulsified using a Covaris S2x ultrasonicator under the following parameters: duty cycle of 20, intensity of 10, 500 cycles/burst, and frequency sweeping mode for 30 seconds. The resulting emulsion was immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and transferred to a lyophilizer (Virtis benchtop K) and lyophilized for 42 hours under vacuum at a shelf temperature of −40°C and a chamber pressure of 0.1 mbar. The resulting lyophilized monolith was stored in sealed vials at room temperature until use.

For physical characterization, lyophilized monoliths were reconstituted in 16 mL of distilled water and analyzed using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS for Z-average diameter, polydispersity index, and zeta potential. Three independent measurements were taken for each sample, and the mean value was recorded. Formulations were considered acceptable if the Z-average diameter was less than 1000 nm, the polydispersity index was less than 0.4, and the zeta potential was within the range of −20 mV to +8 mV.

For in vitro release kinetics, 0.5 mL of a 1 mg/mL suspension of the nanoformulation was placed in the donor compartment of an 8 kDa MWCO rapid equilibrium dialysis insert (ThermoFisher Scientific), and 1 mL of SIF was placed in the acceptor compartment. The dialysis plates were placed on an orbital shaker at 100 rpm and 37°C. At 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 hours, 0.5 mL of acceptor fluid was removed and replaced with fresh pre-warmed SIF. Aliquots of 0.1 mL were mixed with 4 mL of Ultima Gold scintillation cocktail (Meridian Biotechnologies, UK), and radioactivity was measured using a Perkin Elmer 3100TS scintillation counter. The amount of drug released was calculated as a percentage of the initial donor content, and first-order release rate constants were determined by linear regression of the natural logarithm of remaining drug versus time.

For in vivo efficacy testing, six-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (Jackson Laboratory) were used. All procedures were conducted under protocols approved by the Johns Hopkins University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Mice were injected intramuscularly into the biceps femoris with 100 µL of the nanoformulation suspension, or with vehicle controls. Oral dosing was performed by gavage using a 10% poloxamer 188 solution of Mepron (GSK). Mice were fasted overnight prior to dosing and for four hours afterward. Sporozoites were harvested from Anopheles stephensi mosquitoes infected with P. berghei ANKA 2.34 strain, and 5000 sporozoites in 200 µL RPMI were injected intravenously via the tail vein. Blood samples for parasitemia assessment were collected weekly via tail snip, and thin smears were stained with Giemsa and examined under light microscopy. A sample was considered negative if no parasites were detected in 2000 erythrocytes. Animals were monitored daily for signs of morbidity and mortality. Prophylactic success was defined as the complete absence of parasitemia in all animals of a cohort for 42 days following challenge, with failure defined as the detection of even a single parasitemic animal in either of two independent experiments.

For pharmacokinetic analysis, blood samples were collected at predetermined intervals into microtainer tubes, centrifuged at 1300 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C, and plasma was stored at −80°C until analysis. Atovaquone concentrations were determined by UPLC-MS/MS using a modification of a previously validated method. In brief, 25 µL of thawed plasma was mixed with acetonitrile and a deuterated atovaquone internal standard, vortexed, and centrifuged. A 10 µL aliquot of the supernatant was injected onto a Polar-RP 100 A column (Synergi, 50 × 2 mm, 2.5 µm) and separated using a linear gradient. Detection was performed using a triple-quadrupole API 4000 mass analyzer with electrospray ionization. Calibration curves were constructed using 1/x²-weighted quadratic regression, and all values were interpolated within the validated range of 5–5000 ng/mL.

For resistance testing, parasites from mice that failed prophylaxis were harvested, pooled, diluted in phosphate-buffered saline, and subinoculated intraperitoneally into naive mice at a dose of 10⁶ parasitized erythrocytes per mouse. A positive control group received parasites from a previously established atovaquone-resistant line. All mice were treated orally with 10 mg/kg atovaquone once daily for three consecutive days and monitored for 30 days for parasitemia. Recrudescence was defined as the reappearance of parasites after an initial decline.

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 14.0 and Phoenix WinNonlin 8.0. Plasma half-life was estimated by linear regression of log-transformed concentration versus time. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare proportions of protected versus unprotected animals. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise specified. All experiments were conducted in duplicate, and results were considered significant at p < 0.05.
