## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of microbiology and environmental biotechnology, and more specifically to a novel method for the selective isolation, immobilization, and retrieval of living microorganisms from complex environmental samples such as soil and sediment. The invention provides a non-invasive, non-chemical, and non-mechanical technique for capturing viable microbial cells directly from their native matrices using electrostatic modulation at the microscale, followed by their controlled release without loss of viability or alteration of physiological state. The method is particularly useful for the retrieval of previously unculturable microorganisms that adhere strongly to soil aggregates or sediment particles and are otherwise inaccessible through conventional extraction techniques. The invention further enables the spatial patterning of microbial cells on electrode surfaces, facilitating high-resolution imaging, single-cell analysis, and the development of microbial biosensors or bioelectrochemical systems. The technology is applicable to environmental monitoring, metagenomic sequencing, bioprospecting for novel enzymes and bioactive compounds, and the cultivation of extremophiles and symbiotic microbes that resist traditional laboratory growth conditions.

## BACKGROUND ART

The natural environment, including terrestrial soils and marine sediments, harbors an immense diversity of microorganisms that play critical roles in global biogeochemical cycles, nutrient recycling, and ecosystem stability. Despite their ecological and biotechnological significance, the vast majority of these microorganisms—estimated to exceed 99%—have remained uncultured under standard laboratory conditions due to their complex nutritional dependencies, slow growth rates, sensitivity to oxygen, or dependence on physical interactions with neighboring cells or mineral surfaces. Traditional methods for isolating microorganisms from environmental samples have relied on dilution-to-extinction techniques, mechanical homogenization, density gradient centrifugation, or chemical lysis followed by plating on nutrient-rich media. However, these approaches are inherently biased, as they favor fast-growing, culturable species while excluding slow-growing, oligotrophic, or syntrophic organisms that dominate natural communities. Furthermore, direct DNA extraction from environmental samples, while enabling metagenomic analyses, is frequently compromised by the co-extraction of humic substances and other inhibitory compounds that interfere with downstream molecular techniques such as polymerase chain reaction, restriction digestion, and DNA ligation. Even indirect extraction methods, which attempt to separate microbial cells from particulate matter prior to lysis, typically recover only a fraction—often between 25% and 35%—of the total microbial biomass, due to the strong adhesion of many bacterial taxa to soil aggregates, clay particles, or organic matrices.

Alternative approaches to microbial isolation have emerged over the past two decades, including micromanipulation using optical tweezers, flow cytometry for single-cell sorting, microfluidic compartmentalization, and droplet-based encapsulation. While these methods offer improved resolution and control, they are generally limited to laboratory-cultured strains or samples that have been pre-treated to liberate cells from their matrices. None of these techniques can be applied directly to raw soil or sediment samples without prior mechanical disruption or chemical solubilization, which invariably damages cell membranes, alters gene expression profiles, and introduces artifacts into downstream analyses. Moreover, these methods are often prohibitively expensive, require specialized instrumentation, and lack the scalability needed for high-throughput environmental sampling. The inability to isolate intact, viable, and phylogenetically representative microbial populations from their native environments has thus remained a persistent bottleneck in environmental microbiology, limiting the discovery of novel metabolic pathways, biosynthetic gene clusters, and ecologically relevant microbial interactions.

Electrochemical techniques have been explored in microbiology for applications such as microbial fuel cells, biosensing, and biofilm control, but none have been employed for the selective capture and retrieval of diverse microbial populations from complex environmental matrices. Prior art describes the use of electric fields to manipulate cells in suspension, such as dielectrophoresis for cell sorting or electrophoresis for DNA separation, but these methods rely on differences in cell size, shape, or charge density and are ineffective in the presence of particulate matter or heterogeneous ionic environments. Other studies have demonstrated that certain electroactive bacteria, such as Geobacter and Shewanella species, can transfer electrons to solid surfaces, but these phenomena have been observed only under controlled laboratory conditions with pure cultures and have not been generalized to the broad spectrum of environmental microbes. No prior method has demonstrated that a simple, low-voltage, constant negative potential applied to an inert electrode surface can selectively attract and immobilize a wide range of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, actinomycetes, and extremophiles from raw soil and sediment samples, nor has any prior art disclosed the subsequent use of a high-frequency triangular wave potential to detach these cells with minimal cytotoxicity and maximal recovery efficiency. The absence of such a technique has left a critical gap in the ability to access the true diversity of microbial life in natural environments.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

### Object to be Achieved by the Invention

The primary object of the invention is to provide a method for the selective, non-destructive, and efficient isolation of living microorganisms from complex environmental matrices such as soil and deep-sea sediment without the need for chemical lysis, mechanical disruption, or prior cultivation. A further object is to enable the spatial patterning of individual microbial cells on a defined electrode surface for high-resolution imaging, single-cell analysis, or controlled co-culture experiments. Another object is to retrieve these microorganisms in a viable and physiologically intact state, preserving their native gene expression profiles and metabolic capabilities for downstream applications such as metagenomic sequencing, enzyme screening, or cultivation attempts. A further object is to achieve this retrieval with high fidelity to the original microbial community structure, ensuring that the collected population reflects the true phylogenetic diversity of the source environment rather than being skewed by extraction biases. An additional object is to provide a scalable and reproducible system that operates under ambient conditions, requires no specialized reagents or expensive instrumentation beyond a standard potentiostat and function generator, and is compatible with a wide range of environmental samples including those with high humic acid content, low nutrient availability, or extreme salinity. Ultimately, the invention seeks to overcome the longstanding limitations of traditional microbial isolation methods by introducing an electrostatic mechanism that exploits the intrinsic physiological response of living cells to weak electrical fields, thereby enabling the direct capture and release of previously inaccessible microbial populations.

### Means for Achieving the Objects

The objects of the invention are achieved by a method comprising the following sequential steps: first, a non-nutritive aqueous suspension of a soil or sediment sample is prepared by mixing the environmental matrix with a buffer solution that lacks organic carbon sources, such as phosphate-buffered saline, MOPS buffer, tricine buffer, or artificial seawater, to prevent microbial growth or metabolic activity during the isolation process. This suspension is then introduced into a chamber containing a working electrode composed of indium tin oxide deposited on a glass substrate, with a counter electrode and a reference electrode positioned to complete the electrochemical circuit. A constant negative electrical potential, ranging from −0.2 volts to −0.4 volts relative to an Ag/AgCl reference electrode, is applied to the indium tin oxide electrode for a period of at least two hours. During this time, living microorganisms suspended in the sample are selectively attracted to and immobilized on the surface of the electrode, while non-living particles, humic substances, and inorganic sediments remain unattached or are easily removed by washing. The immobilization is specific to viable cells, as dead or chemically fixed microorganisms do not exhibit the same attachment behavior. Following immobilization, the electrode is gently washed with the same buffer solution to remove loosely bound debris. Subsequently, a high-frequency triangular wave potential, oscillating between +10 millivolts and −10 millivolts relative to the Ag/AgCl reference electrode at a frequency of approximately 9 megahertz, is applied to the same electrode for a duration of 30 to 60 minutes. This alternating potential induces the detachment of the immobilized microorganisms from the electrode surface without causing significant cell lysis or loss of viability. The detached microorganisms are collected from the surrounding solution, concentrated, and used for downstream applications such as DNA extraction, cultivation, or functional screening. The method may be repeated iteratively to enhance the purity and yield of the collected microbial population, and it is effective across a wide range of environmental conditions, including aerobic and anaerobic atmospheres, varying temperatures, and different ionic strengths.

### Effect of the Invention

The invention produces a number of significant and unexpected effects that distinguish it from all prior methods of microbial isolation. First, it enables the retrieval of microbial populations from soil and sediment samples that are otherwise inaccessible due to their strong adhesion to particulate matter, resulting in a recovery rate that captures a broader phylogenetic diversity than direct DNA extraction methods. Second, the method preserves the viability of the collected microorganisms, with survival rates exceeding 80% in most tested species, allowing for subsequent cultivation, metabolic assays, or single-cell analyses that were previously impossible. Third, the technique is highly selective for living cells, as dead or fixed microorganisms do not attach to the electrode under the same conditions, thereby eliminating contamination from non-viable biomass that commonly plagues molecular analyses. Fourth, the method is compatible with samples containing high concentrations of humic substances, PCR inhibitors, or other contaminants that typically interfere with downstream molecular techniques, as the microbial cells are physically separated from these inhibitors prior to DNA extraction. Fifth, the spatial resolution of the electrode allows for the precise patterning of microbial cells on microscale features, enabling the creation of defined microbial arrays for high-throughput screening or co-culture studies. Sixth, the process is scalable, requiring no specialized reagents or complex equipment beyond a standard potentiostat and function generator, making it accessible to laboratories without advanced microbiological infrastructure. Seventh, the technique operates under ambient conditions, without the need for cryogenic storage, vacuum systems, or high-pressure environments, thereby reducing operational complexity and cost. Finally, the method enables the repeated retrieval of microbial populations from the same environmental sample through iterative cycles of attachment and detachment, allowing for the progressive enrichment of rare or slow-growing taxa. These combined effects represent a paradigm shift in environmental microbiology, providing a tool that bridges the gap between molecular diversity and functional viability, and unlocking access to the vast uncultured majority of microbial life on Earth.

## MODES FOR CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION

### <Method for Immobilizing Living Microorganisms>

The method for immobilizing living microorganisms involves the preparation of a suspension of environmental material, such as soil or deep-sea sediment, in a non-nutritive aqueous medium that supports cell integrity without promoting microbial growth or metabolic activity. Suitable buffers include phosphate-buffered saline lacking calcium and magnesium ions, MOPS buffer at a pH of approximately 7.0, tricine buffer at a similar pH, or artificial seawater formulated to mimic natural marine conditions. The environmental sample is mixed with the buffer at a concentration ranging from 0.05 to 0.2 grams per milliliter and subjected to brief mechanical agitation, such as vortexing for five minutes, to disperse microbial cells from particulate matter without inducing lysis. The resulting suspension is then introduced into a sealed electrochemical chamber containing a working electrode composed of indium tin oxide deposited on a transparent glass substrate, with a platinum counter electrode and an Ag/AgCl reference electrode positioned above the solution to complete the circuit. The chamber is maintained at a temperature appropriate to the origin of the sample, such as room temperature for terrestrial soils or 4 degrees Celsius for deep-sea sediments. A constant negative electrical potential is then applied to the indium tin oxide electrode, with a magnitude ranging from −0.2 volts to −0.4 volts relative to the Ag/AgCl reference electrode. This potential is maintained for a period of at least two hours, during which time viable microorganisms in suspension are attracted to the electrode surface and become immobilized. The immobilization is not due to electrophoretic forces, as dead cells and non-biological particles do not exhibit the same behavior, nor is it attributable to electroosmotic flow or ionic migration, as the phenomenon persists even in the absence of significant ionic gradients. Instead, the immobilization appears to result from a physiological response of living cells to the weak negative potential, possibly involving the secretion of adhesive extracellular structures such as pilus-like filaments that mediate direct contact with the electrode surface. The process is effective across a wide range of microbial taxa, including Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, actinomycetes, and extremophiles adapted to low-nutrient environments. Following the immobilization period, the electrode is rinsed multiple times with the same buffer solution to remove unattached particles, leaving behind a layer of viable microorganisms firmly adhered to the electrode surface. The immobilized cells remain metabolically active, as demonstrated by the production of formazan crystals upon exposure to the respiratory dye CTC, indicating ongoing electron transport chain activity. The immobilization is stable under ambient conditions and resistant to mechanical disruption, such as scraping with a rubber cell scraper, confirming that the attachment is not a simple physical adhesion but a biologically mediated interaction.

### <Method for Preparing Living Microorganisms>

The method for preparing living microorganisms for retrieval following immobilization involves the application of a high-frequency triangular wave potential to the electrode surface to induce the controlled detachment of the immobilized cells without compromising their viability. After the immobilization step, the electrode is washed thoroughly with fresh buffer solution to remove residual particulates, and the surrounding solution is replaced with a fresh aliquot of the same non-nutritive buffer. A triangular wave potential is then applied to the indium tin oxide electrode, oscillating symmetrically between +10 millivolts and −10 millivolts relative to the Ag/AgCl reference electrode at a frequency of approximately 9 megahertz. This waveform is generated using a function generator connected to a potentiostat through a coiled coaxial cable, which acts as an impedance-matching element to convert the input rectangular wave into a smooth triangular output. The application of this high-frequency potential results in the rapid and reversible oscillation of the local electric field at the electrode-solution interface, which disrupts the adhesive interactions between the microbial cells and the electrode surface. The mechanism of detachment is not fully understood but is believed to involve the periodic modulation of surface charge density, the insertion of water molecules into the interfacial layer, and the mechanical vibration of the extracellular filaments that mediate attachment. The detachment process is non-destructive, as evidenced by the high survival rates of recovered cells, which exceed 80% in most cases and remain comparable to pre-attachment viability levels. The duration of the triangular wave application is optimized between 30 and 60 minutes, with longer durations yielding near-complete detachment. The detached microorganisms are collected from the surrounding solution by gentle centrifugation or filtration and may be concentrated for downstream applications. The method may be repeated iteratively by reapplying the constant negative potential to the same electrode surface, followed by another round of high-frequency detachment, to enrich for rare or slow-growing taxa that may have been initially underrepresented. The entire process can be performed under aerobic or anaerobic conditions, depending on the environmental origin of the sample, and is compatible with a wide range of salinities, pH levels, and temperatures. The retrieved microorganisms retain their native morphology, as confirmed by scanning electron microscopy and atomic force microscopy, and exhibit metabolic activity consistent with their original physiological state, as demonstrated by respiration assays and gene expression profiling. This method thus provides a complete workflow for the preparation of viable, phylogenetically representative microbial populations from complex environmental matrices without the use of chemical agents, mechanical disruption, or cultivation-based enrichment.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

A suspension of garden soil was prepared by mixing 0.1 grams of surface soil with 1 milliliter of phosphate-buffered saline lacking calcium and magnesium ions. The mixture was vortexed for five minutes at maximum speed to disperse microbial cells from soil aggregates. The resulting suspension was diluted to a concentration of 50 micrograms per 5 milliliters and introduced into a three-electrode chamber system comprising a patterned indium tin oxide electrode on a glass substrate, a platinum ring counter electrode, and an Ag/AgCl reference electrode. The chamber was sealed and maintained at room temperature. A constant negative potential of −0.4 volts relative to the Ag/AgCl reference electrode was applied to the indium tin oxide electrode for a period of 24 hours. After this time, the electrode was rinsed three times with fresh phosphate-buffered saline to remove unattached particles. Confocal laser scanning microscopy revealed the presence of densely packed microbial cells exclusively on the indium tin oxide regions of the electrode, with no attachment observed on the surrounding silicon dioxide-coated insulating areas. The cells were stained with the Bacstain CTC kit, which revealed red fluorescent formazan crystals localized at the cell periphery, indicating active respiratory metabolism. Dead Escherichia coli cells, fixed in 70% ethanol and subjected to the same protocol, showed no attachment to the electrode surface, confirming the specificity of the process for living cells. The immobilized microbial population was then subjected to a 9-megahertz triangular wave potential oscillating between +10 millivolts and −10 millivolts for 60 minutes. Following detachment, the surrounding solution was collected and analyzed by live/dead staining, which revealed a survival rate of 91% among the recovered cells. Colony-forming unit counts on Luria-Bertani agar plates demonstrated that 66% of the original viable population was successfully retrieved. The results demonstrated that the method could capture and release a viable microbial population from raw soil without the use of chemical lysis or mechanical disruption.

### Example 2

A sample of deep-sea sediment was collected from a depth of 1,176 meters in Sagami Bay, Japan, using an unmanned submersible. The surface layer of sediment was transferred to a sterile container and stored at 4 degrees Celsius until use. A suspension was prepared by mixing 0.1 grams of sediment with 1 milliliter of artificial seawater formulated to contain 30 grams per liter of sodium chloride, 0.7 grams per liter of potassium chloride, 5.3 grams per liter of magnesium sulfate heptahydrate, 10.8 grams per liter of magnesium chloride hexahydrate, and 1 gram per liter of calcium chloride dihydrate. The mixture was vortexed for five minutes and diluted to a concentration of 50 micrograms per 5 milliliters. The suspension was introduced into a large-scale electrode chamber system comprising a 125 by 85 millimeter indium tin oxide electrode on a glass substrate, with 30-millimeter-diameter platinum and Ag/AgCl electrodes positioned on the lid. A constant negative potential of −0.3 volts relative to the Ag/AgCl reference electrode was applied for two hours at 4 degrees Celsius. After washing the electrode three times with fresh artificial seawater, a 9-megahertz triangular wave potential oscillating between +10 millivolts and −10 millivolts was applied for 30 minutes. The detached microbial suspension was collected and subjected to live/dead staining, revealing a survival rate of 89%. The microbial population was then re-immobilized on a second identical electrode system using the same parameters, and a second round of detachment was performed. The resulting purified microbial suspension was used for DNA extraction using the CTAB method. Polymerase chain reaction amplification of the 16S ribosomal RNA gene yielded a clear amplicon, whereas direct DNA extraction from the original sediment sample failed to produce any amplification product, indicating the presence of PCR inhibitors in the sediment. Phylogenetic analysis of the cloned sequences revealed the presence of 19 bacterial phyla and 23 classes, with Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Actinobacteria being the most abundant. The composition of the electrically retrieved population overlapped with 95% of the phylotypes detected in direct sediment DNA extraction, demonstrating that the method captured a representative sample of the native microbial community.

### Example 3

A series of experiments were conducted to determine the optimal voltage for microbial attachment across different environmental samples. Garden soil suspensions in phosphate-buffered saline were subjected to constant potentials ranging from −0.1 volts to −0.6 volts relative to the Ag/AgCl reference electrode for 24 hours at room temperature. Microbial attachment was quantified by counting cells in randomly selected 50 by 50 micrometer regions under a confocal microscope after staining with SYTO9. Attachment increased linearly from −0.1 volts to −0.4 volts, reaching a maximum at −0.4 volts, beyond which no further increase was observed. Similar results were obtained with deep-sea sediment in artificial seawater, where maximum attachment occurred at −0.3 volts, likely due to the higher ionic strength of the medium altering the electrostatic double layer. Experiments with pure cultures of Escherichia coli, Bacillus subtilis, Shewanella oneidensis, Kocuria rosea, Bacillus halodurans, and Shewanella violacea confirmed that each species exhibited a characteristic optimal attachment potential, ranging from −0.3 to −0.4 volts, regardless of Gram status or environmental origin. The attachment was consistently absent when the potential was set to positive values, and no attachment occurred in the absence of any applied potential. These results demonstrated that the attachment mechanism is voltage-dependent and specific to a narrow range of negative potentials, suggesting a threshold-based physiological response rather than a passive electrostatic phenomenon.

### Example 4

The effect of buffer composition on microbial attachment was evaluated using garden soil suspensions in four different solutions: phosphate-buffered saline, MOPS buffer, tricine buffer, and Luria-Bertani medium. All buffers were adjusted to a pH of 7.0 and maintained at room temperature. A constant potential of −0.4 volts was applied for 24 hours. Microbial attachment was observed in all non-nutritive buffers, with the highest density occurring in phosphate-buffered saline. In contrast, no significant attachment was observed in Luria-Bertani medium, despite the presence of viable cells as confirmed by live/dead staining. This result indicated that the presence of organic nutrients in the medium suppressed the attachment response, possibly by altering microbial metabolism or masking the electrochemical signal. Further experiments with 280 millimolar glucose solution showed no enhancement of attachment compared to buffer alone, confirming that the effect was not due to osmotic pressure or carbon source availability. These findings established that the immobilization process requires a non-nutritive environment, suggesting that the microbial response is triggered by the absence of external energy sources, prompting cells to interact with the electrode as an alternative electron acceptor or energy sink.

### Example 5

The viability of microorganisms following detachment was assessed using multiple complementary methods. A live/dead bacterial viability kit was used to stain cells before and after detachment, with confocal microscopy used to quantify the proportion of live cells. For garden soil, the survival rate was 91% after ±10 millivolts 9-megahertz triangular wave application, 58% after ±6 millivolts, and 17% after ±8 millivolts, indicating that the amplitude of the waveform influenced cytotoxicity. Higher amplitudes (±10 millivolts) resulted in near-complete detachment with minimal cell death, while intermediate amplitudes (±8 millivolts) led to partial detachment and increased cell lysis, suggesting that the detachment mechanism is sensitive to the energy input. Colony-forming unit counts on agar plates confirmed these results, with 88% of Escherichia coli and 48% of Bacillus subtilis recovered as viable colonies after the detachment protocol. The survival rates of the detached cells were comparable to those of control cells that had never been exposed to electrical fields. Furthermore, the morphology of the detached cells, as observed by atomic force microscopy and scanning electron microscopy, remained unchanged, with no signs of membrane rupture, cell shrinkage, or filament degradation. These results demonstrated that the high-frequency triangular wave potential is a non-destructive method for releasing immobilized microorganisms, preserving both structural integrity and metabolic function.

### Example 6

The method was adapted for use under anaerobic conditions to isolate microorganisms from anoxic environments. A sealed anaerobic jar was modified by drilling a small hole through the lid, through which three conductive wires were passed and sealed with epoxy resin to maintain an oxygen-free environment. The three-electrode chamber system was placed inside the jar, and the entire setup was flushed with a gas mixture of 5% carbon dioxide and less than 1% oxygen for 60 minutes. Garden soil suspensions in phosphate-buffered saline were introduced into the chamber, and a constant negative potential of −0.4 volts was applied for 24 hours. Microbial attachment was observed to be enhanced under anaerobic conditions compared to aerobic controls, with a 68-fold increase in cell density on the electrode surface. After detachment using the 9-megahertz triangular wave potential, the recovered cells exhibited higher metabolic activity in respiration assays, suggesting that the anaerobic environment promoted the expression of alternative electron transport pathways. The phylogenetic composition of the anaerobically retrieved population differed from the aerobic counterpart, with an enrichment of Firmicutes and candidate phyla associated with fermentative metabolism. These results demonstrated that the method is compatible with anaerobic conditions and can be used to selectively isolate microorganisms adapted to low-oxygen environments, expanding its applicability to subsurface soils, marine sediments, and other anoxic habitats.

### Example 7

The method was scaled up for the retrieval of microbial populations from larger environmental samples. A 5-gram sample of deep-sea sediment was suspended in 125 milliliters of artificial seawater and introduced into a large electrode chamber system with a 125 by 85 millimeter indium tin oxide electrode. A constant potential of −0.3 volts was applied for two hours, followed by washing and a 30-minute detachment using the 9-megahertz triangular wave potential. The detached suspension was collected, and the process was repeated on a second identical electrode system to enrich the microbial population. After two rounds of attachment and detachment, a total of 1.3 × 10⁸ viable microbial cells were recovered from the 5-gram sediment sample. DNA was extracted from this enriched population using the CTAB method, and the 16S rRNA gene was amplified and sequenced. Phylogenetic analysis revealed 19 bacterial phyla and 23 classes, with 95% of the phylotypes matching those detected in direct sediment DNA extraction. In contrast, direct extraction from the same sediment sample yielded no PCR product due to the presence of humic acid inhibitors. The results demonstrated that the method not only overcomes the limitations of direct DNA extraction but also enables the enrichment of microbial biomass to levels sufficient for comprehensive metagenomic analysis. The scalability of the method was further confirmed by its successful application to multiple sediment samples from different geographic locations, each yielding consistent results in terms of microbial diversity and viability.

### (1) Various Buffers

A systematic evaluation of buffer composition was conducted to determine the influence of ionic strength, pH, and buffering capacity on microbial attachment and detachment. Garden soil suspensions were prepared in phosphate-buffered saline, MOPS buffer, tricine buffer, HEPES buffer, and distilled water, all adjusted to pH 7.0. The constant negative potential of −0.4 volts was applied for 24 hours, and the number of attached cells was quantified. Maximum attachment was observed in phosphate-buffered saline and MOPS buffer, with comparable densities of immobilized microorganisms. Tricine buffer showed slightly lower attachment efficiency, while HEPES buffer resulted in a moderate reduction, and distilled water produced the lowest attachment rate. These differences were attributed to the ionic strength of the buffers, with higher ionic strength enhancing the stability of the electrical double layer and facilitating the interaction between the electrode and microbial surfaces. The pH of the buffer had no significant effect when maintained between 6.5 and 7.5, but attachment was reduced at pH values below 6.0 or above 8.0, suggesting that extreme pH conditions may alter cell surface charge or membrane integrity. The detachment efficiency was unaffected by buffer composition when the 9-megahertz triangular wave potential was applied, indicating that the detachment mechanism is robust across a range of ionic environments. These findings established that the method is compatible with a variety of buffer systems, allowing for flexibility in experimental design depending on the source environment and downstream applications.

### (2) Artificial Seawater

The performance of the method in artificial seawater was evaluated to assess its applicability to marine environments. Deep-sea sediment suspensions were prepared in artificial seawater formulated to mimic natural oceanic conditions, with sodium chloride, potassium chloride, magnesium sulfate, magnesium chloride, and calcium chloride at concentrations corresponding to seawater salinity. A constant potential of −0.3 volts was applied for two hours at 4 degrees Celsius, followed by detachment using the 9-megahertz triangular wave potential. The attachment efficiency in artificial seawater was comparable to that observed in phosphate-buffered saline, with a slight shift in optimal potential from −0.4 volts to −0.3 volts due to the higher ionic strength of the medium. The survival rate of detached cells was 89%, and the phylogenetic diversity of the recovered population matched that of direct sediment DNA extraction. The method was also tested in natural seawater collected from the same site, yielding identical results. These findings confirmed that the method is effective in high-salinity environments and can be applied to marine sediments, hydrothermal vents, and other saline habitats without modification. The compatibility of the method with artificial seawater further enhances its utility for culturing marine extremophiles and studying microbial communities in oceanic ecosystems.

### Example 8

The spatial resolution of the method was demonstrated using a patterned microelectrode array composed of 5-micrometer-diameter circular indium tin oxide islands separated by silicon dioxide insulating regions. A suspension of Escherichia coli in phosphate-buffered saline was introduced into the chamber, and a constant potential of −0.4 volts was applied for 15 hours. Confocal microscopy revealed that microbial cells attached exclusively to the conductive indium tin oxide regions, with no attachment observed on the surrounding insulating areas. The cells formed dense, localized clusters precisely aligned with the electrode geometry, demonstrating that the method can be used to create high-resolution microbial patterns. The same pattern was maintained after detachment and re-immobilization, indicating that the process is reversible and repeatable. This capability opens new possibilities for the construction of microbial biosensors, bioelectronic interfaces, and synthetic microbial consortia with defined spatial organization.

### Example 9

The method was tested for its ability to retrieve microorganisms from a soil sample contaminated with heavy metals. A soil sample from an industrial site was spiked with lead, cadmium, and arsenic at concentrations exceeding regulatory limits. The sample was suspended in phosphate-buffered saline, and the standard attachment and detachment protocol was applied. Despite the presence of toxic metals, viable microbial cells were successfully immobilized and retrieved with a survival rate of 82%. Phylogenetic analysis revealed an enrichment of metal-resistant taxa, including Pseudomonas, Bacillus, and Arthrobacter species, suggesting that the method not only preserves viability but also selects for environmentally relevant microbial populations. This result demonstrated the method’s robustness in contaminated environments and its potential for bioremediation studies and microbial ecology in polluted ecosystems.

### Example 10

The method was applied to a soil sample from a permafrost region to isolate psychrophilic microorganisms. The sample was suspended in phosphate-buffered saline and maintained at 4 degrees Celsius throughout the procedure. A constant potential of −0.4 volts was applied for 24 hours, followed by detachment using the 9-megahertz triangular wave potential. The recovered microbial population exhibited high metabolic activity at low temperatures, as confirmed by respiration assays at 4 degrees Celsius. Phylogenetic analysis revealed the presence of multiple cold-adapted taxa, including members of the genera Psychrobacter and Pseudomonas, which are known to be dominant in permafrost environments. The method successfully captured these slow-growing, low-temperature-adapted organisms without the need for extended incubation or specialized media, demonstrating its utility for studying extremophilic microbial communities in frozen habitats.
