# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The regulation of cellular proliferation and survival in malignant tissues has long been recognized as a central mechanism underlying the progression of cancer. Among the myriad of intracellular signaling pathways that govern these processes, cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) has emerged as a pivotal second messenger with context-dependent roles in both promoting and suppressing tumor growth. Historically, the synthesis of cAMP has been attributed almost exclusively to transmembrane adenylyl cyclases (tmACs), which are activated by G protein-coupled receptors located at the plasma membrane. These tmAC-derived pools of cAMP have been extensively studied for their influence on downstream effectors such as protein kinase A (PKA) and exchange protein activated by cAMP (EPAC), with outcomes ranging from cell cycle arrest to enhanced proliferation depending on cell type, microenvironment, and receptor activation status. However, this paradigm has been fundamentally challenged by the discovery of soluble adenylyl cyclase (sAC), a distinct isoform of adenylyl cyclase that is not membrane-bound and is instead distributed throughout the cytoplasm and nucleus, where it responds directly to bicarbonate ions and calcium. Unlike tmACs, sAC is not regulated by G proteins or neurotransmitters but rather by metabolic and ionic signals, positioning it as a unique sensor of intracellular physiological states such as pH, CO₂ levels, and mitochondrial activity.

In the context of prostate cancer, the role of cAMP signaling has been particularly enigmatic. While some studies have suggested that elevated cAMP levels inhibit proliferation through PKA-mediated suppression of the androgen receptor pathway, others have demonstrated that cAMP can promote tumor growth via EPAC-dependent activation of the B-Raf/MEK/ERK cascade. These conflicting findings may stem not only from differences in experimental models but also from the failure to distinguish between cAMP generated by tmACs versus sAC. The spatial and temporal compartmentalization of cAMP signaling has become increasingly recognized as a critical determinant of cellular response, yet most pharmacological and genetic tools available prior to this invention have lacked the specificity to selectively target sAC without affecting tmACs or other unrelated enzymes. Consequently, the functional contribution of sAC to prostate carcinogenesis remained unexplored and largely speculative.

Prostate cancer is one of the most commonly diagnosed malignancies in men worldwide, with a significant proportion of cases progressing to castration-resistant disease despite androgen deprivation therapy. The molecular drivers of this transition remain incompletely understood, and current therapeutic strategies often fail to address the underlying metabolic and signaling adaptations that sustain tumor cell survival. Recent advances in immunohistochemical techniques and isoform-specific molecular probes have enabled the precise localization and quantification of sAC protein expression in human tissue specimens. Notably, preliminary observations revealed that sAC is not only expressed in normal prostate epithelial cells but is significantly upregulated in malignant glands, particularly at the invasive front of tumors near the prostatic capsule. This pattern of expression—localized to regions of active invasion rather than uniformly distributed throughout the tumor mass—suggested a potential role for sAC in facilitating tumor cell migration, survival under metabolic stress, or evasion of host immune surveillance. Furthermore, the subcellular distribution of sAC, observed in both cytoplasmic and nuclear compartments, hinted at its involvement in multiple regulatory processes, including gene expression, cytoskeletal dynamics, and cell cycle progression.

Despite these compelling observations, no study had previously established a causal link between sAC activity and the proliferation or survival of prostate carcinoma cells. The absence of selective inhibitors capable of distinguishing sAC from tmACs had severely limited experimental inquiry into its function. Although KH7, a small molecule inhibitor of sAC, had been reported to exhibit selectivity over tmACs and soluble guanylyl cyclase in vitro, its application in cancer biology had been confined to studies of sperm motility, renal tubular function, and neuronal signaling. Its potential as a therapeutic agent in solid tumors, particularly in prostate cancer, had never been systematically evaluated. Moreover, the downstream effectors mediating sAC’s influence on cell cycle regulation in epithelial malignancies remained entirely unknown. The possibility that sAC might regulate proliferation through an EPAC-dependent, PKA-independent pathway—distinct from the classical tmAC/cAMP/PKA axis—had not been tested. Similarly, the molecular mechanisms by which sAC might control the G₂/M transition, a critical checkpoint in mitotic progression, had not been investigated in any cancer type.

The clinical implications of such a discovery are profound. If sAC were found to be a necessary driver of prostate cancer cell proliferation and survival, its inhibition could represent a novel therapeutic strategy, particularly for tumors that have become resistant to androgen-targeted therapies. Unlike conventional approaches that target hormone receptors or tyrosine kinases, inhibition of sAC would interfere with a metabolic sensor that integrates cellular energy status, pH, and bicarbonate availability—conditions that are often altered in the tumor microenvironment due to hypoxia, glycolytic metabolism, and acidosis. Targeting sAC would therefore exploit a fundamental vulnerability inherent to the cancer cell’s altered physiology rather than a single genetic mutation. Furthermore, because sAC is expressed at low levels in most normal tissues and is not essential for viability in adult organisms under standard conditions, its inhibition might offer a favorable therapeutic window with minimal systemic toxicity.

This invention arises from the unexpected discovery that sAC is not merely a passive biomarker of prostate cancer but an active and indispensable regulator of tumor cell proliferation and survival. Through a combination of high-resolution immunohistochemical analysis of human prostate tissue specimens, selective pharmacological inhibition, and genetic knockdown approaches, it was demonstrated that sAC is overexpressed in malignant compared to benign prostatic tissue, with a striking enrichment at the invasive tumor margin. Functional studies using the sAC-selective inhibitor KH7 and two independent methods of sAC gene silencing—siRNA and adenoviral shRNA—revealed that suppression of sAC activity leads to a profound inhibition of cell proliferation, induction of apoptosis, and arrest of the cell cycle at the G₂/M phase. Mechanistically, this effect was traced to the disruption of the EPAC/Rap1/B-Raf signaling axis, which is required for the expression of cyclin B₁ and phosphorylation of CDK1, two essential regulators of mitotic entry. Critically, these effects were independent of PKA, androgen receptor status, and tmAC activity, establishing sAC as a novel, non-redundant node in prostate cancer signaling. The invention therefore encompasses not only the identification of sAC as a therapeutic target in prostate cancer but also the development of a method for its selective inhibition to suppress tumor growth and induce cell death, thereby providing a foundation for the design of next-generation therapies for advanced and treatment-resistant prostate malignancies.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

This invention relates to a novel method for treating prostate carcinoma by selectively inhibiting soluble adenylyl cyclase (sAC), a previously unrecognized driver of tumor cell proliferation and survival. The invention is based on the discovery that sAC is overexpressed in malignant prostate tissue compared to benign prostatic epithelium, with a distinct pattern of upregulation at the invasive edge of the tumor, and that its enzymatic activity is essential for the maintenance of cell cycle progression through the G₂/M checkpoint. Inhibition of sAC, whether through pharmacological means or genetic knockdown, results in a significant reduction in intracellular cAMP levels, suppression of the EPAC/Rap1/B-Raf signaling pathway, downregulation of cyclin B₁ and phospho-CDK1, and subsequent arrest of cells in the G₂ phase of the cell cycle, culminating in apoptotic cell death. Importantly, these effects occur independently of protein kinase A (PKA) activity and are observed in both androgen-sensitive and androgen-insensitive prostate cancer cell lines, indicating that sAC functions as a downstream effector of oncogenic signaling that bypasses traditional androgen receptor dependence.

The invention provides a therapeutic strategy that targets sAC using selective inhibitors, such as KH7, or through the use of nucleic acid-based agents designed to silence sAC expression, including small interfering RNA (siRNA) and short hairpin RNA (shRNA) delivered via viral or non-viral vectors. The method is applicable to a broad spectrum of prostate carcinomas, including those that have progressed to castration-resistant disease, and is particularly effective in tumors exhibiting high levels of sAC expression, as determined by immunohistochemical analysis using isoform-specific antibodies. The inhibition of sAC does not affect the viability of normal prostate epithelial cells to the same extent, suggesting a favorable therapeutic index. The mechanism of action involves the disruption of EPAC-mediated activation of Rap1, which in turn leads to reduced phosphorylation of B-Raf and diminished expression of key mitotic regulators, thereby preventing the transition from G₂ to M phase. This pathway is distinct from the classical cAMP/PKA signaling cascade and is not influenced by transmembrane adenylyl cyclase activity, making sAC a unique and specific target for intervention.

The invention further includes diagnostic methods for identifying patients who are most likely to benefit from sAC-targeted therapy, based on the detection of elevated sAC protein levels in prostate tissue biopsies, particularly in the peritumoral and capsular regions. The quantification of sAC expression using the H-score system, which integrates both the intensity and distribution of immunohistochemical staining, enables stratification of patients into high- and low-risk categories for disease progression. The invention also encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising sAC inhibitors in combination with other anti-cancer agents, such as androgen receptor antagonists, chemotherapeutics, or immune checkpoint inhibitors, to enhance therapeutic efficacy and prevent resistance. Additionally, the invention provides for the use of sAC inhibition in the prevention of tumor invasion and metastasis, given the observed overexpression of sAC at the leading edge of tumors and its role in sustaining proliferative capacity under microenvironmental stress conditions. This invention represents a paradigm shift in the understanding of cAMP signaling in prostate cancer, revealing sAC as a central metabolic regulator of tumor cell cycle progression and establishing its inhibition as a viable and novel therapeutic modality.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention disclosed herein is directed to a method for the treatment of prostate carcinoma through the selective inhibition of soluble adenylyl cyclase (sAC), a previously unrecognized and essential mediator of tumor cell proliferation and survival. The method is based on the discovery that sAC is not merely a bystander in prostate cancer biology but an active and indispensable component of a signaling network that sustains the G₂/M transition in the cell cycle, thereby enabling continuous tumor growth. This discovery was made possible through the application of highly specific molecular tools and rigorous experimental validation across multiple biological systems, including human tissue specimens, primary cell cultures, and genetically modified cell lines. The invention encompasses not only the identification of sAC as a therapeutic target but also the development of a comprehensive strategy for its inhibition, including pharmacological agents, nucleic acid-based silencing techniques, and diagnostic criteria for patient selection.

In the context of prostate cancer, the traditional view of cAMP signaling has been dominated by the role of transmembrane adenylyl cyclases (tmACs), which are activated by G protein-coupled receptors at the plasma membrane and generate cAMP pools that regulate downstream effectors such as protein kinase A (PKA) and exchange protein activated by cAMP (EPAC). However, these models have failed to account for the spatial and functional compartmentalization of cAMP signaling within the cell. Soluble adenylyl cyclase, in contrast, is uniquely positioned to generate cAMP in response to intracellular cues such as bicarbonate concentration, calcium levels, and mitochondrial activity, making it a direct sensor of metabolic and ionic changes that occur in the tumor microenvironment. The present invention demonstrates that sAC is overexpressed in human prostate carcinoma tissues compared to benign prostatic glands, with a pronounced enrichment at the invasive margin of the tumor, a region associated with aggressive behavior and poor clinical outcomes. This localized overexpression suggests that sAC is not merely a marker of malignancy but a functional driver of tumor invasion and proliferation.

To establish the causal role of sAC in prostate cancer progression, a series of functional experiments were conducted using two well-characterized human prostate carcinoma cell lines: LNCaP, which is androgen-sensitive, and PC3, which is androgen-insensitive. Both cell lines exhibited robust expression of sAC protein, significantly higher than that observed in normal prostate epithelial cells (PNT2). Pharmacological inhibition of sAC using KH7, a compound known to selectively inhibit sAC at concentrations of 10–30 μM without affecting tmACs or soluble guanylyl cyclase, resulted in a dose-dependent reduction in intracellular cAMP levels and a marked suppression of cell proliferation. This effect was not observed when cells were treated with KH7.15, an inactive structural analog of KH7, confirming that the anti-proliferative effects were specifically attributable to sAC inhibition and not to off-target pharmacological actions. Further validation was achieved through genetic knockdown of sAC using two independent methods: transient transfection with siRNA targeting the human sAC mRNA sequence and stable transduction with an adenoviral vector expressing shRNA against sAC. Both approaches resulted in a reduction of sAC protein expression by more than 80%, as confirmed by Western blot analysis, and recapitulated the anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic effects observed with KH7. These findings collectively demonstrate that sAC is not only overexpressed in prostate cancer but is functionally required for the maintenance of tumor cell viability and growth.

The mechanism by which sAC supports proliferation was investigated through detailed analysis of the cell cycle. Flow cytometric analysis of DNA content following propidium iodide staining revealed that inhibition of sAC led to a significant accumulation of cells in the G₂ phase of the cell cycle, accompanied by a corresponding decrease in the proportion of cells in G₁. This G₂ arrest was observed as early as 12 hours after treatment with KH7 and reached maximal levels by 24 hours, preceding the onset of apoptosis, as evidenced by the delayed appearance of cleaved caspase-3 and increased sub-G₁ population. This temporal sequence indicates that cell cycle arrest is the primary event triggered by sAC inhibition, with apoptosis occurring as a secondary consequence of prolonged mitotic arrest. To determine the molecular mediators of this arrest, the expression of key regulators of the G₂/M transition was examined. Cyclin B₁ and CDK1, two proteins essential for the initiation of mitosis, were found to be significantly downregulated following sAC inhibition, both at the protein level and in their phosphorylated, active forms. In contrast, the expression of cyclins D₁, D₃, and E₂, which govern the G₁/S transition, remained unchanged, confirming the specificity of sAC’s role in the G₂/M checkpoint. These findings establish that sAC functions as a critical upstream regulator of the machinery required for mitotic entry, and its inhibition disrupts this process without affecting earlier phases of the cell cycle.

The downstream signaling pathway responsible for mediating sAC’s effect on cyclin B₁ and CDK1 expression was identified through a systematic investigation of the two primary cAMP effectors: PKA and EPAC. Treatment with the PKA-specific activator N⁶-benzoyl-cAMP had no effect on cell proliferation or cell cycle distribution, even in the presence of sAC inhibition. Similarly, two structurally distinct PKA inhibitors, H-89 and (Rₚ)-cAMP-S, failed to replicate the anti-proliferative effects of sAC inhibition, indicating that PKA is not involved in this signaling axis. In contrast, inhibition of sAC led to a marked reduction in the levels of GTP-bound Rap1, the active form of this small GTPase, which is a direct downstream target of EPAC. This reduction was accompanied by decreased phosphorylation of B-Raf, a kinase activated by Rap1 and a key component of the MAPK signaling cascade. The use of the EPAC-selective activator 8-pCPT-cAMP reversed the effects of sAC inhibition, restoring Rap1-GTP levels, B-Raf phosphorylation, cyclin B₁ expression, and cell proliferation, thereby establishing EPAC/Rap1/B-Raf as the essential signaling pathway downstream of sAC. Importantly, total levels of Rap1 and B-Raf proteins were unaffected, indicating that sAC inhibition specifically modulates the activation state of these proteins rather than their expression. This finding reveals a novel signaling axis—sAC → cAMP → EPAC → Rap1 → B-Raf → MEK → ERK → cyclin B₁/CDK1—that is distinct from the classical tmAC/PKA pathway and is uniquely required for the proliferation of prostate carcinoma cells.

The clinical relevance of this discovery is underscored by the observation that sAC overexpression is not uniform across all prostate cancer cases but is particularly pronounced at the invasive front of the tumor, adjacent to the prostatic capsule. This spatial pattern suggests that sAC may play a role in facilitating tumor cell migration and invasion, potentially by enabling cells to adapt to the metabolic stress encountered at the tumor-host interface. The fact that sAC inhibition suppresses proliferation in both androgen-sensitive and androgen-insensitive cell lines further indicates that this pathway operates independently of androgen receptor signaling, making it a promising target for castration-resistant prostate cancer, a disease state that remains largely incurable and for which few effective therapeutic options exist. The absence of sAC overexpression in benign prostatic tissue, coupled with the minimal impact of sAC inhibition on normal epithelial cell viability, suggests that targeting sAC may offer a high degree of tumor specificity with low systemic toxicity.

The invention further encompasses the development of diagnostic methods for identifying patients who are most likely to respond to sAC-targeted therapy. Immunohistochemical analysis of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded prostate tissue sections using a monoclonal antibody specific for sAC (clone R21-IHC) allows for the quantification of sAC expression using the H-score system, which integrates the percentage of cells staining at three intensity levels (1+, 2+, and 3+) into a single numerical value ranging from 0 to 300. Tumors with an H-score above a predetermined threshold, typically 180, are identified as high-sAC expressers and are considered optimal candidates for treatment with sAC inhibitors. The localization of staining—particularly the presence of strong cytoplasmic and nuclear staining at the capsular edge—is used as an additional criterion for patient stratification. This diagnostic approach enables precision medicine by ensuring that therapy is directed toward those patients whose tumors are biologically dependent on sAC activity, thereby maximizing therapeutic efficacy and minimizing unnecessary treatment in non-responsive individuals.

The invention also includes pharmaceutical compositions comprising sAC inhibitors in combination with other anti-cancer agents. For example, sAC inhibitors may be administered in conjunction with androgen receptor antagonists such as enzalutamide or apalutamide to simultaneously target both androgen-dependent and sAC-dependent proliferation pathways. Similarly, combination with chemotherapeutic agents such as docetaxel or cabazitaxel may enhance cytotoxicity by inducing mitotic catastrophe in cells already arrested in G₂ phase. The use of sAC inhibitors in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as pembrolizumab or nivolumab, is also contemplated, as the induction of apoptosis and release of tumor antigens following sAC inhibition may enhance the immunogenicity of the tumor microenvironment. These combination therapies are designed to overcome resistance mechanisms and improve long-term survival outcomes.

The method of administration for sAC inhibitors may include oral, intravenous, intraperitoneal, or intratumoral delivery, depending on the formulation and the stage of disease. Solid dosage forms, such as tablets or capsules, may be used for chronic administration, while injectable formulations may be preferred for advanced or metastatic disease. The dosage range for sAC inhibitors such as KH7 or its derivatives is expected to be between 0.1 and 50 mg/kg body weight per day, with adjustments made based on patient weight, renal function, and tumor burden. The invention further encompasses the development of novel sAC inhibitors with improved pharmacokinetic properties, including enhanced bioavailability, blood-brain barrier penetration, and metabolic stability, as well as the use of nanoparticle-based delivery systems to achieve targeted delivery to prostate tumor tissue while minimizing exposure to healthy organs.

The invention also provides for the use of sAC inhibition in the prevention of metastasis. Given the overexpression of sAC at the invasive margin of tumors and its role in sustaining proliferation under metabolic stress, it is hypothesized that sAC enables tumor cells to survive the transition from the primary site to distant organs. Inhibition of sAC may therefore reduce the incidence of lymph node and bone metastases, which are the leading causes of mortality in prostate cancer patients. Preclinical models of metastatic prostate cancer, including orthotopic implantation and tail vein injection assays, are used to validate this hypothesis, with endpoints including tumor burden, number of metastatic foci, and overall survival.

In summary, the invention represents a fundamental advance in the understanding of prostate cancer biology by identifying soluble adenylyl cyclase as a critical regulator of tumor cell proliferation and survival through a novel EPAC/Rap1/B-Raf signaling axis. The method of inhibiting sAC to induce G₂/M cell cycle arrest and apoptosis provides a powerful new therapeutic strategy for the treatment of prostate carcinoma, particularly in cases that are resistant to conventional androgen-targeted therapies. The invention further provides diagnostic tools for patient selection and pharmaceutical compositions for combination therapy, establishing a comprehensive framework for the clinical translation of this discovery.

### Example 1

In this example, the expression and localization of soluble adenylyl cyclase (sAC) in human prostate tissue specimens were analyzed using immunohistochemical techniques to determine whether sAC is differentially expressed in benign versus malignant prostatic glands. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections, 5 micrometers in thickness, were obtained from a cohort of patients diagnosed with prostate carcinoma and from age-matched controls with benign prostatic hyperplasia. All tissue samples were processed using a BOND-III Autostainer (Leica Microsystems, Buffalo Grove, IL) with the manufacturer’s Heat-Induced Epitope Retrieval 1 protocol to ensure optimal antigen exposure. The primary antibody used was a monoclonal mouse anti-sAC antibody (clone R21-IHC, CEP Biotech, Inc., Tamarac, FL), which recognizes the somatic isoform of sAC and has been validated for specificity in human tissue applications. The antibody was applied at a dilution of 1:750 and incubated for 60 minutes at room temperature. Following primary antibody binding, a post-primary alkaline phosphatase amplification step was performed for 20 minutes to enhance signal intensity, followed by development with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen for 10 minutes. The sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted under coverslips for microscopic evaluation.

All slides were evaluated by a board-certified urologic pathologist with over two decades of experience in prostate cancer diagnostics, who was blinded to the clinical outcomes of the patients. The pathologist assessed the intensity of sAC staining in both benign and malignant glandular structures, categorizing the staining as weak (1+), moderate (2+), or strong (3+) based on the density of the brown DAB precipitate relative to background tissue. A histology scoring system known as the H-score was then applied to quantify the overall expression level of sAC in each tissue sample. The H-score was calculated using the formula: H-score = (% of cells staining 1+) × 1 + (% of cells staining 2+) × 2 + (% of cells staining 3+) × 3, resulting in a numerical value ranging from 0 to 300. This method accounts for both the proportion of positive cells and the intensity of staining, providing a more comprehensive measure of protein expression than simple semi-quantitative scoring systems.

The analysis revealed that all prostate glands, whether benign or malignant, exhibited at least weak cytoplasmic staining for sAC, indicating that the enzyme is constitutively expressed in normal prostatic epithelium. However, the mean H-score for malignant glands was significantly higher than that of benign glands, with values of 189.2 ± 12.4 and 144.3 ± 9.8, respectively (p < 0.01). This difference was consistent across all tissue samples, regardless of Gleason score or tumor stage, suggesting that sAC overexpression is a common feature of prostate carcinoma. Furthermore, the subcellular localization of sAC was examined in detail. In both benign and malignant tissues, the predominant pattern of staining was diffuse cytoplasmic, with occasional instances of nuclear staining observed in a subset of tumor cells. Apical or luminal border staining was also noted in some glands, particularly those with well-differentiated architecture. Statistical analysis revealed no significant difference in the distribution pattern of sAC between benign and malignant tissues, indicating that the overexpression is quantitative rather than qualitative.

A particularly striking observation was the spatial heterogeneity of sAC expression within malignant tumors. In many cases, the strongest staining was localized to the periphery of the tumor, adjacent to the prostatic capsule, and in extraprostatic foci of invasion. In contrast, the central regions of the tumor exhibited lower levels of sAC expression, comparable to those seen in benign glands. This pattern was not observed in benign prostatic tissue, where sAC staining was uniformly distributed throughout the glandular structures, with no significant difference between peripheral and central regions. The preferential upregulation of sAC at the invasive edge suggests that the enzyme may play a role in supporting the survival and proliferative capacity of tumor cells as they encounter the physical and metabolic challenges of the surrounding stroma. This finding was corroborated by the observation that tumors with high sAC expression at the capsular margin were more likely to exhibit extracapsular extension and positive surgical margins on pathological examination.

To ensure the specificity of the staining, control experiments were performed. Omission of the primary antibody resulted in complete absence of DAB signal, confirming that the staining was antibody-dependent. Pre-absorption of the R21-IHC antibody with excess recombinant sAC protein completely abolished immunoreactivity, further validating antibody specificity. Additionally, staining with an isotype-matched control mouse IgG yielded no detectable signal. The use of a second monoclonal antibody (clone R40), which recognizes only the testicular isoform of sAC, revealed minimal reactivity in prostate tissue, confirming that the somatic isoform is the predominant form expressed in both benign and malignant prostatic epithelium. These findings collectively demonstrate that sAC is not only expressed in prostate tissue but is significantly and selectively upregulated in prostate carcinoma, with a distinct spatial pattern that correlates with aggressive tumor behavior.

The results of this example establish that sAC overexpression is a consistent and quantifiable feature of prostate cancer, detectable using standard histopathological techniques. The H-score system provides a robust, reproducible metric for assessing sAC expression levels in clinical specimens, enabling its potential use as a prognostic biomarker. The localization of sAC at the invasive front of tumors suggests that its function is not merely supportive of general cell survival but is specifically required for tumor progression and invasion. This sets the stage for the subsequent functional studies, which demonstrate that pharmacological or genetic inhibition of sAC leads to profound anti-proliferative effects in prostate cancer cell lines, thereby validating sAC as a therapeutic target.

### Example 2

In this example, the functional role of soluble adenylyl cyclase (sAC) in the proliferation and survival of human prostate carcinoma cells was investigated using pharmacological inhibition with the selective sAC inhibitor KH7 and its inactive analog KH7.15. Two well-characterized human prostate cancer cell lines, LNCaP and PC3, were chosen for their distinct androgen sensitivity profiles: LNCaP cells are androgen-responsive and express the androgen receptor, whereas PC3 cells are androgen-independent and lack functional androgen receptor expression. These models were selected to determine whether sAC’s role in tumor cell growth is dependent on androgen signaling or represents a more fundamental, universal mechanism of proliferation control. Human normal prostate epithelial cells (PNT2) were included as a non-malignant control to assess the selectivity of sAC inhibition for cancerous versus normal tissue.

Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium supplemented with 5% fetal calf serum and maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Twenty-four hours prior to experimentation, the serum concentration was reduced to 2% to synchronize cell cycle progression and minimize confounding effects of serum-derived growth factors. Cells were then treated with KH7 at concentrations ranging from 1 to 50 μM for 48 hours, with KH7.15 used as a negative control at an identical concentration of 20 μM. At the end of the treatment period, cells were harvested, and both adherent and floating populations were collected for analysis. The intracellular concentration of cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) was measured using a direct enzyme immunoassay kit, which quantifies cAMP levels based on competitive binding to a specific antibody and detection via absorbance at 405 nm. The results demonstrated a dose-dependent reduction in cAMP levels in both LNCaP and PC3 cells following KH7 treatment, with maximal inhibition observed at 20 μM, where cAMP levels were reduced by approximately 65% compared to untreated controls. In contrast, treatment with KH7.15 had no significant effect on cAMP levels, confirming that the observed reduction was specifically attributable to sAC inhibition and not to non-specific chemical toxicity.

To assess the impact of sAC inhibition on cell proliferation, cell counts were performed using a Neubauer hemocytometer following staining with 4% trypan blue to distinguish viable from non-viable cells. After 48 hours of treatment with 20 μM KH7, the number of viable LNCaP cells decreased from 6.37 × 10⁵ to 3.25 × 10⁵, representing a 50% reduction, while PC3 cell numbers dropped from 7.12 × 10⁵ to 3.41 × 10⁵, a 52% reduction. In contrast, cells treated with KH7.15 showed no significant change in cell number, remaining within 5% of baseline values. These findings indicate that sAC activity is essential for maintaining the proliferative capacity of both androgen-sensitive and androgen-insensitive prostate cancer cells.

To determine whether the reduction in cell number was due to cell death, two complementary assays were employed. First, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity in the culture medium was measured as an indicator of plasma membrane integrity and necrotic cell death. A significant increase in LDH release was observed in KH7-treated cells compared to controls, with a 2.8-fold increase in LNCaP cells and a 3.1-fold increase in PC3 cells. Second, apoptosis was assessed by flow cytometric analysis of DNA content following propidium iodide staining, which allows for the identification of cells with sub-G₁ DNA content—a hallmark of apoptotic fragmentation. The proportion of cells in the sub-G₁ population increased from 4.2% in untreated LNCaP cells to 18.7% in KH7-treated cells, and from 5.1% to 20.3% in PC3 cells. These increases were accompanied by the appearance of cleaved caspase-3, as detected by Western blotting, confirming the activation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. In contrast, KH7.15 treatment did not induce LDH release or caspase-3 cleavage, reinforcing the specificity of the sAC-dependent effects.

To further validate these findings and rule out the possibility of off-target effects of KH7, the expression of sAC was genetically silenced using two independent methods. First, LNCaP cells were transfected with siRNA duplexes targeting the human sAC mRNA sequence (NM_001167749), while control cells were treated with a scrambled non-targeting siRNA. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 in Opti-MEM medium, followed by a 6-hour incubation and subsequent replacement with normal growth medium containing 1% fetal calf serum for 42 hours, after which serum was restored to 2%. Western blot analysis confirmed an 85% reduction in sAC protein levels in siRNA-treated cells compared to controls. A second approach involved the use of an adenoviral vector expressing short hairpin RNA (shRNA) directed against sAC, constructed using the AdEasy adenoviral vector system. LNCaP cells were infected with 10⁶ viral particles per milliliter, and after 72 hours, sAC expression was again reduced by more than 80%. Both siRNA and shRNA treatments resulted in a significant reduction in cell proliferation, with cell numbers decreasing to 3.25 × 10⁵ and 3.56 × 10⁵, respectively, compared to 6.37 × 10⁵ in control cells. LDH release and caspase-3 cleavage were similarly elevated in both genetic knockdown models, mirroring the effects observed with KH7. In contrast, cells transfected with scrambled siRNA or infected with control adenovirus expressing a randomized shRNA sequence showed no significant changes in proliferation, viability, or apoptosis markers.

These results collectively demonstrate that the inhibition of sAC, whether achieved pharmacologically or genetically, leads to a consistent and reproducible suppression of prostate cancer cell proliferation and induction of apoptosis. The fact that these effects are observed in both androgen-sensitive and androgen-insensitive cell lines, and that they are recapitulated using two distinct genetic silencing methods, strongly supports the conclusion that sAC is a critical and non-redundant regulator of prostate cancer cell survival. The specificity of these effects is further confirmed by the absence of phenotypic changes in cells treated with the inactive analog KH7.15 or control siRNA/shRNA. This example establishes sAC as a viable therapeutic target in prostate cancer and provides a foundation for the development of targeted therapies aimed at disrupting its enzymatic activity.

### Example 3

In this example, the mechanism by which soluble adenylyl cyclase (sAC) inhibition suppresses prostate cancer cell proliferation was investigated through detailed analysis of the cell cycle and the expression of key regulatory proteins governing the G₂/M transition. Following the observation that sAC inhibition leads to a significant reduction in cell number and induction of apoptosis, it was hypothesized that this effect might be mediated by cell cycle arrest, a common mechanism by which anti-proliferative agents exert their effects. To test this hypothesis, flow cytometric analysis of DNA content was performed on LNCaP cells treated with either 20 μM KH7, KH7.15, or vehicle control for 24 and 48 hours. Cells were fixed in 70% ethanol, treated with RNase A to remove RNA, and stained with propidium iodide before analysis on a FACSCalibur flow cytometer. The distribution of cells across the cell cycle phases—G₁, S, and G₂/M—was quantified using FlowJo software.

The results revealed a marked accumulation of cells in the G₂ phase following sAC inhibition. In untreated control cells, the percentage of cells in G₂ phase was approximately 14.2%, whereas in KH7-treated cells, this proportion increased to 32.6% after 24 hours and reached 38.1% after 48 hours. Concurrently, the percentage of cells in G₁ phase decreased from 61.5% to 42.3% and 38.9%, respectively. The S-phase fraction remained relatively unchanged, indicating that sAC inhibition does not impede DNA synthesis but rather prevents the transition from G₂ to M phase. In contrast, cells treated with KH7.15 exhibited no significant deviation from the control distribution, with G₂ phase percentages remaining at 13.8% and 15.1% at 24 and 48 hours, respectively. Similar results were obtained in cells subjected to sAC knockdown via siRNA or adenoviral shRNA, with G₂ phase proportions increasing to 34.7% and 36.2%, respectively, compared to 15.0% in control-transfected cells. These findings indicate that sAC is required for the timely progression of cells through the G₂/M checkpoint and that its inhibition induces a reversible arrest at this critical juncture.

To determine the temporal sequence of events leading to cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, a time-course analysis was conducted. LNCaP cells were treated with KH7, and samples were collected at 0, 6, 12, 24, and 48 hours. The percentage of cells in G₂ phase was monitored at each time point, and the appearance of cleaved caspase-3 was assessed by Western blotting. The data revealed that the accumulation of cells in G₂ phase began as early as 12 hours after treatment, with a statistically significant increase observed at this time point (p < 0.05). By 24 hours, the G₂ population had nearly doubled compared to baseline, whereas cleaved caspase-3 was not detectable until 24 hours and became prominent only at 48 hours. This temporal dissociation—cell cycle arrest preceding apoptosis—strongly suggests that the induction of apoptosis is a consequence of prolonged mitotic arrest rather than a direct result of sAC inhibition. This finding is consistent with the known biology of G₂/M arrest, where failure to properly initiate mitosis triggers a prolonged checkpoint activation that ultimately leads to apoptotic cell death through the activation of p53 and other stress-response pathways.

To identify the molecular mediators of this G₂/M arrest, the expression of key regulators of mitotic entry was analyzed by Western blotting. Cyclin B₁, which forms a complex with CDK1 to drive entry into mitosis, and CDK1 itself, along with its phosphorylated (active) form at Thr161, were examined. Treatment with KH7 resulted in a 66% reduction in cyclin B₁ protein levels and a 64% reduction in total CDK1, with a more pronounced 78% decrease in phospho-CDK1 (Thr161). Similar reductions were observed in cells subjected to sAC knockdown via siRNA (70% for cyclin B₁, 75% for CDK1, and 78% for phospho-CDK1) and adenoviral shRNA (68%, 72%, and 77%, respectively). In contrast, the expression of cyclins D₁, D₃, and E₂, which regulate the G₁/S transition, remained unchanged following sAC inhibition, confirming the specificity of the effect on the G₂/M checkpoint. The phosphorylation status of CDK1 was further confirmed by probing for inhibitory phosphorylation at Tyr15, which was not altered by KH7 treatment, indicating that the reduction in phospho-CDK1 (Thr161) reflects a loss of activating phosphorylation rather than an increase in inhibitory modifications.

To determine whether these changes in cyclin B₁ and CDK1 expression were mediated through transcriptional or post-translational mechanisms, quantitative real-time PCR was performed to assess mRNA levels. No significant differences were observed in the transcript levels of cyclin B₁ or CDK1 following sAC inhibition, suggesting that the reduction in protein abundance is likely due to enhanced degradation or impaired translation rather than transcriptional repression. This is consistent with the known regulation of cyclin B₁ by the ubiquitin-proteasome system, which is activated during prolonged G₂ arrest. The inhibition of sAC may therefore disrupt signaling pathways that stabilize cyclin B₁ or promote its synthesis, leading to its rapid turnover and failure to accumulate to the threshold required for mitotic entry.

The specificity of these effects was further confirmed by examining the expression of other cell cycle regulators and signaling molecules. The levels of phospho-B-Raf, a downstream target of Rap1 and a known activator of the MEK/ERK pathway, were significantly reduced following sAC inhibition, while total B-Raf and Rap1A protein levels remained unchanged. The phosphorylation of B-Raf at Ser445, a site associated with its activation, was decreased by 75% in KH7-treated cells, mirroring the reduction in phospho-CDK1. This finding links sAC inhibition to the suppression of the EPAC/Rap1/B-Raf signaling axis, which has been previously implicated in the regulation of mitotic progression through MEK/ERK-dependent stabilization of cyclin B₁. The absence of changes in other kinases such as Akt, ERK1/2, or JNK further supports the specificity of the sAC/EPAC/Rap1/B-Raf pathway in mediating this effect.

In summary, this example demonstrates that sAC is a critical regulator of the G₂/M transition in prostate cancer cells, acting through the EPAC/Rap1/B-Raf pathway to maintain the expression and activation of cyclin B₁ and CDK1. The inhibition of sAC leads to a rapid and specific downregulation of these mitotic regulators, resulting in cell cycle arrest at the G₂/M checkpoint. This arrest is not a secondary consequence of apoptosis but rather the primary event that precedes and triggers apoptotic cell death. The findings establish sAC as a central node in the control of mitotic progression in prostate carcinoma and provide a mechanistic basis for the anti-proliferative effects observed in previous examples. This example not only elucidates the molecular pathway through which sAC supports tumor growth but also identifies cyclin B₁ and phospho-CDK1 as downstream biomarkers of sAC activity, which may be used to monitor therapeutic response in clinical settings.

### Example 4

In this example, the downstream signaling mediators of soluble adenylyl cyclase (sAC) in prostate carcinoma cells were systematically investigated to determine whether the anti-proliferative effects of sAC inhibition are mediated through protein kinase A (PKA) or exchange protein activated by cAMP (EPAC). Given that cAMP is a well-established activator of both PKA and EPAC, and that both pathways have been implicated in the regulation of cell proliferation in various cancer types, it was essential to delineate which of these effectors is responsible for transmitting the signal from sAC to the cell cycle machinery. The hypothesis tested was that sAC-derived cAMP activates EPAC, which in turn stimulates Rap1 and B-Raf to promote cyclin B₁ expression and mitotic entry, independently of PKA.

To evaluate the role of PKA, three distinct experimental approaches were employed. First, the PKA-specific activator N⁶-benzoyl-cAMP was applied to LNCaP cells at a concentration of 100 μM, either alone or in combination with 20 μM KH7. This compound is a membrane-permeable cAMP analog that selectively activates PKA without stimulating EPAC. The results showed that treatment with N⁶-benzoyl-cAMP had no effect on cell proliferation, cell cycle distribution, or LDH release in control cells, nor did it reverse the anti-proliferative effects of KH7. In fact, the combination of N⁶-benzoyl-cAMP and KH7 produced the same degree of growth suppression as KH7 alone, indicating that PKA activation does not compensate for sAC inhibition. Second, two structurally distinct PKA inhibitors were used: H-89, a competitive ATP-binding site inhibitor, and (Rₚ)-cAMP-S, a cAMP analog that binds to the regulatory subunits of PKA and prevents its activation. Both compounds were applied at concentrations previously shown to fully inhibit PKA activity in prostate cancer cells (3 μM and 100 μM, respectively). Neither inhibitor affected cell proliferation, nor did they induce apoptosis or G₂/M arrest. Furthermore, when administered in combination with KH7, neither H-89 nor (Rₚ)-cAMP-S altered the magnitude of the anti-proliferative effect, confirming that PKA is not involved in mediating the consequences of sAC inhibition.

To investigate the role of EPAC, the activity of its direct downstream target, Rap1, was assessed. Rap1, a small GTPase of the Ras family, is activated by EPAC through the exchange of GDP for GTP, and its active form (Rap1-GTP) initiates signaling cascades that regulate cell adhesion, migration, and proliferation. A pulldown assay using glutathione S-transferase fusion protein containing the Ras-binding domain of RalGDS was performed to specifically isolate and quantify GTP-bound Rap1. LNCaP cells were treated with KH7, KH7.15, or vehicle control for 24 hours, and cell lysates were incubated with the GST-RalGDS beads. Western blot analysis using an anti-Rap1A antibody revealed a 72% reduction in Rap1-GTP levels following KH7 treatment, whereas KH7.15 had no effect. Total Rap1A levels remained unchanged, confirming that sAC inhibition specifically reduces the activation state of Rap1 rather than its expression. This finding was corroborated by the observation that phosphorylation of B-Raf, a direct downstream effector of Rap1, was reduced by 75% in KH7-treated cells, while total B-Raf levels were unaffected.

To confirm that EPAC activation is sufficient to rescue the phenotype induced by sAC inhibition, the EPAC-selective activator 8-pCPT-cAMP was used. This compound is a membrane-permeable cAMP analog that activates EPAC with high specificity and negligible affinity for PKA. When LNCaP cells were treated with 100 μM 8-pCPT-cAMP in combination with 20 μM KH7, the reduction in Rap1-GTP levels was completely reversed, and B-Raf phosphorylation was restored to near-control levels. Furthermore, the anti-proliferative effects of KH7 were abrogated: cell numbers returned to baseline, the G₂/M arrest was eliminated, LDH release was suppressed, and cleaved caspase-3 was no longer detectable. These results demonstrate that EPAC activation is not only necessary but also sufficient to bypass the requirement for sAC in maintaining cell proliferation and survival.

To further validate the specificity of this pathway, the expression of other cAMP effectors and signaling molecules was examined. The phosphorylation status of CREB, a canonical PKA substrate, was unaffected by KH7 treatment, whereas it was robustly induced by forskolin, a tmAC activator. The levels of phospho-Akt, phospho-ERK1/2, and phospho-JNK were also unchanged, indicating that the effects of sAC inhibition are confined to the EPAC/Rap1/B-Raf axis. Additionally, the use of a dominant-negative EPAC mutant (EPAC-R279A) in transient transfection experiments resulted in a phenotype identical to that of sAC inhibition, with reduced Rap1-GTP, decreased cyclin B₁ expression, and G₂/M arrest, further confirming the central role of EPAC in this signaling cascade.

The significance of these findings lies in the identification of a novel, non-canonical cAMP signaling pathway in prostate cancer that operates independently of PKA and tmACs. While previous studies have attributed cAMP-mediated proliferation to PKA activation, particularly in the context of androgen receptor signaling, this example demonstrates that in prostate carcinoma, the sAC-derived cAMP pool functions exclusively through EPAC to sustain mitotic progression. This distinction is critical for therapeutic development, as it implies that targeting sAC will not interfere with other cAMP-dependent processes regulated by PKA, such as metabolism, ion transport, or gene transcription, thereby minimizing off-target effects. Moreover, the fact that EPAC activation rescues the phenotype induced by sAC inhibition confirms that the primary function of sAC in this context is to generate a localized pool of cAMP that activates EPAC, rather than serving as a general source of intracellular cAMP.

This example establishes that the anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic effects of sAC inhibition are mediated exclusively through the EPAC/Rap1/B-Raf signaling axis. The pathway is linear and highly specific: sAC generates cAMP → cAMP activates EPAC → EPAC exchanges GDP for GTP on Rap1 → GTP-bound Rap1 activates B-Raf → B-Raf phosphorylates MEK → MEK activates ERK → ERK stabilizes cyclin B₁ → cyclin B₁/CDK1 complex drives mitotic entry. Disruption of any step in this cascade leads to G₂/M arrest and subsequent apoptosis. This pathway is distinct from the classical tmAC/cAMP/PKA pathway and is not influenced by androgen receptor status, making it a uniquely suited target for the treatment of castration-resistant prostate cancer. The invention therefore encompasses not only the inhibition of sAC but also the targeted activation of EPAC as a potential therapeutic strategy, although the latter would be contraindicated in this context due to its pro-tumorigenic effects. The identification of this pathway provides a molecular rationale for the development of sAC inhibitors as precision therapeutics and positions EPAC/Rap1/B-Raf as a biomarker signature for patient stratification and treatment monitoring.
