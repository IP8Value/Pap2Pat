# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of mechanical ventilation for patients requiring respiratory support in critical care environments. More specifically, it pertains to a novel mode of pressure-controlled mechanical ventilation that employs a closed-loop, algorithm-driven targeting system designed to optimize ventilator settings by maximizing alveolar minute ventilation while simultaneously minimizing tidal volume and inspiratory pressure, thereby reducing the risk of ventilator-induced lung injury. The invention integrates real-time physiological modeling of respiratory system mechanics—including alveolar ventilation requirements, dead space ratio, and time constants of inspiration and expiration—to dynamically determine the optimal combination of respiratory frequency and tidal volume under a fixed inspiratory pressure constraint above positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP). This mode, termed mid-frequency ventilation (MFV), operates independently of clinician input for the selection of frequency and tidal volume, relying instead on a mathematical optimization framework grounded in the physical principles of gas exchange and respiratory mechanics. The system is implemented through a programmable mechanical ventilator capable of continuous, real-time adjustment of ventilatory parameters without manual intervention, and is particularly suited for patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome, obesity-related respiratory compromise, or other restrictive lung conditions where lung protection is paramount. Unlike existing adaptive modes that prioritize minimizing the patient’s work of breathing or maintaining a fixed minute ventilation target, MFV is uniquely configured to prioritize the preservation of alveolar integrity by favoring high-frequency, low-tidal-volume ventilation patterns that reduce transpulmonary pressure gradients and prevent both atelectrauma and volutrauma. The invention further encompasses the integration of this algorithm into standard clinical ventilators through firmware and control system modifications that enable the device to autonomously compute and apply ventilator settings based on continuously updated estimates of respiratory system characteristics, thereby eliminating the variability inherent in human decision-making and ensuring consistent, physiology-driven ventilation strategies across diverse patient populations.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Mechanical ventilation remains a cornerstone of intensive care medicine, providing life-sustaining respiratory support to patients with acute respiratory failure. Despite decades of technological advancement, the fundamental challenge of balancing adequate gas exchange with the prevention of ventilator-induced lung injury persists. Clinical guidelines have long advocated for low tidal volume ventilation, particularly in patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome, based on evidence demonstrating reduced mortality when tidal volumes are limited to 6 mL/kg of predicted body weight. However, the practical implementation of such guidelines remains inconsistent, as ventilator settings are typically selected by clinicians based on experience, institutional protocols, and subjective interpretation of arterial blood gases, often without real-time integration of individualized respiratory mechanics. Even among experienced practitioners, variability in initial ventilator settings is substantial, with studies showing wide disparities in tidal volume, respiratory rate, and positive end-expiratory pressure selection across similar clinical scenarios. This variability is compounded by the complexity of respiratory physiology, where the relationship between tidal volume, frequency, dead space, and alveolar ventilation is nonlinear and highly dependent on individual patient characteristics such as lung compliance, airway resistance, and expiratory time constants. 

Existing automated ventilation modes, such as adaptive support ventilation (ASV), attempt to reduce this variability by employing closed-loop algorithms that adjust ventilator settings in response to measured physiological parameters. ASV, for example, calculates optimal tidal volume and respiratory rate based on an estimate of the patient’s respiratory system mechanics and aims to minimize the work of breathing by selecting settings that approximate the most efficient breathing pattern for the patient. While ASV has demonstrated clinical utility in certain populations, its optimization criterion—minimization of work of breathing—does not inherently prioritize lung protection. In fact, in some clinical contexts, particularly in restrictive lung disease, ASV may select tidal volumes that exceed recommended protective thresholds, or may favor lower respiratory rates that increase the risk of alveolar overdistension. Moreover, ASV does not explicitly account for the distinction between total minute ventilation and alveolar minute ventilation, which is critical in conditions where dead space ventilation is elevated, such as in pulmonary embolism or severe obstructive disease. As a result, even when ASV achieves target minute ventilation, it may fail to maximize the efficiency of gas exchange or minimize the mechanical stress on the lung parenchyma.

Furthermore, current ventilator technology lacks a mode that systematically prioritizes the reduction of tidal volume as a primary objective, while maintaining or enhancing alveolar ventilation through increased frequency. Conventional ventilation strategies, whether volume-controlled or pressure-controlled, are typically designed around fixed or clinician-selected tidal volumes, with frequency adjusted as a secondary parameter to meet minute ventilation goals. This paradigm reflects historical assumptions about the physiological limits of respiratory frequency and the perceived risks of high-frequency ventilation, including air trapping, auto-PEEP, and inadequate expiratory flow. However, these assumptions are increasingly challenged by emerging evidence that, under controlled conditions and with appropriate inspiratory pressure constraints, high-frequency, low-tidal-volume ventilation can enhance alveolar recruitment, reduce transpulmonary pressure swings, and improve oxygenation without compromising CO₂ elimination. The theoretical foundation for such an approach has been established in computational models and experimental simulations, demonstrating that for a given inspiratory pressure above PEEP, there exists a unique combination of frequency and tidal volume that maximizes alveolar minute ventilation while minimizing the mechanical energy delivered to the lung. This optimal point lies at a frequency higher than traditionally used in clinical practice, yet lower than that of high-frequency oscillatory ventilation, hence the term “mid-frequency ventilation.”

Despite this theoretical promise, no commercially available ventilator currently implements a mode that autonomously selects frequency and tidal volume based on the explicit goal of maximizing alveolar ventilation while minimizing tidal volume. Existing modes either rely on clinician input for key parameters or optimize for other objectives, such as minimizing patient effort, maintaining a fixed minute ventilation, or stabilizing airway pressure. Moreover, the interaction between ventilator waveform morphology, inspiratory time constant, and expiratory flow dynamics has not been fully integrated into automated control systems. For instance, the shape of the pressure waveform—particularly its rise time and plateau characteristics—has a profound impact on the efficiency of alveolar recruitment and gas distribution, yet no current mode adjusts waveform morphology in concert with frequency and tidal volume selection. The absence of such a mode represents a significant gap in the evolution of intelligent ventilation systems, leaving clinicians without a tool that can consistently and autonomously implement the most lung-protective ventilation strategy across a spectrum of disease states. The present invention fills this gap by introducing a novel algorithmic framework that defines, computes, and applies the optimal combination of frequency and tidal volume based on a rigorous physiological model that explicitly maximizes alveolar minute ventilation under a fixed inspiratory pressure constraint, while simultaneously minimizing tidal volume and associated mechanical stress on the lung parenchyma.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention introduces a novel mode of mechanical ventilation known as mid-frequency ventilation (MFV), which autonomously determines and applies optimal ventilator settings by maximizing alveolar minute ventilation while minimizing tidal volume under a fixed inspiratory pressure constraint above positive end-expiratory pressure. Unlike conventional ventilation modes that rely on clinician-selected parameters or optimize for the minimization of work of breathing, MFV employs a closed-loop, real-time computational algorithm that integrates dynamic estimates of respiratory system mechanics—including alveolar minute ventilation requirement, dead space ratio, inspiratory time constant, and expiratory time constant—to calculate the precise combination of respiratory frequency and tidal volume that achieves the highest possible alveolar ventilation for a given inspiratory pressure. The algorithm is grounded in a mathematical model derived from the equation of motion of the respiratory system, which accounts for the nonlinear relationship between pressure, volume, flow, and resistance, and is calibrated to ensure that the selected settings remain within safe mechanical limits to prevent volutrauma and barotrauma. The system operates in pressure-controlled continuous mandatory ventilation mode, with a fixed inspiratory-to-expiratory ratio that is adjusted dynamically in response to changes in respiratory frequency to maintain adequate expiratory time and minimize the risk of air trapping. 

The invention is implemented through a programmable mechanical ventilator equipped with sensors that continuously monitor airway pressure, flow, and volume, and a computational unit that processes these measurements in real time to update estimates of respiratory system compliance, resistance, and dead space. Based on these updated parameters, the algorithm recalculates the optimal frequency and tidal volume every breath cycle or at intervals not exceeding five seconds, ensuring that the ventilator adapts to changes in patient condition without requiring manual intervention. The target alveolar minute ventilation is determined either from a preset clinical goal or derived from the patient’s metabolic demand, as estimated from body weight, temperature, and oxygen consumption. The algorithm then identifies the frequency-tidal volume pair that maximizes alveolar ventilation for the given inspiratory pressure, subject to constraints that prevent tidal volumes from falling below a physiologically minimal threshold and ensure that expiratory time remains sufficient to allow complete lung emptying. 

A key innovation of the invention is its explicit prioritization of alveolar ventilation over total minute ventilation, recognizing that the elimination of carbon dioxide and the maintenance of oxygenation depend not on the volume of gas delivered to the airways, but on the volume of gas that reaches the alveoli. By minimizing tidal volume and increasing frequency, MFV reduces the mechanical energy imparted to the lung per breath, thereby decreasing the risk of stretch-induced injury, while simultaneously enhancing gas exchange efficiency through more uniform alveolar recruitment and improved ventilation-perfusion matching. The system further incorporates a waveform optimization component that ensures the pressure waveform delivered to the patient is as close to a rectangular shape as possible, minimizing the time required for pressure rise and maximizing the efficiency of alveolar inflation. This waveform morphology is achieved through precise control of the ventilator’s pressure delivery mechanism, which is calibrated to respond with minimal delay and maximal fidelity to the computed pressure profile. 

The invention is designed to function independently of clinician input for the selection of frequency and tidal volume, although it permits the clinician to set upper and lower bounds for respiratory rate, tidal volume, and inspiratory pressure as safety parameters. It does not require the clinician to estimate dead space, compliance, or resistance, as these are continuously calculated by the system using real-time measurements and a validated physiological model. The system also does not rely on fixed assumptions about patient weight or predicted body weight, as it adapts to the actual respiratory mechanics observed during ventilation. In clinical testing, MFV has demonstrated the ability to deliver tidal volumes significantly lower than those selected by experienced clinicians or by existing adaptive modes such as ASV, while maintaining or improving alveolar ventilation and reducing peak and mean airway pressures. In restrictive lung conditions such as acute respiratory distress syndrome and morbid obesity, MFV consistently produces lower end-inspiratory volumes and higher end-expiratory volumes, indicating enhanced alveolar recruitment and reduced overdistension. In obstructive conditions, the system adapts by reducing respiratory rate and increasing inspiratory time to accommodate prolonged expiratory time constants, thereby preventing auto-PEEP while still maintaining alveolar ventilation targets. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in mechanical ventilation, moving from a model of clinician-dependent parameter selection to one of autonomous, physiology-driven optimization that prioritizes lung protection as the primary objective.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The detailed implementation of mid-frequency ventilation (MFV) encompasses a comprehensive integration of physiological modeling, real-time sensor feedback, computational optimization, and precise pressure waveform control within a mechanical ventilator system. The invention operates as a closed-loop, pressure-controlled, continuous mandatory ventilation mode that autonomously determines and applies the optimal combination of respiratory frequency and tidal volume to maximize alveolar minute ventilation while minimizing tidal volume, all under a fixed inspiratory pressure constraint above positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP). The system is composed of four interdependent components: a physiological modeling engine, a real-time parameter estimation module, an optimization algorithm, and a pressure delivery control system. Each component functions in a continuous, dynamic feedback loop, with data from the patient’s respiratory mechanics being sampled at a rate of at least 500 Hz to ensure temporal fidelity and responsiveness to rapid changes in lung condition.

The physiological modeling engine is the foundational element of the invention and is based on a modified form of the equation of motion of the respiratory system, which describes the relationship between airway pressure, flow, volume, resistance, and compliance. The equation is expressed as:  
Paw = PEEP + (V / C) + (Flow × R),  
where Paw is the airway pressure, V is the tidal volume, C is the respiratory system compliance, R is the airway resistance, and Flow is the instantaneous inspiratory flow rate. However, unlike conventional implementations that treat compliance and resistance as static parameters, the modeling engine in MFV continuously recalculates these values on a breath-by-breath basis using a recursive least-squares estimation algorithm that incorporates measurements of pressure, flow, and volume from the ventilator’s sensors. The model further accounts for the dynamic nature of dead space by estimating the anatomical and physiological dead space fraction based on the difference between total minute ventilation and alveolar minute ventilation, as inferred from end-tidal carbon dioxide concentration and arterial blood gas measurements when available. The model also incorporates the time constants of inspiration and expiration, τ_i and τ_e, which are derived from the exponential decay of flow during inspiration and expiration, respectively, and are used to determine the minimum expiratory time required to prevent air trapping. These time constants are updated every breath, allowing the system to adapt to changes in bronchial tone, airway secretions, or pulmonary edema without requiring manual recalibration.

The real-time parameter estimation module receives input from the ventilator’s pressure, flow, and volume transducers and processes this data through a series of signal filtering and noise-reduction algorithms to extract accurate estimates of compliance, resistance, and dead space. The module employs a Kalman filter-based estimator that accounts for measurement uncertainty and physiological variability, ensuring that transient artifacts such as coughing, patient movement, or circuit condensation do not unduly influence the model’s output. The estimated parameters are then fed into the optimization algorithm, which operates as a constrained nonlinear optimization problem. The objective function of the algorithm is defined as:  
Maximize: V_alv × f  
Subject to:  
V_tidal ≤ V_max  
f ≥ f_min  
f ≤ f_max  
V_alv = (V_tidal − V_d) × f  
P_insp ≤ P_max  
τ_e ≥ 3 × τ_e_estimated  
where V_alv is alveolar minute ventilation, f is respiratory frequency, V_tidal is tidal volume, V_d is estimated dead space volume, V_max is the maximum allowable tidal volume (set by the clinician or derived from predicted body weight), f_min and f_max are the minimum and maximum allowable frequencies, P_insp is the inspiratory pressure applied above PEEP, P_max is the maximum allowable inspiratory pressure, and τ_e_estimated is the most recently calculated expiratory time constant. The constraint τ_e ≥ 3 × τ_e_estimated ensures that the expiratory phase is sufficiently long to allow at least 95% of the lung volume to be exhaled, thereby preventing the accumulation of auto-PEEP. The optimization is performed using a gradient-based search algorithm that iteratively adjusts V_tidal and f within the defined constraints to locate the global maximum of alveolar minute ventilation. The algorithm is initialized using the most recent clinically accepted values for tidal volume and frequency, and then explores the solution space by perturbing these values in small increments, evaluating the resulting alveolar ventilation, and converging toward the optimal point. This process occurs every breath cycle or at intervals not exceeding five seconds, ensuring that the system responds rapidly to changes in patient condition.

Once the optimal frequency and tidal volume are determined, the pressure delivery control system translates these parameters into a precise pressure waveform that is delivered to the patient. The system is designed to generate a pressure waveform that is as close to rectangular as possible, with a rise time of less than 100 milliseconds and a plateau phase that maintains constant pressure for the duration of the inspiratory time. This waveform morphology is critical to the efficiency of alveolar recruitment, as it minimizes the time required for pressure to reach the target level and ensures uniform distribution of gas throughout the lung parenchyma. The control system achieves this by modulating the pressure output of the ventilator’s pneumatic valve with high-resolution feedback control, using a proportional-integral-derivative controller that continuously compares the measured airway pressure to the target pressure profile and adjusts the valve position with millisecond precision. The inspiratory time is calculated as the quotient of tidal volume divided by the inspiratory flow rate, which is itself determined by the system’s internal model of the patient’s inspiratory time constant. The expiratory time is then calculated as the inverse of the respiratory frequency minus the inspiratory time, and the system ensures that this expiratory time meets the constraint τ_e ≥ 3 × τ_e_estimated. If the calculated expiratory time is insufficient, the system automatically reduces the respiratory frequency until the constraint is satisfied, thereby preventing the development of auto-PEEP even in patients with severely prolonged expiratory time constants, such as those with severe obstructive lung disease.

The system further incorporates a safety protocol that prevents the delivery of tidal volumes below a physiologically minimal threshold, defined as 2.5 mL/kg of predicted body weight, to avoid the risk of alveolar collapse and atelectrauma. This threshold is not fixed but is dynamically adjusted based on the patient’s estimated functional residual capacity, as inferred from the end-expiratory volume and the compliance of the respiratory system. If the optimization algorithm suggests a tidal volume below this threshold, the system instead selects the lowest possible tidal volume that maintains alveolar recruitment, even if it results in a slightly lower alveolar minute ventilation. Similarly, the system imposes an upper limit on inspiratory pressure to prevent barotrauma, with the maximum allowable pressure set by the clinician or derived from a default value based on the patient’s predicted body weight and the severity of lung injury. The system also monitors for signs of patient-ventilator asynchrony, such as premature cycling or double triggering, and if detected, temporarily suspends optimization and reverts to a stable, clinician-defined backup mode until the asynchrony resolves. This ensures that the system remains safe and reliable even in the presence of unpredictable physiological events.

The implementation of MFV requires integration into a standard mechanical ventilator through firmware modifications that enable real-time computation, high-frequency data acquisition, and precise pressure waveform control. The ventilator must be equipped with high-resolution pressure and flow sensors capable of sampling at 500 Hz or higher, a microprocessor capable of executing the optimization algorithm in less than 10 milliseconds per breath, and a pressure delivery system with a response time of less than 50 milliseconds. The system is designed to be compatible with both single-limb and dual-limb ventilator circuits, and it automatically compensates for circuit compliance and resistance by incorporating these factors into the physiological model. The user interface of the ventilator displays the current settings of frequency, tidal volume, inspiratory pressure, alveolar minute ventilation, and estimated compliance and resistance, along with a graphical representation of the pressure waveform and the optimization trajectory over the past 30 seconds. The clinician may override the system at any time by manually adjusting any parameter, at which point the system enters a semi-automatic mode, continuing to calculate optimal settings but awaiting clinician confirmation before applying them. The system also logs all parameter changes, optimization decisions, and safety interventions for retrospective analysis and quality assurance.

In clinical application, MFV is initiated by the clinician selecting the MFV mode and setting the target alveolar minute ventilation, which may be derived from a standard formula based on predicted body weight and metabolic rate, or may be manually entered based on arterial blood gas targets. The clinician also sets the maximum allowable inspiratory pressure and the minimum and maximum allowable respiratory frequencies. The system then initializes with a default tidal volume and frequency, begins measuring respiratory mechanics, and initiates the optimization process. Within the first 30 to 60 seconds, the system converges to the optimal settings, which are then maintained unless the patient’s condition changes. In patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome, MFV consistently selects tidal volumes between 3.5 and 5.0 mL/kg and respiratory frequencies between 30 and 45 breaths per minute, resulting in alveolar minute ventilation equivalent to or greater than that achieved by conventional modes, while reducing end-inspiratory volumes by up to 30% and peak airway pressures by up to 15%. In patients with morbid obesity, MFV similarly reduces tidal volume and increases frequency, while maintaining end-expiratory volumes at levels that promote alveolar recruitment without inducing overdistension. In patients with obstructive lung disease, MFV reduces respiratory frequency and increases inspiratory time to accommodate prolonged expiratory time constants, thereby preventing auto-PEEP while still achieving adequate alveolar ventilation. In all cases, the system demonstrates superior consistency compared to clinician-selected settings, eliminating the variability inherent in human decision-making and ensuring that lung-protective ventilation is delivered uniformly across all patients, regardless of clinician experience or institutional protocol.

The invention further includes a self-calibration feature that allows the system to adapt to changes in the ventilator circuit, such as the addition or removal of humidifiers, filters, or bacterial filters, by automatically recalibrating the circuit compliance and resistance during a brief initialization sequence performed at the start of each ventilation session. This ensures that the physiological model remains accurate even when external components are altered, and eliminates the need for manual recalibration. The system also includes a predictive maintenance function that monitors the performance of the pressure delivery system and alerts the clinician if the response time of the valve exceeds acceptable limits, indicating potential mechanical degradation. This feature enhances the reliability and longevity of the system in high-acuity clinical environments.

In summary, the invention represents a fundamental advancement in mechanical ventilation by introducing a mode that autonomously selects ventilator settings based on the explicit goal of maximizing alveolar ventilation while minimizing tidal volume and inspiratory pressure. Through the integration of real-time physiological modeling, nonlinear optimization, and high-precision pressure waveform control, MFV provides a consistent, evidence-based, and lung-protective approach to ventilation that is superior to existing modes in its ability to prevent ventilator-induced lung injury. The system is fully automated, requires minimal clinician input after initiation, and adapts seamlessly to changes in patient condition, making it suitable for use in intensive care units, emergency departments, and transport settings where consistent, high-quality ventilation is essential. The invention thus fulfills a long-standing need in critical care medicine for a ventilation mode that prioritizes lung protection as its primary objective, rather than merely achieving a target minute ventilation or minimizing patient effort.
