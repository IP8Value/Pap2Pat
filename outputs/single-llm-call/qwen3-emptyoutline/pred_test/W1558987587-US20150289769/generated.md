# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of medical imaging and cardiovascular diagnostics, specifically to a novel quantitative index for assessing left ventricular diastolic function using cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging. The invention provides a non-invasive, objective, and reproducible method for evaluating myocardial relaxation and tissue compliance through the analysis of myocardial strain dynamics during the cardiac cycle. This index, referred to herein as the Strain Relaxation Index (SRI), is derived from tagged CMR data and integrates temporal characteristics of myocardial deformation—specifically the timing of post-systolic strain peak relative to systolic strain peak, normalized by the magnitude of early diastolic strain rate and the total relaxation window—to yield a single, physiologically meaningful metric that reflects the efficiency and completeness of ventricular filling. The invention is particularly useful in the clinical assessment of patients with suspected or established diastolic dysfunction, including those with hypertension, diabetes, obesity, or aging-related cardiac remodeling, where conventional echocardiographic parameters may be limited by angle dependence, load sensitivity, or inter-observer variability. The SRI enables the detection of subtle, early-stage diastolic impairment prior to the onset of overt symptoms or structural changes, thereby facilitating earlier intervention and improved long-term outcomes. Furthermore, the SRI is uniquely positioned to correlate with myocardial mass-to-volume ratio, a known marker of pathological remodeling, thereby offering a dual assessment of both functional and structural components of diastolic performance. The method is implemented through automated or semi-automated image processing algorithms that extract circumferential strain curves from tagged CMR sequences, identify key temporal landmarks in the strain waveform, and compute the SRI without requiring manual tracing or operator-dependent measurements. This invention represents a significant advancement over existing diagnostic tools by providing a direct, mechanics-based quantification of diastolic function that is independent of Doppler assumptions, geometric approximations, or acoustic window limitations, and is fully compatible with standard clinical CMR protocols.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Diastolic dysfunction is a prevalent and often underdiagnosed condition characterized by impaired left ventricular relaxation, increased myocardial stiffness, and elevated filling pressures, which collectively compromise the heart’s ability to accept and store blood during the diastolic phase of the cardiac cycle. It is a hallmark feature of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), a condition that accounts for nearly half of all heart failure cases and carries a prognosis comparable to that of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction. Despite its clinical significance, the diagnosis of diastolic dysfunction remains challenging due to the lack of a universally accepted, reproducible, and physiologically grounded metric. Current diagnostic criteria rely predominantly on transthoracic echocardiography, which employs a combination of mitral inflow velocities (E and A waves), tissue Doppler imaging (TDI) of mitral annular motion (e’ wave), and the ratio of E to e’ (E/e’) to estimate left ventricular filling pressure and relaxation velocity. Additional parameters such as deceleration time of the E wave, isovolumetric relaxation time, and left atrial volume index are also incorporated into algorithmic scoring systems to classify diastolic function into grades ranging from normal to severely impaired. However, these parameters are inherently load-dependent, influenced by heart rate, age, body habitus, and technical factors such as Doppler angle and sample volume placement. Moreover, they reflect global hemodynamic states rather than intrinsic myocardial properties, making them less reliable in patients with comorbidities such as atrial fibrillation, valvular disease, or hypertension, where multiple confounding factors coexist.

Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) has emerged as a powerful modality for the comprehensive assessment of cardiac structure and function, offering superior spatial resolution, three-dimensional volumetric quantification, and the ability to characterize myocardial tissue properties without ionizing radiation. Tagged CMR, in particular, allows for the direct tracking of myocardial deformation through the application of spatial modulation of magnetization (SPAMM) or other tagging techniques that create transient grid-like patterns on the myocardium, enabling precise measurement of strain and strain rate throughout the cardiac cycle. While CMR has been extensively used to quantify systolic function, its application to diastolic assessment has been limited primarily to the measurement of filling rates, time-to-peak filling, and passive filling volumes, none of which directly capture the active mechanical processes underlying myocardial relaxation. Furthermore, existing CMR-based indices of diastolic function have not integrated the temporal dynamics of strain development and recovery in a manner that reflects the underlying biophysical mechanisms of relaxation and compliance.

Recent studies have highlighted the importance of myocardial mass-to-volume ratio (MVR) as a structural correlate of diastolic dysfunction. MVR, calculated as the ratio of left ventricular myocardial mass to end-diastolic volume, increases in conditions characterized by concentric remodeling, such as chronic hypertension and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, where the myocardium thickens without proportional chamber enlargement. This increase in MVR reflects a shift toward a stiffer, less compliant myocardial architecture, which directly impedes efficient diastolic filling. However, MVR alone is a static, geometric parameter that does not provide insight into the dynamic mechanical behavior of the myocardium during relaxation. There exists a critical gap in the diagnostic landscape: a method that simultaneously quantifies both the mechanical efficiency of myocardial relaxation and the structural stiffness of the ventricular wall in a unified, physiologically coherent metric.

The present invention addresses this gap by introducing the Strain Relaxation Index (SRI), a novel, CMR-derived parameter that integrates three fundamental components of diastolic mechanics: (1) the temporal delay between the peak of systolic strain and the subsequent post-systolic strain, which reflects the duration and completeness of active myocardial relaxation; (2) the magnitude of the early diastolic strain rate, which serves as a direct measure of tissue compliance and the rate at which the myocardium recoils following contraction; and (3) the total available relaxation time, defined as the interval between the end of systole and the onset of the next cardiac cycle, which normalizes the index for heart rate variability and ensures physiological relevance across different cardiac rhythms. By combining these elements into a single dimensionless index, SRI captures the interplay between active relaxation kinetics and passive tissue properties, providing a more comprehensive and mechanistically grounded assessment of diastolic function than any existing parameter.

Prior art in the field includes methods for measuring myocardial strain from tagged CMR, such as harmonic phase analysis (HARP), spatial modulation of magnetization (SPAMM), and displacement encoding with stimulated echoes (DENSE). These techniques have been successfully applied to quantify systolic strain, torsion, and twist, but have not been systematically adapted to define diastolic indices based on post-systolic strain behavior. Some studies have noted the presence of post-systolic shortening in ischemic or hypertrophied myocardium, but these observations have been treated as qualitative anomalies rather than quantitative biomarkers. Other approaches have attempted to correlate CMR-derived strain rates with echocardiographic e’ velocities, but these correlations have been limited by small sample sizes, lack of normalization, and failure to account for the temporal sequence of strain events. Furthermore, no prior method has combined the timing of strain recovery with the magnitude of early diastolic recoil and the physiological time window for relaxation into a single, validated index that correlates with both structural remodeling (MVR) and established functional parameters (E/e’, e’).

The absence of such an index has resulted in a diagnostic disconnect between structural and functional assessments of diastolic function. Clinicians are often forced to reconcile conflicting findings—such as a normal e’ velocity in the presence of elevated MVR or a normal E/e’ ratio in a patient with markedly impaired relaxation—leading to diagnostic uncertainty and delayed therapeutic intervention. The present invention resolves this disconnect by providing a unified, CMR-based metric that is inherently linked to both the mechanical behavior of the myocardium and its structural architecture. By leveraging the high spatial and temporal resolution of tagged CMR, the SRI enables the detection of subclinical diastolic impairment in populations at risk, including those with metabolic syndrome, prehypertension, or early-stage hypertrophy, before echocardiographic abnormalities become apparent. Importantly, the SRI is not subject to the acoustic window limitations of echocardiography, is independent of Doppler assumptions regarding pressure gradients, and is not confounded by atrial contraction or preload variations. Its derivation from fully automated image processing algorithms ensures reproducibility across operators and imaging platforms, making it suitable for large-scale clinical deployment and longitudinal monitoring. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the evaluation of diastolic function, moving from a collection of indirect, hemodynamic surrogates to a direct, mechanics-based quantification of myocardial relaxation and compliance.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention discloses a novel, quantitative method for assessing left ventricular diastolic function using cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging, based on the derivation and application of a Strain Relaxation Index (SRI). The SRI is a physiologically grounded, mechanics-based metric that integrates three key components of myocardial behavior during the relaxation phase of the cardiac cycle: the temporal interval between the peak of systolic strain and the subsequent post-systolic strain peak, the magnitude of the early diastolic strain rate, and the total available time for relaxation, normalized by the cardiac cycle duration. This index is computed from circumferential strain curves derived from tagged CMR images acquired at the mid-ventricular short-axis plane, using automated image processing algorithms that identify strain landmarks with sub-millisecond precision. The SRI is expressed in units of time normalized by time, rendering it dimensionless and universally comparable across subjects regardless of heart rate, age, or body size. The invention provides a robust, reproducible, and highly sensitive method for detecting early and subclinical diastolic dysfunction, particularly in populations with concentric remodeling, hypertension, diabetes, or aging-related cardiac changes, where conventional echocardiographic parameters may be inconclusive or misleading.

The SRI is calculated as the difference between the time of occurrence of the post-systolic strain peak and the time of occurrence of the systolic strain peak, divided by the peak magnitude of the early diastolic strain rate, and further normalized by the total relaxation time, which is defined as the interval between the end of systole and the onset of the next cardiac cycle. This formulation ensures that the index reflects not only the speed of myocardial recoil (captured by the strain rate) but also the completeness of relaxation (captured by the post-systolic delay) and the physiological context of the relaxation process (captured by the normalization to total relaxation time). A higher SRI value indicates prolonged post-systolic strain, reduced early diastolic recoil, and/or relatively shorter relaxation time—all of which are hallmarks of impaired diastolic function. Conversely, a lower SRI value reflects efficient, rapid, and complete myocardial relaxation, consistent with normal diastolic performance. The SRI has been shown to correlate strongly with the myocardial mass-to-volume ratio (MVR), a structural marker of pathological remodeling, demonstrating that it captures not only functional impairment but also the underlying architectural changes that drive it. This dual sensitivity to both mechanics and morphology distinguishes the SRI from all previously described diastolic indices.

The method of the invention is implemented through a computerized system that processes tagged CMR image sequences using harmonic phase analysis or equivalent strain extraction algorithms to generate circumferential strain-time curves for the mid-ventricular myocardium. The system automatically identifies the systolic strain peak, the post-systolic strain peak, and the early diastolic strain rate peak by detecting local maxima and minima in the strain waveform, with temporal resolution determined by the frame rate of the CMR acquisition. The total relaxation time is computed as the difference between the R-R interval, derived from the electrocardiographic gating signal, and the systolic interval, defined as the time between the onset of the QRS complex and the end of systolic strain. The SRI is then computed using a predefined mathematical formula that combines these temporal and magnitude parameters. The resulting index is displayed as a numerical value, optionally accompanied by color-coded thresholds or percentile rankings relative to a normative database, enabling immediate clinical interpretation. The system may be integrated into standard CMR workstations and requires no additional hardware beyond existing tagging sequences and image processing software.

The invention offers several critical advantages over existing diagnostic approaches. First, it provides a direct, mechanics-based assessment of diastolic function that is independent of Doppler assumptions, acoustic windows, or geometric approximations, making it applicable to all patients regardless of body habitus or arrhythmia. Second, it is highly sensitive to early-stage diastolic impairment, detecting abnormalities before changes in e’ velocity or E/e’ ratio become apparent, thereby enabling earlier intervention. Third, it correlates strongly with structural remodeling as quantified by MVR, providing a unified metric that links function and structure—a critical gap in current diagnostic paradigms. Fourth, it is fully automated, eliminating inter-observer variability and enabling high-throughput, standardized clinical deployment. Fifth, it is compatible with routine CMR protocols and does not require contrast agents, additional scan time, or specialized sequences, making it readily adoptable in clinical practice. Sixth, the SRI has been validated against established echocardiographic parameters of diastolic function, including septal and lateral e’ velocities and the E/e’ ratio, demonstrating statistically significant correlations that confirm its physiological relevance. Finally, the SRI exhibits a graded increase across categories of diastolic dysfunction, from normal to severely impaired, providing a continuous, quantitative scale that can be used for risk stratification, therapeutic monitoring, and prognostic prediction.

The invention further encompasses a computer-readable medium containing instructions that, when executed by a processor, cause the system to perform the steps of acquiring tagged CMR images, extracting circumferential strain curves, identifying key strain landmarks, computing the SRI according to the defined formula, and outputting the result for clinical interpretation. The invention also includes a diagnostic system comprising a CMR scanner, a strain analysis module, and a display unit configured to render the SRI value alongside conventional volumetric and functional parameters. The invention further includes a method of diagnosing diastolic dysfunction in a subject by measuring the SRI and comparing it to a reference threshold, wherein a value exceeding a predetermined cutoff indicates the presence of diastolic dysfunction. The invention further includes a method of monitoring the progression or regression of diastolic dysfunction over time by serially measuring the SRI in the same subject under identical imaging conditions. The invention further includes a method of selecting a therapeutic intervention based on the SRI value, wherein a higher SRI triggers initiation or intensification of therapies targeting myocardial stiffness, such as sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors, angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors, or lifestyle modifications aimed at reducing afterload and improving compliance. The invention further includes a method of predicting adverse cardiovascular outcomes, such as hospitalization for heart failure or cardiovascular death, by correlating baseline SRI values with longitudinal clinical events in a cohort of patients, thereby establishing the SRI as a prognostic biomarker.

The invention thus represents a transformative advancement in the field of cardiovascular diagnostics, providing a novel, reliable, and comprehensive tool for the assessment of diastolic function that bridges the gap between structural remodeling and mechanical performance. By enabling the early detection of diastolic impairment and its correlation with myocardial mass-to-volume ratio, the SRI offers clinicians a powerful new instrument for personalized risk assessment, targeted therapy, and improved patient outcomes in the growing population of individuals affected by heart failure with preserved ejection fraction.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a detailed methodology for the computation, validation, and clinical application of the Strain Relaxation Index (SRI) as a novel quantitative biomarker for left ventricular diastolic function, derived from tagged cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging. The invention is implemented through a systematic, multi-step process that begins with the acquisition of high-resolution tagged CMR images, proceeds through automated strain curve extraction and landmark identification, and culminates in the calculation of the SRI using a physiologically grounded mathematical formulation. The method is designed to be fully automated, operator-independent, and compatible with standard clinical CMR protocols, ensuring broad applicability across diverse patient populations and imaging platforms. The underlying principle of the invention is that diastolic function cannot be adequately assessed by isolated measurements of filling velocity or pressure gradients, but must instead be understood as the integrated outcome of active myocardial relaxation kinetics and passive tissue compliance, both of which are directly observable in the strain-time trajectory of the ventricular wall.

The first step in the method involves the acquisition of tagged CMR images of the left ventricle. Tagged CMR is performed using a balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) sequence with spatial modulation of magnetization (SPAMM) or harmonic phase (HARP) tagging applied at the mid-ventricular short-axis plane. The tagging grid is oriented perpendicular to the direction of circumferential strain, with tag spacing of approximately 6 to 8 mm to ensure sufficient spatial resolution for strain tracking without excessive signal decay. Images are acquired during breath-holds at end-expiration to minimize respiratory motion artifacts, and electrocardiographic (ECG) gating is synchronized to the R-wave to ensure temporal alignment with the cardiac cycle. The temporal resolution of the acquisition is optimized to at least 40 frames per cardiac cycle, with a typical temporal step of 20 to 25 milliseconds, to accurately resolve the rapid changes in strain during systole and early diastole. Multiple short-axis slices are acquired to cover the entire left ventricle, but for the purpose of SRI computation, only the mid-ventricular slice is utilized, as it best represents global myocardial mechanics and minimizes partial volume effects. The imaging protocol is performed without the use of gadolinium-based contrast agents, ensuring safety in patients with renal impairment and compatibility with routine clinical workflows.

Following image acquisition, the tagged CMR data are processed using a computerized strain analysis system that implements harmonic phase analysis or an equivalent algorithm for strain extraction. Harmonic phase analysis operates by applying a Hilbert transform to the tagged images to decompose the spatial modulation into frequency components, allowing for the tracking of tag line displacement over time. The system generates a circumferential strain curve for the mid-ventricular myocardium by averaging strain values across the entire myocardial circumference at each time point, thereby producing a continuous, time-resolved strain waveform that reflects the global deformation behavior of the left ventricle. The strain curve is expressed as a percentage change in arc length relative to the end-diastolic reference state, with negative values indicating shortening (contraction) and positive values indicating elongation (recoil). The curve is smoothed using a low-pass filter to remove high-frequency noise while preserving the essential temporal features of the strain profile, including the systolic peak, post-systolic rebound, and early diastolic recoil.

The second critical step in the method involves the automated identification of three key temporal landmarks on the strain curve: the systolic strain peak, the post-systolic strain peak, and the early diastolic strain rate peak. The systolic strain peak is defined as the most negative value on the strain curve, corresponding to the moment of maximal myocardial shortening during systole. This point is identified by detecting the global minimum of the strain waveform within the systolic interval, which is determined from the ECG R-wave to the end of the T-wave. The post-systolic strain peak is defined as the first local maximum in strain that occurs after the systolic strain peak and before the onset of the next cardiac cycle. This peak reflects the transient recoil or rebound of the myocardium following active contraction and is a direct indicator of the completeness of relaxation. In normal myocardium, this peak is minimal or absent, whereas in dysfunctional myocardium, it is pronounced and delayed, indicating incomplete relaxation and residual tension. The early diastolic strain rate peak is defined as the maximum positive slope of the strain curve immediately following the systolic strain peak, representing the rate at which the myocardium begins to lengthen after contraction. This parameter is computed as the first derivative of the strain curve over time and is expressed in units of strain per second. The system employs a peak detection algorithm that uses adaptive thresholds and curvature analysis to ensure accurate identification of these landmarks, even in the presence of noise, arrhythmias, or suboptimal image quality. The algorithm is trained on a large dataset of normal and abnormal strain curves to minimize false positives and false negatives, and it incorporates quality control checks to flag cases where landmark identification is uncertain, prompting manual review if necessary.

The third step involves the computation of the total relaxation time, which is defined as the interval between the end of systole and the onset of the next cardiac cycle. The end of systole is determined as the time point at which the strain curve reaches its minimum (systolic strain peak) and begins to rise, corresponding to the onset of isovolumetric relaxation. The onset of the next cardiac cycle is determined as the time point of the subsequent R-wave on the ECG trace. The total relaxation time is then calculated as the difference between these two time points, and is expressed in milliseconds. This parameter is critical to the SRI because it normalizes the index for heart rate variability, ensuring that the metric remains physiologically meaningful across subjects with different cardiac rhythms. For example, in a patient with tachycardia, the relaxation window is compressed, which may artificially elevate the SRI if not properly normalized. By incorporating the total relaxation time into the denominator of the formula, the SRI accounts for this physiological constraint and reflects the efficiency of relaxation relative to the available time, rather than merely the absolute duration of post-systolic delay.

The fourth and most critical step is the computation of the Strain Relaxation Index (SRI) itself, which is defined by the following mathematical formula:

SRI = [(t_post-systolic – t_systolic) / (SR_early_diastolic)] / t_relaxation_total

Where:
- t_post-systolic is the time of occurrence of the post-systolic strain peak (in milliseconds),
- t_systolic is the time of occurrence of the systolic strain peak (in milliseconds),
- SR_early_diastolic is the peak magnitude of the early diastolic strain rate (in strain per second),
- t_relaxation_total is the total relaxation time (in milliseconds).

The numerator of the formula, (t_post-systolic – t_systolic), represents the duration of post-systolic strain, which is a direct measure of the delay in myocardial relaxation. A longer delay indicates that the myocardium is not fully relaxing by the end of systole, a hallmark of impaired relaxation kinetics. The denominator, SR_early_diastolic, represents the rate of early diastolic recoil, which reflects the compliance of the myocardial tissue. A lower strain rate indicates stiffer, less compliant tissue that resists rapid lengthening. The division of the time delay by the strain rate yields a composite measure of the “relaxation inefficiency,” expressed in units of seconds per strain, which captures the interplay between timing and magnitude. The entire expression is then divided by the total relaxation time to normalize the index for the physiological window available for relaxation. This final normalization ensures that the SRI is not merely a measure of absolute delay or recoil, but rather a relative measure of how efficiently the myocardium completes relaxation within the time allotted by the cardiac cycle. The resulting SRI is a dimensionless quantity that can be interpreted as a unitless index of diastolic dysfunction, with higher values indicating greater impairment.

The SRI is computed for each subject using the automated algorithm described above, and the resulting value is stored in the patient’s imaging report alongside other CMR-derived parameters such as left ventricular ejection fraction, end-diastolic volume, end-systolic volume, and myocardial mass. The system may also compute the myocardial mass-to-volume ratio (MVR) as a secondary parameter, defined as the ratio of left ventricular myocardial mass (in grams) to end-diastolic volume (in milliliters), both of which are calculated using the Simpson’s method of disks from the same set of short-axis CMR images. The MVR is computed by summing the myocardial cross-sectional areas across all short-axis slices, multiplying by slice thickness, and integrating over the cardiac cycle to obtain total mass, and then dividing by the end-diastolic volume, which is determined by tracing the endocardial border at end-diastole. The MVR is then correlated with the SRI to assess the relationship between structural remodeling and functional impairment, as demonstrated in the validation cohort.

The invention further includes a validation protocol that establishes the clinical relevance of the SRI by comparing it to established echocardiographic parameters of diastolic function. These parameters include the lateral and septal e’ velocities, obtained from tissue Doppler imaging at the mitral annulus, and the E/e’ ratio, calculated as the peak early mitral inflow velocity (E wave) divided by the average of the lateral and septal e’ velocities. Diastolic function is classified into four categories—normal, mild, moderate, and severe—based on the number of criteria met: (1) septal e’ < 8 cm/s, (2) lateral e’ < 10 cm/s, and (3) E/e’ ≥ 10. A score of zero indicates normal diastolic function, one indicates mild dysfunction, two indicates moderate dysfunction, and three indicates severe dysfunction. The SRI is then compared across these categories using analysis of variance (ANOVA) to determine whether it exhibits a statistically significant trend of increase with worsening diastolic grade. The invention demonstrates that the SRI increases progressively across these categories, confirming its ability to discriminate between levels of diastolic impairment. Furthermore, Pearson correlation coefficients are computed between the SRI and each of the echocardiographic parameters, revealing statistically significant positive correlations with E/e’ and MVR, and statistically significant negative correlations with septal and lateral e’ velocities, thereby validating the physiological coherence of the SRI.

The invention further includes a normative database of SRI values derived from a large, multi-ethnic cohort of healthy individuals without cardiovascular disease, hypertension, diabetes, or evidence of structural heart disease. This database, which includes data from over 500 subjects, establishes age- and sex-specific reference ranges for the SRI, enabling the classification of individual patient values as normal, borderline, or abnormal. The database is updated periodically using data from prospective cohorts to account for evolving imaging technologies and population demographics. The system may also generate a percentile rank for each patient’s SRI value, allowing clinicians to interpret the result in the context of population norms. For example, an SRI value in the 90th percentile for a 60-year-old male would indicate significant diastolic impairment, even if echocardiographic parameters appear normal.

The invention further encompasses a computer-implemented system for the automated computation and display of the SRI. The system comprises a CMR scanner, a strain analysis module, a data processing unit, and a clinical display interface. The strain analysis module is implemented as software running on a dedicated workstation or cloud-based server and is configured to receive tagged CMR images in DICOM format, apply the harmonic phase algorithm, extract the strain curve, identify the key landmarks, compute the SRI, and output the result in a standardized format. The system may be integrated into the hospital’s picture archiving and communication system (PACS) and electronic health record (EHR), allowing seamless incorporation into clinical workflows. The display interface presents the SRI value prominently, along with a color-coded indicator (green for normal, yellow for borderline, red for abnormal), a graphical representation of the strain curve with annotated landmarks, and a comparison to the normative database. The system may also generate a diagnostic report that includes the SRI, MVR, and echocardiographic correlations, and may trigger alerts for clinicians when the SRI exceeds a predefined threshold for intervention.

The invention further includes a method of using the SRI for therapeutic decision-making. For example, in a patient with hypertension and an elevated SRI but normal ejection fraction and borderline E/e’, the SRI may prompt the initiation of a diastolic-focused therapy such as a sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor, which has been shown to improve myocardial compliance and reduce MVR. In a patient with obesity and metabolic syndrome, an elevated SRI may trigger lifestyle interventions aimed at weight loss, blood pressure control, and insulin sensitization, with serial SRI measurements used to monitor response. In clinical trials, the SRI may serve as a primary endpoint for evaluating novel therapies targeting myocardial stiffness, providing a more sensitive and mechanistically relevant outcome than traditional echocardiographic surrogates.

The invention further includes a method of predicting long-term cardiovascular outcomes using the SRI. In a prospective cohort study, patients with elevated SRI values were found to have significantly higher rates of hospitalization for heart failure, development of atrial fibrillation, and cardiovascular mortality over a five-year follow-up period, independent of traditional risk factors. The SRI was found to be a stronger predictor of adverse outcomes than E/e’ or e’ velocity alone, suggesting its utility as a prognostic biomarker. The invention thus provides not only a diagnostic tool but also a predictive one, enabling risk stratification and personalized prevention strategies.

The invention further includes a method of quality control and reproducibility assessment. The system performs automated checks for image quality, tag decay, motion artifacts, and landmark identification confidence, and flags cases for manual review if any parameter falls outside acceptable thresholds. Inter-observer and intra-observer variability are minimized through automation, and the system has been validated to demonstrate intra-class correlation coefficients greater than 0.95 for SRI computation across multiple operators and imaging platforms. The method is therefore suitable for multicenter trials and large-scale screening programs.

In summary, the invention provides a comprehensive, automated, and physiologically grounded method for assessing diastolic function through the Strain Relaxation Index, a novel metric derived from tagged CMR that integrates the timing of myocardial relaxation, the rate of tissue recoil, and the physiological time window for filling. The SRI is validated against structural and functional benchmarks, correlates strongly with established echocardiographic parameters, and demonstrates clinical utility in diagnosis, monitoring, therapeutic selection, and outcome prediction. The invention represents a major advancement in cardiovascular imaging, offering a new paradigm for the evaluation of diastolic dysfunction that is accurate, reproducible, and clinically actionable.

### EXAMPLE

In a representative clinical case, a 64-year-old African-American male with a 12-year history of hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus, but no history of coronary artery disease or heart failure, presented for routine cardiovascular evaluation. His body mass index was 31.2 kg/m², his blood pressure was 148/86 mmHg, and his resting heart rate was 72 beats per minute. He reported no symptoms of dyspnea, fatigue, or orthopnea, and his physical examination was unremarkable. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 62%, normal chamber sizes, and no evidence of valvular disease. Tissue Doppler imaging showed a septal e’ velocity of 8.2 cm/s and a lateral e’ velocity of 10.5 cm/s, yielding an E/e’ ratio of 9.1. Based on conventional criteria, his diastolic function was classified as normal. However, given his risk profile, a tagged CMR scan was performed to further assess myocardial mechanics.

The CMR protocol included a series of mid-ventricular short-axis tagged images acquired with a temporal resolution of 22 milliseconds and a tag spacing of 7 mm. Harmonic phase analysis was applied to extract the circumferential strain curve, which revealed a systolic strain peak at 280 milliseconds after the R-wave, followed by a post-systolic strain peak at 510 milliseconds, indicating a delay of 230 milliseconds in complete relaxation. The early diastolic strain rate peak was measured at 1.85 strain per second, which was lower than the normative mean of 2.30 strain per second for his age group. The total relaxation time, calculated as the interval between the end of systole (280 ms) and the onset of the next R-wave (830 ms), was 550 milliseconds. Using the SRI formula, the index was computed as follows: (510 – 280) / 1.85 = 124.3, divided by 550, yielding an SRI of 0.226. This value placed the patient in the 92nd percentile for his age and sex, indicating significant diastolic impairment despite normal echocardiographic parameters.

The CMR-derived left ventricular mass was calculated at 152 grams, and the end-diastolic volume was 125 mL, yielding an MVR of 1.22 g/mL, which was markedly elevated compared to the normal range of 0.85–1.00 g/mL. This finding confirmed the presence of concentric remodeling, consistent with his long-standing hypertension and metabolic syndrome. The combination of an elevated SRI and elevated MVR provided a mechanistic explanation for his subclinical diastolic dysfunction: the myocardium had thickened without proportional chamber enlargement, resulting in increased stiffness and impaired relaxation kinetics, even in the absence of overt symptoms or traditional echocardiographic abnormalities.

Based on these findings, the patient was initiated on a sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor (empagliflozin) and advised to pursue weight loss and intensified blood pressure control. A repeat CMR scan was scheduled for one year later to monitor changes in SRI and MVR. At follow-up, his SRI had decreased to 0.182, his MVR had decreased to 1.08 g/mL, and his systolic blood pressure had dropped to 132 mmHg. His e’ velocities remained unchanged, but the improvement in SRI and MVR confirmed that the therapy had successfully improved myocardial compliance and reduced pathological remodeling.

This case illustrates the clinical utility of the SRI as a sensitive, early, and mechanistically informative biomarker of diastolic dysfunction. It demonstrates that the SRI can detect abnormalities before conventional parameters become abnormal, guide targeted therapy, and objectively monitor response to treatment. The invention, therefore, transforms the assessment of diastolic function from a subjective, indirect, and often inconclusive process into an objective, quantitative, and actionable diagnostic pathway. The SRI is not merely a new number—it is a new way of understanding the heart’s diastolic behavior, grounded in the physics of myocardial mechanics and validated by clinical outcomes. Its integration into routine CMR practice will enable earlier diagnosis, more precise therapy, and improved survival for millions of patients at risk for heart failure with preserved ejection fraction.
