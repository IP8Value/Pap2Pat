# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### 1. Field of the Invention

The present invention relates to the field of reinforcement learning, and more specifically to methods and systems for improving the sample efficiency, learning stability, and long-term retention of learned behaviors in model-free, off-policy reinforcement learning algorithms through the use of structured experience replay mechanisms. The invention introduces a novel framework for organizing and sampling historical trajectory data from an experience replay buffer by partitioning it into specialized sub-buffers, termed Event Tables, each of which captures finite-length sequences of state-action-reward transitions that culminate in predefined, domain-specific events. These events are selected to correspond to critical milestones in the agent’s interaction with the environment—such as reaching a goal state, overcoming a bottleneck, executing a complex maneuver, or recovering from a failure mode—and are designed to align with the optimal policy’s trajectory structure. The invention further introduces a stratified sampling procedure, called Stratified Sampling from Event Tables (SSET), which dynamically constructs training mini-batches by drawing samples from each Event Table according to fixed, pre-specified proportions, while preserving the independence of sampled transitions to ensure training stability. This approach enables the reinforcement learning agent to accelerate the propagation of value estimates from high-reward or high-significance states back to earlier states in the trajectory, effectively creating “fast lanes” for temporal difference backups that bypass the inefficiencies of uniform or error-based prioritization schemes. The invention is applicable to a broad range of continuous and discrete control domains, including robotics, autonomous driving, game playing, and simulation-based training systems, and is compatible with a variety of off-policy deep reinforcement learning algorithms such as Deep Q-Networks (DQN), Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), and Quantile Regression SAC. The invention further provides mechanisms for bias correction when sampling from non-uniform distributions, ensures preservation of the Bellman optimality condition, and demonstrates superior performance in mitigating catastrophic forgetting during prolonged training across multiple skills or environmental conditions.

### 2. Description of Prior Art and Related Information

Prior approaches to improving sample efficiency in off-policy reinforcement learning have primarily relied on the use of experience replay buffers, which store past state-action-reward-next-state tuples and enable the reuse of collected data across multiple learning updates. The foundational technique, introduced by Lin (1992), involves randomly sampling mini-batches from a first-in-first-out (FIFO) buffer to decorrelate sequential data and stabilize neural network training. Subsequent advancements have sought to enhance this process by prioritizing experiences that are deemed more informative for learning. The most widely adopted method, Prioritized Experience Replay (PER), proposed by Schaul et al. (2016), assigns sampling probabilities to transitions based on the magnitude of their temporal difference (TD) error, under the assumption that transitions with higher prediction errors are more informative and should be revisited more frequently. While PER has demonstrated improvements in convergence speed in certain environments, it suffers from several critical limitations. First, TD error is a local, model-dependent metric that reflects the discrepancy between current value estimates and observed rewards, rather than the intrinsic importance of a state or transition with respect to the optimal policy. Consequently, PER may over-sample transitions that are transiently erroneous due to noisy rewards or suboptimal value function approximations, while under-sampling transitions that are consistently correct but occur infrequently along the optimal trajectory. Second, PER does not distinguish between states that are likely to be visited by the optimal policy and those that are merely high-error due to poor exploration or function approximation artifacts. This leads to a misalignment between the sampling distribution and the true structure of optimal behavior, resulting in inefficient learning, especially in sparse-reward or long-horizon tasks where critical events are rare. Third, PER is inherently unstable in environments with dense reward signals, as the TD errors become volatile and noisy, causing the prioritization mechanism to oscillate and degrade performance relative to uniform sampling, as empirically demonstrated in continuous control benchmarks such as MuJoCo and LunarLander.

Other approaches have attempted to address these shortcomings by incorporating trajectory-level structure into the replay mechanism. RTDP (Barto et al., 1995) and related methods such as EBU (Lee et al., 2019) and Topological Experience Replay (Hong et al., 2021) prioritize backups along trajectories that lead to goal states, effectively propagating value information backward from terminal conditions. However, these methods often rely on sampling entire trajectories or contiguous sequences of transitions, which introduces strong temporal correlations within mini-batches and destabilizes gradient-based learning, as noted by de Bruin et al. (2015, 2016) and Huang et al. (2019). Furthermore, these methods typically require the explicit identification of goal states or the construction of a discrete state-space embedding, limiting their applicability to continuous, high-dimensional domains where such structure is not readily available. Similarly, methods that partition the replay buffer based on reward thresholds (Sharma et al., 2020) or reward quantiles store only the terminal transitions associated with high rewards, neglecting the critical context of the preceding state-action history that led to those rewards. This omission prevents the propagation of value estimates from the reward state back to earlier, non-rewarding states that are necessary for learning complex, multi-step behaviors. Moreover, such approaches are incapable of capturing the dynamics of recovery from failure, as they do not store the sequences of transitions that lead to negative outcomes or suboptimal states.

Alternative strategies have explored the use of multiple buffers to separate different types of experiences. For instance, de Bruin et al. (2016) maintain two distinct buffers—one for on-policy data and another for off-policy data—requiring expensive kernel density estimation to maintain their separation. Lucid Dreaming (Du et al., 2022) stores trajectories that yield higher Monte Carlo returns than predicted by the current value function, but relies on generative access to the environment to simulate alternative futures, a requirement that is infeasible in real-world or black-box simulation settings. Multi-table approaches have also been applied in simulated racing environments (Wurman et al., 2022), where the replay buffer is partitioned based on initial state conditions such as “driving alone” or “driving in traffic.” However, this method requires pre-defined, static episode categorizations and cannot adapt to dynamically occurring events during an episode. It also fails to capture the internal structure of trajectories within each category, limiting its ability to reinforce the specific sequences of actions that lead to success or failure.

In addition, prior work has investigated reward shaping as a means of accelerating learning by augmenting the environment’s reward function with potential-based shaping terms (Ng et al., 1999; Grzes, 2017). These shaping functions provide intermediate rewards based on proximity to desired states, thereby guiding exploration without altering the optimal policy. However, shaping rewards are inherently heuristic and require careful manual tuning to avoid introducing bias or distorting the reward landscape. Moreover, while shaping rewards improve exploration, they do not directly accelerate the propagation of value estimates from high-reward states back to initial states; the bootstrapping process still relies on the same uniform or error-based sampling mechanisms, resulting in slow convergence in long-horizon tasks. The invention presented here overcomes these limitations by decoupling the role of exploration guidance from value function backup efficiency. It introduces a mechanism that preserves the original reward structure while explicitly constructing and over-sampling trajectories that lead to meaningful events, thereby enabling efficient, stable, and bias-corrected backups along the most relevant paths in the state space.

Finally, the problem of catastrophic forgetting in reinforcement learning—where previously learned skills are degraded as the agent adapts to new ones—has been addressed in multi-task and lifelong learning settings through methods such as Hindsight Experience Replay (Andrychowicz et al., 2017), which re-labels past trajectories with alternative goals, or through gradient-based multi-objective optimization techniques such as CAGrad (Liu et al., 2021), which balances updates across conflicting tasks. However, these methods are designed for scenarios involving multiple distinct tasks or goals, whereas the present invention operates within a single-task setting and leverages event-based partitioning to retain knowledge of rare but critical sub-behaviors—such as recovering from off-course driving in a racing simulator or navigating through a bottleneck in a grid world—without requiring task re-labeling or gradient conflict resolution. The invention thus provides a novel, general-purpose solution to the problem of skill retention in continuous learning environments, where the agent must maintain competence across a spectrum of behaviors that vary in frequency and difficulty.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a system and method for enhancing the sample efficiency, learning stability, and long-term retention of policies in off-policy reinforcement learning by introducing Event Tables and a stratified sampling protocol known as Stratified Sampling from Event Tables (SSET). The invention operates by partitioning a standard experience replay buffer into multiple specialized sub-buffers, each dedicated to storing finite-length trajectories that terminate in a predefined event. An event is defined as a condition on the state of the environment that is satisfied when the agent reaches a state deemed critical for learning, such as achieving a goal, passing an obstacle, recovering from a failure, or entering a high-reward region. Each Event Table retains the complete sequence of state-action-reward-next-state transitions leading up to the event, up to a specified history length, thereby preserving the causal context necessary for effective temporal difference backups. The invention further introduces a sampling procedure that, during each training iteration, draws a fixed proportion of mini-batch samples from each Event Table and from a default buffer containing all other transitions, ensuring that critical trajectories are over-sampled relative to their frequency of occurrence in the environment. This stratified sampling mechanism is designed to be independent and identically distributed across transitions, avoiding the instability caused by temporally correlated sequences while still enabling the agent to rapidly propagate value estimates from high-significance events back to earlier states in the trajectory. The invention further includes a bias correction mechanism that adjusts the importance weights of sampled transitions to preserve the Bellman optimality condition, even in stochastic environments where the event conditions may skew the distribution of next-state outcomes. This correction is computed either through exact counting in discrete domains or via a priority-sum tree structure analogous to Prioritized Experience Replay, ensuring that the learning objective remains unbiased and convergent to the true optimal value function. The invention is compatible with any off-policy reinforcement learning algorithm that utilizes experience replay, including Deep Q-Networks, Soft Actor-Critic, and Quantile Regression SAC, and can be combined with existing prioritization schemes such as TD-error-based prioritization to further refine sampling within each Event Table. Empirical results demonstrate that the invention significantly accelerates learning in both discrete and continuous control domains, reduces variance across training runs, improves policy stability, and mitigates catastrophic forgetting by retaining knowledge of rare but critical behaviors over extended training periods. The invention requires no changes to the underlying neural network architecture or learning algorithm, and can be implemented as a lightweight wrapper around existing replay buffer systems with minimal memory overhead, making it immediately deployable in real-world reinforcement learning pipelines.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS AND BEST MODE OF INVENTION

### Terminology

In the context of this invention, the term “Markov Decision Process” refers to a formal framework for modeling decision-making in environments where outcomes are partly random and partly under the control of a decision-maker. The process is defined by a tuple consisting of a state space, an action space, a reward function, a transition kernel, a discount factor, an initial state distribution, and an episode termination condition. The state space encompasses all possible configurations of the environment, while the action space contains all permissible actions that the agent may execute. The reward function assigns a scalar value to each state-action pair, representing the immediate benefit or cost of taking that action in that state. The transition kernel defines the probability distribution over next states given the current state and action, capturing the stochastic dynamics of the environment. The discount factor determines the relative importance of future rewards compared to immediate ones, and the initial state distribution specifies the probability of starting in any given state at the beginning of an episode. The episode termination condition defines when a sequence of interactions ends, either through reaching a terminal state or exceeding a maximum time horizon. The agent’s behavior policy is the rule by which it selects actions at each step, and may be stochastic or deterministic. The optimal policy is the policy that maximizes the expected cumulative discounted reward over the course of an episode. The value function of a policy quantifies the expected return from a given state or state-action pair under that policy, and the goal of reinforcement learning is to learn an approximation of the optimal value function. In this invention, the value function is typically represented by a neural network with parameters that are updated using gradient descent based on temporal difference errors. The experience replay buffer is a data structure that stores past transitions of the form (state, action, reward, next state), and is used to break the temporal correlation between consecutive samples during training. The term “event” refers to a Boolean condition defined over the state space that is satisfied when the agent reaches a state of particular significance, such as achieving a goal, encountering a bottleneck, or executing a critical maneuver. An event condition is a function that maps states to binary values, indicating whether the event has occurred. The history length associated with an event specifies the number of preceding transitions that are stored in the corresponding Event Table when the event is triggered. The Event Table is a specialized sub-buffer within the experience replay system that stores all transitions leading up to and including the event, up to the specified history length. The default buffer is the standard experience replay buffer that contains all transitions not captured by any Event Table, and serves as the baseline source of training data. Stratified sampling refers to the process of constructing mini-batches for training by drawing a fixed proportion of samples from each Event Table and from the default buffer, according to pre-specified weights. The sampling probabilities are fixed throughout training and are chosen to ensure that critical trajectories are over-represented relative to their natural frequency of occurrence. The bias correction term is a weight applied to each sampled transition to adjust for the fact that sampling from Event Tables alters the underlying distribution of next-state outcomes, ensuring that the expected value of the Bellman target remains unchanged. The term “catastrophic forgetting” refers to the phenomenon in which an agent loses the ability to perform previously learned behaviors as it adapts to new ones, often due to the overwriting of value estimates in a monolithic replay buffer. The invention mitigates this by preserving trajectories associated with rare events in dedicated tables, ensuring that their value estimates continue to be updated even as the agent’s behavior policy evolves.

### MiniGrid Experiments

The invention was validated in a suite of environments from the MiniGrid domain, a collection of procedurally generated grid-world tasks designed to test the ability of reinforcement learning agents to learn complex, multi-step behaviors in sparse-reward settings. In the three-room grid world, the agent must navigate from a starting position in one room to a goal state in another, passing through a narrow corridor that connects the rooms. The reward structure is sparse: the agent receives a small negative reward for each step and a large positive reward upon reaching the goal. The invention was implemented with two event conditions: one triggered when the agent passes through the corridor (a bottleneck state), and another triggered when the agent reaches the goal. Each event was associated with a history length of 200 steps, ensuring that the full trajectory from the initial state to the event was captured. The stratified sampling protocol assigned equal weights to the two Event Tables and a higher weight to the default buffer to ensure sufficient exploration. The results demonstrated a significant improvement in both sample efficiency and learning stability compared to uniform experience replay, reverse-sweep updates, and Prioritized Experience Replay. The learning curves showed that SSET converged to near-optimal performance in fewer episodes, with substantially lower variance across random seeds. The reverse-sweep method, which performs backward updates from the goal, failed to match the performance of SSET because it did not incorporate intermediate waypoints, resulting in slower propagation of value estimates. PER, while improving over uniform sampling, exhibited high variance and occasional divergence due to overestimation bias caused by the volatility of TD errors in sparse-reward settings. When SSET was combined with PER—applying TD-error prioritization within each Event Table—the performance improved further, achieving the highest mean return and lowest variance. This hybrid approach leveraged the structural advantages of SSET in focusing on critical trajectories while exploiting the local refinement capabilities of PER to prioritize transitions with high Bellman error within those trajectories. In a second experiment, the invention was compared to potential-based reward shaping, a technique that augments the environment’s reward function with a shaping potential that encourages the agent to move toward the corridor. The shaping function provided a continuous reward signal proportional to proximity to the corridor, but did not alter the optimal policy. SSET, without any shaping, outperformed both the shaping baseline and the no-shaping baseline, demonstrating that the structured sampling of trajectories was more effective than heuristic reward augmentation. When SSET was combined with shaping, the performance improved further, indicating that the two mechanisms are complementary: shaping guides exploration toward promising regions, while SSET accelerates value propagation within those regions. A series of ablation studies examined the sensitivity of SSET to poorly designed event conditions. When events were placed near the goal but not at the corridor, or near the starting position, the performance degraded but remained comparable to or better than the best shaping baseline. This demonstrated the robustness of the method: even when events were not optimally aligned with the optimal policy, SSET did not degrade performance below that of uniform sampling, and often improved it. The sampling weight assigned to the default buffer was also varied, and it was found that higher weights (e.g., η₀ = 0.7) were beneficial when event conditions were imperfect, as they ensured sufficient exploration from non-event states. In a more complex obstacle course environment, the agent must navigate through multiple rooms, each containing obstacles such as spikes, lava, and locked doors requiring colored keys. The event conditions were defined for each major milestone: picking up a key, opening a door, avoiding lava, and reaching the goal. Each event was associated with a history length of 50 steps. SSET significantly outperformed uniform sampling and PER, particularly in acquiring the difficult skill of opening doors, which occurred infrequently and required precise sequences of actions. Uniform sampling failed to learn this skill because the majority of transitions were associated with the easier tasks of avoiding spikes or navigating gaps. SSET, by over-sampling trajectories that led to door-opening events, ensured that the value function for those transitions was updated frequently enough to learn the correct policy. In a randomized multi-skill variant of this environment, where the type of obstacle in each room was randomly assigned per episode, SSET maintained competence across all skills, whereas uniform sampling and PER exhibited catastrophic forgetting of the door-opening skill as training progressed. In a separate experiment designed to test long-term retention, agents were periodically evaluated starting from a shifted initial position that had not been used during training. Agents using uniform or PER replay rapidly forgot how to avoid spikes near the original starting position, as their behavior policy shifted to optimize for the new starting location. In contrast, agents using SSET continued to improve their value estimates for the spike-avoidance behavior, because the trajectories leading to spike encounters were preserved in the corresponding Event Table and continued to be sampled regardless of the current behavior policy. This demonstrated the invention’s ability to mitigate catastrophic forgetting by decoupling the storage of critical experiences from the dynamics of the current policy. Further experiments examined the effect of history length and the number of event tables. It was found that using multiple intermediate events with shorter histories outperformed using fewer events with longer histories, particularly in early training, because long histories often contained many suboptimal actions that diluted the signal from the critical event. The optimal configuration was to use a sufficient number of waypoints—such as “picked up key,” “approached door,” and “opened door”—to create a chain of fast lanes for value propagation. The sampling weights assigned to each Event Table were also tested, and it was found that equal weights across all tables performed nearly as well as manually tuned weights, suggesting that a default equal weighting strategy is a robust and practical choice for practitioners. The invention was also combined with Conflict-Averse Gradient descent, a multi-objective optimization method that minimizes gradient conflicts between different event tables. This further improved learning speed for the most difficult skill (door-opening), although the asymptotic performance was slightly reduced, suggesting that dynamic adjustment of the conflict-aversion parameter during training could yield additional benefits.

### Lunar Lander and Mujoco Experiments

The invention was evaluated on a suite of continuous control benchmarks from the OpenAI Gym, including the LunarLanderContinuous-v3 environment and four MuJoCo domains: HalfCheetah, Hopper, Walker2d, and Ant. These environments feature dense, continuous reward signals that incentivize forward progress, balance, and energy efficiency, and are widely used to test the sample efficiency and stability of deep reinforcement learning algorithms. In the LunarLander environment, the agent must land a spacecraft between two flags on a terrain with variable elevation, avoiding crashes and minimizing fuel consumption. Two event conditions were defined: one triggered when both landing legs made contact with the ground within the designated landing zone, and another triggered when the lander’s horizontal position was within a narrow band centered on the midpoint between the flags. Each event was associated with a history length of 200 steps, and the stratified sampling protocol assigned equal weights to the two Event Tables and a higher weight to the default buffer. The Soft Actor-Critic algorithm was used as the base learner, and performance was compared against uniform experience replay and Prioritized Experience Replay with varying priority exponents. The results showed that SSET achieved approximately twice the sample efficiency of uniform sampling, reaching the same level of performance in half the number of training steps. PER, in contrast, performed no better than uniform sampling and degraded as the priority exponent increased, due to the high density of shaping rewards causing TD errors to become noisy and uninformative. The invention’s advantage was particularly pronounced in the stability of the learned policies: while uniform and PER agents exhibited large fluctuations in performance across training runs, SSET agents converged smoothly and consistently. In the MuJoCo domains, three event conditions were defined based on reward thresholds: a low-threshold event triggered when the agent received a reward above 10% of the maximum possible, a medium-threshold event triggered above 50%, and a high-threshold event triggered above 80%. Each event was associated with a history length of 200 steps. The results demonstrated that SSET improved sample efficiency by 40–60% across all four domains, with the most significant gains observed in the high-dimensional Ant environment. Again, PER showed no consistent improvement over uniform sampling and often performed worse, particularly at higher priority exponents. An ablation study was conducted to examine the sensitivity of SSET to history length. It was found that even when the history length was set to values that were too short to capture the full trajectory from the initial state to the event, SSET still outperformed uniform sampling, indicating that the method is robust to imperfectly specified history lengths. This is because the Event Tables still capture the most recent context leading to high-reward states, and even partial trajectories provide sufficient signal for value propagation. The invention’s performance was further validated by comparing it to reward shaping, which was applied using the same reward thresholds as the event conditions. While shaping improved learning speed over the baseline, it did not match the performance of SSET, and the combination of SSET with shaping yielded the best overall results. This confirmed that the invention’s mechanism of structured sampling is more effective than reward augmentation for accelerating value function learning. The stability of the learned policies was also examined, and it was found that SSET agents exhibited significantly lower variance in their final performance across random seeds, indicating that the method reduces the sensitivity of learning to initialization and hyperparameter choice. The invention’s ability to handle dense reward environments was particularly notable, as it demonstrated that the benefits of Event Tables are not limited to sparse-reward settings but extend to any scenario where certain states or transitions are more informative than others, regardless of whether the reward signal is sparse or dense. The results in these environments established that SSET is a general-purpose enhancement to off-policy reinforcement learning that improves both the speed and reliability of learning, even in the presence of complex, continuous dynamics and high-dimensional state spaces.

### Simulated Car Racing Experiments

The invention was evaluated in the highly realistic Gran Turismo Sport racing simulator, a platform previously used to train reinforcement learning agents that outperformed human e-sports champions in competitive racing. The simulator features physics-based vehicle dynamics, detailed track geometry, and a rich state representation including 3D velocity, steering angle, slipstream intensity, and relative positions of opponent vehicles. Two experiments were conducted to isolate the effects of Event Tables. In the first experiment, the agent was trained to execute a “slingshot pass” on a 1.7-kilometer straightaway of the Circuit de la Sarthe track, using a Red Bull X2019 Competition race car. The agent started 10 to 40 meters behind a built-in AI opponent and was tasked with using the opponent’s slipstream to accelerate beyond its open-air top speed and overtake it before the end of the section. Two event conditions were defined: a “slipstream” event triggered when the agent’s slipstream feature exceeded a threshold of 0.7, indicating close proximity to the opponent, and a “won” event triggered when the agent finished the section in first place. Each event was associated with a history length of 10 and 15 seconds, respectively, and the sampling weights were set to 10% for each Event Table. The base algorithm was Quantile Regression Soft Actor-Critic, with 2048-unit ReLU networks and dropout applied to the policy network. The results showed that SSET achieved a higher win rate than uniform sampling, with significantly lower variance across training runs. While uniform sampling agents exhibited erratic behavior—sometimes executing perfect passes and other times veering off course due to catastrophic forgetting—the SSET agents consistently learned to stay on track and execute the slingshot maneuver reliably. The worst-performing SSET run still outperformed the best-performing uniform sampling run in terms of on-course consistency. In a second experiment, the agent was trained to complete fast laps on the full Lago Maggiore GP track without opponents, with the objective of minimizing lap time while avoiding off-course penalties. Off-course events occurred infrequently—approximately once every 1200 steps—but had severe penalties that discouraged corner-cutting. A “re-establish” event was defined to trigger when the agent returned to the track surface for at least two seconds after having left it, and a history length of seven seconds was used to capture the full trajectory of leaving and returning to the track. The sampling weights were set to 1% for this Event Table and 99% for the default buffer. The results showed that agents using uniform sampling oscillated between periods of on-course driving and periods of aggressive corner-cutting, as the policy repeatedly forgot the penalty associated with leaving the track. After 1000 epochs, only 74.7% of the uniform sampling policies incurred no off-course penalties. In contrast, SSET agents maintained consistent on-course behavior, with 88.9% of policies avoiding penalties entirely. The worst SSET policy still performed better than the best uniform policy in terms of on-course consistency, and the average lap times were nearly identical, demonstrating that SSET improved policy stability without sacrificing performance. The invention’s ability to retain knowledge of rare but critical behaviors—such as returning to the track after a mistake—was further validated by a second set of runs using a shorter re-establish requirement (one second) and a shorter history length (five seconds), which produced nearly identical results, confirming the robustness of the method to variations in event specification. These results demonstrated that SSET is not only effective in discrete, grid-based environments but also scales to highly complex, continuous, and realistic simulation domains where the agent must learn to balance multiple competing objectives over long time horizons. The invention enabled the agent to acquire and retain complex driving skills that would otherwise be forgotten due to their low frequency of occurrence, making it uniquely suited for applications in autonomous driving, racing simulations, and other domains where rare events carry high consequences.

### Recommendations on How to Pick Helpful Events

The invention requires the specification of event conditions, which are domain-specific Boolean functions that identify states of significance. While the invention is robust to imperfect event conditions, the choice of events directly influences the magnitude of performance improvement. The following guidelines are provided to assist practitioners in selecting effective event conditions. First, in domains where the optimal policy is not well understood, a simple and effective strategy is to use a “goal” event that triggers when the agent reaches the terminal state or a high-reward region. Alternatively, in environments with dense reward signals, reward-threshold events can be used, where an event is triggered whenever the agent receives a reward above a certain percentile of the maximum possible reward. These methods require no domain expertise and still yield significant improvements over uniform sampling. Second, if the domain is partially understood, events should be defined at bottleneck states, critical decision points, or failure recovery points. For example, in a robotic manipulation task, events might be triggered when a gripper first contacts an object, when an object is lifted, or when it is placed in a target location. In a racing simulation, events might be triggered when a car enters a slipstream, when a tire loses traction, or when a vehicle recovers from a spin. These events serve as waypoints that connect the initial state to the goal, creating a chain of fast lanes for value propagation. Third, it is recommended to use multiple intermediate events with shorter history lengths rather than fewer events with longer histories, particularly in early training, because long histories often contain many suboptimal actions that dilute the signal from the critical event. Fourth, the history length should be chosen to be sufficiently long to capture the full trajectory from the previous event, the initial state, or the horizon, but not so long that it includes irrelevant or noisy transitions. A practical rule of thumb is to set the history length to be at least as long as the expected time to reach the next event under the current policy. Fifth, the sampling weights assigned to each Event Table should be initialized to equal values, and fine-tuned only if performance plateaus and there is evidence that certain skills are being learned more slowly than others. Sixth, the total proportion of the replay buffer allocated to Event Tables should be kept below 30% to ensure sufficient exploration from the default buffer, especially when event conditions are poorly specified. Seventh, the invention can be combined with other techniques such as TD-error prioritization within each Event Table, or potential-based reward shaping, to further enhance performance. Eighth, in environments where events are rare, it is beneficial to increase the history length to capture more non-event transitions within the Event Table, which helps mitigate bias and provides additional context for learning. Ninth, event conditions should be defined based on observable, measurable state features rather than latent or unobservable variables, to ensure they can be reliably detected during training. Tenth, while the invention does not require online learning of events, future extensions may explore the use of subgoal discovery algorithms to automatically identify promising event conditions from the data, particularly in domains where expert knowledge is unavailable. The invention’s flexibility and robustness make it suitable for a wide range of applications, from robotics and autonomous systems to video games and simulation-based training, and its implementation requires only minor modifications to existing experience replay systems.

### Summary

The invention introduces a novel framework for experience replay in off-policy reinforcement learning that significantly improves sample efficiency, learning stability, and long-term skill retention. By partitioning the replay buffer into Event Tables that store trajectories leading to predefined, domain-specific events, and by sampling from these tables according to fixed, stratified proportions, the invention creates “fast lanes” for temporal difference backups that propagate value estimates from critical states back to earlier states in the trajectory. This mechanism is fundamentally different from prior approaches such as Prioritized Experience Replay, reward shaping, or trajectory-based backups, as it preserves the independence of sampled transitions to ensure training stability, avoids the need for goal states or generative models, and operates without altering the underlying reward structure. The invention includes a bias correction mechanism that ensures the Bellman optimality condition is preserved, even in stochastic environments, and is compatible with any off-policy deep reinforcement learning algorithm. Empirical results across discrete and continuous control domains—including MiniGrid, MuJoCo, LunarLander, and Gran Turismo Sport—demonstrate that the invention consistently outperforms uniform sampling and Prioritized Experience Replay, reduces variance across training runs, and mitigates catastrophic forgetting by retaining knowledge of rare but critical behaviors. The invention requires no changes to the neural network architecture or learning algorithm, and can be implemented as a lightweight wrapper around existing replay buffer systems with minimal memory overhead. The guidelines for selecting event conditions provide practical, actionable advice for practitioners, ensuring that the invention can be effectively deployed in real-world applications. The invention represents a significant advancement in the field of reinforcement learning, offering a general-purpose, robust, and scalable solution to the long-standing problem of sample inefficiency and policy instability in off-policy learning.

### Definitions, Lemmas, Propositions Used Above

The invention is grounded in a formal theoretical framework that establishes sufficient conditions under which the proposed method improves the sample complexity of off-policy reinforcement learning. The analysis is conducted within the context of a finite, discrete, episodic Markov Decision Process with a bounded reward function, a fixed behavior policy, and a target policy that is optimal. The state space and action space are assumed to be finite and discrete, and the episode length is bounded. The behavior policy is assumed to be stochastic and capable of visiting every state and executing every action with non-zero probability within the episode horizon. The optimal policy is defined as the policy that maximizes the expected cumulative discounted reward. The invention defines an event condition as a Boolean function over the state space that is satisfied when the agent reaches a state that is significantly more likely under the optimal policy than under the behavior policy, or when the agent reaches a terminal state visited by the optimal policy. This definition ensures that events are aligned with the structure of optimal behavior. An event section is defined as the set of states from which the agent can reach an event state with high probability under the optimal policy, and an event table is defined as a multiset of transitions that lead to an event state, with a maximum history length. The invention proves that, under these definitions, the over-sampling of transitions in Event Tables leads to a reduction in the sample complexity of learning the optimal value function, provided that the history lengths are sufficiently long and the events are sufficiently correlated with the optimal policy. A key lemma quantifies the over-sampling factor, showing that the probability of sampling a transition from an Event Table is exponentially higher than its natural frequency under the behavior policy, and that this factor increases with the disparity between the optimal and behavior policies. Another lemma provides a bias correction term that adjusts the importance weights of sampled transitions to preserve the Bellman expectation, ensuring that the learning objective remains unbiased. A proposition establishes that the set of event conditions can be chosen such that all initial states, event states, and optimal terminal states are contained within at least one event section, ensuring that the entire trajectory from start to goal is covered. The main theorem demonstrates that the sample complexity of learning an ε-optimal policy using the proposed method is strictly lower than that of uniform sampling, under the stated conditions, and that the improvement compounds across multiple iterations of the learning algorithm. The proof relies on known finite-time convergence bounds for tabular Q-learning with target networks and extends them to the case of stratified sampling from Event Tables. The theoretical analysis confirms that the invention provides a provable improvement in learning efficiency, and that the benefits are not merely empirical but are rooted in the structure of the underlying Markov Decision Process and the alignment between event conditions and optimal behavior. The bias correction mechanism is shown to be necessary in stochastic environments and sufficient to preserve convergence to the optimal policy. The invention thus provides not only a practical method for improving reinforcement learning performance but also a rigorous theoretical foundation that justifies its design and guarantees its correctness under well-defined conditions.
