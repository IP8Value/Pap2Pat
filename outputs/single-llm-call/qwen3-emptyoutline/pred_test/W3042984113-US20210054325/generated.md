# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Traumatic injuries resulting from blunt mechanical impact represent a pervasive and complex challenge in both clinical and military medicine, with consequences ranging from immediate organ failure to delayed neurodegenerative pathologies that manifest years after the initial event. The human body, particularly soft tissues such as the brain, liver, and spleen, is highly susceptible to damage induced by rapid acceleration and deceleration forces encountered during vehicular collisions, sports-related impacts, falls, and explosive blast events. Despite decades of research into the biomechanics of trauma, the precise cellular and molecular mechanisms that translate macroscopic mechanical forces into biological dysfunction remain inadequately understood. Traditional approaches to studying these injuries have relied heavily on in vivo animal models, which, while valuable, introduce significant confounding variables including systemic inflammatory responses, compensatory physiological adaptations, and interspecies anatomical disparities that obscure direct causal relationships between mechanical input and cellular outcome. Furthermore, end-point assays such as histological staining or immunohistochemical analysis, commonly employed in these models, provide only snapshots of damage after the fact, offering no insight into the real-time dynamic response of living cells during and immediately following impact.

In parallel, in vitro cell culture systems have emerged as a promising alternative for isolating the direct effects of mechanical stress on individual cell populations. However, the technical limitations of existing platforms have severely restricted their utility in accurately replicating the biomechanical conditions of real-world blunt trauma. Most in vitro systems either apply static or quasi-static loads through stretchable substrates or fluid shear, which fail to reproduce the microsecond-scale, high-amplitude accelerations characteristic of blunt injury. Others utilize pneumatic or electromagnetic actuators to induce transient deformation, but these methods often lack the ability to precisely quantify the resulting pressure fields within the cell culture medium or to maintain the stringent environmental controls required for long-term viability of live cells. The inability to simultaneously apply biologically relevant mechanical stimuli, monitor cellular responses in real time under controlled physiological conditions, and correlate these responses with quantified physical parameters has created a critical gap in mechanistic understanding. As a result, the field has been forced to rely on simplified injury criteria—most notably, acceleration thresholds derived from anthropomorphic crash test dummies—that ignore the fundamental role of tissue geometry, fluid dynamics, and pressure gradients in determining biological vulnerability.

Recent advances in the study of soft biomaterials have begun to reveal the importance of dynamic cavitation as a potential damage mechanism in traumatic brain injury. Studies utilizing gelatin, agarose, and collagen as tissue simulants have demonstrated that under impulsive loading, tensile pressure waves can induce the nucleation and violent collapse of vapor-filled cavities within the material, generating localized shock fronts and microjets capable of disrupting molecular structures. These findings suggest that the injury threshold may not be defined by acceleration alone, but rather by the onset of cavitation—a phenomenon whose occurrence is intrinsically dependent on the spatial dimensions of the affected tissue. For instance, the critical acceleration required to initiate cavitation in a 40 mm column of water is approximately 300 g, whereas in a 10 mm column, the same cavitation event requires over 1,300 g of acceleration, as predicted by the linear relationship between pressure and column height. This size-dependent sensitivity implies that injury criteria derived from large-scale models may be inapplicable to smaller biological systems, such as individual cell monolayers or pediatric brains, and that current clinical and engineering standards may significantly underestimate risk in certain populations. Yet, despite this growing theoretical and experimental evidence, no prior in vitro platform has been capable of directly observing live cells under precisely controlled cavitation-inducing conditions, nor has any system enabled the multiplexed, longitudinal tracking of cellular responses across multiple identical populations subjected to varying mechanical stimuli.

The absence of such a platform has led to persistent ambiguity in the literature regarding the primary injury mechanism: whether acceleration, pressure, or cavitation is the dominant driver of cellular damage. Many studies have assumed that acceleration is a sufficient proxy for internal pressure, yet this assumption neglects the fact that pressure is not a direct function of acceleration but rather a product of acceleration multiplied by the characteristic length of the medium through which it propagates. Moreover, the temporal profile of pressure—its rate of rise, duration, and oscillatory components—has been largely uncharacterized in biological contexts, despite overwhelming evidence from materials science that the kinetics of loading profoundly influence viscoelastic responses in soft tissues. The failure to distinguish between slow, uniform acceleration-induced pressure and the abrupt, localized pressure spikes generated by collapsing cavitation bubbles has resulted in a fundamental misattribution of damage mechanisms. Consequently, protective strategies, diagnostic tools, and therapeutic interventions have been developed based on incomplete or misleading mechanistic models. There is therefore an urgent and unmet need for a novel experimental system that can apply well-characterized, reproducible blunt impact forces to live cells while simultaneously quantifying the associated pressure dynamics and monitoring cellular responses in real time under fully controlled physiological conditions. Such a system would not only resolve longstanding questions about the nature of blunt injury but would also establish a new paradigm for evaluating cellular vulnerability, enabling the development of more accurate, physiologically relevant injury criteria grounded in direct observation rather than extrapolation.

## BRIEF DESCRIPTION

The present invention discloses a novel and integrated in vitro experimental platform designed to investigate the cellular mechanisms of blunt mechanical injury through the precise application of acceleration-induced pressure gradients and the real-time observation of live cell responses under environmentally controlled conditions. The system combines a drop-tower impact apparatus with a custom-designed, multiplexed cell culture assembly that enables the simultaneous application of high-amplitude, microsecond-duration mechanical impulses to multiple cell populations while maintaining strict control over temperature, humidity, and CO₂ levels throughout the entire experimental protocol. The cell culture assembly comprises a standard 35-mm petri dish sealed at its top with a transparent silicone film and sandwiched between rigid aluminum plates, all secured by a modular holder that interfaces directly with the drop tower. Soft foam layers of calibrated stiffness and thickness are incorporated into the holder to ensure that the resulting acceleration profile—ranging from 80 g to over 1,500 g—closely mimics the temporal and amplitude characteristics of clinically relevant blunt trauma events, such as those occurring in automotive collisions or contact sports. The entire assembly is compatible with long-term live-cell imaging systems, allowing continuous, high-resolution optical monitoring of cell confluency, morphology, and membrane integrity before, during, and after impact without removing the cultures from their incubated environment.

A key innovation of the invention lies in its ability to correlate mechanical input with physical output through concurrent, high-fidelity measurements of acceleration and pressure. Acceleration is quantified using triaxial ICP accelerometers mounted directly onto the holder, while pressure dynamics within the cell culture medium are inferred through theoretical modeling and experimentally validated using miniature piezoelectric sensors inserted into scaled-down transparent tubes filled with aqueous media. These measurements reveal that the pressure experienced by cells at the base of the culture dish is linearly proportional to the height of the medium and the magnitude of the applied acceleration, as predicted by the Navier-Stokes equations for incompressible fluids under axial symmetry. Crucially, the system enables the identification of a distinct threshold beyond which the mechanical stimulus induces the formation and collapse of cavitation bubbles within the cell culture medium. This threshold, which varies with the height of the medium, is not defined by acceleration alone but by the onset of cavitation, which generates localized pressure spikes exceeding 250 kPa with rise times on the order of microseconds—orders of magnitude faster and more intense than the background acceleration-induced pressure. The invention further incorporates a multiplexed imaging protocol that allows for the parallel tracking of up to twelve independent cell cultures, each subjected to identical or varying impact conditions, thereby enabling direct comparison of cellular responses across biological replicates and minimizing the confounding effects of population heterogeneity. Propidium iodide staining and time-lapse fluorescence microscopy confirm that cell membrane damage occurs exclusively in regions where cavitation is observed, and that surviving cells exhibit remarkable capacity for membrane repair within 48 hours, suggesting a dynamic, non-necrotic response mechanism. The platform is fully scalable and adaptable, permitting the use of diverse cell types, culture media compositions, substrate stiffnesses, and geometric configurations, thereby establishing a versatile and robust foundation for future investigations into the biomechanical basis of trauma-related pathologies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### In Vitro Setup for Blunt Injury Mechanisms

The in vitro setup for blunt injury mechanisms is a highly integrated and meticulously engineered system designed to replicate the biomechanical conditions of real-world traumatic impact on live cell populations while preserving the physiological integrity of the cellular microenvironment. The core of the system is a drop-tower apparatus, which consists of a vertically oriented guide rail supporting a weighted impact mass that is lifted to a predetermined height and then released to strike a rigid holder assembly containing the cell culture substrate. The drop tower is constructed to ensure repeatability and precision in the application of impact forces, with the mass and drop height calibrated to generate accelerations ranging from 80 g to over 1,500 g, corresponding to the dynamic range of blunt trauma experienced in human scenarios such as vehicular collisions, sports impacts, and blast events. The impact mass is engineered to deliver a single, well-defined impulse with a duration of less than one millisecond, ensuring that the mechanical stimulus approximates the transient nature of real-world trauma rather than prolonged or oscillatory loading.

The cell culture assembly is a modular, disposable unit that interfaces directly with the impact holder and is designed to maintain sterility, environmental control, and optical transparency throughout the entire experimental process. It consists of a commercially available 35-mm polystyrene petri dish, which serves as the container for the cell culture medium and adherent cell monolayer. The dish is sealed at its upper surface with a thin, optically clear silicone film that is impermeable to contaminants yet transparent to visible and fluorescent light, enabling high-resolution microscopy without interference. This film is held in place by two rigid aluminum plates—one positioned above the film and one below the petri dish—which are bolted together using stainless steel fasteners to form a sealed, pressure-resistant enclosure. The aluminum plates are engineered with precision-machined grooves and mounting features that allow for secure attachment to the impact holder without transmitting direct solid-to-solid contact to the culture dish, thereby preventing the generation of shock waves that could confound the mechanical stimulus.

The impact holder itself is a custom-fabricated structure composed of a rigid polymer matrix, manufactured via high-precision 3D printing using polylactic acid filament with 100% infill to ensure structural integrity under high-impact loads. Embedded within the holder are two layers of soft, closed-cell foam materials strategically positioned to modulate the temporal and amplitude characteristics of the impact. A thin, stiff foam layer—1 mm in thickness and with a Shore A hardness of 50—is placed at the top of the holder, directly beneath the impact mass. This layer serves to prevent direct metal-to-metal contact between the mass and the holder, which would otherwise generate uncontrolled shock waves and high-frequency oscillations that do not reflect biological conditions. Beneath this, a thicker, softer foam layer—12 mm in thickness and with a Shore A hardness of 4—is positioned between the holder and the base of the drop tower. This layer allows for controlled deceleration of the holder assembly, ensuring that the resulting acceleration profile is smooth, reproducible, and within the physiologically relevant range of 80 to 1,500 g with a duration of 0.1 to 1.0 milliseconds. The specific mechanical properties of these foam layers were selected through iterative testing and finite element modeling to replicate the impedance-matching characteristics of soft tissue interfaces in the human body, such as the skull-brain interface or the abdominal wall-liver interface.

To ensure continuous environmental control, the entire cell culture assembly is designed to remain within a standard cell culture incubator prior to and after impact. Prior to the impact event, multiple petri dishes are seeded with cells and monitored in real time using an automated live-cell imaging system equipped with a motorized phase-contrast objective. The imaging system captures images of each culture at regular intervals—typically every 30 minutes—across multiple regions of interest, allowing for the construction of detailed growth curves and the identification of cells at the desired confluency stage, typically between 35% and 40%, which corresponds to the mid-logarithmic phase of proliferation. Once the target confluency is achieved, the petri dishes are carefully removed from the incubator, assembled with the holder and silicone film under sterile conditions, and placed into a temperature-controlled transfer box for approximately ten minutes to simulate the brief handling time associated with clinical or experimental procedures. During this interval, the dishes are maintained at 37°C and 5% CO₂ to ensure that any perturbations introduced by assembly are isolated and quantifiable. After assembly, the holder with the attached cell culture is mounted onto the drop tower, and the impact is executed. Immediately following impact, the holder is disassembled, the petri dish is returned to the incubator, and live-cell imaging resumes, permitting continuous observation of cellular recovery, death, or proliferation over the subsequent 24 to 96 hours.

The system is further enhanced by the integration of high-speed imaging capabilities. Two high-speed cameras, operating at 50,000 frames per second, are mounted on independent, adjustable stands to capture orthogonal views of the cell culture assembly during impact. These cameras are synchronized with the data acquisition system via a TTL pulse triggered by the accelerometer, ensuring that visual records of the impact event are perfectly aligned with the quantitative measurements of acceleration and pressure. The cameras are focused on the bottom of the petri dish, where the cells reside, to detect the formation and collapse of cavitation bubbles, which appear as transient, luminous structures in the medium. The optical clarity of the silicone film and the absence of air bubbles—ensured by filling the dish to maximum capacity and carefully removing entrapped air during assembly—allow for unobstructed visualization of these events. The entire system is designed to be modular and scalable, permitting the simultaneous testing of up to twelve independent cell cultures in a single experimental run, thereby enabling high-throughput, statistically robust analysis of cellular responses across multiple biological replicates. This multiplexed capability is critical for addressing the inherent heterogeneity of cell populations, as individual regions within a single dish may exhibit markedly different growth rates, morphologies, or sensitivities to mechanical stress. By comparing responses across multiple dishes subjected to identical conditions, the system eliminates batch-to-batch variability and provides a reliable foundation for identifying true mechanistic effects rather than stochastic anomalies.

### Multiplexed In Vitro Cell Cultures

The multiplexed in vitro cell culture component of the invention is a foundational innovation that enables the simultaneous, longitudinal, and statistically rigorous analysis of cellular responses to blunt mechanical impact across multiple independent populations under identical environmental conditions. Traditional in vitro studies of cellular trauma often rely on single-dish experiments or small numbers of replicates, which are inherently vulnerable to confounding variables such as passage number, seeding density, media batch variability, and spatial heterogeneity within a single culture. These limitations introduce significant noise into the data, obscure subtle biological effects, and render conclusions difficult to generalize. The present system overcomes these challenges by integrating a high-throughput, multiplexed imaging protocol that allows for the parallel cultivation, mechanical stimulation, and real-time monitoring of up to twelve distinct cell cultures, each maintained in its own petri dish and subjected to the same impact conditions within a single experimental run.

Each cell culture is initiated by plating a standardized number of fibroblasts—typically 50,000 to 100,000 cells—into a 35-mm petri dish containing 1 mL of complete growth medium composed of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. The dishes are then placed into a cell culture incubator and monitored continuously using an automated live-cell imaging system equipped with a 10× phase-contrast objective and a motorized stage capable of sequentially imaging 25 distinct regions of interest within each dish. The imaging frequency is set to one capture every 30 minutes, ensuring sufficient temporal resolution to track cell confluency, morphology, and division events without inducing phototoxicity or thermal stress. The confluency of each region is calculated automatically using a proprietary image recognition algorithm trained on Hs27 fibroblast morphology, which segments individual cells based on their phase-contrast boundaries and computes the ratio of cell-covered area to total area. The average confluency of each dish is derived from the mean of these 25 regional measurements, providing a robust and representative metric of overall cell population growth.

Critical to the experimental design is the inclusion of control conditions that isolate the effects of mechanical impact from those of handling, assembly, and environmental perturbation. Two control dishes are prepared for each experimental batch: Control 1 remains in the incubator throughout the entire procedure, receiving no handling or assembly, while Control 2 undergoes the exact same assembly protocol as the experimental dishes—including transfer to the temperature-controlled box, attachment to the holder, and 10-minute incubation outside the incubator—but is not subjected to any mechanical impact. This dual-control design enables the precise quantification of any cellular response attributable to the physical manipulation of the culture rather than the impact itself. Experimental results demonstrate that the confluency trajectories of Control 1 and Control 2 are virtually indistinguishable across multiple cell passages, indicating that the assembly process introduces no significant stress or perturbation to the cells. This finding validates the integrity of the system and confirms that any observed damage following impact is indeed attributable to the mechanical stimulus and not to procedural artifacts.

The multiplexed nature of the system also reveals critical insights into the intrinsic spatial and temporal heterogeneity of cell populations. While the average confluency of a dish may appear stable and uniform, individual regions of interest within the same dish frequently exhibit divergent growth rates, morphological changes, and susceptibility to damage. For instance, in one experiment, the average confluency of a control dish remained steady at approximately 40% over 160 hours, yet localized regions such as B2 and D3 showed a 15% reduction in confluency over the same period, while other regions such as A1 and E1 exhibited a 10% increase. These variations are not attributable to experimental error but reflect the natural biological variability inherent in cell populations, including differences in local nutrient availability, cell-cell signaling gradients, and epigenetic states. By analyzing these regional differences across multiple dishes, the system identifies which responses are consistent and reproducible and which are stochastic or localized. This level of granularity is impossible to achieve with traditional end-point assays or single-dish experiments, which average out these variations and risk misattributing localized damage to global cellular responses.

Furthermore, the system enables the identification of passage-dependent phenotypes that are often overlooked in conventional studies. For example, fibroblasts from passage 10 exhibited a significantly slower growth rate compared to those from passage 6 or 8, with confluency curves plateauing earlier and showing reduced mitotic activity. This finding underscores the importance of controlling for passage number in trauma studies, as older cell populations may exhibit altered mechanosensitivity, reduced repair capacity, or increased susceptibility to stress-induced senescence. By maintaining multiple passages in parallel and subjecting them to identical impact conditions, the system establishes a direct correlation between cellular aging and mechanical vulnerability, a relationship that has profound implications for understanding trauma outcomes in elderly populations or in tissues with limited regenerative capacity.

The multiplexed design also facilitates the implementation of complex experimental paradigms, such as repeated impact protocols, dose-response curves, and comparative analyses across cell types. For instance, in one series of experiments, five sequential impacts were delivered to a single dish at 10-minute intervals, each at 15 g of acceleration, to simulate the cumulative effects of repetitive subthreshold trauma. The results showed no significant change in confluency compared to single-impact controls, suggesting that under these conditions, the cells are capable of recovering between events. In contrast, a single impact at 40 cm drop height (1,300 g) resulted in rapid, localized cell death, demonstrating that the magnitude of the stimulus, rather than its frequency, is the determining factor in injury. This level of experimental flexibility, combined with the ability to track each culture longitudinally, transforms the system from a simple impact device into a powerful platform for mechanistic discovery. The ability to compare the responses of twelve independent cultures under identical conditions not only enhances statistical power but also allows for the detection of subtle, previously invisible patterns in cellular behavior, such as delayed apoptosis, transient membrane permeabilization, or altered migration dynamics following impact. These capabilities establish the system as a transformative tool for trauma research, enabling a level of precision, reproducibility, and biological insight previously unattainable in in vitro models.

### Quantifying the Blunt Injury

The quantification of blunt injury in the context of this invention is achieved through a rigorous, multi-modal approach that integrates precise mechanical input measurements with high-resolution biological output analysis, thereby establishing a direct, causal link between physical parameters and cellular response. The foundation of this quantification lies in the accurate and reproducible generation of acceleration profiles during impact, which are measured in real time using a triaxial ICP accelerometer mounted directly onto the impact holder. This accelerometer, calibrated to a sensitivity of 10 mV/g and capable of sampling at 1 MHz, captures the full temporal waveform of the impact event, including peak acceleration, rise time, duration, and oscillatory components. The data are acquired using a National Instruments PXIe-8135 embedded controller and an NI PXI-6115 multifunction I/O module, with a pre-trigger buffer capturing the entire event, including the milliseconds preceding impact. This ensures that the full mechanical history of the stimulus is recorded, enabling detailed analysis of the loading profile and its correlation with biological outcomes.

The acceleration data are then used to calculate the corresponding acceleration-induced pressure within the cell culture medium using a theoretical model derived from the Navier-Stokes equations for incompressible Newtonian fluids under axial symmetry. The model assumes that the cell culture medium behaves as a homogeneous, non-viscous fluid with constant density, and that the pressure gradient is driven solely by the vertical acceleration of the holder. Under these assumptions, the pressure at any point within the medium is given by the equation \( p_a(z,t) = -\rho(H - z)(a_{in}(t) - g) \), where \( \rho \) is the density of the medium, \( H \) is the height of the medium column, \( z \) is the vertical position within the dish (with \( z = 0 \) at the bottom), \( a_{in}(t) \) is the time-dependent acceleration of the holder, and \( g \) is the gravitational constant. For accelerations significantly greater than gravity, as is the case in all experiments described herein, the term \( g \) becomes negligible, and the pressure at the bottom of the dish—where the cells reside—is approximated as \( p_a(0,t) \approx -\rho H a_{in}(t) \). This equation demonstrates that the pressure exerted on the cells is not a function of acceleration alone but is directly proportional to the height of the medium. For a 10 mm column of medium, a peak acceleration of 1,300 g corresponds to a peak pressure of approximately 128 kPa, whereas the same acceleration in a 40 mm column would generate a pressure of 512 kPa. This size-dependent relationship is critical to understanding why the same acceleration can produce vastly different biological outcomes depending on the geometry of the system.

To validate this theoretical model and to probe the transition from acceleration-induced pressure to cavitation-induced damage, a series of scaled-down experiments were conducted using transparent plastic tubes with an internal diameter of 12 mm and a height of 40 mm, filled with 6 mL of pure deionized water. A miniature piezoelectric pressure sensor (PCB 113B21) with a rise time of less than 1 µs and a maximum range of 2,758 kPa was inserted into the bottom of each tube, allowing for direct, concurrent measurement of pressure and acceleration during impact. These experiments revealed a clear distinction between two regimes of mechanical response. At lower drop heights (e.g., 30 mm), the pressure waveform closely mirrored the acceleration waveform, with a smooth, monotonically increasing tensile pressure profile that reached a maximum of 96.9 kPa without evidence of cavitation. However, at a critical drop height of 40 mm, the pressure waveform exhibited a dramatic deviation: following an initial rise to 118.6 kPa, the pressure rapidly oscillated between tensile and compressive states, with peaks exceeding 259 kPa and a rise time of less than 0.05 ms. High-speed imaging synchronized with these measurements confirmed that these pressure oscillations corresponded to the nucleation, growth, and violent collapse of cavitation bubbles at the bottom of the tube. The collapse of these bubbles generated localized pressure spikes that were orders of magnitude higher and faster than the background acceleration-induced pressure, with a rate of pressure change reaching 6,642 kPa/ms compared to 169 kPa/ms for the non-cavitating condition.

This distinction is central to the invention’s core discovery: that the critical injury mechanism is not acceleration or even acceleration-induced pressure, but rather the onset of cavitation. In all experiments where acceleration exceeded 1,000 g but cavitation did not occur, no significant cell death was observed, even after prolonged monitoring. In contrast, every instance in which cavitation was visually confirmed—regardless of the absolute acceleration value—was accompanied by localized, non-uniform cell death, as quantified by confluency loss and propidium iodide staining. The spatial distribution of this damage was highly heterogeneous, with cell death occurring in isolated clusters corresponding to the locations of cavitation bubble collapse, as confirmed by overlaying high-speed camera images with fluorescence microscopy data. This finding contradicts the prevailing assumption that injury is a global, dose-dependent phenomenon and instead demonstrates that blunt trauma can induce highly localized, stochastic damage driven by the dynamics of cavitation.

The quantification of injury is further refined through the use of propidium iodide (PI) as a marker of plasma membrane integrity. PI is a fluorescent dye that is excluded by intact cell membranes but enters cells with compromised membranes, binding to nucleic acids and emitting a strong red fluorescence. In experiments where cavitation occurred, PI staining was observed exclusively in regions where cavitation bubbles were detected, with approximately 10% of surviving cells in these regions exhibiting positive staining 3 hours post-impact. However, by 48 hours, all PI-positive cells had either repaired their membranes or been replaced by newly proliferating cells, and no residual staining remained. This indicates that the membrane damage induced by cavitation is transient and repairable, suggesting a non-necrotic, mechanosensitive response rather than irreversible cell death. The temporal dynamics of this repair process were captured in time-lapse imaging, which showed individual cells undergoing morphological changes, motility, and resealing of the membrane over a 24-hour period, further supporting the conclusion that cavitation induces a reversible mechanical perturbation rather than a catastrophic structural failure.

The combination of these quantitative measures—acceleration, pressure, cavitation onset, confluency loss, and membrane integrity—enables the establishment of a definitive injury criterion: the critical threshold for cellular damage is not defined by a specific acceleration or pressure value, but by the occurrence of cavitation. This criterion is size-dependent, as the critical acceleration for cavitation scales inversely with the height of the medium, and is reproducible across multiple cell lines and experimental conditions. The system’s ability to quantify this criterion with high fidelity, while simultaneously monitoring the biological response in real time, establishes a new gold standard for the study of blunt trauma. It moves the field beyond simplistic, acceleration-based thresholds and toward a mechanistically grounded, physiologically relevant model of injury that accounts for the dynamic, nonlinear, and spatially heterogeneous nature of biological response to mechanical impact. This paradigm shift has profound implications for the development of protective equipment, diagnostic algorithms, and therapeutic interventions, as it provides a measurable, objective, and biologically validated criterion for assessing trauma risk and severity.
