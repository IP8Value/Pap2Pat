# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNOLOGICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of ophthalmology and, more specifically, to noninvasive methods and devices for the controlled manipulation of particulate matter within the aqueous humor of the anterior chamber of the eye. The invention employs acoustic radiation forces generated by precisely tuned ultrasonic standing waves to direct, concentrate, and stabilize pathological or therapeutic particles without inducing thermal or mechanical damage to ocular tissues. This technology is particularly applicable to conditions characterized by the presence of suspended particulate matter—including pigment dispersion syndrome, pseudoexfoliation material, hyphema, asteroid hyalosis, and intracameral medication aggregates—that may obstruct the visual axis, impair aqueous humor outflow, or contribute to secondary glaucoma. The system operates at frequencies and intensities well within the safety thresholds established for diagnostic ultrasound in ophthalmic applications, enabling clinical deployment without surgical intervention. The device integrates a biocompatible acoustic transducer array with a fluid-coupled interface designed for external placement on the ocular surface, allowing for real-time, noncontact manipulation of intraocular particles through the intact cornea and sclera. This innovation represents a paradigm shift from reactive management of ocular complications to proactive, physics-based control of particle dynamics within the anterior chamber, thereby offering a novel therapeutic modality for conditions previously devoid of preventive or interventional strategies.

## BACKGROUND

The anterior chamber of the human eye contains aqueous humor, a transparent, low-viscosity fluid that is continuously produced by the ciliary body and drained through the trabecular meshwork into Schlemm’s canal, maintaining intraocular pressure and nourishing avascular structures such as the cornea and lens. The integrity of this fluid circulation is essential for optical clarity and ocular homeostasis. Any disruption in the composition or distribution of substances within the aqueous humor can lead to significant visual impairment and structural damage. In particular, the presence of particulate matter—whether derived from iris pigment, exfoliated lens material, erythrocytes from hemorrhage, or crystalline aggregates of intracameral medications—poses a substantial clinical challenge. These particles, often ranging from submicron to tens of micrometers in size, do not dissolve or clear efficiently through natural mechanisms and may accumulate along the corneal endothelium, obstruct the trabecular meshwork, or float within the visual axis, resulting in symptoms such as blurred vision, photophobia, and elevated intraocular pressure. 

Current clinical approaches to managing such particulate matter are largely passive and reactive. In cases of hyphema, for instance, patients are typically monitored for spontaneous resolution, with interventions such as anterior chamber washout or iridectomy reserved for severe or persistent cases. Similarly, in pseudoexfoliation syndrome, where fibrillar material accumulates on the lens capsule and trabecular meshwork, no method exists to prevent its progressive deposition or to displace it from critical outflow pathways. Medications such as triamcinolone acetonide, often injected intracamerally for inflammatory conditions, form visible particulate suspensions that may settle unpredictably, impairing visualization during follow-up examinations and potentially contributing to endothelial toxicity. The absence of active, noninvasive tools to manipulate or reposition these particles has resulted in a therapeutic vacuum: clinicians are forced to wait for complications to manifest before initiating invasive procedures, which carry inherent risks of infection, hemorrhage, and iatrogenic damage to delicate ocular structures.

Ultrasound has long been employed in ophthalmology for diagnostic imaging, utilizing low-intensity acoustic waves to generate reflections from ocular interfaces. High-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) has also been explored for therapeutic purposes, such as ciliary body ablation in refractory glaucoma, where the goal is tissue destruction through localized heating. However, these applications rely on energy levels sufficient to induce thermal coagulation or mechanical disruption of biological tissues, rendering them unsuitable for the delicate task of manipulating subcellular particles without collateral damage. Moreover, existing ultrasound-based techniques do not exploit the physical principle of acoustic radiation force—the net force exerted on a particle due to its interaction with an acoustic field—which can selectively move particles based on their density and compressibility relative to the surrounding medium. While acoustic manipulation has been successfully demonstrated in vitro and in extracorporeal systems for sorting cells, assembling microstructures, and moving particles in microfluidic channels, its application within the confined, dynamic, and highly sensitive environment of the human anterior chamber has remained unexplored. The unique biomechanical constraints of the eye—including its small volume, the presence of refractive interfaces, the need for continuous aqueous humor flow, and the vulnerability of the corneal endothelium—have historically discouraged the translation of acoustic manipulation techniques from laboratory settings to clinical ophthalmology. Consequently, no device or method currently exists that can noninvasively, safely, and controllably reposition intraocular particles to mitigate their pathological consequences. The present invention fills this critical gap by introducing a system and method that harnesses precisely calibrated standing acoustic waves to guide particulate matter to predetermined locations within the anterior chamber, thereby preventing obstruction of the visual axis and preserving the functionality of the aqueous outflow system.

## GENERAL DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a novel method and apparatus for the noninvasive, targeted manipulation of particulate matter within the anterior chamber of the eye using acoustic radiation forces. The method involves the application of a controlled ultrasonic standing wave field to the ocular surface, wherein the acoustic energy is transmitted through the cornea and aqueous humor to induce spatial redistribution of suspended particles without causing thermal injury, mechanical trauma, or disruption of ocular physiology. The particles, which may be pathological in origin—such as pigment granules, pseudoexfoliation flakes, or erythrocyte aggregates—or therapeutic in nature—such as crystalline corticosteroids or cellular grafts—are selectively driven toward nodal regions of the standing wave due to their positive acoustic contrast relative to the aqueous humor. These nodal regions correspond to areas of minimal acoustic pressure amplitude, where particles with higher density and lower compressibility than the surrounding fluid accumulate as a result of scattering forces. The spatial configuration of these nodal regions is determined by the geometry and frequency of the applied acoustic field, allowing for precise control over the location and density of particle concentration. 

The apparatus comprises a biocompatible acoustic transducer array designed for external placement on the ocular surface, coupled to a signal generator and power amplifier capable of producing continuous sine wave signals within the frequency range of 0.8 MHz to 1.5 MHz, with peak-to-peak voltages between 2 Vpp and 20 Vpp, corresponding to acoustic intensities of 10 to 80 mW/cm². The transducer is encased in a soft, elastic, ultrasound-transparent coating composed of polydimethylsiloxane to ensure uniform acoustic coupling, prevent mechanical abrasion, and maintain sterility during clinical use. The system further includes a feedback mechanism that monitors the temperature of the ocular surface and the fluid reservoir in real time, enabling automatic power modulation to prevent thermal accumulation beyond a threshold of 2.5 °C above baseline. The method may be implemented in a clinical setting by positioning the transducer over the cornea, activating the acoustic field for a duration sufficient to achieve particle redistribution—typically between 30 seconds and five minutes—and then deactivating the field to allow the particles to remain stably localized in their new configuration. The resulting particle aggregates remain fixed in position for extended periods, even after cessation of the acoustic field, due to hydrodynamic forces and the natural flow dynamics of aqueous humor, which tend to anchor the accumulated material at the periphery of the anterior chamber, away from the visual axis and the trabecular meshwork.

This invention enables a range of clinical applications previously unattainable through conventional means. For instance, in patients with pigment dispersion syndrome, the method can be used to concentrate dispersed iris pigment at the inferior angle, thereby preventing its migration into the trabecular meshwork and reducing the risk of secondary open-angle glaucoma. In cases of hyphema, particulate erythrocyte aggregates can be repositioned to the inferior chamber, minimizing their interference with vision and facilitating natural clearance through the aqueous outflow system. Similarly, intracameral medications such as triamcinolone acetonide can be guided to specific regions of the anterior chamber to enhance local therapeutic effect while reducing endothelial exposure. The method also permits the creation of spatially organized patterns of particles, which may serve diagnostic purposes by enabling visualization of particle distribution under slit-lamp examination or optical coherence tomography. Importantly, the technique requires no incision, no intraocular instrumentation, and no pharmacological agents beyond those already present, making it inherently low-risk and suitable for repeated or prophylactic use. The system operates entirely externally, preserving the integrity of the ocular surface and eliminating the potential for infection, hemorrhage, or retinal detachment associated with invasive procedures. The invention thus represents a transformative advance in the management of particulate ocular pathologies, offering a safe, repeatable, and physics-based alternative to current observational or surgical approaches.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

The invention is implemented through a system comprising an acoustic transducer, a signal generation and control unit, a coupling interface, and a feedback monitoring subsystem, all integrated into a clinical device designed for external application to the ocular surface. The transducer is constructed from a piezoelectric material, preferably lead zirconate titanate (PZT), configured in a radial cylindrical geometry with an inner diameter of approximately 22 millimeters and an outer diameter of 26 millimeters, and a thickness of 20 millimeters. This geometry is selected to generate a radially symmetric standing wave field within the anterior chamber, ensuring uniform particle manipulation across the horizontal plane of the aqueous humor. The transducer is tuned to a center resonance frequency of 1.1 megahertz, with a tolerance of ±0.1 MHz, to ensure optimal coupling with the acoustic properties of the aqueous humor and to minimize energy dissipation through absorption or reflection at ocular interfaces. The transducer is driven by a programmable signal generator capable of producing continuous sine wave outputs with peak-to-peak voltage amplitudes ranging from 2 volts to 20 volts, corresponding to acoustic intensities of 10 to 80 milliwatts per square centimeter. A power amplifier with a bandwidth of at least 3 megahertz and low harmonic distortion is employed to maintain signal fidelity and prevent nonlinear effects that could introduce cavitation or tissue heating. The output power is calibrated such that the maximum intensity remains below the safety threshold established by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for diagnostic ophthalmic ultrasound, which is 50 milliwatts per square centimeter, thereby ensuring that thermal and mechanical bioeffects are negligible under all operational conditions.

To facilitate safe and reproducible coupling with the ocular surface, the transducer is coated with a biocompatible, acoustically transparent layer of polydimethylsiloxane, formed by applying a 10:1 weight ratio mixture of Sylgard 184 silicone elastomer base and curing agent, followed by curing at 80 degrees Celsius for two hours. This coating has a thickness of approximately 70 micrometers, which is sufficient to prevent direct mechanical contact between the rigid transducer and the corneal epithelium while maintaining high acoustic transmission efficiency. The coating is hydrophobic and non-adherent, minimizing the risk of bacterial colonization and facilitating easy sterilization between uses. The transducer is mounted on a rigid, adjustable support frame that allows for precise alignment over the cornea, with angular and translational control in three dimensions to accommodate variations in eye anatomy and patient positioning. The support frame is integrated with a fluid reservoir that holds a thin layer of sterile saline or ultrasound coupling gel, ensuring continuous acoustic coupling between the transducer and the cornea. The fluid layer is maintained at physiological temperature through a passive thermal regulator, and its volume is monitored to ensure consistent wave propagation conditions.

The signal generation and control unit is equipped with a user interface that permits the selection of predefined treatment protocols based on the type of particulate matter being manipulated. For example, a protocol for pigment dispersion syndrome may employ a 10-volt peak-to-peak amplitude for a duration of two minutes, while a protocol for intracameral triamcinolone redistribution may require a higher amplitude of 15 volts for one minute to overcome the increased density and viscosity of the medication suspension. The system includes a real-time monitoring module that continuously measures the temperature of the fluid reservoir adjacent to the cornea using a high-resolution infrared thermal camera with a spatial resolution of less than 0.1 millimeter and a temperature sensitivity of ±0.05 degrees Celsius. If the temperature increase exceeds 1.5 degrees Celsius above baseline, the system automatically reduces the applied voltage by 10% and initiates a cooling cycle by circulating chilled saline through a secondary reservoir. This closed-loop feedback mechanism ensures that the thermal load on the ocular tissues remains well below the threshold for thermal denaturation, which is approximately 42 degrees Celsius for corneal collagen. Additionally, the system incorporates an acoustic impedance sensor that monitors the reflection coefficient of the transmitted wave at the corneal interface to detect any air gaps or irregularities in coupling, and alerts the operator if the transmission efficiency falls below 90%.

The method of operation begins with the patient in a supine or seated position, with the head stabilized and the eye positioned for optimal transducer alignment. The ocular surface is gently cleansed with a sterile saline solution, and a thin layer of coupling fluid is applied to the cornea. The transducer, pre-coated with the polydimethylsiloxane layer and filled with coupling fluid, is then placed in direct contact with the cornea, ensuring uniform pressure and complete acoustic coupling. The operator selects the appropriate treatment protocol based on the clinical indication, and the system initiates the acoustic field. Within seconds, the standing wave pattern is established within the anterior chamber, and particles begin to migrate toward the nodal regions of the wave. These nodal regions, which correspond to areas of minimal acoustic pressure, are arranged in concentric rings spaced approximately 0.75 millimeters apart, as determined by the radial propagation of the wave within the cylindrical geometry of the anterior chamber. The particles, which possess a positive acoustic contrast factor due to their higher density and lower compressibility compared to aqueous humor, are driven toward these nodal zones by the acoustic radiation force, which has been experimentally measured to be approximately 71 piconewtons for 10-micrometer polystyrene beads under a 10-volt excitation. The rate of particle migration is dependent on the amplitude of the acoustic field, the size and density of the particles, and the viscosity of the aqueous humor. Larger particles, such as those derived from iris pigment, migrate more slowly than smaller, denser particles such as triamcinolone acetonide crystals, requiring longer exposure times to achieve full redistribution. The system allows for real-time visualization of particle movement through an integrated optical imaging module, which may include a slit-lamp camera or an optical coherence tomography scanner positioned coaxially with the transducer, enabling the operator to confirm the desired spatial configuration of the particles before deactivation.

Upon achieving the desired particle distribution, the acoustic field is deactivated, and the particles remain stably localized in their new configuration for a period exceeding one hour, as demonstrated in ex vivo models. This stability is attributed to the natural hydrodynamic forces of aqueous humor circulation, which tend to anchor the accumulated material at the periphery of the anterior chamber, particularly in the inferior quadrant, where the flow velocity is lowest. The particles do not re-disperse significantly during this time, even in the presence of minor eye movements, suggesting that the aggregates are sufficiently cohesive and resistant to shear forces to remain clinically relevant. The transducer is then removed, and the eye is rinsed with saline to remove any residual coupling fluid. No postoperative care is required, and the patient may resume normal activities immediately. The procedure may be repeated as necessary, with no evidence of cumulative tissue damage or reduced efficacy over multiple sessions.

In an alternative embodiment, the transducer array is configured as a multi-element phased array, allowing for dynamic steering of the acoustic field without physical movement of the device. Each element of the array can be independently controlled to generate interference patterns that shift the location of the nodal zones in real time, enabling the operator to guide particles along a predetermined trajectory within the anterior chamber. This embodiment is particularly useful for complex clinical scenarios in which particles are distributed unevenly or require relocation to a specific anatomical region, such as the inferior angle for trabecular meshwork protection or the pupillary margin for visual axis clearance. The phased array system is controlled by a computer algorithm that maps the spatial distribution of particles using real-time OCT imaging and calculates the optimal phase and amplitude settings for each transducer element to achieve the desired particle trajectory. This closed-loop adaptive control system enhances precision and reduces treatment time, making it suitable for integration into automated surgical platforms or robotic ophthalmic systems.

In another embodiment, the system is adapted for use in conjunction with intracameral drug delivery. For example, when administering a suspension of endothelial cells for corneal transplantation, the acoustic field can be activated immediately following injection to organize the cells into a uniform monolayer along the posterior corneal surface, enhancing graft adherence and reducing the risk of cell clumping or uneven distribution. Similarly, when delivering anti-VEGF agents or corticosteroids, the acoustic manipulation can be used to concentrate the medication at the site of pathology—such as the macula or the iris root—thereby increasing local bioavailability and reducing systemic exposure. The method may also be employed diagnostically, as the spatial patterns formed by the particles under acoustic manipulation serve as a visual biomarker for particle composition, size, and concentration, allowing clinicians to differentiate between, for instance, pigment granules and crystalline medication aggregates based on their distinct aggregation kinetics and spatial profiles.

Safety evaluations conducted in ex vivo porcine eyes demonstrated no macroscopic or microscopic signs of corneal endothelial damage, stromal edema, lens opacity, or retinal disruption following exposure to acoustic fields at intensities up to 80 milliwatts per square centimeter for durations of up to five minutes. Thermal imaging confirmed that surface temperature increases remained below 2.5 degrees Celsius, even under maximum power conditions. Optical coherence tomography scans revealed no structural alterations in the corneal layers, and histological analysis showed intact endothelial cell morphology, normal collagen architecture, and absence of inflammatory infiltrates. These findings confirm that the acoustic forces employed in this invention are orders of magnitude below the thresholds for mechanical cavitation, thermal denaturation, or acoustic streaming-induced shear stress, which have been documented to occur at intensities exceeding 500 milliwatts per square centimeter and frequencies below 500 kilohertz. Furthermore, the absence of any adverse effects on the trabecular meshwork or Schlemm’s canal suggests that the natural aqueous outflow pathways remain unimpeded, even when particles are concentrated in the angle region, as the accumulated material is held in a spatially confined zone that does not obstruct the meshwork’s functional architecture.

The method is applicable to a broad spectrum of clinical conditions. In pigment dispersion syndrome, the technique can be used to sequester pigment granules at the inferior angle, preventing their migration into the trabecular meshwork and thereby reducing the risk of progressive glaucomatous damage. In pseudoexfoliation syndrome, the fibrillar material that accumulates on the lens capsule and anterior iris surface can be redirected to the peripheral anterior chamber, minimizing its interference with intraocular lens positioning and reducing the likelihood of secondary angle closure. In cases of hyphema, erythrocyte aggregates can be guided to the inferior chamber, improving visual clarity and facilitating natural clearance through the aqueous humor flow, thereby reducing the need for surgical washout. For patients with asteroid hyalosis, where calcium-lipid particles float in the vitreous and may extend into the anterior chamber, the method can be adapted to manipulate these particles away from the visual axis, enhancing visual function. In the context of intracameral drug delivery, the technique can be used to optimize the spatial distribution of medications such as triamcinolone acetonide, ganciclovir, or bevacizumab, ensuring that therapeutic concentrations are maintained at the target tissue while minimizing exposure to the corneal endothelium. Finally, in corneal endothelial transplantation, the method can be employed to align donor endothelial cells into a continuous sheet on the posterior corneal surface, improving graft survival and visual outcomes.

The invention is designed for use in outpatient clinical settings, including ophthalmology clinics, ambulatory surgical centers, and emergency departments. The device is compact, portable, and compatible with standard sterilization protocols, making it suitable for single-use or multi-use applications depending on regulatory classification. The software interface is intuitive, with pre-programmed protocols for common indications, and includes safety interlocks that prevent operation if the transducer is misaligned, if coupling is inadequate, or if the thermal threshold is exceeded. The system may be integrated with existing diagnostic platforms such as slit-lamp biomicroscopes or OCT systems to provide real-time feedback and documentation of treatment efficacy. The method requires no anesthesia beyond topical numbing, no incisions, no intraocular instrumentation, and no postoperative medications, making it a minimally disruptive intervention with high patient acceptance. The long-term stability of the particle aggregates, the absence of tissue damage, and the repeatability of the procedure position this invention as a foundational technology for the emerging field of noninvasive ocular particle engineering, offering a paradigm shift in the management of conditions that have, until now, been treated only reactively or not at all.
