## BACKGROUND

Autism spectrum disorder represents a complex neurodevelopmental condition characterized by persistent deficits in social communication and interaction, alongside restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities. The disorder manifests early in development, often becoming clinically apparent during the toddler and preschool years, yet the current standard of care relies heavily on behavioral observation, parental interview, and clinical judgment, resulting in a median age of diagnosis exceeding four years in the United States. This delay significantly impedes the opportunity to initiate early behavioral interventions, which have been consistently demonstrated to improve long-term cognitive, linguistic, and adaptive outcomes while reducing the lifelong burden on families and healthcare systems. Despite decades of research, no biological test exists that can reliably identify autism spectrum disorder prior to the emergence of overt behavioral symptoms. While genetic analyses have identified hundreds of rare variants associated with autism, these account for fewer than one in five cases and are of limited utility in the general population due to low penetrance, heterogeneity, and the absence of a single causal gene or pathway. Furthermore, genetic testing often requires extensive counseling, yields inconclusive results in the majority of cases, and cannot account for the dynamic interplay between environmental exposures, epigenetic regulation, and metabolic perturbations that likely contribute to the pathophysiology of the disorder. Metabolomics, the comprehensive study of small-molecule biochemicals within a biological system, offers a compelling alternative pathway for diagnostic discovery. Unlike genomic approaches that reflect inherited potential, metabolomic profiles capture the real-time biochemical state of an organism, integrating influences from diet, gut microbiota, environmental toxins, mitochondrial function, and cellular energy metabolism—all of which have been implicated in autism spectrum disorder through prior observational studies. Previous metabolomic investigations have focused primarily on urine, cerebrospinal fluid, or post-mortem brain tissue, each of which presents significant limitations for clinical translation. Urine samples are subject to diurnal variation, hydration status, and renal excretion dynamics that obscure systemic metabolic signals. Cerebrospinal fluid collection is invasive and impractical for routine pediatric screening. Post-mortem tissue does not permit early intervention. Blood plasma, by contrast, is a readily accessible, minimally invasive, and systemic biofluid that reflects the integrated metabolic output of multiple organ systems, including the brain, liver, gut, and immune cells. However, prior attempts to identify plasma-based metabolomic signatures for autism have been limited in scope, often relying on single-platform detection methods that fail to capture the full chemical diversity of the metabolome, or have suffered from small sample sizes, inadequate statistical validation, or lack of independent replication. Moreover, existing studies have not employed the rigorous combination of orthogonal chromatographic separations, high-resolution mass spectrometry, and machine learning-based feature selection that is necessary to distinguish true biological signals from analytical noise, batch effects, and confounding variables such as age, sex, diet, or medication use. The field has lacked a validated, reproducible, and scalable method capable of identifying a consistent, quantifiable, and biologically interpretable panel of metabolites that can reliably differentiate children with autism spectrum disorder from their typically developing peers at a clinically meaningful threshold of accuracy. There is therefore a critical and unmet need for a blood-based diagnostic test that is objective, non-invasive, rapid, and capable of being implemented in pediatric clinics, developmental screening programs, and primary care settings to enable diagnosis before the age of three, when neuroplasticity is greatest and intervention is most effective. Such a test must be grounded in a robust analytical framework that accounts for the chemical complexity of human plasma, employs multiple complementary detection modalities to maximize metabolite coverage, and utilizes statistically validated classification algorithms that are not susceptible to overfitting or population-specific bias. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel diagnostic system that combines five orthogonal mass spectrometry-based metabolomic platforms, a stringent data preprocessing pipeline, a recursive feature selection algorithm based on variable importance in projection, and a support vector machine classifier trained on a defined set of 80 metabolite features that collectively achieve a classification accuracy exceeding 80% in an independent validation cohort. This system represents the first clinically viable, metabolite-based diagnostic tool for autism spectrum disorder that is capable of being standardized, commercialized, and deployed at scale.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel diagnostic system for the detection of autism spectrum disorder in children between the ages of four and six years using a panel of metabolite biomarkers measured in blood plasma. The system comprises a method for the quantitative profiling of small-molecule metabolites in plasma samples using a combination of gas chromatography-mass spectrometry and four distinct liquid chromatography-high resolution mass spectrometry methods, each employing different chromatographic phases and ionization polarities to maximize chemical coverage. The resulting mass spectral data are processed through a multi-stage computational pipeline that includes peak detection, retention time alignment, normalization to internal standards, and stringent filtering to remove noise, contaminants, and low-confidence features. From this processed dataset, a subset of 80 metabolite features is selected using a recursive feature elimination algorithm based on variable importance in projection scores derived from partial least squares discriminant analysis and support vector machine classification models trained on a discovery cohort of 61 children, including 39 diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder and 22 typically developing controls. These 80 features are then used to train a support vector machine classifier with a linear kernel that achieves a classification accuracy of at least 80%, a sensitivity of at least 85%, and a specificity of at least 75% when applied to an independent validation cohort of 21 children, including 13 with autism spectrum disorder and 8 typically developing controls. The metabolite features include both annotated and unannotated mass spectral signals corresponding to known biochemicals such as homocitrulline, aspartate, glutamate, citric acid, succinic acid, dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate, creatinine, isoleucine, glutaric acid, 3-aminoisobutyric acid, and various medium-chain fatty acids, as well as novel, uncharacterized molecular entities whose mass-to-charge ratios and retention times are uniquely associated with autism spectrum disorder. The system further includes a standardized protocol for blood collection, plasma preparation, and sample storage that ensures metabolic stability and minimizes pre-analytical variability, including overnight fasting, venipuncture during morning hours, immediate placement of blood samples on ice, rapid plasma separation, aliquoting into cryovials, and storage at −80°C prior to analysis. The diagnostic system is implemented as a laboratory-developed test that may be deployed in clinical laboratories equipped with high-resolution mass spectrometers and computational infrastructure capable of executing the feature selection and classification algorithms. The invention does not rely on genetic testing, behavioral scoring, or subjective clinical judgment, but instead provides an objective, quantitative, and reproducible measure of metabolic dysregulation that is directly correlated with the presence of autism spectrum disorder. The 80-feature signature is sufficiently robust to withstand minor variations in sample handling, instrument calibration, and operator technique, and demonstrates consistent performance across diverse demographic subgroups within the validation cohort. The invention further encompasses a computer-readable medium containing executable instructions that, when executed by a processor, perform the steps of receiving mass spectral data from a plasma sample, applying the feature selection algorithm to extract the 80 metabolite features, normalizing their intensities, and applying the trained support vector machine model to generate a binary classification output indicating the likelihood of autism spectrum disorder. The invention further includes a diagnostic kit comprising a set of internal standards, extraction reagents, chromatographic columns, mass spectrometry calibration solutions, and software modules configured to execute the classification algorithm, enabling standardized implementation across multiple testing sites. The invention represents a paradigm shift in the diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder by replacing subjective, behavior-based assessments with an objective, biochemically grounded, and scalable blood test that can be administered as early as age four, thereby enabling timely intervention and improving long-term outcomes for affected children.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF ILLUSTRATIVE EMBODIMENTS

The diagnostic system of the present invention is designed to detect autism spectrum disorder in children between the ages of four and six years through the quantitative analysis of metabolite profiles in blood plasma. The system is composed of three core components: a standardized sample collection and preparation protocol, a multi-platform metabolomic profiling methodology, and a machine learning-based classification algorithm that identifies a specific panel of 80 metabolite features capable of distinguishing children with autism spectrum disorder from typically developing peers with high accuracy. Each component is interdependent and necessary for the system to function as intended. The sample collection protocol begins with the selection of a pediatric subject who is between four and six years of age and who has not been previously diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder or any other neurodevelopmental, psychiatric, or systemic medical condition that could confound the metabolic profile. Prior to blood collection, the subject must fast for at least eight hours, and blood is drawn between the hours of 8:00 a.m. and 10:00 a.m. to minimize circadian variation in metabolite concentrations. A 9.6 mL EDTA-containing Vacutainer tube is used for venipuncture, and the sample is immediately inverted six to eight times to ensure homogenous anticoagulation and placed on ice within thirty seconds of collection. The sample is transported to the laboratory within two hours via a courier service using a biohazard container with coolant packs to maintain a temperature between 2°C and 8°C. Upon arrival, the sample is centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C to separate plasma from cellular components. The supernatant plasma is aliquoted into 50 µL volumes and immediately frozen at −80°C. Plasma samples are stored in a controlled environment with no more than three freeze-thaw cycles, and all samples are processed within twelve months of collection to preserve metabolic integrity. For analysis, plasma aliquots are thawed on ice and extracted using a methanol-water solution at a ratio of 8:1, containing a mixture of stable isotope-labeled internal standards to correct for variability in extraction efficiency, ionization suppression, and instrument drift. The extraction is performed at −20°C for ten minutes with agitation, followed by centrifugation at 18,400 × g for 20 minutes at 4°C to precipitate proteins and particulates. The supernatant is transferred to a fresh tube and subjected to a second centrifugation under identical conditions to ensure complete removal of debris. The resulting clear supernatant is evaporated to dryness under a nitrogen stream or in a SpeedVac concentrator and then reconstituted in 45 µL of a 50:50 mixture of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid in water, also containing additional internal standards for liquid chromatography-high resolution mass spectrometry analysis. The reconstituted sample is transferred to a high-performance liquid chromatography autosampler vial and analyzed using five distinct analytical platforms. The first platform is gas chromatography-mass spectrometry, which employs a gas chromatograph coupled to a time-of-flight mass spectrometer to separate and detect volatile and semi-volatile metabolites such as organic acids, amino acids, and short-chain fatty acids. The second through fifth platforms are liquid chromatography-high resolution mass spectrometry methods, each utilizing a different chromatographic stationary phase and ionization polarity to maximize metabolite coverage. The first LC-HRMS method employs hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography with a BEH Amide column and electrospray ionization in positive mode to detect polar metabolites such as nucleotides, amino acids, and sugar derivatives. The second LC-HRMS method uses the same hydrophilic interaction column but with electrospray ionization in negative mode to detect acidic metabolites such as organic acids, bile acids, and phospholipid fragments. The third LC-HRMS method employs a C8 reversed-phase column with electrospray ionization in positive mode to detect moderately hydrophobic metabolites such as steroids, acylcarnitines, and certain lipids. The fourth LC-HRMS method uses the same C8 column but with electrospray ionization in negative mode to detect anionic species such as carboxylates, sulfate conjugates, and oxidized lipids. Each LC-HRMS method is performed on a high-resolution quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer capable of acquiring data at a resolution of at least 40,000 full width at half maximum and a mass accuracy of less than 5 ppm. The mass spectrometer is calibrated prior to each analytical batch using a mixture of sodium formate and cesium iodide to ensure accurate mass assignment. Data are acquired over a mass-to-charge ratio range of 50 to 1,200 with a scan rate of at least five spectra per second. Each sample is analyzed once per platform, and all samples are randomized across analytical batches to prevent batch effects from influencing classification outcomes. The raw mass spectral data from all five platforms are converted into an open-source mzData format and processed using a unified computational pipeline. The pipeline begins with peak detection using the XCMS algorithm, which identifies features based on their mass-to-charge ratio and retention time. Retention time drift is corrected using the obiwarp algorithm, which applies a non-linear warping function to align chromatographic peaks across samples. Features are grouped into bins based on overlapping mass and retention time windows, and missing values are imputed using iterative peak filling based on the intensity and retention time of neighboring features. A series of quality filters are then applied to remove features that are below a minimum abundance threshold, exhibit poor peak shape, contain non-biological mass defects, or correspond to known contaminants such as plasticizers, detergents, or column bleed products. Features that are present in fewer than 80% of samples within a diagnostic group are excluded. The remaining features are normalized to the median intensity of a panel of seven internal standards spiked into each sample prior to extraction, ensuring that variations in sample volume, extraction efficiency, and instrument sensitivity do not bias the relative abundance measurements. The normalized feature intensities from all five platforms are combined into a single matrix containing 4,572 features. From this matrix, a univariate statistical analysis is performed using Welch’s t-test to identify features that exhibit statistically significant differences in abundance between children diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder and typically developing controls. Features with an uncorrected p-value less than 0.05 are retained for further analysis. Each retained feature is then manually reviewed to confirm the integrity of its extracted ion chromatogram, ensuring that the peak is symmetrical, well-resolved, and consistently integrated across all samples in both diagnostic groups. Features with poor peak shape, co-eluting interferences, or inconsistent integration are excluded. This process results in a final set of 179 features that are used as input for classification modeling. Two machine learning algorithms are employed: partial least squares discriminant analysis and support vector machine classification with a linear kernel. Both algorithms are trained on a subset of 61 samples, comprising 39 children with autism spectrum disorder and 22 typically developing controls, which are randomly selected to ensure balanced representation across age, sex, ethnicity, and IQ score. A nested cross-validation procedure is implemented to prevent overfitting and to generate robust estimates of feature importance. The training set is resampled 100 times using an 80:20 split, with 80% of the samples used for training and 20% for internal validation within each resample. For each resample, a partial least squares discriminant analysis model and a support vector machine model are trained, and the variable importance in projection score is calculated for each of the 179 features. Recursive feature elimination is performed in steps of 20 features, with the least important features removed iteratively until the optimal subset is identified based on the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. The variable importance scores from all 100 resamples are averaged to generate a consensus VIP score for each feature, which reflects its consistent contribution to classification across multiple iterations. The 179 features are then ranked by their average VIP score, and feature subsets of increasing size are evaluated for their ability to classify the independent validation set of 21 samples, which includes 13 children with autism spectrum disorder and 8 typically developing controls. The validation set is not used in any stage of model training or feature selection and serves solely as an external test of generalizability. The classification performance of each feature subset is evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve metrics. The subset that achieves the highest combined performance across all metrics is selected as the final diagnostic signature. In this instance, the 80-feature subset trained using the support vector machine algorithm achieves an accuracy of 81%, a sensitivity of 85%, a specificity of 75%, and an area under the curve of 84% on the validation set. This performance is significantly superior to random classification, as confirmed by permutation testing in which the diagnostic labels are randomly shuffled and the same modeling procedure is repeated, yielding an average area under the curve of 0.52, which is indistinguishable from chance. The 80 features include both annotated and unannotated metabolites. Annotated features are identified by matching their exact mass-to-charge ratio and retention time to known chemical standards in public databases such as METLIN, MassBank, and the SteminaMetDB database, with a mass error tolerance of less than 20 ppm and a retention time deviation of less than 35 seconds. Confirmation of chemical identity is performed using tandem mass spectrometry, in which precursor ions are fragmented under controlled collision energies and the resulting product ion spectra are compared to reference spectra in the same databases. Confirmed metabolites include homocitrulline, which is significantly decreased in children with autism spectrum disorder, as well as aspartate, glutamate, citric acid, succinic acid, dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate, creatinine, isoleucine, glutaric acid, 3-aminoisobutyric acid, and several medium-chain fatty acids including methylhexa-, tetra-, and hepta-decanoic acids. Unannotated features are retained in the model if they exhibit consistent, statistically significant differences in abundance and high VIP scores, even in the absence of a known chemical identity. These unannotated features may represent novel metabolites, previously uncharacterized derivatives, or metabolites that are not present in existing databases. The diagnostic system is implemented as a laboratory-developed test that requires a high-resolution mass spectrometer, a liquid chromatography system capable of performing both hydrophilic interaction and reversed-phase separations, and a computational workstation with sufficient memory and processing power to execute the classification algorithm. The algorithm is implemented as a software module that accepts a data file containing the normalized intensities of the 80 features and outputs a binary classification result indicating whether the sample is consistent with autism spectrum disorder or typical development. The software includes a quality control module that flags samples with missing data, outlier intensities, or insufficient internal standard recovery, and requires manual review before a diagnostic result is issued. The system may be deployed in clinical laboratories as a reflex test following a positive autism screening questionnaire or as a primary diagnostic tool in specialized developmental clinics. The diagnostic result is reported as a probability score between 0 and 1, with a threshold of 0.65 used to classify a sample as consistent with autism spectrum disorder. The system does not provide a definitive diagnosis but rather a quantitative biomarker profile that, when interpreted in conjunction with clinical evaluation, significantly improves diagnostic certainty. The invention further includes a diagnostic kit comprising a set of seven stable isotope-labeled internal standards, a methanol-water extraction solution, a 0.1% formic acid reconstitution solution, a set of four chromatographic columns (two HILIC and two C8), a calibration mixture for mass spectrometry, and a software license key that unlocks the classification algorithm. The kit is designed for use by trained laboratory personnel and includes detailed standard operating procedures for sample preparation, instrument calibration, data acquisition, and result interpretation. The invention further encompasses a computer-readable storage medium containing executable instructions that, when executed by a processor, perform the steps of receiving mass spectral data from a plasma sample, applying the feature selection algorithm to extract the 80 metabolite features, normalizing their intensities, and applying the trained support vector machine model to generate a binary classification output indicating the likelihood of autism spectrum disorder. The invention further includes a method for training the classification model using a larger dataset to refine the feature set, update the VIP scores, and improve the threshold for classification, thereby enabling continuous improvement of the diagnostic system as additional data become available. The invention further includes a method for validating the diagnostic system in independent cohorts from diverse geographic, ethnic, and socioeconomic backgrounds to ensure generalizability across populations. The invention further includes a method for integrating the diagnostic system into electronic health record systems to enable automated ordering, result interpretation, and clinical decision support. The invention further includes a method for monitoring the stability of the 80 metabolite features over time to ensure that the diagnostic signature remains robust in the face of evolving analytical platforms, reagent formulations, and environmental exposures. The invention does not claim any single metabolite as a biomarker, but rather the specific combination of 80 features as a unified diagnostic signature. The invention further excludes any method that relies on genetic testing, behavioral scoring, or neuroimaging as a primary diagnostic criterion. The invention is not intended to replace clinical judgment but to augment it with an objective, quantitative, and reproducible biological measure that reduces diagnostic uncertainty and enables earlier intervention.

### EXAMPLES

In a first example, a plasma sample is collected from a 5-year-old male child who presents with delayed speech, limited eye contact, and repetitive hand-flapping behaviors. The child has no history of seizures, genetic syndromes, or metabolic disorders. The sample is collected after an overnight fast between 8:30 a.m. and 9:00 a.m. using a 9.6 mL EDTA Vacutainer tube, immediately placed on ice, and transported to the laboratory within one hour. Plasma is separated by centrifugation at 2,000 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C, and a 50 µL aliquot is extracted using 450 µL of 8:1 methanol-water containing seven internal standards. The sample is vortexed for ten minutes at 4°C, centrifuged at 18,400 × g for 20 minutes at 4°C, and the supernatant is evaporated to dryness. The dried extract is reconstituted in 45 µL of 50:50 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid in water. The reconstituted sample is analyzed using five analytical platforms: gas chromatography-mass spectrometry, hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography with electrospray ionization in positive mode, hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography with electrospray ionization in negative mode, reversed-phase C8 liquid chromatography with electrospray ionization in positive mode, and reversed-phase C8 liquid chromatography with electrospray ionization in negative mode. The raw data from each platform are processed using the XCMS algorithm, retention times are aligned using the obiwarp algorithm, and features are normalized to the median intensity of the internal standards. The resulting feature matrix is imported into the diagnostic software, which extracts the intensities of the 80 predefined metabolite features, applies the trained support vector machine model, and outputs a classification score of 0.82. Since this score exceeds the threshold of 0.65, the result is reported as consistent with autism spectrum disorder. The child is referred for comprehensive behavioral evaluation, which confirms a diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder. In a second example, a plasma sample is collected from a 5-year-old female child who presents with age-appropriate language development, social engagement, and play behaviors. The sample is collected and processed identically to the first example. The diagnostic software extracts the 80 metabolite features and calculates a classification score of 0.31, which is below the threshold of 0.65. The result is reported as consistent with typical development. Behavioral assessment confirms the absence of autism spectrum disorder. In a third example, a plasma sample is collected from a 4-year-old male child with a history of gastrointestinal distress and a gluten- and casein-free diet. The system is capable of being implemented in a clinical laboratory setting with standard instrumentation and computational infrastructure, and it provides a quantitative, objective, and biologically grounded diagnostic signal that significantly improves the accuracy and timeliness of autism spectrum disorder detection.
