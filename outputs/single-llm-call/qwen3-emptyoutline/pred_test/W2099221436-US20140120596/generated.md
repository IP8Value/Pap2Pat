# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to a novel method for the pretreatment of lignocellulosic biomass to enhance enzymatic saccharification and improve the overall yield of fermentable sugars for biofuel and biochemical production. More specifically, the invention pertains to an alkaline twin-screw extrusion (ATSE) process that utilizes a uniquely configured co-rotating twin-screw extruder equipped with alternating transport screw elements and reversed screw elements to simultaneously apply intense mechanical shear, compression, and thermal energy to lignocellulosic feedstock in the presence of an alkaline reagent, thereby disrupting the lignin-carbohydrate complex and significantly increasing the accessibility of cellulose and hemicellulose to enzymatic hydrolysis. The method is particularly suited for the pretreatment of agricultural residues such as corn stover, wheat straw, rice straw, and bagasse, as well as forestry wastes including sawdust and wood chips, without requiring prior size reduction beyond minimal cutting into short lengths. The process operates at moderate temperatures, typically not exceeding 100°C, and eliminates the need for external heating systems by leveraging internally generated frictional heat. Furthermore, the method enables operation at high solid-to-liquid ratios, minimizes chemical consumption, and facilitates the recovery and reuse of alkaline reagents through integration with established pulp mill infrastructure, making it economically viable and environmentally sustainable for industrial-scale deployment.

The invention finds application in the bioenergy sector, particularly in biorefineries aiming to convert non-food biomass into ethanol, butanol, organic acids, and other platform chemicals via consolidated bioprocessing. It is also applicable in the production of value-added lignin derivatives such as phenolic resins, concrete admixtures, and carbon fiber precursors, where the recovered lignin exhibits favorable molecular weight characteristics and low polydispersity. The method is further compatible with existing wastewater treatment and chemical recovery systems used in kraft pulping operations, thereby reducing capital expenditure and operational complexity compared to conventional pretreatment technologies. The invention overcomes the limitations of prior extrusion-based pretreatments, which typically require high temperatures, low biomass loading, or additional heating, and surpasses the efficiency of many chemical pretreatments by achieving superior delignification and sugar retention with reduced reagent usage and energy input.

## BACKGROUND

The global demand for renewable energy and sustainable chemical feedstocks has intensified efforts to replace fossil-derived products with bio-based alternatives derived from abundant, non-food lignocellulosic biomass. Agricultural residues and forestry waste streams, including corn stover, wheat straw, rice straw, sugarcane bagasse, and sawdust, represent vast, underutilized resources that, if efficiently converted into fermentable sugars, could significantly contribute to the decarbonization of transportation fuels and industrial chemistry. However, the inherent structural complexity of lignocellulosic materials—characterized by the intimate association of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin within a rigid, recalcitrant matrix—poses a formidable barrier to enzymatic hydrolysis. The crystalline nature of cellulose, the branched and amorphous structure of hemicellulose, and the hydrophobic, three-dimensional aromatic polymer network of lignin collectively impede enzyme access, reduce hydrolysis rates, and limit sugar yields, rendering raw biomass unsuitable for direct biological conversion.

To overcome this recalcitrance, numerous pretreatment strategies have been developed and investigated over the past several decades. These include acid hydrolysis, alkali treatment, steam explosion, ammonia fiber explosion (AFEX), hot water extraction, organosolv processes, and microbial pretreatments. While some of these methods demonstrate high sugar yields under controlled laboratory conditions, their scalability and economic feasibility remain constrained by a combination of operational challenges. Acid-based pretreatments, for instance, often lead to the formation of fermentation inhibitors such as furfural and hydroxymethylfurfural, necessitate corrosion-resistant equipment, and generate acidic effluents that are costly to neutralize and treat. Steam explosion and AFEX require high-pressure vessels, significant energy input, and complex control systems, while alkali-based methods such as lime pretreatment often demand extended residence times ranging from hours to weeks, rendering them impractical for continuous industrial operations. Furthermore, many of these technologies operate at elevated temperatures exceeding 150°C, necessitating substantial external heating, which increases both capital and operational costs.

Extrusion-based pretreatment methods have emerged as promising alternatives due to their ability to combine mechanical disruption with chemical modification in a single, continuous unit operation. Several studies have explored the use of single-screw and twin-screw extruders for biomass pretreatment, often in conjunction with water, dilute acid, or alkali solutions. However, these prior approaches suffer from critical limitations. Many require the biomass to be pre-ground into fine particles (typically 1–8 mm in size), adding an energy-intensive preprocessing step that diminishes overall process efficiency. Others operate at temperatures above 110°C, requiring external heating systems that increase energy consumption and complicate reactor design. Additionally, the biomass-to-liquid ratios in these systems are often low, resulting in large volumes of dilute spent liquor that are expensive to concentrate, recover, or dispose of. Moreover, the screw configurations employed in existing extruders are typically designed for homogeneous mixing or conveying, lacking the specific mechanical architecture necessary to generate the intense localized shear, compression, and fibrillation required for effective lignin disruption without excessive carbohydrate degradation.

Recent attempts to improve extrusion pretreatment have focused on modifying screw geometry, increasing residence time, or integrating chemical injection points. Nevertheless, none have successfully addressed the fundamental trade-off between delignification efficiency and sugar preservation. High alkali concentrations, while effective in removing lignin, invariably lead to significant solubilization and degradation of hemicellulose, reducing the total recoverable sugar yield. Conversely, low alkali dosages fail to achieve sufficient delignification, leaving the cellulose matrix inaccessible to enzymes. Furthermore, the absence of a controlled heat preservation step following extrusion in many prior systems limits the extent of chemical reactions between the alkali and the biomass, resulting in suboptimal structural modification. The lack of a standardized, scalable, and energy-efficient method capable of achieving high delignification (>70%), high glucan recovery (>95%), and high total sugar yield (>75%) under mild conditions remains a persistent gap in the field.

The present invention fills this gap by introducing a novel twin-screw extruder configuration that integrates alternating transport screw elements and reversed screw elements to generate a unique flow regime characterized by repeated compression, shear, and reorientation of biomass fibers. This configuration, combined with the controlled addition of dilute sodium hydroxide at a high solid-to-liquid ratio and a subsequent heat preservation phase, enables the synergistic disruption of lignin-carbohydrate complexes through a combination of mechanical fibrillation, alkaline swelling, and thermal activation—all without the need for external heating or pre-grinding. The process achieves delignification levels comparable to or exceeding those of conventional methods while preserving a significantly higher proportion of fermentable carbohydrates. The resulting pretreated biomass exhibits enhanced porosity, increased specific surface area, and improved enzymatic digestibility, enabling high sugar yields under standard hydrolysis conditions. Importantly, the method is designed for seamless integration into existing pulp and paper mill infrastructure, allowing for the recovery of alkali and lignin using mature technologies such as black liquor evaporation and causticizing, thereby reducing capital investment and operational complexity. This invention thus represents a transformative advancement in biomass pretreatment technology, offering a scalable, cost-effective, and sustainable pathway for the commercial conversion of lignocellulosic feedstocks into renewable fuels and chemicals.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for the pretreatment of lignocellulosic biomass to enhance enzymatic saccharification and maximize the recovery of fermentable sugars, comprising the steps of feeding a lignocellulosic feedstock into a twin-screw extruder equipped with alternating transport screw elements and reversed screw elements, introducing an aqueous alkaline solution into the extruder at a biomass-to-liquid ratio of at least 1:1.5 by weight, and subjecting the feedstock to mechanical compression, shear, and frictional heating within the extruder to generate a pretreated biomass slurry with a temperature of at least 90°C, followed by sealing the slurry in a closed container and maintaining it at ambient temperature for a period of at least one hour to allow for continued alkaline penetration and structural modification. The feedstock is subsequently washed to remove residual alkali and subjected to enzymatic hydrolysis under standard conditions to release monomeric sugars. The transport screw elements are configured to convey the feedstock axially through the extruder, while the reversed screw elements are arranged to oppose the direction of conveyance, thereby creating zones of high pressure and shear where the biomass fibers are compressed, fragmented, and fibrillated. The reversed screw elements include skewed slots through which the biomass is forced under pressure, inducing mechanical delamination and increasing internal porosity. The alkaline solution is introduced downstream of the initial compression zone to ensure uniform distribution and penetration into the disrupted biomass structure. The method operates without external heating, relying solely on the heat generated by mechanical friction between the biomass fibers and the extruder screws to elevate the temperature of the mixture to between 90°C and 105°C. The alkaline reagent is sodium hydroxide, and its dosage is maintained between 0.04 and 0.10 grams per gram of dry biomass, with an optimal range of 0.05 to 0.08 grams per gram of dry biomass. The heat preservation step is conducted without additional heating, and the duration is between one and ten hours, with a preferred duration of one to three hours to balance process efficiency and sugar yield. The biomass feedstock is prepared by cutting into pieces of 2 to 5 centimeters in length, eliminating the need for grinding or milling. The washed pretreated solids retain at least 95% of the original glucan content and at least 60% of the original xylan content, while achieving a lignin reduction of at least 65% compared to untreated biomass. The enzymatic hydrolysis of the pretreated biomass, conducted at a solid loading of 2% (w/v) using commercial cellulase and β-glucosidase enzymes, results in a glucan conversion of at least 80% and a xylan conversion of at least 85% after 48 hours of hydrolysis, yielding a total sugar recovery of at least 75% based on the total carbohydrates present in the original feedstock. The method further includes the recovery of lignin from the spent liquor via spray drying or evaporation, yielding a lignin product with a weight-average molecular weight greater than 20,000 g/mol and a polydispersity index less than 1.5, suitable for use in the production of phenolic resins, concrete additives, or carbon fiber precursors. The process is conducted continuously at a throughput rate of at least 100 kilograms of dry biomass per hour, and the entire system is designed for integration with existing pulp mill chemical recovery systems, enabling the regeneration and reuse of sodium hydroxide and the combustion of spent liquor for energy recovery. The invention offers a significant improvement over prior art by achieving high delignification and sugar yield under mild conditions, without external heating, without pre-grinding, and with minimal chemical consumption, thereby reducing capital and operating costs while enhancing environmental sustainability.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention is directed to a method for the pretreatment of lignocellulosic biomass that combines mechanical disruption, chemical modification, and thermal activation in a single, continuous, and energy-efficient process. The method is based on the use of a specially designed twin-screw extruder that incorporates a unique arrangement of transport screw elements and reversed screw elements, which together generate a highly controlled and intense mechanical environment that effectively disrupts the lignin-carbohydrate complex without causing significant degradation of fermentable carbohydrates. The process begins with the preparation of lignocellulosic feedstock, which may include agricultural residues such as corn stover, wheat straw, rice straw, sugarcane bagasse, or forestry by-products such as sawdust, wood chips, or bark. The feedstock is first cut into pieces of approximately 2 to 5 centimeters in length using standard industrial choppers or grinders. This minimal size reduction is sufficient to ensure smooth feeding into the extruder and eliminates the need for energy-intensive grinding or milling, which is a common requirement in many prior pretreatment technologies. The feedstock is then conveyed continuously into the inlet of a twin-screw extruder, which is constructed from hardened steel or corrosion-resistant alloys to withstand the abrasive nature of the biomass and the alkaline environment.

The extruder consists of two co-rotating, intermeshing screws, each composed of a series of alternating transport screw elements and reversed screw elements. The transport screw elements are standard helical flights oriented in the direction of feedstock movement, designed to propel the biomass axially through the extruder barrel. The reversed screw elements, in contrast, are configured with helical flights oriented in the opposite direction to the transport elements, creating zones of high compression and backpressure. The number of transport and reversed screw elements may vary depending on the desired residence time and shear intensity, but a preferred configuration includes four transport screw elements followed by four reversed screw elements, repeated in sequence along the length of the extruder barrel. This arrangement ensures that the biomass undergoes multiple cycles of forward conveyance and backward compression, resulting in repeated fibrillation, fragmentation, and delamination of the fibrous structure. The reversed screw elements are further modified to include skewed slots or perforations arranged along their helical profile. As the biomass is compressed between the transport and reversed elements, it is forced through these slots under high pressure, inducing mechanical shearing that separates individual fibers and increases the internal porosity of the material. This structural modification is critical to enhancing enzyme accessibility during subsequent hydrolysis.

Simultaneously with mechanical disruption, an aqueous alkaline solution is introduced into the extruder through one or more injection ports located downstream of the initial compression zone. The alkaline solution is preferably a dilute aqueous solution of sodium hydroxide, with a concentration ranging from 1% to 10% by weight. The solution is metered into the extruder at a biomass-to-liquid ratio of at least 1:1.5 by weight, and more preferably between 1:1.8 and 1:2.2, to ensure sufficient chemical penetration while minimizing the volume of spent liquor generated. This high solid-to-liquid ratio is a key innovation of the invention, as it allows for the efficient use of alkali, reduces downstream water treatment costs, and facilitates the concentration and recovery of lignin from the spent stream. The alkaline solution is introduced after the initial mechanical disruption to ensure that the biomass fibers are already partially fibrillated, thereby enhancing the penetration of the alkali into the internal structure of the biomass rather than merely reacting with the outer surface. The sodium hydroxide dosage is maintained between 0.04 and 0.10 grams per gram of dry biomass, with an optimal range of 0.05 to 0.08 grams per gram of dry biomass. At these concentrations, the alkali effectively solubilizes lignin and hemicellulose without causing excessive degradation of cellulose, preserving the structural integrity of the glucan fraction.

As the biomass passes through the extruder, the intense mechanical forces generated by the interaction between the screws and the biomass fibers produce significant frictional heating. The temperature of the biomass mixture rises rapidly due to this internal energy generation, reaching a peak temperature of between 90°C and 105°C, with a preferred operating temperature of approximately 99°C. This temperature elevation is achieved without any external heating source, making the process inherently energy-efficient. The heat generated is sufficient to accelerate the alkaline delignification reaction, promote the swelling of cellulose and hemicellulose, and facilitate the dissolution of acetyl groups and other hemicellulose-derived compounds. The absence of external heating not only reduces energy consumption but also simplifies the design and maintenance of the extruder, eliminating the need for insulated heating jackets, steam coils, or electrical resistance elements.

Upon exiting the extruder, the pretreated biomass slurry is immediately transferred to a sealed container or insulated holding vessel, where it is maintained at ambient temperature for a period of at least one hour, and preferably between one and ten hours. This heat preservation step is critical to the success of the invention. Although the extruder generates sufficient heat to initiate chemical reactions, the prolonged exposure of the biomass to the alkaline environment under static conditions allows for deeper penetration of the alkali into the internal matrix of the fibers, further disrupting the lignin-carbohydrate bonds and enhancing the solubilization of lignin. During this phase, the temperature of the slurry gradually declines to ambient levels, but the chemical reactions continue due to the high reactivity of the alkali with the exposed cellulose and hemicellulose surfaces. The heat preservation step is conducted without any external heating or cooling, relying solely on the thermal inertia of the biomass mass and the insulating properties of the container. This step distinguishes the invention from prior extrusion methods, which typically proceed directly to washing or hydrolysis without allowing for extended chemical interaction. The duration of the heat preservation is optimized to balance the extent of delignification with the preservation of hemicellulose. A preservation time of one hour is sufficient to achieve approximately 70% lignin reduction and 78% total sugar yield, while longer durations of up to ten hours yield marginal improvements in delignification at the cost of increased process time and equipment footprint.

Following the heat preservation phase, the pretreated biomass is washed with water to remove residual alkali and dissolved lignin and hemicellulose components. The washing is performed using a series of filtration steps, typically through a 300-mesh nylon cloth or equivalent fine mesh screen, until the effluent reaches a neutral pH. The washed solids are then collected and may be stored at refrigerated temperatures (4°C) prior to enzymatic hydrolysis, or processed immediately. The washing step is designed to recover as much of the soluble sugars as possible while minimizing water usage, and the spent wash water may be combined with the primary spent liquor for subsequent lignin recovery. The recovered spent liquor, which contains dissolved lignin, hemicellulose-derived sugars, and residual alkali, is directed to a concentration system such as a spray dryer or evaporator, where it is concentrated to form a semi-solid residue. This residue can be further processed to recover sodium hydroxide through conventional causticizing techniques or used directly as a source of high-purity lignin. The lignin recovered by this method exhibits a weight-average molecular weight greater than 20,000 g/mol and a polydispersity index less than 1.5, indicating that the alkaline extrusion process preserves the structural integrity of the lignin polymer, making it suitable for valorization into high-value products such as phenolic resins, concrete admixtures, or carbon fiber precursors.

The pretreated biomass solids are then subjected to enzymatic hydrolysis under standard conditions using a mixture of commercial cellulase and β-glucosidase enzymes. The hydrolysis is typically conducted at a solid loading of 2% (w/v) in a sodium citrate buffer at pH 4.8 and a temperature of 50°C, with continuous agitation at 100 rpm for a duration of 48 hours. The enzyme loading is maintained at approximately 20 filter paper units (FPU) per gram of substrate for cellulase and 5 international units (IU) per gram of substrate for β-glucosidase. Under these conditions, the glucan conversion exceeds 80%, and the xylan conversion exceeds 85%, resulting in a total sugar yield of at least 75% based on the total carbohydrates present in the original feedstock. The high enzymatic digestibility is attributed to the combined effects of mechanical fibrillation, increased surface area, reduced lignin content, and enhanced porosity induced by the ATSE process. Scanning electron microscopy confirms that the pretreated biomass exhibits a highly disrupted structure, with individual fibers separated, delaminated, and perforated, revealing a porous, sponge-like morphology that facilitates enzyme penetration. The enzymatic hydrolysis kinetics are significantly accelerated compared to untreated biomass, with the majority of sugar release occurring within the first 24 hours, indicating that the pretreatment has effectively removed the physical and chemical barriers to enzymatic action.

The entire process is designed for continuous, industrial-scale operation. The extruder is capable of processing at least 100 kilograms of dry biomass per hour, with a preferred throughput of 200 kilograms per hour. The system is fully automated, with sensors monitoring temperature, pressure, screw speed, and feed rate to ensure consistent product quality. The extruder is driven by variable-frequency electric motors, and the screw speed is maintained between 250 and 350 revolutions per minute, with a preferred setting of 325 rpm. At this speed, the residence time of the biomass within the extruder is approximately 30 seconds, which is sufficient to achieve complete mechanical and chemical modification. The energy consumption of the system is approximately 240 kilowatt-hours per ton of dry biomass, which is significantly lower than the energy requirements of steam explosion, AFEX, or high-temperature extrusion methods. The process generates minimal wastewater, as the high solid-to-liquid ratio reduces the volume of liquid effluent by more than 50% compared to conventional pretreatments. The spent liquor, which contains dissolved lignin and hemicellulose sugars, can be processed using existing black liquor recovery systems employed in modern pulp mills, enabling the regeneration of sodium hydroxide through causticizing and the combustion of organic matter for steam and electricity generation. This integration with established industrial infrastructure significantly reduces capital investment and operational complexity, making the process economically viable for large-scale deployment.

The invention further encompasses the use of alternative alkaline reagents, such as sodium carbonate, sodium silicate, or potassium hydroxide, although sodium hydroxide remains the preferred reagent due to its high solubility, reactivity, and compatibility with existing chemical recovery systems. The method is also adaptable to a wide range of lignocellulosic feedstocks, including those with high silica content such as rice straw and wheat straw, although the presence of silica may necessitate adjustments in the chemical recovery process to prevent scaling in evaporators. The process is scalable from pilot to industrial scale without significant modifications to the screw configuration or operating parameters, and the extruder design is modular, allowing for the addition of multiple extrusion stages to further enhance delignification or sugar recovery if needed. The method is also compatible with downstream fermentation processes, as the pretreated biomass generates minimal fermentation inhibitors such as furfural, hydroxymethylfurfural, or phenolic compounds, which are commonly produced in acid-based pretreatments. The absence of these inhibitors eliminates the need for detoxification steps, further reducing process complexity and cost.

The invention represents a paradigm shift in biomass pretreatment technology by integrating mechanical, chemical, and thermal modifications into a single, continuous, and energy-efficient unit operation. Unlike prior methods that rely on external heating, high-pressure vessels, or pre-grinding, the ATSE process achieves superior delignification and sugar yield through the synergistic action of a uniquely configured screw system and a controlled heat preservation step. The method is robust, scalable, and economically feasible, offering a practical solution for the commercial conversion of lignocellulosic biomass into renewable fuels and chemicals.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In this example, the alkaline twin-screw extrusion (ATSE) pretreatment method was evaluated using corn stover collected from agricultural fields in Qingdao, Shandong Province, China. The corn stover was air-dried to a moisture content of approximately 8% and cut into pieces measuring between 2 and 5 centimeters in length. The chemical composition of the untreated corn stover, as determined by the modified National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) analytical procedure, consisted of 38.2% glucan, 22.1% xylan, 19.4% acid-insoluble lignin, 1.8% ash, and 18.5% moisture. The pretreatment was conducted using a pilot-scale twin-screw extruder manufactured by Tianzheng Co., China, with a capacity of 200 kilograms of dry biomass per hour. The extruder was equipped with eight alternating screw elements, consisting of four transport screw elements and four reversed screw elements, each arranged in sequence along the barrel. The reversed screw elements were fitted with skewed slots measuring 3 millimeters in width and 15 millimeters in length, oriented at a 45-degree angle relative to the screw axis to maximize shear and fibrillation. The extruder operated at a screw speed of 325 revolutions per minute, with a feed rate of 200 kilograms per hour, resulting in a residence time of approximately 30 seconds.

Sodium hydroxide solution at a concentration of 5% (w/w) was introduced into the extruder via a metering pump downstream of the initial compression zone, at a biomass-to-liquid ratio of 1:2 (w/w). The NaOH dosage was set at 0.06 grams per gram of dry biomass, corresponding to 12 grams of NaOH per 100 grams of dry corn stover. The temperature of the biomass slurry exiting the extruder was measured using thermocouples embedded within the barrel wall and was recorded at 99°C, achieved entirely through frictional heating with no external heating applied. The pretreated slurry was immediately transferred into sealed polyethylene bags and held at ambient temperature (22–25°C) for a period of one hour to allow for continued alkaline penetration and structural modification. Following the heat preservation step, the slurry was washed with tap water through a 300-mesh nylon cloth until the effluent pH stabilized at 6.8–7.2. The washed solids were then dried at 45°C to constant weight and ground using a laboratory blender to a particle size suitable for compositional analysis.

Compositional analysis revealed that the pretreated biomass retained 96.1% of the original glucan content and 78.3% of the original xylan content, while the acid-insoluble lignin content was reduced by 71.2% compared to the untreated feedstock. The total sugar recovery, calculated as the sum of glucan and xylan retained in the solid fraction, was 87.2% of the original carbohydrate content. The spent liquor, collected during washing and prior to the heat preservation step, was concentrated using a spray dryer (FH-1500, Shanghai Gaoji, China) to recover lignin. The recovered lignin exhibited a weight-average molecular weight (Mw) of 22,780 g/mol, a number-average molecular weight (Mn) of 16,430 g/mol, and a polydispersity index (PDI) of 1.386, indicating that the lignin structure remained largely intact and was suitable for downstream valorization. The enzymatic hydrolysis of the pretreated solids was conducted under standard NREL conditions using a mixture of Celluclast 1.5 L and Novozyme 188 at loadings of 20 FPU/g and 5 IU/g, respectively, at 50°C, pH 4.8, and 100 rpm for 48 hours. The glucan conversion reached 82.7%, and the xylan conversion reached 89.4%, resulting in a total sugar yield of 77.6% based on the total carbohydrates in the original biomass. The hydrolysate contained 467 grams of fermentable sugars per kilogram of dry corn stover, demonstrating the high efficiency of the ATSE pretreatment in liberating sugars without significant degradation.

### Example 2

This example was designed to evaluate the effect of varying sodium hydroxide dosage on the performance of the ATSE pretreatment method. Corn stover prepared as described in Example 1 was subjected to ATSE pretreatment under identical conditions, except that the NaOH dosage was systematically varied from 0.04 to 0.10 grams per gram of dry biomass, with increments of 0.02 grams per gram. The biomass-to-liquid ratio was maintained at 1:2, the screw speed at 325 rpm, and the heat preservation time at one hour. The temperature of the extruded slurry was consistently maintained at 99°C across all trials. The pretreated solids were washed, dried, and analyzed for composition, and enzymatic hydrolysis was performed under identical conditions as in Example 1.

At the lowest NaOH dosage of 0.04 g/g, the lignin reduction was only 48.7%, and the glucan recovery was 95.8%, while xylan recovery was 82.1%. The enzymatic hydrolysis yielded a glucan conversion of 65.3% and a xylan conversion of 71.2%, resulting in a total sugar yield of 63.8%. This indicated that the alkali concentration was insufficient to effectively disrupt the lignin matrix, leaving a significant portion of cellulose inaccessible to enzymes. At a dosage of 0.06 g/g, as in Example 1, the lignin reduction increased to 71.2%, glucan recovery remained at 96.1%, xylan recovery improved to 78.3%, and total sugar yield reached 77.6%. At 0.08 g/g, lignin reduction further increased to 80.3%, but xylan recovery dropped to 71.5%, likely due to increased solubilization and degradation of hemicellulose under more aggressive alkaline conditions. The glucan conversion rose to 84.1%, but the total sugar yield was 76.9%, slightly lower than at 0.06 g/g. At the highest dosage of 0.10 g/g, lignin reduction reached 82.9%, but xylan recovery declined sharply to 63.2%, and glucan recovery decreased to 93.4%, indicating that excessive alkali led to partial degradation of both hemicellulose and cellulose. The total sugar yield was 72.1%, and the enzymatic hydrolysis yielded a glucan conversion of 83.2% and a xylan conversion of 78.5%. The data demonstrated that while higher alkali dosages improved delignification, they also increased carbohydrate loss, with 0.06 g/g NaOH providing the optimal balance between lignin removal and sugar preservation. The total sugar yield peaked at 77.6% under this condition, confirming that the 0.06 g/g dosage was the most effective for maximizing overall sugar recovery.

### Example 3

This example investigated the influence of heat preservation time on the effectiveness of the ATSE pretreatment method. Corn stover was pretreated using the same extruder configuration and NaOH dosage of 0.06 g/g biomass, with a biomass-to-liquid ratio of 1:2 and a screw speed of 325 rpm. The heat preservation time was varied from 0 to 10 hours, with intervals of 1, 2, 3, and 10 hours. The pretreated slurry was sealed in plastic bags immediately after extrusion and stored at ambient temperature (22–25°C) for the specified duration. The temperature of the slurry was monitored throughout the preservation period and was observed to decline from 99°C to approximately 25°C within the first 30 minutes, with no external heating applied. The solids were then washed, dried, and subjected to compositional analysis and enzymatic hydrolysis as described in prior examples.

When no heat preservation was applied (0 hours), the lignin reduction was 51.3%, glucan recovery was 95.9%, xylan recovery was 80.1%, and total sugar yield was 73.2%. The enzymatic hydrolysis resulted in a glucan conversion of 71.5% and a xylan conversion of 79.4%. Extending the preservation time to one hour increased lignin reduction to 71.2%, xylan recovery to 78.3%, and total sugar yield to 77.6%, with glucan conversion rising to 82.7% and xylan conversion to 89.4%. Further increasing the preservation time to two hours resulted in only a marginal improvement in delignification (73.1%) and total sugar yield (78.1%), with xylan recovery decreasing slightly to 77.8%. At three hours, lignin reduction reached 74.5%, and total sugar yield was 78.3%, with no significant change in enzymatic digestibility. At 10 hours, lignin reduction plateaued at 75.2%, and total sugar yield increased only slightly to 78.5%, with xylan recovery declining to 76.9%. The data indicated that the majority of the beneficial chemical and structural modifications occurred within the first hour of preservation, with diminishing returns beyond three hours. The one-hour preservation time was therefore determined to be optimal, as it provided nearly the same level of delignification and sugar yield as longer durations while minimizing process time, equipment volume, and operational complexity. The results confirmed that the heat preservation step is essential for achieving maximum pretreatment efficacy, as the immediate washing of the extruded slurry without preservation resulted in significantly lower sugar yields and enzymatic digestibility. The invention thus demonstrates that the combination of mechanical extrusion and a short, ambient-temperature heat preservation phase is critical to achieving high-performance biomass pretreatment without the need for external heating or prolonged reaction times.
