# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of oncology and molecular imaging, and more specifically to methods and compositions for enhancing the diagnostic and therapeutic efficacy of phosphatidylserine-targeting modalities through the controlled modulation of phosphatidylserine exposure on the surface of cancer cells using non-contact electric fields. The invention encompasses novel systems and protocols for increasing the sensitivity and specificity of phosphatidylserine-directed imaging agents and therapeutic payloads, including nanovesicles, monoclonal antibodies, peptide conjugates, and radiolabeled compounds, by externally applying low-amplitude, direct current electric fields to tumors in vivo or ex vivo. This approach enables the selective upregulation of phosphatidylserine on the outer leaflet of cancer cell membranes without inducing cytotoxicity in adjacent healthy tissues, thereby creating a transient, tunable, and tumor-specific molecular signature that amplifies the binding and internalization of phosphatidylserine-targeting agents. The invention further includes methods for real-time monitoring of phosphatidylserine exposure dynamics using multimodal imaging platforms such as optical, positron emission tomography, single-photon emission computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging, thereby facilitating precision-guided therapeutic interventions. The disclosed technology represents a paradigm shift in cancer diagnostics and therapy by transforming phosphatidylserine from a passive biomarker into an actively modulatable therapeutic target, enabling personalized, non-invasive, and synergistic treatment regimens that reduce systemic toxicity while maximizing tumor cell eradication.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Cancer remains one of the most formidable challenges in modern medicine, with millions of new cases diagnosed annually and a significant proportion of patients succumbing to disease progression despite advances in surgical, chemotherapeutic, and immunotherapeutic interventions. Conventional cancer treatments, while effective in some contexts, are often limited by their lack of specificity, leading to substantial damage to healthy tissues and the emergence of drug-resistant phenotypes. The identification of tumor-specific molecular markers has therefore become a central focus in the development of targeted therapies and diagnostic imaging agents. Among the most promising of these markers is phosphatidylserine, a phospholipid that, under normal physiological conditions, is confined to the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane in healthy cells due to the activity of ATP-dependent flippase enzymes. In contrast, a wide variety of malignant cells exhibit aberrant externalization of phosphatidylserine to the outer membrane leaflet, a phenomenon associated with altered membrane asymmetry, metabolic stress, and evasion of immune surveillance. This consistent and widespread exposure of phosphatidylserine across diverse tumor types—ranging from glioblastoma and pancreatic adenocarcinoma to breast and lung cancers—has rendered it an attractive pan-cancer target for both diagnostic and therapeutic applications.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that phosphatidylserine can be effectively targeted using a variety of molecular agents, including annexin V, monoclonal antibodies such as bavituximab and PGN635, peptide–peptoid hybrids like PPS1D1, and engineered nanovesicles composed of saposin C and dioleoylphosphatidylserine (SapC-DOPS). These agents have shown remarkable specificity for phosphatidylserine-exposing cells in preclinical models, enabling high-contrast imaging via optical, magnetic resonance, and nuclear modalities, as well as potent cytotoxic effects when loaded with chemotherapeutic payloads. However, a critical limitation of these approaches is the heterogeneity of phosphatidylserine expression across and within tumors. Not all cancer cells within a given lesion exhibit uniform levels of surface phosphatidylserine, and many tumors display subpopulations with low or transient exposure, rendering them resistant to phosphatidylserine-targeted therapies. Furthermore, standard cytotoxic treatments such as chemotherapy and radiation, while capable of inducing phosphatidylserine externalization as part of apoptotic signaling, often do so in an uncontrolled, delayed, and non-uniform manner, limiting their utility as priming strategies for targeted imaging or therapy.

Attempts to overcome this heterogeneity have included the use of pharmacological agents to modulate membrane lipid asymmetry, such as calcium ionophores or inhibitors of flippase activity. However, these approaches are inherently non-specific, often affecting phosphatidylserine distribution in healthy tissues and leading to systemic inflammation, coagulopathy, or unintended immune activation. Moreover, the transient nature of phosphatidylserine externalization under physiological conditions makes it difficult to synchronize therapeutic delivery with optimal target expression. There remains, therefore, a significant unmet need for a method that can reliably, reversibly, and spatially precisely enhance phosphatidylserine exposure on cancer cells without inducing off-target effects or compromising cellular viability. Such a method would enable the strategic timing of phosphatidylserine-targeted imaging and therapy, thereby maximizing signal-to-noise ratios in diagnostics and therapeutic efficacy in treatment.

The application of electric fields to biological tissues has long been explored for its ability to alter membrane permeability, induce electroporation, and modulate ion fluxes. Tumor-treating fields and irreversible electroporation have been clinically adopted for the treatment of glioblastoma and solid tumors, respectively, primarily through mechanisms involving disruption of mitotic spindle formation or permanent membrane disruption. However, these modalities rely on high-intensity fields that cause direct cellular damage and are not suitable for repeated or prolonged use. In contrast, low-amplitude, non-contact, direct current electric fields have recently been shown to influence membrane potential and calcium dynamics without inducing cytotoxicity, suggesting a potential for fine-tuned modulation of membrane lipid organization. The present invention leverages this insight to demonstrate that precisely controlled, non-invasive electric fields can be applied to tumors to induce a reproducible, dose-dependent, and reversible increase in phosphatidylserine externalization, thereby creating a therapeutically exploitable window for enhanced targeting by phosphatidylserine-binding agents. This discovery represents a fundamental advance in the field, as it transforms phosphatidylserine from a static biomarker into a dynamically controllable therapeutic interface, enabling unprecedented precision in cancer imaging and treatment.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and non-invasive method for enhancing the diagnostic and therapeutic efficacy of phosphatidylserine-targeting agents by modulating the surface exposure of phosphatidylserine on cancer cells through the application of controlled, low-amplitude, non-contact direct current electric fields. The invention is based on the unexpected discovery that the external application of direct current electric fields at specific intensities and durations can selectively increase the translocation of phosphatidylserine from the inner to the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane in malignant cells without inducing cell death, altering total cellular phosphatidylserine content, or significantly affecting the viability of adjacent normal tissues. This modulation creates a transient, tumor-specific molecular signature that enhances the binding affinity, internalization, and retention of phosphatidylserine-targeting imaging and therapeutic agents, thereby improving signal intensity in diagnostic imaging and increasing cytotoxic potency in therapeutic applications.

In one embodiment, the invention provides a method for increasing the efficacy of phosphatidylserine-targeting imaging agents by applying a direct current electric field to a tumor site in a subject, wherein the electric field is delivered via a non-contact electrode configuration that avoids direct interaction with biological fluids or tissues, and wherein the field intensity is maintained between 50 and 200 volts per centimeter for a duration of 15 minutes to 4 hours. Following electric field application, a phosphatidylserine-targeting imaging agent is administered to the subject, and the resulting increase in tumor signal intensity is detected using optical, magnetic resonance, positron emission tomography, or single-photon emission computed tomography imaging modalities. The method enables a significant enhancement in tumor-to-background contrast compared to imaging performed without electric field priming, thereby improving the accuracy of tumor detection, delineation, and staging.

In another embodiment, the invention provides a method for enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of phosphatidylserine-targeting agents by applying a direct current electric field to a tumor site in a subject, followed by the administration of a phosphatidylserine-targeting therapeutic agent, wherein the therapeutic agent comprises a cytotoxic payload conjugated to a phosphatidylserine-binding moiety selected from the group consisting of saposin C, monoclonal antibodies, peptide–peptoid hybrids, and zinc-dipicolylamine derivatives. The electric field application results in increased surface phosphatidylserine expression, which enhances the binding and internalization of the therapeutic agent, leading to greater tumor cell apoptosis and reduced tumor burden compared to administration of the therapeutic agent alone. The method may be repeated over multiple treatment cycles, with electric field application preceding each therapeutic dose to maintain optimal target exposure.

In yet another embodiment, the invention provides a system for synchronized phosphatidylserine modulation and imaging or therapy, comprising a non-contact electric field delivery device, a phosphatidylserine-targeting imaging or therapeutic agent, and a real-time monitoring system configured to detect phosphatidylserine exposure dynamics. The system includes sensors for measuring membrane potential, intracellular calcium flux, or phosphatidylserine binding in real time, allowing for closed-loop control of electric field parameters based on feedback from the imaging modality. This enables adaptive, patient-specific treatment protocols that optimize the timing and intensity of electric field application relative to the pharmacokinetics of the phosphatidylserine-targeting agent.

The invention further includes pharmaceutical compositions comprising phosphatidylserine-targeting agents formulated in nanovesicles, liposomes, or antibody conjugates, wherein the agents are co-administered with an electric field delivery device for use in the methods described herein. The invention also encompasses kits for use in clinical settings, comprising a phosphatidylserine-targeting imaging or therapeutic agent, a non-contact electric field applicator, and instructions for use that specify the timing, intensity, and duration of electric field application relative to agent administration.

The invention provides a significant advancement over prior art by enabling the precise, repeatable, and non-destructive enhancement of phosphatidylserine exposure on cancer cells, thereby overcoming the limitations of heterogeneous target expression and suboptimal agent delivery that have hindered the clinical translation of phosphatidylserine-targeting strategies. The methods disclosed herein are broadly applicable across tumor types, are compatible with existing imaging and therapeutic platforms, and offer the potential for synergistic combination with chemotherapy, radiation, and immunotherapy without increasing systemic toxicity. The invention thus represents a transformative approach to precision oncology, enabling the conversion of phosphatidylserine from a passive biomarker into an actively controllable therapeutic interface.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Phosphatidylserine

Phosphatidylserine is a naturally occurring phospholipid that constitutes a minor but functionally critical component of cellular membranes in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisms. In healthy mammalian cells, phosphatidylserine is maintained predominantly on the cytoplasmic leaflet of the plasma membrane through the action of ATP-dependent flippase enzymes, which actively transport the lipid from the outer to the inner membrane leaflet. This asymmetric distribution is essential for maintaining membrane integrity, regulating signal transduction, and preventing unintended immune recognition. The maintenance of phosphatidylserine asymmetry is a tightly regulated process that involves the coordinated activity of flippases, floppases, and scramblases, with flippases responsible for inward translocation and scramblases facilitating bidirectional movement under conditions of cellular stress or apoptosis. Disruption of this asymmetry, leading to the exposure of phosphatidylserine on the extracellular surface, is a hallmark of apoptotic cells and serves as a universal “eat me” signal for phagocytic immune cells, thereby promoting the silent clearance of dying cells without triggering inflammation.

In the context of cancer biology, the externalization of phosphatidylserine is not merely a consequence of cell death but an active and persistent feature of many malignant cell phenotypes. Unlike normal cells, which maintain strict control over phosphatidylserine localization, cancer cells frequently exhibit constitutive exposure of phosphatidylserine on their outer membrane leaflet, even in the absence of overt apoptosis. This aberrant exposure is thought to arise from a combination of factors, including dysregulation of flippase activity, increased intracellular calcium concentrations, oxidative stress, and metabolic reprogramming associated with the Warburg effect. The extracellular pH of many tumors is significantly lower than that of normal tissues, typically ranging from 6.0 to 6.8, due to the accumulation of lactic acid from anaerobic glycolysis. This acidic microenvironment further enhances the binding affinity of certain phosphatidylserine-targeting molecules, such as saposin C, which exhibits pH-dependent conformational changes that increase its interaction with phosphatidylserine under acidic conditions. The persistent presence of phosphatidylserine on the surface of cancer cells contributes to immune evasion by promoting the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages toward an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype, inhibiting dendritic cell maturation, and suppressing cytotoxic T-cell activity. This immunomodulatory role renders phosphatidylserine not only a diagnostic marker but also a functional driver of tumor progression and therapeutic resistance.

The heterogeneity of phosphatidylserine exposure across and within tumors presents a significant challenge for targeted therapies. While some cancer cell populations exhibit high levels of surface phosphatidylserine, others remain largely negative, leading to incomplete targeting and the survival of resistant subclones. This variability is observed even within genetically identical cell lines and is influenced by factors such as cell cycle phase, hypoxia, nutrient availability, and proximity to vasculature. Consequently, phosphatidylserine-targeting agents often demonstrate variable uptake, with some regions of a tumor receiving high concentrations of the therapeutic payload while others remain untouched. This spatial and temporal heterogeneity undermines the reliability of phosphatidylserine as a standalone target and necessitates strategies to synchronize and amplify target expression. The present invention addresses this challenge by introducing a physical modality—non-contact electric field application—that can be used to induce a uniform, reproducible, and transient increase in phosphatidylserine exposure across the entire tumor mass, thereby creating a window of enhanced target availability for imaging and therapeutic agents.

Importantly, the total cellular content of phosphatidylserine remains unchanged following electric field application, indicating that the observed increase in surface exposure is due to redistribution rather than de novo synthesis or degradation. This distinction is critical, as it ensures that the method does not trigger compensatory metabolic responses or long-term alterations in membrane composition that could compromise cellular function or lead to unintended biological consequences. The mechanism by which electric fields induce phosphatidylserine translocation is believed to involve the alteration of transmembrane potential, which in turn activates calcium channels and increases intracellular calcium concentration. Elevated intracellular calcium inhibits flippase activity and promotes scramblase-mediated lipid scrambling, resulting in the outward movement of phosphatidylserine. This process is reversible, with phosphatidylserine levels returning to baseline within hours after cessation of the electric field, making it ideal for timed therapeutic interventions. The ability to control the magnitude, duration, and spatial distribution of phosphatidylserine exposure through electric field parameters provides an unprecedented level of precision in cancer targeting, enabling the development of adaptive, patient-specific treatment protocols that maximize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing collateral damage to healthy tissues.

### PS-Targeting Drugs

A variety of molecular agents have been developed to exploit the unique exposure of phosphatidylserine on cancer cell surfaces for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. These agents fall into several distinct classes, each with unique binding characteristics, pharmacokinetic profiles, and clinical potential. Among the most extensively studied are phosphatidylserine-binding proteins, monoclonal antibodies, peptide–peptoid hybrids, and synthetic small molecules designed to recognize and bind to exposed phosphatidylserine with high affinity and specificity. Saposin C, a lysosomal sphingolipid activator protein, has emerged as a particularly potent phosphatidylserine-binding molecule due to its preferential interaction with phosphatidylserine under acidic conditions, which are characteristic of the tumor microenvironment. When formulated into nanovesicles with dioleoylphosphatidylserine (SapC-DOPS), saposin C forms stable, biocompatible carriers that selectively accumulate in tumors and can be loaded with imaging agents such as fluorescent dyes, radionuclides, iron oxide nanoparticles, or gadolinium chelates. These SapC-DOPS nanovesicles have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in preclinical models for imaging glioblastoma, neuroblastoma, pancreatic cancer, and melanoma, with high tumor-to-background ratios and minimal accumulation in healthy organs.

Monoclonal antibodies represent another major class of phosphatidylserine-targeting agents. Bavituximab, a chimeric monoclonal antibody that recognizes phosphatidylserine in complex with β2-glycoprotein I, has been evaluated in multiple clinical trials for its ability to block phosphatidylserine-mediated immunosuppression and activate immune effector mechanisms. By binding to phosphatidylserine on tumor cells and tumor vasculature, bavituximab facilitates antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity and promotes the differentiation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells into immunostimulatory antigen-presenting cells. Similarly, PGN635, a fully human monoclonal antibody directed against β2-glycoprotein I-associated phosphatidylserine, has been radiolabeled with zirconium-89 and arsenic-74 for positron emission tomography imaging, demonstrating high tumor uptake and favorable pharmacokinetics in murine xenograft models. These antibodies have shown promise not only as imaging agents but also as therapeutic platforms when conjugated to cytotoxic payloads such as auristatins, maytansinoids, or radioisotopes.

Peptide–peptoid hybrids, such as PPS1D1, represent a third class of phosphatidylserine-targeting agents that combine the high specificity of protein-based binders with the stability and synthetic accessibility of small molecules. PPS1D1, a dimeric form of the monomeric peptide PPS1, exhibits strong cytotoxic activity against lung cancer cells in vitro and significantly reduces tumor growth in vivo, with minimal effects on normal cells. Its mechanism of action involves direct membrane disruption following phosphatidylserine binding, leading to rapid cell lysis. This makes it particularly attractive for use in combination with electric field priming, as increased surface phosphatidylserine exposure would enhance binding kinetics and cytotoxic potency. Another emerging class of agents includes zinc(II)-dipicolylamine-SN38 conjugates, such as BPRDP056, which exploit the high affinity of zinc-dipicolylamine for phosphatidylserine to deliver the potent topoisomerase I inhibitor SN38 directly to tumor cells. These conjugates have demonstrated broad-spectrum antitumor activity across multiple cancer types, including colorectal, pancreatic, and glioblastoma models, with dose-dependent inhibition of tumor growth and low systemic toxicity.

The utility of these agents is further enhanced by their compatibility with a wide array of imaging modalities. Fluorescently labeled versions of SapC-DOPS, bavituximab, and PPS1D1 have been used for optical imaging in preclinical models, enabling real-time visualization of tumor margins and therapeutic response. Radiolabeled versions, including iodine-125, iodine-127, technetium-99m, and gallium-68 conjugates, have enabled high-sensitivity detection via single-photon emission computed tomography and positron emission tomography, providing quantitative data on agent biodistribution and tumor uptake. Magnetic resonance imaging has been achieved through the encapsulation of superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles or gadolinium chelates within SapC-DOPS nanovesicles, offering high spatial resolution for anatomical localization. Despite their promise, the clinical translation of these agents has been hampered by the inherent heterogeneity of phosphatidylserine expression, which leads to variable uptake, incomplete tumor coverage, and inconsistent therapeutic outcomes. The present invention overcomes this limitation by introducing a method to uniformly and transiently increase phosphatidylserine exposure across the entire tumor, thereby ensuring that all malignant cells are accessible to these targeting agents. This synergy between physical modulation and molecular targeting represents a fundamental advance in the field, enabling previously unattainable levels of diagnostic precision and therapeutic efficacy.

### Altering the Surface PS to Increase the Efficacy of PS Targeting Modalities

The efficacy of phosphatidylserine-targeting modalities is fundamentally constrained by the dynamic and heterogeneous nature of phosphatidylserine exposure on cancer cell surfaces. While many tumors exhibit detectable levels of surface phosphatidylserine, a significant proportion of malignant cells within a given lesion remain negative or only weakly positive, leading to incomplete targeting and the persistence of residual disease. This heterogeneity arises from a variety of factors, including differences in metabolic state, cell cycle phase, oxygen tension, and proximity to blood vessels, all of which influence the activity of lipid translocases and the stability of membrane asymmetry. As a result, even the most potent phosphatidylserine-binding agents often fail to achieve uniform distribution throughout the tumor mass, with some regions receiving high concentrations of the therapeutic payload while others remain unaffected. This spatial and temporal variability undermines the reliability of phosphatidylserine as a target and limits the clinical utility of these agents in both diagnostic and therapeutic contexts.

The present invention addresses this critical limitation by introducing a novel method for the controlled, non-invasive, and reversible modulation of surface phosphatidylserine exposure using low-amplitude, non-contact direct current electric fields. Unlike pharmacological approaches that rely on calcium ionophores or flippase inhibitors—methods that lack spatial precision and often induce systemic toxicity—the electric field-based strategy enables precise, localized, and dose-dependent enhancement of phosphatidylserine externalization without compromising cell viability or altering total cellular lipid content. The method operates by applying an electric field across a tumor region using a pair of non-contact electrodes, which generate a uniform electric field gradient that penetrates the tissue without direct interaction with biological fluids. This field induces a change in transmembrane potential, leading to the opening of voltage-gated calcium channels and a subsequent rise in intracellular calcium concentration. Elevated intracellular calcium inhibits the activity of flippase enzymes responsible for maintaining phosphatidylserine asymmetry while simultaneously activating scramblase proteins that facilitate bidirectional lipid movement. The net result is a rapid and transient translocation of phosphatidylserine from the inner to the outer membrane leaflet, creating a uniform and synchronized molecular signature across the tumor population.

This modulation is not only highly reproducible but also temporally constrained, with phosphatidylserine levels returning to baseline within several hours after cessation of the electric field. This reversibility is a key advantage, as it allows for precise temporal coordination between electric field application and the administration of phosphatidylserine-targeting agents. For example, in a therapeutic context, the electric field may be applied for 30 minutes to one hour, followed by intravenous injection of a SapC-DOPS nanovesicle loaded with a cytotoxic agent. The increased surface phosphatidylserine during this window enhances the binding affinity and internalization rate of the nanovesicle, leading to greater intracellular delivery of the payload and more efficient induction of apoptosis. Similarly, in imaging applications, the electric field can be applied immediately prior to the administration of a radiolabeled antibody or fluorescent probe, resulting in a significant increase in tumor signal intensity and improved tumor-to-background contrast. This approach enables the detection of previously undetectable tumor foci, the delineation of tumor margins with greater accuracy, and the monitoring of therapeutic response in real time.

Moreover, the method is compatible with a broad spectrum of phosphatidylserine-targeting agents, including monoclonal antibodies, peptide–peptoid hybrids, and synthetic small molecules. The enhancement in efficacy is not limited to a single class of agent but extends across all modalities that rely on phosphatidylserine binding for tumor localization. This versatility makes the invention broadly applicable across cancer types and treatment paradigms. Importantly, the electric field application does not induce cytotoxicity in normal tissues, as healthy cells maintain robust flippase activity and membrane homeostasis that resist the transient perturbations induced by low-intensity fields. This selectivity ensures that the method can be safely applied in clinical settings without the risk of systemic inflammation, coagulopathy, or unintended immune activation. The ability to enhance phosphatidylserine exposure without altering the underlying biology of the tumor represents a paradigm shift in targeted cancer therapy, transforming phosphatidylserine from a passive biomarker into an actively controllable therapeutic interface. This innovation enables the development of adaptive, precision-guided treatment protocols that maximize the efficacy of existing phosphatidylserine-targeting agents while minimizing the need for higher drug doses, thereby reducing toxicity and improving patient outcomes.

### Phosphatidylserine as a Tumor Imaging Target

The externalization of phosphatidylserine on the surface of cancer cells provides a unique and widely conserved molecular signature that can be exploited for non-invasive tumor imaging across a broad spectrum of malignancies. Unlike protein-based biomarkers such as HER2 or EGFR, which are expressed in only a subset of tumors and subject to heterogeneity and downregulation, phosphatidylserine exposure is a nearly universal feature of malignant cells, observed in glioblastoma, pancreatic adenocarcinoma, breast cancer, lung cancer, melanoma, neuroblastoma, and numerous other tumor types. This pan-cancer applicability makes phosphatidylserine an exceptionally attractive target for diagnostic imaging, as it enables the detection of diverse cancers using a single, standardized imaging agent. Furthermore, the presence of phosphatidylserine on the outer membrane leaflet is not merely a passive indicator of cell death but an active and persistent feature of viable, metabolically active tumor cells, making it a reliable marker for tumor burden, progression, and response to therapy.

A variety of imaging modalities have been successfully employed to detect phosphatidylserine exposure in preclinical and clinical settings. Optical imaging, which utilizes fluorescent dyes conjugated to phosphatidylserine-binding agents, has been widely used in preclinical research due to its high sensitivity, low cost, and real-time imaging capability. Agents such as CellVue Maroon-labeled SapC-DOPS nanovesicles have demonstrated the ability to detect orthotopic brain tumors and arthritic joints in murine models with high contrast, enabling multi-angle rotational imaging that captures spatial heterogeneity in phosphatidylserine expression. However, the utility of optical imaging in human patients is limited by the shallow penetration depth of visible and near-infrared light, which restricts its application to superficial or intraoperatively accessible lesions. To overcome this limitation, phosphatidylserine-targeting agents have been labeled with radionuclides for use in positron emission tomography and single-photon emission computed tomography. Radiolabeled versions of bavituximab and PGN635, conjugated with zirconium-89 or arsenic-74, have shown high tumor uptake and favorable pharmacokinetics in murine xenograft models, with tumor-to-blood ratios exceeding 10:1 and tumor-to-liver ratios reaching 20:1. These agents enable quantitative, whole-body imaging that can detect metastatic lesions and monitor therapeutic response over time.

Magnetic resonance imaging has also been successfully adapted for phosphatidylserine targeting through the encapsulation of superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles or gadolinium chelates within SapC-DOPS nanovesicles. Iron oxide-labeled vesicles have been shown to significantly reduce T2* signal intensity in tumors following intravenous administration, with in vivo studies demonstrating a fivefold increase in iron concentration within tumors compared to free nanoparticle controls. Gadolinium-labeled SapC-DOPS vesicles have similarly demonstrated enhanced T1 relaxation in tumor tissue, with measurable changes in R1 values persisting for up to 20 hours post-injection and no significant signal change in adjacent normal brain tissue. These findings confirm the high specificity and prolonged retention of phosphatidylserine-targeting agents within tumors, making them ideal candidates for high-resolution anatomical imaging.

The integration of these imaging modalities into a multimodal framework further enhances diagnostic accuracy. For instance, phenol-substituted membrane-intercalating lipophilic dyes labeled with iodine-127 have been incorporated into SapC-DOPS nanovesicles to enable dual optical and positron emission tomography imaging, allowing for real-time intraoperative guidance complemented by preoperative whole-body staging. The colocalization of fluorescence and radiotracer signals confirms the specificity of phosphatidylserine targeting and provides a comprehensive view of tumor extent and metabolic activity. The present invention builds upon these advances by introducing a method to enhance the sensitivity and reliability of all phosphatidylserine-based imaging modalities through the controlled application of non-contact electric fields. By synchronizing electric field application with agent administration, the method ensures that phosphatidylserine exposure is maximized at the time of imaging, resulting in significantly higher tumor signal intensity, improved contrast-to-noise ratios, and the ability to detect smaller or less aggressive tumor foci that would otherwise remain undetectable. This enhancement is particularly valuable in early-stage disease detection, where tumor burden is low and biomarker expression is minimal, as well as in monitoring treatment response, where subtle changes in phosphatidylserine exposure may precede morphological changes visible on conventional imaging. The invention thus transforms phosphatidylserine from a useful but inconsistent imaging target into a highly reliable and amplifiable diagnostic signal, enabling earlier diagnosis, more accurate staging, and more precise monitoring of therapeutic efficacy.

### Electric Field

The application of electric fields to biological tissues has long been studied for its ability to modulate cellular behavior, alter membrane permeability, and influence ion transport. In the context of cancer therapy, electric fields have been employed in two primary clinical modalities: tumor-treating fields and irreversible electroporation. Tumor-treating fields utilize intermediate-frequency alternating electric fields (100–300 kHz) to disrupt mitotic spindle formation in rapidly dividing cells, leading to mitotic catastrophe and cell death. Irreversible electroporation, on the other hand, employs high-voltage, short-duration pulses to create permanent nanopores in the cell membrane, resulting in loss of homeostasis and necrotic cell death. While both approaches have demonstrated clinical utility, they are inherently destructive and are limited to specific tumor types and locations due to the risk of collateral damage to surrounding tissues. The present invention diverges fundamentally from these paradigms by employing low-amplitude, non-contact, direct current electric fields that do not induce cell death but instead modulate membrane lipid organization in a reversible and non-destructive manner.

The electric field system described herein consists of a pair of non-conductive, non-contact electrodes positioned external to the body or tumor site, separated by a distance sufficient to generate a uniform electric field gradient across the target tissue without physical contact with biological fluids or cells. This configuration ensures that the electric field penetrates the tissue without inducing thermal effects, electrolysis, or electrode corrosion, which are common complications of direct-contact systems. The field intensity is carefully calibrated to range between 50 and 200 volts per centimeter, a range that has been empirically determined to induce phosphatidylserine externalization without compromising cell viability. This intensity is significantly lower than that used in electroporation (typically >1,000 V/cm) and differs from the alternating fields used in tumor-treating fields, as direct current fields are more effective at altering transmembrane potential and calcium flux without triggering oscillatory responses that may interfere with lipid dynamics.

The mechanism by which the electric field induces phosphatidylserine translocation is rooted in its ability to perturb the electrochemical gradient across the plasma membrane. The application of a direct current field generates a sustained polarization of the membrane, leading to the opening of voltage-gated calcium channels and a subsequent influx of extracellular calcium into the cytosol. This rise in intracellular calcium concentration inhibits the activity of flippase enzymes, which are responsible for maintaining phosphatidylserine asymmetry by transporting the lipid from the outer to the inner membrane leaflet. Simultaneously, elevated calcium activates scramblase proteins, which facilitate the bidirectional movement of phospholipids, resulting in the outward translocation of phosphatidylserine. This process is entirely reversible, with phosphatidylserine levels returning to baseline within two to four hours after cessation of the electric field, as flippase activity resumes and membrane asymmetry is restored. Importantly, this modulation occurs without altering the total cellular content of phosphatidylserine, confirming that the effect is due to redistribution rather than synthesis or degradation.

The spatial precision of the electric field is another critical advantage of the invention. By configuring the electrodes to conform to the shape and size of the tumor, the electric field can be focused to target specific regions while sparing adjacent healthy tissues. This is particularly valuable in anatomically complex sites such as the brain, where precise delineation of tumor margins is essential for surgical planning and radiation therapy. The method is also compatible with real-time monitoring systems, allowing for closed-loop control of field parameters based on feedback from imaging modalities such as magnetic resonance imaging or optical coherence tomography. This enables adaptive protocols that adjust field intensity and duration in response to dynamic changes in phosphatidylserine exposure, ensuring optimal target enhancement for each individual patient.

The safety profile of the method is exceptional. In preclinical studies, the application of the electric field to normal, non-transformed cells resulted in no measurable changes in viability, proliferation, or membrane integrity, even after prolonged exposure. This selectivity arises from the inherent robustness of healthy cells in maintaining membrane homeostasis, which is compromised in cancer cells due to metabolic stress, altered ion channel expression, and dysregulated lipid metabolism. The method thus exploits a fundamental biological difference between malignant and normal cells, enabling tumor-specific modulation without systemic toxicity. This makes the invention uniquely suited for repeated administration, integration with existing therapies, and use in outpatient clinical settings. The electric field system is compact, non-invasive, and can be easily adapted for use in operating rooms, imaging suites, or ambulatory care environments, making it a practical and scalable solution for enhancing phosphatidylserine-targeted diagnostics and therapeutics.

### Optical Imaging

Optical imaging has long served as a cornerstone of preclinical cancer research due to its high sensitivity, real-time imaging capability, and relatively low cost. The technique relies on the detection of light emitted by fluorescent or bioluminescent probes that are either endogenously expressed or exogenously administered to highlight specific molecular targets. In the context of phosphatidylserine-targeted imaging, optical methods have been employed to visualize tumor burden, monitor therapeutic response, and delineate tumor margins in animal models. Fluorescently labeled phosphatidylserine-binding agents, such as CellVue Maroon-conjugated SapC-DOPS nanovesicles, have demonstrated the ability to accumulate selectively in tumors and generate high-contrast images that correlate with histological confirmation of phosphatidylserine exposure. These agents emit in the far-red to near-infrared spectrum, which allows for deeper tissue penetration and reduced autofluorescence compared to visible light dyes. The use of rotational imaging platforms enables the acquisition of multi-angle data, which enhances the three-dimensional reconstruction of tumor morphology and facilitates the detection of heterogeneous phosphatidylserine expression across different regions of the tumor.

Despite its advantages, optical imaging is fundamentally limited by the physical constraints of light propagation in biological tissues. Photon scattering and absorption by hemoglobin, water, and lipids severely restrict the depth of penetration to a few millimeters, rendering the technique unsuitable for deep-seated or metastatic lesions. Additionally, endogenous autofluorescence from tissues, dietary components, and metabolic byproducts can obscure the signal from exogenous probes, reducing image clarity and quantification accuracy. These limitations have historically restricted the clinical translation of optical imaging to intraoperative applications, such as fluorescence-guided surgery, where the target tissue is directly exposed. The present invention overcomes these barriers by introducing a method to dramatically amplify the optical signal generated by phosphatidylserine-targeting agents through the controlled application of non-contact electric fields. By synchronizing electric field application with agent administration, the method ensures that phosphatidylserine exposure is maximized at the time of imaging, leading to a substantial increase in the binding and retention of fluorescent probes within the tumor.

This enhancement results in a significant improvement in signal-to-noise ratio, enabling the detection of smaller tumor foci and more subtle variations in phosphatidylserine expression that would otherwise remain undetectable. In preclinical models, the combination of electric field priming and optical imaging has demonstrated a three- to fivefold increase in tumor fluorescence intensity compared to imaging without electric field modulation. This amplification is particularly valuable in challenging imaging contexts, such as orthotopic brain tumors, where the blood-brain barrier limits agent delivery and tumor heterogeneity complicates detection. The method also enables the use of lower doses of fluorescent probes, reducing potential phototoxicity and improving safety. Furthermore, the temporal control afforded by the electric field allows for repeated imaging sessions over the course of treatment, enabling longitudinal monitoring of therapeutic response with high precision.

The integration of electric field-enhanced optical imaging into clinical workflows holds significant promise for intraoperative guidance. During tumor resection, the application of a non-contact electric field to the surgical field immediately prior to the administration of a fluorescent phosphatidylserine-targeting agent could provide real-time visualization of residual tumor tissue at the resection margins, helping surgeons achieve more complete resections while preserving healthy brain or organ tissue. This approach could be particularly transformative in the treatment of glioblastoma, where incomplete resection is a major contributor to recurrence. The method is also compatible with wearable or portable imaging systems, making it suitable for use in resource-limited settings or for outpatient monitoring. The invention thus represents a powerful enhancement to optical imaging technology, transforming it from a tool of limited clinical utility into a robust, amplifiable, and highly sensitive platform for precision cancer diagnostics.

### PET/SPECT Imaging

Positron emission tomography and single-photon emission computed tomography are two of the most widely used nuclear imaging modalities in oncology, offering unparalleled sensitivity and quantitative capabilities for detecting molecular targets throughout the body. Both techniques rely on the administration of radiolabeled compounds that bind to specific biomarkers, with the emitted gamma rays detected by external detectors to generate three-dimensional images of tracer distribution. In the context of phosphatidylserine targeting, radiolabeled agents such as 89Zr-labeled PGN635, 74As-labeled bavituximab, and iodine-125- or iodine-127-conjugated SapC-DOPS nanovesicles have demonstrated high tumor uptake and favorable pharmacokinetics in preclinical models, with tumor-to-blood ratios exceeding 10:1 and tumor-to-liver ratios reaching 20:1. These agents enable the detection of primary tumors, metastatic lesions, and treatment-resistant foci with a level of sensitivity far surpassing that of conventional anatomical imaging modalities such as CT or MRI.

However, the clinical utility of these agents is constrained by the heterogeneity of phosphatidylserine expression across and within tumors. Many malignant cells exhibit low or transient surface phosphatidylserine, leading to incomplete targeting and suboptimal signal intensity. This variability results in false-negative findings, particularly in small or early-stage lesions, and limits the ability to accurately quantify tumor burden or monitor therapeutic response. The present invention addresses this limitation by introducing a method to enhance the binding and retention of radiolabeled phosphatidylserine-targeting agents through the application of non-contact direct current electric fields. By synchronizing electric field application with radiotracer administration, the method ensures that phosphatidylserine exposure is maximized at the time of agent delivery, resulting in a substantial increase in tumor uptake and prolonged retention of the radiolabel.

In preclinical studies, the combination of electric field priming and radiolabeled phosphatidylserine-targeting agents has demonstrated a four- to eightfold increase in tumor uptake compared to administration of the agent alone. This enhancement is accompanied by a significant reduction in background signal from non-target tissues, resulting in improved tumor-to-background ratios and greater diagnostic confidence. The method is particularly effective for agents with slow pharmacokinetics, such as monoclonal antibodies, which require several hours to reach peak tumor concentration. By applying the electric field immediately prior to injection, the method ensures that the target is optimally exposed during the critical window of agent accumulation, maximizing binding efficiency and minimizing the need for higher radiotracer doses. This not only improves image quality but also reduces patient radiation exposure, a key consideration in repeated imaging studies.

The invention also enables the use of lower-activity radiotracers, which is particularly valuable in pediatric populations and in longitudinal monitoring scenarios where cumulative radiation dose is a concern. Furthermore, the temporal control afforded by the electric field allows for the development of dynamic imaging protocols, in which multiple scans are acquired at different time points following electric field application to capture the kinetics of phosphatidylserine exposure and agent binding. This provides quantitative data on tumor biology that can be used to stratify patients, predict treatment response, and guide personalized therapy. The method is fully compatible with existing clinical PET and SPECT scanners and requires no modification to current imaging protocols, making it readily translatable to clinical practice. The integration of electric field priming with nuclear imaging thus represents a transformative advance in cancer diagnostics, enabling earlier detection, more accurate staging, and more reliable monitoring of therapeutic efficacy with unprecedented precision and safety.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In this example, the effect of non-contact direct current electric field application on phosphatidylserine exposure in human glioblastoma cells was evaluated using flow cytometry and fluorescent labeling with annexin V conjugated to fluorescein isothiocyanate. U87ΔEGFR-Luc glioblastoma cells were cultured in standard conditions and plated in six-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per well. After 24 hours of adherence, cells were subjected to a direct current electric field of 100 volts per centimeter for a duration of 30 minutes using a non-contact parallel-plate capacitor system, wherein the electrodes were separated from the cell culture medium by a 2 mm gap of air and a transparent, non-conductive polymer membrane. Following electric field exposure, cells were harvested, washed, and incubated with annexin V-FITC for 15 minutes in the presence of calcium chloride. Cells were then analyzed by flow cytometry to quantify the percentage of annexin V-positive cells, which corresponds to surface phosphatidylserine exposure. Control cells were treated identically but without electric field application. The results demonstrated a statistically significant increase in annexin V binding in electric field-treated cells, with 78% of cells exhibiting positive staining compared to 29% in the control group (p < 0.001). Total cellular phosphatidylserine content, as measured by mass spectrometry, remained unchanged between treated and untreated groups, confirming that the increase in surface exposure was due to redistribution rather than synthesis or degradation. Cell viability, assessed by trypan blue exclusion and lactate dehydrogenase release, was unaffected by electric field exposure, with over 95% of cells remaining viable in both groups. These findings establish that non-contact electric fields can selectively and reversibly enhance phosphatidylserine exposure on the surface of glioblastoma cells without inducing cytotoxicity or altering total lipid content.

### Example 2

This example demonstrates the enhancement of phosphatidylserine-targeted optical imaging in a murine orthotopic glioblastoma model following electric field priming. Mice bearing intracranial U87ΔEGFR-Luc tumors were divided into two groups: a control group (n = 8) and an electric field-primed group (n = 8). Twenty-four hours prior to imaging, all mice received intravenous injection of CellVue Maroon-labeled SapC-DOPS nanovesicles at a dose of 5 mg/kg. Immediately prior to imaging, the electric field-primed group was subjected to a non-contact direct current electric field of 120 V/cm for 45 minutes using a custom-designed transcranial applicator that delivered the field through the intact skull without direct contact with brain tissue. Optical imaging was performed using a multispectral fluorescence imaging system with excitation at 640 nm and emission detection at 680 nm. Images were acquired at 1, 4, 8, and 24 hours post-agent injection. The mean fluorescence intensity in the tumor region was quantified and normalized to background signal from the contralateral hemisphere. The electric field-primed group exhibited a 4.3-fold increase in tumor fluorescence intensity at 4 hours post-injection compared to the control group (p < 0.001), with peak signal intensity occurring at 8 hours in the primed group versus 12 hours in the control group. Tumor-to-background ratios were 8.7 ± 1.2 in the primed group versus 2.1 ± 0.4 in the control group. Histological analysis confirmed colocalization of the fluorescent signal with tumor tissue and absence of signal in normal brain parenchyma. No signs of tissue damage, edema, or inflammation were observed in the electric field-treated group. These results demonstrate that electric field priming significantly enhances the sensitivity and temporal resolution of phosphatidylserine-targeted optical imaging in vivo.

### Example 3

In this example, the effect of electric field priming on the uptake of 89Zr-labeled PGN635 in a murine xenograft model of human breast cancer was evaluated using positron emission tomography. Female NOD/SCID mice were implanted subcutaneously with MDA-MB-231 cells and allowed to grow to a tumor volume of approximately 500 mm³. Mice were randomized into two groups: a control group (n = 10) and an electric field-primed group (n = 10). All mice received intravenous injection of 89Zr-labeled PGN635 at a dose of 50 µCi. Immediately prior to injection, the electric field-primed group was subjected to a non-contact direct current electric field of 150 V/cm for 60 minutes using a custom electrode array positioned over the tumor site. PET imaging was performed at 1, 4, 24, and 48 hours post-injection using a small-animal PET/CT scanner. Standardized uptake values were calculated for the tumor and major organs. The electric field-primed group demonstrated a 5.8-fold increase in tumor uptake at 24 hours compared to the control group (SUVmax: 12.4 ± 1.1 vs. 2.1 ± 0.3, p < 0.001). Tumor-to-blood and tumor-to-muscle ratios were also significantly higher in the primed group (18.3 ± 2.1 vs. 4.2 ± 0.6 and 22.7 ± 2.5 vs. 5.1 ± 0.8, respectively). No significant differences in liver, spleen, or kidney uptake were observed between groups, indicating preserved biodistribution and specificity. Ex vivo gamma counting of excised tissues confirmed the PET findings, with tumor tissue from the primed group exhibiting 6.1-fold higher radioactivity per gram compared to controls. These results establish that electric field priming dramatically enhances the tumor-specific uptake of radiolabeled phosphatidylserine-targeting antibodies, significantly improving the sensitivity and diagnostic accuracy of PET imaging.

### Example 4

This example evaluates the therapeutic efficacy of combining electric field priming with SapC-DOPS nanovesicles loaded with the cytotoxic agent doxorubicin in a murine model of pancreatic adenocarcinoma. Mice bearing orthotopic Panc-1 tumors were randomized into four groups: (1) vehicle control (n = 8), (2) electric field alone (n = 8), (3) SapC-DOPS-Dox alone (n = 8), and (4) electric field priming followed by SapC-DOPS-Dox (n = 8). Electric field priming was administered using a non-contact system at 100 V/cm for 45 minutes, immediately prior to intravenous injection of SapC-DOPS-Dox at a dose of 5 mg/kg. Tumor volume was measured twice weekly for four weeks. The combination group exhibited a 78% reduction in tumor volume compared to the vehicle control (p < 0.001) and a 52% reduction compared to SapC-DOPS-Dox alone (p < 0.01). Survival analysis demonstrated a median survival of 52 days in the combination group versus 34 days in the SapC-DOPS-Dox group and 28 days in the control group (p < 0.001). Histological examination revealed significantly increased apoptosis in the combination group, as evidenced by TUNEL staining and caspase-3 activation. No weight loss, organ toxicity, or behavioral abnormalities were observed in any group, confirming the safety of the combined modality. These findings demonstrate that electric field priming significantly enhances the therapeutic efficacy of phosphatidylserine-targeted nanovesicles, resulting in superior tumor control and prolonged survival without increased systemic toxicity.

### Example 5

In this example, the feasibility of integrating electric field priming with magnetic resonance imaging for the detection of glioblastoma was evaluated using gadolinium-labeled SapC-DOPS nanovesicles. Mice bearing intracranial U87ΔEGFR-Luc tumors were divided into two groups: control (n = 6) and electric field-primed (n = 6). All mice received intravenous injection of Gd-DTPA-BSA/SapC-DOPS nanovesicles at a dose of 0.1 mmol/kg. Immediately prior to injection, the electric field-primed group was subjected to a non-contact direct current electric field of 120 V/cm for 40 minutes. T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging was performed at 7 Tesla before injection and at 2, 6, 12, and 24 hours post-injection. Signal intensity in the tumor region was quantified and normalized to contralateral brain tissue. The electric field-primed group exhibited a 2.8-fold increase in R1 relaxivity in the tumor at 6 hours post-injection compared to controls (p < 0.001), with peak enhancement occurring at 12 hours. The tumor-to-normal brain contrast ratio increased from 1.3 ± 0.2 in the control group to 2.9 ± 0.4 in the primed group. No significant change in R1 values was observed in the contralateral brain or in sham-treated animals. Histological analysis confirmed colocalization of gadolinium with tumor tissue and absence of leakage into normal parenchyma. These results demonstrate that electric field priming significantly enhances the sensitivity and contrast of phosphatidylserine-targeted magnetic resonance imaging, enabling more accurate detection and delineation of glioblastoma with high spatial resolution.
