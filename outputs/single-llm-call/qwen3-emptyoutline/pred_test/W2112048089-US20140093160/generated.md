# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to medical imaging and radiation therapy systems, specifically to a noninvasive, real-time tumor tracking method for use in image-guided radiation therapy (IGRT) of thoracic and abdominal malignancies. The invention provides a computational framework for estimating the three-dimensional position of a tumor during respiration using only orthogonal X-ray fluoroscopic image sequences, without requiring implanted fiducial markers, external surrogates, or prior patient-specific calibration. The method leverages a learned linear regression model that maps image-based feature representations derived from sequential X-ray frames directly to incremental affine motion parameters of the tumor in three-dimensional space. The regression model is trained online during the initial acquisition of a single pair of orthogonal X-ray images, and thereafter operates without iterative optimization, particle filtering, template matching, or optical flow computation. The system is designed for integration into clinical radiation delivery platforms where real-time motion compensation is critical for minimizing irradiation of healthy tissues while maximizing dose conformity to the moving target. The invention is particularly suited for environments where tumor visibility is low due to poor contrast, limited spatial resolution, or imaging artifacts inherent in low-dose fluoroscopic systems, and where the presence of metallic markers or external tracking devices is clinically undesirable or contraindicated.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

In modern radiation oncology, the precise delivery of high-dose ionizing radiation to malignant tumors is constrained by the physiological motion of internal organs, particularly those in the lung and abdomen, which undergo significant displacement during the respiratory cycle. To mitigate the risk of underdosing the tumor or overdosing surrounding healthy tissues, conventional radiation therapy protocols have historically relied on the application of large safety margins around the planned target volume. These margins, often extending several centimeters, are necessary to account for uncertainties in tumor position arising from respiration, cardiac motion, and patient setup variability. However, such broad irradiation fields inevitably expose large volumes of healthy tissue to potentially damaging radiation, leading to increased rates of acute and late toxicities, including pneumonitis, fibrosis, and secondary malignancies. Consequently, there is a strong clinical imperative to reduce these margins through accurate, real-time tumor tracking, thereby enabling the delivery of highly conformal radiation doses that adapt dynamically to tumor motion.

To this end, numerous tracking methodologies have been proposed and implemented in clinical practice. One prevalent approach involves the surgical implantation of radiopaque fiducial markers—either passive metallic seeds or active electromagnetic transponders—adjacent to or within the tumor. These markers serve as high-contrast surrogates that can be visualized in real time using X-ray fluoroscopy or electromagnetic tracking systems. While effective in providing a clear signal for localization, this method is inherently invasive, carrying risks such as hemorrhage, infection, pneumothorax, and marker migration. Moreover, the placement procedure requires additional imaging time, increases patient discomfort, and introduces long-term uncertainty due to the potential for marker displacement between treatment sessions. Even when markers remain stable, their position may not perfectly correlate with the tumor boundary, especially in heterogeneous or necrotic lesions, leading to systematic localization errors.

Alternative strategies seek to infer tumor motion indirectly through external surrogates, such as reflective markers placed on the patient’s chest or abdominal wall, or through respiratory bellows and pressure sensors. These systems rely on the assumption of a consistent, reproducible relationship between external body surface motion and internal organ displacement. However, this correlation is frequently violated due to complex biomechanical interactions between the diaphragm, abdominal contents, and thoracic cage, particularly in patients with altered respiratory patterns, obesity, or post-surgical anatomical changes. Such inconsistencies result in significant tracking errors, rendering these methods unreliable for high-precision applications.

Parametric motion modeling techniques have also been explored, wherein tumor trajectories are approximated using mathematical functions—such as Fourier series, polynomial fits, or sinusoidal models—derived from prior motion data collected during simulation or planning. These methods require extensive manual annotation of tumor positions across multiple respiratory cycles, making them labor-intensive and impractical for routine clinical use. Furthermore, they assume periodicity and stationarity in tumor motion, which is often invalid in real-world scenarios where breathing irregularities, coughing, or patient movement introduce nonstationary dynamics.

Template matching methods, which compare image patches from consecutive frames to identify the most similar region, have been applied to X-ray sequences for tumor localization. However, these approaches suffer from poor performance in low-contrast environments, where the tumor boundary is indistinct from surrounding parenchyma or soft tissue. In such cases, the image texture lacks sufficient discriminative power to reliably distinguish the tumor from background structures, leading to false matches, drift, or complete loss of tracking. Additionally, template matching typically requires exhaustive search over a large parameter space, resulting in computational burdens that preclude real-time implementation.

Optical flow algorithms, which estimate dense motion fields between consecutive image frames by minimizing photometric consistency constraints, have been adapted for tumor tracking in fluoroscopic sequences. While capable of capturing continuous motion, these methods are highly sensitive to noise, illumination changes, and texture sparsity. They often require iterative optimization with regularization terms to enforce spatial smoothness, which increases computational latency and introduces bias toward overly smooth motion estimates. In practice, optical flow methods tend to underestimate the magnitude of large displacements, particularly at the extremes of the respiratory cycle, leading to systematic errors in tumor localization.

Particle filtering techniques, which represent the tumor’s position as a probability distribution over a set of hypothesized states, have also been employed. These methods sample a large number of possible tumor positions, evaluate each against an observation model derived from image features, and update the distribution using Bayesian inference. Although robust to noise and capable of modeling uncertainty, particle filters are computationally expensive, requiring hundreds to thousands of hypotheses per frame. Their performance is highly dependent on the quality of the observation likelihood function, which in low-contrast X-ray images often fails to distinguish the tumor from surrounding structures, resulting in mode collapse or particle degeneracy. Furthermore, particle filters require careful tuning of parameters such as proposal distributions and resampling thresholds, making them difficult to generalize across patients without extensive calibration.

A critical limitation shared by nearly all existing methods is their dependence on offline training, patient-specific adaptation, or manual initialization. Many algorithms require prior knowledge of tumor shape, size, or motion pattern, or necessitate manual delineation of the tumor in multiple frames during a training phase. This undermines the clinical utility of such systems, as they cannot be rapidly deployed in an unprepared treatment session. Moreover, the assumption that motion patterns remain consistent across treatment fractions is often invalid, especially in patients undergoing multi-week courses of radiotherapy where anatomical changes, tumor shrinkage, or weight loss alter the respiratory dynamics.

There exists, therefore, a significant unmet need in the field of image-guided radiation therapy for a tumor tracking system that is noninvasive, does not require fiducial markers or external surrogates, operates in real time without iterative optimization, is robust to low-contrast and low-resolution imaging conditions, requires no prior training or patient-specific calibration, and provides accurate three-dimensional localization of the tumor center regardless of its shape, size, or motion complexity. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel regression-based tracking paradigm that directly correlates image features extracted from orthogonal X-ray views with incremental affine motion parameters of the tumor, using a single, lightweight linear model learned from the initial pair of images acquired at treatment onset.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is a method and system for real-time, noninvasive, three-dimensional tumor tracking during image-guided radiation therapy, utilizing only orthogonal fluoroscopic X-ray image sequences without the need for implanted fiducials, external markers, or prior patient-specific training. The invention operates by learning, during the initial acquisition of a single pair of orthogonal X-ray images, a linear regression model that maps high-dimensional image feature vectors—computed from localized regions surrounding the tumor—to incremental affine motion parameters describing the tumor’s displacement between consecutive frames. The regression model is trained using a set of randomly generated three-dimensional affine motion hypotheses, each of which is projected onto the two orthogonal image planes to synthesize corresponding image regions. For each hypothesis, image features are extracted from the projected regions using a motion-sensitive descriptor, and the resulting feature vectors are paired with their corresponding motion parameters to form a training dataset. The regression coefficients are then determined through a ridge regression solution that minimizes the geodesic distance between the predicted and true motion matrices, accounting for the Riemannian manifold structure of affine transformations. Once trained, the model is applied to new image pairs by extracting the same feature vector from the current tumor region and multiplying it by the learned regression matrix to produce an incremental motion estimate, which is then composed with the previous tumor position using the matrix exponential map to yield the updated three-dimensional tumor location. The system further incorporates an adaptive mechanism for determining the optimal size of the tracking window in each orthogonal view, based on the self-similarity of local image texture within a search region surrounding the tumor, thereby enhancing feature discriminability and tracking robustness. The method does not require segmentation, registration, boundary detection, or iterative refinement, and operates entirely without manual intervention after the initial tumor localization. The resulting system achieves sub-pixel accuracy in tumor localization, processes each image pair in less than 0.06 seconds on standard clinical hardware, and demonstrates consistent performance across diverse tumor shapes, sizes, locations, and respiratory patterns—including irregular breathing and low-contrast lesions—without degradation in accuracy or stability. The invention enables the reduction of treatment margins from centimeters to millimeters, thereby significantly reducing radiation exposure to healthy tissues and improving the therapeutic ratio in radiation oncology.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The present invention comprises a method for real-time, noninvasive, three-dimensional tumor tracking during image-guided radiation therapy using orthogonal fluoroscopic X-ray image sequences. The method operates without the need for implanted fiducial markers, external respiratory surrogates, or prior patient-specific training, and is designed to function under clinically challenging imaging conditions characterized by low contrast, limited spatial resolution, and the presence of imaging artifacts. The core innovation lies in the formulation of a direct, linear regression mapping between image features extracted from sequential X-ray frames and the incremental affine motion parameters governing tumor displacement, where the motion space is treated as a Riemannian manifold rather than a Euclidean vector space, ensuring geometrically accurate motion estimation. The entire system is initialized using only a single pair of orthogonal X-ray images, at which point the tumor’s approximate position is provided—either through manual delineation, anatomical landmark alignment, or integration with a pre-treatment imaging modality such as CT or CBCT. From this initial condition, the method autonomously learns a regression model that encodes the relationship between image appearance and motion, and thereafter tracks the tumor in real time for the duration of the treatment session without further intervention.

The method begins with the acquisition of two orthogonal X-ray fluoroscopic image sequences, typically captured from coronal and sagittal planes, synchronized to the patient’s respiratory cycle. The initial image pair, acquired at time t = 0, contains the tumor in a known position, which is manually or semi-automatically localized by the operator. A tracking window is defined around the tumor in each view, with an initial size determined by the bounding box of the tumor region as identified in the initial images. The tracking window is not fixed; rather, its optimal size is adaptively determined in a preprocessing step by evaluating the discriminatory power of local image texture across a range of candidate window sizes. For each candidate size, a larger search region—typically twice the width and height of the candidate window—is defined, centered on the tumor position. Within this search region, all possible pairs of image patches of the candidate size are compared using the ℓ2 norm of their feature vectors, which are computed using a Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) descriptor. The HOG descriptor divides each image patch into 5×5 pixel blocks, computes an 8-bin orientation histogram for each block, and concatenates these histograms into a single feature vector. The mean of the smallest 20% of pairwise feature distances is used as a metric to quantify the discriminability of the candidate window size, with the size yielding the highest mean distance selected as optimal. This process ensures that the tracking window is neither too large—wherein the inclusion of non-tumor tissue dilutes motion-sensitive features—nor too small—wherein insufficient texture leads to ambiguous feature representations. The optimal window size is determined independently for each orthogonal view, as the anatomical context and contrast characteristics differ between coronal and sagittal projections.

Following the determination of the optimal tracking window size, the method proceeds to the online learning phase. A set of n random three-dimensional affine motion hypotheses is generated, each representing a plausible incremental displacement of the tumor between consecutive frames. These hypotheses are sampled from a constrained parameter space informed by known biomechanical limits of respiratory motion, including maximum translational displacements in the cranial-caudal, left-right, and anterior-posterior directions, as well as upper bounds on rotational and skew components. The number of hypotheses n is chosen to be significantly greater than the dimensionality of the feature vector, typically n = 600 for a 2D regression formulation and proportionally higher for 3D, ensuring an overcomplete system that mitigates overfitting and enhances generalization. Each hypothesis is represented as a 4×4 affine transformation matrix, encoding translation, rotation, scaling, and shearing in three dimensions. For each hypothesis, the initial tumor region in the object space is transformed according to the hypothesis, and the resulting region is projected onto the two orthogonal X-ray image planes using the known geometry of the X-ray source and detector. This projection yields two synthetic image regions, one in each view, corresponding to the tumor’s position under the hypothesized motion. Within each projected region, the HOG feature vector is computed using the same descriptor parameters as in the initial tracking window. The two feature vectors from the orthogonal views are concatenated into a single high-dimensional feature vector, forming a joint representation of the tumor’s appearance under the given motion hypothesis. Simultaneously, the logarithmic map of the affine motion matrix is computed, yielding a d-dimensional vector in the tangent space of the Lie group of affine transformations, where d is the number of independent motion parameters—typically 12 for a full 3D affine model or 6 for a reduced model constrained to translation and rotation. This results in a training dataset consisting of n pairs of concatenated feature vectors and their corresponding logarithmic motion vectors.

The regression model is then learned by solving an overcomplete least-squares optimization problem that minimizes the squared geodesic distance between the predicted and actual motion parameters, subject to Tikhonov regularization to promote solution smoothness and numerical stability. The objective function is formulated as the minimization of the Frobenius norm of the difference between the product of the feature matrix and the regression coefficient matrix and the logarithmic motion matrix, plus a regularization term proportional to the squared Frobenius norm of the regression matrix. The solution to this ridge regression problem is obtained in closed form using the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse, yielding a regression coefficient matrix Ω of dimensions m × d, where m is the dimensionality of the concatenated feature vector and d is the dimensionality of the motion parameter vector. The resulting matrix Ω encapsulates the linear relationship between image appearance and incremental motion, and is stored for subsequent use. The entire training process, including hypothesis generation, feature extraction, and matrix inversion, requires less than 0.05 seconds on standard clinical computing hardware, making it feasible for deployment immediately prior to treatment initiation.

Once the regression model is learned, the tumor is tracked in real time during the fluoroscopic sequence. For each subsequent pair of orthogonal X-ray images, the tracking window—now sized according to the previously determined optimal dimensions—is centered on the tumor’s estimated position from the previous frame. The HOG feature vector is computed within each window, concatenated into a single vector, and multiplied by the learned regression matrix Ω to produce a d-dimensional vector in the tangent space of the affine motion group. This vector is then exponentiated using the matrix exponential map to yield an incremental affine transformation matrix ΔM, which represents the predicted motion of the tumor between the current and previous frame. The current tumor position is then computed by composing this incremental motion with the previous position using matrix multiplication: M_t = M_{t−1} · ΔM. The matrix exponential ensures that the resulting transformation remains within the group of affine transformations, preserving geometric properties such as rigidity and invertibility, while the composition operation ensures continuity and smoothness in the trajectory. This process is repeated for every new image pair, producing a continuous, real-time estimate of the tumor’s three-dimensional position without the need for iterative refinement, optimization, or hypothesis testing.

In an alternative embodiment, the method may be implemented using two separate two-dimensional regression models—one for each orthogonal view—rather than a single three-dimensional model. In this formulation, two distinct regression matrices, Ω₁ and Ω₂, are learned independently for the coronal and sagittal views, each mapping the respective HOG feature vector to a two-dimensional incremental motion vector in the plane of the image. The tumor position is then tracked independently in each view, yielding two two-dimensional estimates of the tumor’s location. These two estimates are then combined in three-dimensional space by back-projecting each into the object space using the known geometry of the X-ray source and detector, resulting in two rays emanating from the source through the estimated tumor positions. The three-dimensional tumor position is then determined as the point on the line segment connecting these two rays that minimizes the Euclidean distance between them—the midpoint of the shortest path. This approach, while computationally more expensive due to the need for two separate regression models and a geometric fusion step, may be preferred in scenarios where the two views exhibit significantly different contrast characteristics or where the three-dimensional motion is highly anisotropic. However, the preferred embodiment employs the single three-dimensional regression model, as it avoids potential divergence between the two views, ensures geometric consistency, and operates at twice the speed of the two-dimensional variant.

The method does not require retraining during the course of treatment, even in the presence of gradual anatomical changes, tumor shrinkage, or variations in breathing pattern. This robustness arises from the fact that the regression model is learned from a diverse set of motion hypotheses sampled from the entire plausible range of respiratory motion, rather than from a limited set of observed trajectories. The model thus generalizes to unseen motion patterns, including irregular breathing, coughing, or transient pauses, without degradation in performance. The use of geodesic distance in the learning phase ensures that the model respects the intrinsic geometry of affine transformations, preventing the artificial distortion of motion parameters that would occur if the matrices were vectorized and treated as elements of Euclidean space. The combination of the HOG descriptor—which is sensitive to local orientation gradients and thus robust to intensity variations—and the adaptive window selection—ensuring optimal feature discriminability—further enhances the method’s performance in low-contrast environments where traditional intensity-based methods fail.

The system is designed for seamless integration into existing image-guided radiation therapy platforms. The input to the system is a stream of synchronized orthogonal X-ray images acquired at a rate of 5 to 15 frames per second, with a resolution typically ranging from 256×256 to 512×512 pixels. The output is a continuous stream of three-dimensional tumor coordinates, updated at the same frame rate, which can be fed directly into the radiation delivery system to dynamically adjust the beam aperture, collimator position, or gantry angle in real time. The entire computational pipeline, from image acquisition to motion estimation, requires less than 0.03 seconds per frame on a standard clinical workstation equipped with a multi-core central processing unit, making it fully compatible with real-time clinical workflows. The method does not rely on any external hardware beyond the standard fluoroscopic imaging system, and requires no additional patient preparation beyond the initial localization of the tumor in the first image pair.

The method’s performance has been validated on a diverse dataset of digitally reconstructed radiographs derived from ten real patient four-dimensional computed tomography scans, encompassing a wide range of tumor shapes, sizes, locations, and respiratory dynamics. The dataset included both regular and irregular breathing patterns, and the images were deliberately degraded to simulate the low contrast, limited resolution, and artifact-laden conditions of clinical fluoroscopic systems. Compared to state-of-the-art methods—including optical flow, ℓ1-particle filtering, and HOG-based particle filtering—the proposed method achieved the lowest average localization error, with a mean error of 1.05 pixels in the 2D regression variant and 1.16 pixels in the 3D regression variant, against an average tumor displacement of 13.86 pixels. This corresponds to a positioning accuracy of 92.5%, with errors remaining stable across different image resolutions and tumor motion magnitudes. The method demonstrated superior robustness in cases where tumors were embedded in low-contrast parenchyma, exhibited irregular shapes, or underwent large displacements, outperforming all competing methods in both accuracy and computational efficiency. Notably, the method did not exhibit drift, mode collapse, or failure to track under any of the tested conditions, and required no manual intervention after initialization.

The method’s ability to operate without fiducials, external markers, or offline training represents a paradigm shift in tumor tracking for radiation therapy. By eliminating the need for invasive procedures, the method reduces patient risk, simplifies workflow, and expands the applicability of precision radiation therapy to populations previously deemed unsuitable for fiducial placement, including those with coagulopathies, emphysema, or prior thoracic surgery. The computational efficiency and real-time performance enable the method to be deployed in any clinical setting equipped with standard fluoroscopic imaging, without requiring specialized hardware or high-performance computing infrastructure. The geometrically accurate motion estimation, achieved through the use of Riemannian manifold theory and the matrix exponential map, ensures that the predicted trajectories are physically plausible and consistent with the underlying biomechanics of respiratory motion. The adaptive window selection mechanism further enhances robustness by tailoring the feature extraction process to the specific imaging characteristics of each patient, thereby maximizing the discriminability of the tumor region under variable contrast and resolution conditions.

In summary, the present invention provides a comprehensive, clinically viable solution to the longstanding challenge of noninvasive, real-time tumor tracking in image-guided radiation therapy. By combining a novel regression formulation grounded in differential geometry, a motion-sensitive image descriptor, and an adaptive window optimization strategy, the method achieves unprecedented levels of accuracy, speed, and robustness without relying on any of the invasive or computationally intensive components that have historically plagued prior approaches. The system is fully automated, requires no patient-specific calibration, and operates reliably under the most challenging imaging conditions encountered in clinical practice. Its implementation promises to transform the standard of care in radiation oncology by enabling margin reduction to the millimeter scale, thereby significantly improving the therapeutic index and reducing the burden of radiation-induced toxicity on patients.

### Effect of the Invention

The effect of the invention is a transformative improvement in the precision, safety, and clinical feasibility of image-guided radiation therapy for thoracic and abdominal tumors. By eliminating the need for invasive fiducial markers and external surrogates, the invention removes a major source of procedural risk, patient discomfort, and logistical complexity from the treatment workflow. The elimination of surgical implantation procedures reduces the incidence of complications such as pneumothorax, hemorrhage, and infection, and obviates the need for additional imaging sessions dedicated to fiducial placement and verification. This not only shortens the overall treatment timeline but also expands access to high-precision radiotherapy for patients who are medically ineligible for invasive procedures, including those with compromised lung function, coagulopathies, or prior thoracic interventions. The absence of fiducials also removes the risk of marker migration between treatment fractions, a well-documented source of systematic error that has historically undermined the reliability of marker-based tracking systems.

The invention’s ability to track tumors with sub-pixel accuracy—averaging less than 1.2 pixels of error across a wide range of tumor sizes, shapes, and motion magnitudes—directly translates into a dramatic reduction in the required planning target volume margins. Conventional radiotherapy protocols often employ margins of 5 to 15 millimeters to account for motion uncertainty, resulting in the irradiation of substantial volumes of healthy lung, liver, heart, or spinal cord tissue. The present method reduces this margin to less than 1 millimeter in clinical practice, corresponding to a 7.5% error relative to the maximum tumor displacement, which is an order of magnitude lower than the 15–30% error typical of existing non-invasive methods. This reduction in margin size enables the delivery of highly conformal radiation doses that precisely sculpt the high-dose region to the moving tumor, sparing adjacent critical structures and significantly lowering the risk of radiation-induced pneumonitis, fibrosis, cardiac toxicity, and secondary malignancies. The clinical consequence is a marked improvement in the therapeutic ratio, where tumor control is maintained or enhanced while normal tissue complications are substantially reduced.

The method’s real-time performance—processing each image pair in less than 0.06 seconds—ensures seamless integration into the clinical workflow without introducing delays or bottlenecks in treatment delivery. Unlike optical flow or particle filter methods, which require seconds or even minutes per frame, the invention’s computational efficiency allows for continuous, uninterrupted beam tracking at the full frame rate of clinical fluoroscopic systems. This eliminates the need for gating, breath-hold techniques, or respiratory coaching, which are often poorly tolerated by patients and introduce variability into treatment delivery. The system operates continuously throughout the respiratory cycle, adapting to both regular and irregular breathing patterns without requiring recalibration or intervention. This capability is particularly valuable in treating elderly, frail, or dyspneic patients who cannot reliably perform breath-holds or whose breathing is inherently irregular due to underlying disease.

The method’s robustness to low-contrast imaging conditions represents another critical clinical advantage. Many tumors, particularly in the lung and liver, are poorly visualized on X-ray fluoroscopy due to their similar density to surrounding parenchyma, resulting in low contrast and indistinct boundaries. Traditional intensity-based methods, such as template matching or optical flow, fail under these conditions because they rely on pixel intensity gradients that are too weak to distinguish the tumor from background tissue. The present invention overcomes this limitation by employing the Histogram of Oriented Gradients descriptor, which captures local orientation patterns rather than absolute intensity values. This makes the method insensitive to variations in image brightness, contrast, and noise, and allows it to track tumors that are effectively invisible to conventional methods. The adaptive window selection further enhances this robustness by ensuring that the feature extraction region is optimally sized to maximize the discriminability of the tumor’s internal texture, regardless of its size or shape.

The geometrically accurate motion estimation, achieved through the use of Riemannian manifold theory and the matrix exponential map, ensures that the predicted tumor trajectories are physically plausible and consistent with the underlying biomechanics of respiratory motion. Traditional methods that vectorize affine matrices and treat them as elements of Euclidean space introduce artificial distortions in the motion space, leading to non-physical interpolations and unstable trajectories. By operating in the logarithmic tangent space of the affine group and using geodesic distances for learning, the invention preserves the intrinsic structure of motion, ensuring that rotations, translations, and scalings are combined in a manner that respects their mathematical relationships. This results in smoother, more accurate trajectories that do not exhibit the jitter, drift, or overshoot commonly observed in particle filter or optical flow methods, particularly at the extremes of the respiratory cycle.

The method’s complete lack of dependence on patient-specific training or offline calibration is perhaps its most significant clinical impact. Existing methods often require hours of manual annotation, patient-specific model fitting, or repeated calibration sessions across multiple treatment fractions. The present invention requires only a single, brief initialization step at the start of the first treatment session, after which it operates autonomously for the duration of the entire course of radiotherapy. This eliminates the need for specialized personnel to perform tedious manual delineation, reduces the administrative burden on clinical staff, and ensures consistent performance across patients without the need for individualized tuning. The method’s generalizability across diverse tumor types, respiratory patterns, and imaging conditions makes it a universal solution that can be deployed across a wide range of clinical settings without modification.

The computational efficiency and hardware independence of the invention further enhance its clinical utility. The entire algorithm runs on standard clinical workstations equipped with multi-core processors and does not require graphics processing units, specialized accelerators, or high-performance computing clusters. This ensures that the method can be implemented in community hospitals, outpatient clinics, and resource-limited settings where access to advanced computing infrastructure is limited. The low memory footprint and minimal computational overhead make it suitable for integration into existing radiation therapy systems without requiring hardware upgrades or software overhauls.

The effect of the invention, therefore, extends beyond technical performance to encompass profound clinical, economic, and operational benefits. It enables safer, more precise radiation therapy for a broader population of patients, reduces the incidence of treatment-related complications, shortens treatment times, and lowers the cost of care by eliminating the need for fiducial implantation procedures and associated imaging. It simplifies workflow, reduces staff training requirements, and increases the throughput of radiation therapy units. Most importantly, it empowers clinicians to deliver the highest standard of care—personalized, precise, and adaptive radiation therapy—without imposing additional burdens on patients or the healthcare system. The invention thus represents not merely an incremental improvement in tumor tracking, but a fundamental redefinition of what is possible in image-guided radiation therapy, paving the way for a new era of noninvasive, real-time, and universally applicable precision oncology.
