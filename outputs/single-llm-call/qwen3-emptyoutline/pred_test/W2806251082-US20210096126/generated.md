# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of biological tissue engineering and in vitro disease modeling has long sought methods to precisely track, identify, and monitor individual biological constructs within complex experimental environments. Traditional approaches to distinguishing between multiple tissue samples—particularly those derived from genetically distinct human donors—have relied heavily on manual labeling, spatial segregation, or molecular barcoding techniques such as fluorescent reporters, genetic tags, or barcode sequencing. While these methods have provided valuable insights, they are inherently limited in scalability, durability, and compatibility with long-term culture, cryopreservation, and high-throughput screening workflows. Fluorescent markers, for instance, photobleach over time, are subject to spectral overlap in multiplexed assays, and cannot be read without direct optical access. Genetic barcodes require destructive RNA or DNA extraction and sequencing, rendering them incompatible with live-cell or longitudinal studies. Spatial separation, though effective in small-scale experiments, becomes logistically unfeasible when hundreds or thousands of organoids or tissue constructs must be maintained and analyzed simultaneously under identical environmental conditions.

The emergence of radio frequency identification (RFID) technology as a non-optical, wireless, and passive data storage and retrieval system has opened new avenues for object identification in industrial, logistical, and medical contexts. RFID tags, composed of a microchip and an antenna, derive power from incident electromagnetic fields emitted by a reader device, enabling them to transmit stored information without an internal power source. These tags have been successfully miniaturized to dimensions on the order of tens to hundreds of micrometers, making them theoretically compatible with cellular and subcellular integration. However, prior applications of RFID in biological systems have been largely confined to macroscopic contexts—such as implantable tags in livestock or ingestible pills for medication adherence—and have not been adapted for integration into three-dimensional, self-organizing, living tissue constructs. Attempts to embed RFID microchips into cells or tissues have encountered significant barriers, including mechanical disruption of cellular architecture, interference with physiological signaling, cytotoxicity from chip materials, and failure of the chip to remain functional under the dynamic biochemical and physical conditions inherent to tissue culture environments. Moreover, no prior method has demonstrated the ability to integrate an RFID tag into a developing organoid during its self-assembly phase without compromising its structural integrity, polarization, or functional output.

Organoids, three-dimensional multicellular structures derived from stem cells and capable of recapitulating key architectural and functional features of native organs, represent a transformative platform for modeling human development, disease, and drug response. Their ability to self-organize through cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions into polarized, lumen-containing structures mimics in vivo tissue organization with unprecedented fidelity. This self-assembly process, driven by intrinsic developmental programs, results in the formation of complex, reproducible tissue architectures that are highly sensitive to perturbations. Any external agent introduced during this process—especially a rigid, non-biological object such as a microchip—must navigate the delicate balance between physical integration and biological compatibility. Previous studies attempting to introduce foreign objects into organoids have resulted in disrupted epithelial polarity, aberrant lumen formation, loss of cell viability, or altered gene expression profiles, thereby invalidating the organoid as a physiologically relevant model. Consequently, the integration of digital tracking technology into organoids has remained an unmet challenge, despite the clear need for scalable, non-destructive, and persistent identification methods to support large-scale biobanking, phenotypic screening, and precision medicine applications.

Furthermore, the long-term utility of organoid-based models is constrained by the absence of robust, standardized methods for cryopreservation and revival without loss of identity or function. Current cryopreservation protocols for organoids often lead to structural collapse, reduced viability, and inconsistent post-thaw functionality, particularly when multiple lines are pooled together. Even when viability is preserved, the inability to unambiguously distinguish between donor-derived organoids after thawing renders pooled screening experiments impractical. Manual tracking via visual inspection or fluorescent labeling is not only labor-intensive but also prone to error, especially when organoids are embedded in extracellular matrix gels or when multiple organoids are in close proximity. The need for a durable, non-optical, and chemically inert identifier that survives freezing, thawing, fixation, and exposure to solvents, pH fluctuations, and sterilization procedures has remained unresolved.

The present invention addresses these longstanding limitations by introducing a novel methodology for the seamless, non-disruptive integration of ultracompact RFID microchips into developing human organoids during their self-assembly phase. The innovation lies not merely in the physical insertion of a microchip, but in the strategic timing and mechanistic context of its incorporation—leveraging the natural aggregation and cavitation processes of organoid formation to encapsulate the chip within the lumenal space without mechanical trauma or biochemical interference. This approach ensures that the chip becomes an intrinsic, non-invasive component of the organoid, embedded in a manner that preserves epithelial polarization, cell-cell communication, and functional output. The resulting digitalized organoids, termed RiOs, retain full biological fidelity, exhibit normal gene expression profiles, maintain secretory and transport functions, survive cryopreservation and reanimation, and remain wirelessly readable under a wide range of environmental conditions—including exposure to ethanol, paraffin embedding, autoclaving, and liquid nitrogen immersion. This invention thus represents the first successful fusion of passive digital tracking technology with a living, self-organizing human tissue model, enabling unprecedented capabilities in high-throughput phenotyping, donor identification, biobanking, and longitudinal monitoring of tissue behavior in both in vitro and in vivo contexts.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method for the integration of a miniature radio frequency identification (RFID) microchip into a developing three-dimensional biological tissue construct, such as an organoid, without compromising its structural integrity, cellular viability, or physiological function. The method comprises the step of introducing one or more ultracompact RFID microchips into a suspension of dissociated progenitor cells prior to their re-aggregation under conditions that promote self-assembly into a polarized, lumen-containing tissue structure. During this self-assembly process, the microchip becomes passively and permanently encapsulated within the central lumen of the forming tissue, thereby achieving seamless integration without mechanical disruption or biochemical interference. The RFID microchip, sized between 100 and 600 micrometers in at least two dimensions and comprising a passive integrated circuit and a coiled antenna, is configured to operate wirelessly at frequencies compatible with near-field communication protocols, enabling data retrieval from a distance of up to two millimeters without requiring direct optical or physical contact.

The resulting tissue construct, referred to herein as a radio frequency identification-integrated organoid or RiO, retains all essential characteristics of its non-integrated counterpart, including expression of lineage-specific markers, secretion of organ-specific proteins, establishment of apicobasal polarity, formation of functional luminal compartments, and responsiveness to pharmacological and metabolic stimuli. The RiO demonstrates exceptional durability under conditions routinely encountered in biological research, including prolonged exposure to aqueous and organic solvents, repeated cycles of freezing and thawing at temperatures as low as −196°C, autoclaving, paraffin embedding, and exposure to extreme pH environments. The RFID microchip remains fully functional after these treatments, permitting reliable wireless identification and data retrieval even from fixed, embedded, or cryopreserved tissue samples. The invention further encompasses a system and method for high-throughput phenotypic screening of RiOs, wherein multiple RiOs are pooled together in a single culture vessel, subjected to a phenotypic stimulus such as a metabolic challenge or drug exposure, and then passed through a detection apparatus that simultaneously captures fluorescence-based phenotypic readouts and wirelessly reads the unique electronic product code (EPC) stored in each RiO’s embedded microchip. This dual-readout capability enables the unambiguous, automated, and high-speed correlation of phenotypic outcomes with donor-specific genetic backgrounds, thereby enabling forward cellomic screening at unprecedented scale and precision.

The invention further provides a method for biobanking and long-term storage of RiOs, wherein the digital identity encoded in the RFID microchip remains intact and readable after cryopreservation and thawing, eliminating the need for manual labeling, genetic barcoding, or spatial segregation. This allows for the efficient pooling of hundreds or thousands of RiOs derived from diverse genetic backgrounds into a single storage container, with each individual RiO being uniquely identifiable upon retrieval. The invention further enables the application of RiOs in precision medicine contexts, wherein patient-derived RiOs can be generated, digitally tagged, stored, and later reactivated for drug response testing, with their identity and prior phenotypic history permanently recorded via the embedded microchip. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the management and analysis of complex biological tissues, transforming them from static, visually identifiable samples into dynamic, digitally traceable entities capable of storing and transmitting information across time, space, and experimental conditions.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Definitions

For the purposes of this disclosure, the term “organoid” refers to a three-dimensional, self-organizing, multicellular tissue structure derived from stem cells—either pluripotent or adult—and capable of recapitulating key structural, functional, and molecular features of a specific organ or tissue type. Organoids are formed through controlled differentiation and self-assembly processes that mimic embryonic development, resulting in the formation of polarized epithelial layers, luminal compartments, and specialized cell types arranged in a manner that reflects the native architecture of the organ from which they are derived. Examples include liver organoids, intestinal organoids, brain organoids, kidney organoids, pancreatic organoids, and lung organoids, each exhibiting organ-specific gene expression, secretory functions, and physiological responses. Organoids may be derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), embryonic stem cells (ESCs), or adult tissue-resident stem cells, and may be cultured in extracellular matrix gels such as Matrigel or laminin-rich basement membrane extracts.

The term “ultracompact RFID microchip,” as used herein, refers to a passive radio frequency identification device with at least one dimension measuring between 100 and 600 micrometers, comprising a silicon-based integrated circuit containing a memory unit and a coiled antenna structure designed to resonate at a specific radio frequency when exposed to an interrogating electromagnetic field. The microchip does not contain an internal power source and derives operational energy from the incident electromagnetic field generated by an external reader device. The memory unit is capable of storing a unique identifier, such as an Electronic Product Code (EPC), and may be configured to store additional data such as timestamps, environmental conditions, or experimental metadata. The microchip is encapsulated within a biocompatible, non-corrosive, and non-leaching protective coating, typically composed of silicon dioxide, parylene, or a similar inert polymer, to ensure stability in aqueous, organic, and biological environments. The antenna is designed to be flexible, non-rigid, and sufficiently small to permit integration into developing tissue structures without inducing mechanical stress or disrupting cellular processes.

The term “radio frequency identification-integrated organoid” or “RiO” refers to a three-dimensional organoid structure into which one or more ultracompact RFID microchips have been seamlessly and permanently integrated during the self-assembly phase of its formation. The RiO retains all biological characteristics of a standard organoid, including the presence of polarized epithelial cells, functional luminal spaces, expression of organ-specific markers, secretion of organ-specific proteins, and responsiveness to physiological and pharmacological stimuli. The RFID microchip is located within the lumenal or central cavity of the RiO, surrounded by living cells and extracellular matrix, and is not in direct contact with the cytoplasm of any individual cell. The RiO is capable of being wirelessly interrogated at any point during its lifecycle, including during live culture, cryopreservation, thawing, fixation, embedding, or storage, without loss of microchip functionality or biological integrity.

The term “self-assembly process,” as used herein, refers to the spontaneous organization of dissociated progenitor cells into a three-dimensional tissue structure through cell-cell adhesion, cell-matrix interactions, and intrinsic developmental signaling pathways, resulting in the formation of a polarized, lumen-containing architecture. This process typically occurs following the re-aggregation of dissociated cells in a three-dimensional matrix or under suspension culture conditions that promote cell clustering and morphogenesis. The self-assembly process is distinct from directed assembly methods that rely on external scaffolds, molds, or mechanical manipulation. In the context of this invention, the self-assembly process is the critical mechanism by which the ultracompact RFID microchip becomes passively and irreversibly encapsulated within the developing organoid without causing structural damage or functional impairment.

The term “wireless interrogation” refers to the non-contact, non-optical retrieval of data from the RFID microchip via the transmission of an electromagnetic signal from a reader device, which induces a resonant response in the microchip’s antenna, thereby powering the integrated circuit and enabling the transmission of stored information back to the reader. Wireless interrogation does not require direct optical access, physical penetration, or destruction of the tissue sample and may be performed through biological matrices, plastic culture vessels, cryovials, paraffin blocks, or fixed tissue sections.

The term “forward cellomic screening” refers to a high-throughput phenotypic analysis strategy in which a pooled population of genetically distinct organoids—each uniquely identified by an embedded RFID microchip—is subjected to a phenotypic stimulus, and the resulting phenotypic readouts are correlated with the identity of each individual organoid via wireless RFID detection. This approach enables the identification of genotype-phenotype relationships without prior knowledge of the genetic basis for the observed variation, distinguishing it from reverse genetics or transcriptomic-based screening methods. Forward cellomic screening permits the discovery of novel disease-associated phenotypes, drug responders, or genetic modifiers through unbiased, large-scale, organoid-level phenotyping.

The term “cryopreservation” refers to the process of preserving biological tissue at ultra-low temperatures, typically below −130°C, using cryoprotective agents and controlled-rate freezing or vitrification techniques to prevent ice crystal formation and maintain cellular viability. Cryopreservation of RiOs is performed using standard protocols compatible with organoid preservation, including slow freezing in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)-containing media or rapid vitrification in cryoprotectant solutions, followed by storage in liquid nitrogen or vapor-phase freezers. The embedded RFID microchip remains fully functional after cryopreservation and thawing, permitting immediate wireless identification of the RiO upon retrieval.

The term “biobanking” refers to the systematic collection, storage, and management of biological samples for future research and clinical use. In the context of this invention, biobanking of RiOs involves the long-term cryopreservation of thousands of organoids derived from diverse genetic backgrounds, each uniquely tagged with an RFID microchip, enabling efficient retrieval, tracking, and phenotypic analysis without the need for manual labeling, spatial segregation, or genetic barcoding.

The term “electronic product code” or “EPC” refers to a unique alphanumeric identifier stored within the memory unit of the RFID microchip, which serves as a digital fingerprint for the RiO. The EPC may be assigned during manufacturing or during the integration process and may be linked to a database containing donor information, genetic data, culture history, phenotypic records, or experimental metadata. The EPC is immutable, non-replicable, and readable without destruction of the tissue sample.

The term “phenotypic stimulus” refers to any biological, chemical, or physical agent applied to an RiO to induce a measurable change in its structure, function, or molecular profile. Examples include exposure to free fatty acids to induce steatosis, administration of drugs to assess toxicity or efficacy, modulation of pH or oxygen tension, exposure to pathogens, or induction of oxidative stress. The phenotypic response may be quantified using fluorescence imaging, metabolite assays, gene expression analysis, protein secretion measurements, or morphological scoring.

The term “dual-readout detection system” refers to an integrated apparatus capable of simultaneously capturing a fluorescence-based phenotypic signal and wirelessly reading the EPC from an RFID microchip as an RiO passes through a defined flow path. The system comprises a microfluidic flow channel, a syringe pump for controlled sample delivery, an RFID reader antenna positioned to detect the microchip during transit, and a fluorescence microscope or photodetector aligned to capture the emission signal from fluorescent reporters. The system enables real-time, high-throughput correlation of phenotypic outcomes with donor identity in pooled RiO populations.

The term “biocompatible encapsulation” refers to the protective coating surrounding the RFID microchip that prevents direct interaction between the chip’s metallic or semiconductor components and the biological environment. The encapsulation material is chemically inert, non-toxic, non-leaching, and resistant to degradation under physiological conditions, including exposure to enzymes, reactive oxygen species, acidic or alkaline environments, and organic solvents. The encapsulation is designed to permit the transmission of electromagnetic signals without attenuation, ensuring reliable wireless communication.

The term “lumenal encapsulation” refers to the specific positioning of the RFID microchip within the central cavity or lumen of the organoid, where it is surrounded by epithelial cells and extracellular matrix but not in direct contact with the cytoplasm of any individual cell. This positioning is achieved through the self-assembly process and ensures that the microchip does not interfere with cell signaling, polarity establishment, or metabolic exchange.

The term “passive integration” refers to the method by which the RFID microchip is incorporated into the organoid without the use of active delivery mechanisms such as microinjection, electroporation, or viral vectors. Instead, the microchip is introduced into the cell suspension prior to re-aggregation and becomes entrapped within the forming lumen as a result of the natural physical forces driving tissue morphogenesis. Passive integration avoids cellular damage, transfection-related stress, and artificial perturbations to gene expression or signaling pathways.

The term “digitalized organoid” refers to an organoid that contains an embedded RFID microchip capable of storing and transmitting a unique digital identifier, thereby transforming the organoid from a biological sample into a digitally traceable entity. Digitalized organoids enable persistent, non-destructive, and automated tracking across experimental stages, storage conditions, and analytical platforms.

The term “donor-specific organoid” refers to an organoid derived from stem cells originating from a single individual, whether healthy or diseased, and possessing a unique genetic background. Donor-specific organoids may be used to model inherited diseases, assess individual drug responses, or study population-level phenotypic variation. In the context of this invention, each donor-specific organoid is uniquely identified by its embedded RFID microchip, enabling unambiguous tracking even when pooled with hundreds of other organoids.

The term “high-throughput screening” refers to the automated, rapid, and parallel analysis of large numbers of biological samples under controlled experimental conditions. In the context of this invention, high-throughput screening of RiOs enables the simultaneous testing of hundreds or thousands of organoids for phenotypic responses to drugs, toxins, or metabolic challenges, with each response linked to a specific donor via RFID identification.

The term “longitudinal monitoring” refers to the repeated or continuous observation of an RiO over an extended period of time, from initial formation through culture, cryopreservation, thawing, and subsequent experimental challenges. The embedded RFID microchip enables consistent identification of the same RiO across multiple time points, allowing for the tracking of phenotypic evolution, drug response dynamics, or disease progression in a single sample.

The term “environmental resilience” refers to the ability of the embedded RFID microchip to maintain its structural integrity and wireless functionality after exposure to a wide range of physical and chemical conditions commonly encountered in biological research, including but not limited to: temperatures ranging from 4°C to −196°C, exposure to ethanol, formalin, paraffin, xylene, autoclaving, UV irradiation, enzymatic digestion, pH fluctuations from 4.0 to 10.0, and immersion in aqueous or organic solvents. Environmental resilience ensures that the digital identity of the RiO remains accessible regardless of the processing steps applied to the tissue.

The term “non-destructive identification” refers to the ability to retrieve the unique identity of an RiO without altering, damaging, or destroying the tissue sample. Unlike genetic barcoding or fluorescent labeling, which may require fixation, lysis, or photobleaching, non-destructive identification via RFID allows for repeated interrogation of the same RiO throughout its lifecycle, enabling sequential experiments, re-screening, or archival storage with full traceability.

The term “organ-scale microchip” refers to an RFID microchip whose physical dimensions are specifically engineered to be compatible with the size and architecture of organoids, typically ranging from 400 to 600 micrometers in length and width, and less than 100 micrometers in thickness. This size range ensures that the microchip is large enough to be reliably detected by standard RFID readers but small enough to be passively integrated into organoids without disrupting their self-assembly or function.

The term “self-organizing tissue construct” refers to any three-dimensional biological structure formed through intrinsic cellular behaviors without external scaffolding or mechanical guidance, including but not limited to organoids, spheroids, cysts, or organotypic cultures. The invention is applicable to any such construct that undergoes a self-assembly process leading to the formation of a lumen or central cavity.

The term “tissue-compatible interface” refers to the surface chemistry and physical properties of the RFID microchip’s encapsulation that minimize immune recognition, protein adsorption, or inflammatory response when exposed to biological fluids or tissues. This interface ensures that the microchip does not trigger cellular stress responses, alter gene expression profiles, or induce apoptosis or necrosis in surrounding cells.

The term “persistent digital identity” refers to the unchanging, non-erasable, and non-degradable digital identifier stored in the RFID microchip, which remains associated with the RiO throughout its entire lifecycle, from initial culture to long-term storage and subsequent experimental use. This identity is independent of the biological state of the tissue and is not subject to dilution, silencing, or loss through cell division or differentiation.

The term “multiplexed phenotypic analysis” refers to the simultaneous measurement of multiple phenotypic parameters—such as lipid accumulation, bile transport, protein secretion, calcium flux, or mitochondrial membrane potential—in a pooled population of RiOs, with each parameter correlated to the unique EPC of the corresponding RiO. This enables the construction of multidimensional phenotypic profiles for each donor line, facilitating the discovery of complex genotype-phenotype relationships.

The term “digital twin organoid” refers to a RiO whose embedded RFID microchip stores not only its identity but also a complete digital record of its experimental history, including culture conditions, phenotypic responses, drug exposures, and cryopreservation events. This digital twin may be linked to a cloud-based database, enabling remote access, real-time data logging, and automated experimental tracking across laboratories and institutions.

### Examples

The following examples illustrate the implementation and utility of the invention in various experimental contexts. These examples are presented for the purpose of demonstrating the feasibility, robustness, and broad applicability of the invention and are not intended to limit its scope in any way.

In a first example, human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) were differentiated into posterior foregut endoderm progenitors using a sequential exposure to Activin A and FGF4 in the presence of CHIR99021, a GSK3β inhibitor, over a period of six days. At day six, the resulting organoid-like aggregates were dissociated into single cells using enzymatic digestion with Accutase, and the resulting cell suspension was mixed with ultracompact RFID microchips, each measuring 460 × 480 × 80 micrometers and encapsulated in a parylene-C coating. The microchips, each pre-assigned a unique Electronic Product Code (EPC), were introduced into the cell suspension at a concentration of one microchip per 100 cells. The suspension was then plated into low-adhesion 96-well plates and allowed to re-aggregate for 24 hours under static culture conditions. Following re-aggregation, the cell-microchip clusters were embedded in a 2% laminin-rich basement membrane matrix and cultured for an additional three days under standard organoid maintenance conditions, including the addition of Wnt3a, R-spondin, Noggin, and EGF. By day nine, fully formed liver organoids were observed, each containing a single, centrally located microchip embedded within the lumenal space. The organoids exhibited normal epithelial morphology, with clear apicobasal polarity, tight junction formation, and a well-defined central lumen. Immunofluorescence staining confirmed the presence of hepatocyte markers such as albumin (ALB), alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), and hepatocyte nuclear factor 4 alpha (HNF4A), with ALB+ cells surrounding the lumen and HNF4A localized to the nucleus. No differences in marker expression or cellular organization were observed between RiOs and control organoids lacking microchips. Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis demonstrated equivalent expression levels of ALB, AFP, AAT, and other liver-specific genes in RiOs compared to control organoids. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) of culture supernatants revealed comparable levels of albumin secretion, with RiOs secreting 152.9 ng/mL of albumin after 24 hours, versus 149.8 ng/mL in control organoids—a difference of less than 2%. These results demonstrate that the integration of the RFID microchip during the self-assembly phase does not impair the differentiation, polarization, or secretory function of the organoid.

In a second example, the functional capacity of RiOs was assessed through bile acid transport and lipid metabolism assays. The RiOs and control organoids were exposed to cholyl-lysyl-fluorescein (CLF), a fluorescent substrate for the bile salt efflux pump (BSEP), and rhodamine 123, a substrate for the multidrug resistance protein 1 (MDR1). Within ten minutes of exposure, both fluorescent compounds were internalized by the epithelial cells of the RiOs and actively transported into the lumenal space, where they accumulated in a manner indistinguishable from control organoids. The fluorescence intensity within the lumen increased over time, while cytoplasmic signal decreased, confirming the functional activity of the bile transport machinery. Similarly, RiOs were treated with a mixture of free fatty acids (oleic acid and palmitic acid) to induce steatosis, followed by staining with BODIPY 493/503, a lipid-specific fluorescent dye. Lipid droplets were clearly visible within the cytoplasm of hepatocyte-like cells in both RiOs and control organoids, with no significant difference in the intensity or distribution of BODIPY signal. Quantitative analysis using ImageJ software confirmed that the mean fluorescence intensity per organoid was identical between the two groups, indicating that lipid accumulation was not affected by the presence of the microchip. Iron accumulation was similarly assessed using ammonium iron sulfate and FeRhoNox 540/575, a fluorescent iron sensor, with no observable differences in iron uptake or distribution between RiOs and controls. These results confirm that RiOs retain full functional capacity in key hepatic processes, including bile transport, lipid metabolism, and metal homeostasis.

In a third example, the durability of the embedded RFID microchip was tested under a series of extreme environmental conditions. RiOs were subjected to temperatures ranging from 4°C to −196°C, including slow freezing in DMSO-containing media, rapid vitrification in cryoprotectant solutions, and direct immersion in liquid nitrogen. After thawing, all RiOs retained their structural integrity, and the microchips remained fully functional, as confirmed by wireless interrogation using a standard RFID reader. The microchips were also exposed to autoclaving at 121°C for 20 minutes under dry and moist heat conditions, immersion in 70% ethanol for 24 hours, fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours, and embedding in paraffin wax using standard histological protocols. After each treatment, the RiOs were rehydrated and interrogated wirelessly, and all microchips successfully transmitted their EPC without signal degradation or data loss. Even after prolonged exposure to ethanol and formalin, the microchip’s antenna remained intact, and its memory unit retained all stored information. These results demonstrate that the microchip is highly resilient to the physical and chemical stresses commonly encountered in biological research, including sterilization, fixation, embedding, and cryopreservation.

In a fourth example, a dual-readout detection system was developed to enable simultaneous fluorescence imaging and RFID interrogation of RiOs in a high-throughput format. The system consisted of a microfluidic flow channel fabricated from polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), a precision syringe pump capable of delivering samples at rates of 1–10 microliters per minute, an RFID reader antenna positioned adjacent to the flow path, and a fluorescence microscope equipped with a high-speed camera. RiOs were labeled with BODIPY to visualize lipid accumulation and then loaded into the system. As each RiO passed through the flow channel, the fluorescence camera captured a high-resolution image of the lipid signal, while the RFID antenna simultaneously read the EPC of the embedded microchip. The system processed over 200 RiOs per hour with 100% accuracy in both fluorescence detection and RFID identification. The data were automatically logged into a database, correlating each EPC with its corresponding lipid intensity. This system enabled the rapid, automated screening of pooled RiOs without manual sorting or visual identification, demonstrating the feasibility of high-throughput phenotypic analysis at scale.

In a fifth example, a forward cellomic screening experiment was conducted using a pooled population of RiOs derived from seven different iPSC lines, including two lines from patients with Wolman disease, a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the lysosomal acid lipase (LAL) gene. Each RiO was individually tagged with a unique EPC prior to pooling. The pooled RiOs were cultured together in a single well and exposed to free fatty acids to induce steatosis. After 72 hours, the RiOs were harvested and passed through the dual-readout detection system. Fluorescence imaging revealed significant variation in lipid accumulation across the population, with two RiOs exhibiting markedly higher BODIPY signal intensity than the others. The RFID reader immediately identified the EPCs of these two RiOs, and cross-referencing with the donor database confirmed that both corresponded to iPSC lines derived from Wolman disease patients. All other RiOs, derived from healthy donors, exhibited significantly lower lipid accumulation. This experiment demonstrated that the RiO platform enables the unbiased, high-throughput identification of disease-associated phenotypes in a pooled population, without prior knowledge of which lines harbor the disease mutation. The ability to link a phenotypic outcome directly to a genetic origin via a persistent digital identifier represents a transformative advance in precision medicine and disease modeling.

In a sixth example, RiOs were cryopreserved using a slow-freezing protocol and stored in liquid nitrogen for a period of six months. Upon thawing, the RiOs were re-cultured under standard conditions and allowed to recover for seven days. All RiOs regained their original morphology, exhibited normal proliferation rates, and re-established functional polarity. The embedded microchips remained fully functional, and wireless interrogation confirmed that each RiO retained its original EPC. The RiOs were then subjected to a second round of fatty acid exposure and BODIPY staining, and lipid accumulation was again measured. The cryopreserved RiOs exhibited lipid accumulation profiles identical to those observed prior to freezing, confirming that both biological function and digital identity were preserved through long-term storage. This example demonstrates the viability of RiOs for biobanking applications, wherein thousands of donor-specific organoids can be stored in a single container, each uniquely identifiable upon retrieval.

In a seventh example, RiOs were generated from iPSC lines derived from patients with cystic fibrosis, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, and cholestatic liver disease. Each RiO was tagged with a unique EPC and cultured under standardized conditions. The RiOs were then exposed to a panel of 50 pharmacological compounds known to modulate liver function, including drugs, toxins, and metabolic modulators. After 48 hours of exposure, the RiOs were analyzed using the dual-readout system to quantify changes in viability, bile transport, and lipid accumulation. The data revealed distinct drug response profiles for each genetic background, with certain compounds inducing steatosis only in RiOs derived from patients with specific mutations. These findings were validated using traditional single-line cultures, confirming the predictive power of the RiO platform. The ability to screen hundreds of drug candidates against genetically diverse organoids in a single experiment, with each response linked to a specific donor, demonstrates the potential of RiOs to accelerate drug discovery and personalize therapeutic regimens.

In an eighth example, RiOs were implanted subcutaneously into immunodeficient mice to assess their survival and functionality in vivo. After two weeks, the mice were euthanized, and the implanted RiOs were excised and analyzed. The RiOs remained viable, retained their epithelial structure, and continued to secrete albumin into the host circulation. The embedded microchips remained functional, and wireless interrogation confirmed their identity and location. This example demonstrates that RiOs are not only compatible with in vitro culture but also capable of surviving and functioning in a living organism, opening the possibility for in vivo tracking of transplanted tissues, monitoring of graft rejection, or real-time assessment of therapeutic interventions.

In a ninth example, RiOs were generated from iPSCs derived from 100 different donors, including healthy individuals and patients with a variety of inherited liver diseases. Each RiO was tagged with a unique EPC and stored in a centralized biobank. A database was created linking each EPC to the donor’s genetic sequence, clinical history, and prior phenotypic data. Researchers from multiple institutions were granted access to the biobank and requested specific RiOs for their experiments. Upon retrieval, the RiOs were thawed, interrogated wirelessly to confirm identity, and immediately used in phenotypic assays. The entire process—from request to experimental use—was completed in under 48 hours, with zero misidentification or contamination. This example demonstrates the scalability and logistical utility of the RiO platform for large-scale, multi-institutional research initiatives.

In a tenth example, RiOs were used to model the progression of non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) over time. A single RiO was cultured for 60 days, with periodic wireless interrogation to confirm identity and record culture conditions. At day 14, the RiO was exposed to a high-fat diet mimetic, and at day 30, it was treated with a pro-inflammatory cytokine cocktail. At day 60, the RiO exhibited fibrotic remodeling, increased expression of collagen and alpha-smooth muscle actin, and reduced albumin secretion—hallmarks of advanced NASH. The entire phenotypic trajectory of the RiO was digitally recorded via its embedded microchip, creating a longitudinal digital twin of disease progression. This example demonstrates the potential of RiOs to serve as dynamic, real-time models of chronic disease, with each step of the disease process permanently documented and accessible for future analysis.

These examples collectively demonstrate the robustness, versatility, and transformative potential of the invention. The integration of ultracompact RFID microchips into developing organoids via passive, self-assembly-mediated encapsulation enables the creation of digitalized biological tissues that retain full physiological function while acquiring persistent, non-destructive, and high-throughput digital identities. The resulting RiOs represent a new class of biological entities—simultaneously living tissues and digital data carriers—that overcome the longstanding limitations of manual labeling, genetic barcoding, and spatial segregation in tissue engineering and precision medicine. The invention enables scalable biobanking, forward cellomic screening, longitudinal monitoring, and automated phenotypic analysis at a level of precision and throughput previously unattainable, thereby establishing a new paradigm for the study and application of human tissues in research and clinical settings.
