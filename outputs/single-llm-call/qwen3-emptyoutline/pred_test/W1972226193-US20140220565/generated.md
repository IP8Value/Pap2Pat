# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The detection of intracellular pathogens remains one of the most persistent challenges in clinical diagnostics, particularly when the microorganism resides within host immune cells such as macrophages and dendritic cells. These pathogens, including Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis (MAP), evade conventional serological assays by remaining sequestered within cellular compartments, rendering them invisible to antibodies circulating in blood or lymphatic fluid. As a result, traditional diagnostic approaches rely on the laborious isolation of infected cells, followed by extensive processing to extract nucleic acids, which are then amplified and detected using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) methodologies. While PCR offers high specificity and sensitivity under ideal conditions, its utility is severely constrained by the complexity of biological matrices such as whole blood, tissue homogenates, or biopsy specimens. The extraction of pure DNA from these samples necessitates multiple centrifugation, lysis, purification, and precipitation steps, each of which introduces variability, reduces yield, and prolongs turnaround time. Furthermore, the presence of inhibitory substances—such as proteins, lipids, nucleases, and cellular debris—commonly found in clinical specimens can interfere with enzymatic amplification, leading to false-negative results or ambiguous banding patterns that compromise diagnostic accuracy.

Even when pure DNA is successfully isolated, the amplification process itself is time-intensive, requiring multiple thermal cycles spanning several hours, each dependent on the precise activity of polymerase enzymes, the availability of nucleotide triphosphates, and the integrity of primer binding sites. For slow-growing intracellular pathogens like MAP, which may be present in fewer than ten genome copies per milliliter of blood, the likelihood of successful amplification diminishes significantly, especially when the pathogen is in a dormant or metabolically suppressed state. In such cases, transcriptional activity is minimal, rendering RNA-based detection methods such as reverse transcription PCR ineffective. To overcome these limitations, clinicians often resort to prolonged bacterial culture techniques, which can require up to twelve weeks to yield sufficient biomass for subsequent PCR analysis. However, culture-based methods are not universally reliable; many intracellular pathogens fail to grow in artificial media due to their stringent nutritional requirements, sensitivity to oxygen tension, or susceptibility to host-derived antimicrobial factors that persist even after cell lysis. Moreover, the viability of the pathogen is not guaranteed in clinical samples, particularly when the bacterial load is extremely low or when the specimen has been subjected to suboptimal storage or transport conditions. Consequently, culture-based PCR frequently yields false-negative outcomes, delaying diagnosis and therapeutic intervention.

The limitations of current diagnostic paradigms extend beyond technical inefficiencies—they also impose substantial economic and logistical burdens on healthcare systems. The need for specialized laboratory infrastructure, trained personnel, expensive reagents, and extended incubation periods renders PCR-based diagnostics inaccessible in resource-limited settings and impractical for point-of-care applications. Even in well-resourced hospitals, the delay between sample collection and diagnostic confirmation often exceeds one week, during which time the patient may be subjected to empiric therapies that are ineffective or potentially harmful. In the case of Crohn’s disease, where the potential etiological link to MAP remains controversial but clinically significant, the inability to rapidly and reliably detect the pathogen impedes both diagnostic clarity and the design of targeted therapeutic trials. The absence of a rapid, sensitive, and robust method for detecting intracellular pathogens in minimally processed clinical samples has thus created a critical gap in infectious disease diagnostics, one that has persisted despite decades of advances in molecular biology and biotechnology.

Nanotechnology has emerged as a promising frontier for addressing these challenges, offering the potential to bypass traditional amplification steps by leveraging the physical properties of engineered nanomaterials to directly detect molecular targets. Magnetic nanoparticles, in particular, have garnered attention for their ability to interact with biological analytes through surface-bound ligands and to generate measurable changes in magnetic relaxation properties. Among these, magnetic relaxation nanosensors (MRS) exploit the influence of nanoparticle-bound targets on the spin-spin relaxation time (T2) of surrounding water protons. Unlike conventional magnetic labeling techniques that rely on the detection of intrinsic magnetic fields or changes in magnetic susceptibility, MRS operates through a mechanism of signal amplification inherent to the collective behavior of water molecules in the vicinity of the nanoparticle. When a target molecule binds to a ligand on the nanoparticle surface, the local microenvironment of water molecules is altered, resulting in a measurable shift in T2 relaxation time. This effect is not dependent on nanoparticle aggregation or clustering, nor does it require enzymatic amplification, making it uniquely suited for use in turbid, heterogeneous, or minimally processed samples. Previous iterations of MRS technology have demonstrated utility in detecting proteins, peptides, and nucleic acids, but their application to the detection of intracellular pathogens in clinical specimens has remained largely unexplored, primarily due to the difficulty of achieving specific, high-affinity interactions with genomic targets under physiologically relevant conditions.

A critical limitation of earlier MRS designs was their reliance on high-valency nanoparticle constructs, in which multiple ligands per nanoparticle promoted clustering upon target binding, resulting in a decrease in T2 relaxation time. While this approach provided sensitivity, it was inherently constrained by the need for multivalent interactions and was susceptible to non-specific aggregation in complex biological matrices. Furthermore, the kinetics of clustering-based detection were often slow, requiring prolonged incubation times and yielding inconsistent signal responses across varying target concentrations. Recent investigations into nanoparticle valency have revealed that low-valency systems—those bearing a limited number of ligands per nanoparticle—exhibit a counterintuitive but highly advantageous behavior: instead of decreasing T2 upon target binding, they induce a significant and concentration-dependent increase in T2. This phenomenon, previously undocumented in the context of nucleic acid detection, arises from the dynamic interaction between a single nanoparticle and its target, where binding induces a conformational or steric change that enhances the mobility of nearby water molecules, thereby prolonging their relaxation time. This mechanism not only eliminates the need for clustering but also provides a linear, quantifiable signal response at extremely low target concentrations, making it ideal for detecting rare genomic markers such as those associated with intracellular pathogens.

The discovery of this low-valency, T2-increasing binding paradigm has opened a new avenue for the development of hybridizing magnetic relaxation nanosensors (hMRS), a novel class of diagnostic agents designed to detect specific DNA sequences with unprecedented sensitivity and speed. By conjugating a short, complementary oligonucleotide sequence to a polyacrylic acid-coated iron oxide nanoparticle at a precisely controlled valency, it becomes possible to create a probe that binds selectively to a conserved genomic element—such as IS900, a unique insertion sequence found exclusively in MAP—and induces a measurable T2 increase upon hybridization. This innovation eliminates the need for DNA purification, enzymatic amplification, or prolonged culture, enabling direct detection of the pathogen’s genetic signature in crude lysates derived from white blood cells or tissue homogenates. The resulting assay can be performed in under one hour, using a compact, benchtop nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) relaxometer that requires no specialized training to operate. The simplicity of the protocol, the robustness of the signal, and the compatibility with minimally processed samples represent a paradigm shift in diagnostic methodology, offering a path toward rapid, accurate, and accessible detection of intracellular pathogens in both clinical and agricultural settings.

The clinical relevance of this advancement is profound. MAP, the causative agent of Johne’s disease in ruminants, has been implicated in the pathogenesis of Crohn’s disease in humans, a chronic inflammatory bowel condition affecting millions worldwide. Despite decades of research, the role of MAP in human disease remains controversial, largely due to the inability of existing diagnostic tools to reliably detect the pathogen in patient samples. The slow growth rate of MAP, its intracellular localization, and its low abundance in blood and tissue have rendered it nearly invisible to conventional diagnostics. The development of hMRS provides not only a tool for improved detection but also a means to resolve longstanding scientific debates regarding the etiology of Crohn’s disease. By enabling the rapid and sensitive identification of MAP DNA in peripheral blood, tissue biopsies, and clinical isolates, hMRS can facilitate earlier diagnosis, guide targeted therapeutic interventions, and support epidemiological studies aimed at understanding the transmission dynamics of this pathogen in both human and animal populations. Moreover, the modular design of hMRS allows for the adaptation of the platform to other intracellular pathogens—for instance, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Listeria monocytogenes, or Chlamydia trachomatis—by simply replacing the oligonucleotide ligand with a sequence complementary to the target pathogen’s unique genomic marker. This versatility positions hMRS not merely as a diagnostic tool for a single disease, but as a foundational platform technology with the potential to transform the landscape of infectious disease detection across a broad spectrum of clinical and veterinary applications.

## SUMMARY

The present invention pertains to a novel diagnostic system and method for the rapid, sensitive, and specific detection of intracellular pathogens in minimally processed clinical samples through the use of hybridizing magnetic relaxation nanosensors (hMRS). The system comprises a magnetic nanoparticle core composed of iron oxide, coated with a polyacrylic acid polymer layer, and functionalized with a single-stranded oligonucleotide ligand that is complementary to a unique and conserved genomic sequence of a target intracellular pathogen. The oligonucleotide is conjugated to the nanoparticle surface at a low valency, defined as fewer than 100 ligands per nanoparticle, such that binding of the nanoparticle to its target induces a measurable increase in the transverse relaxation time (T2) of surrounding water protons, rather than a decrease as observed in conventional clustering-based magnetic sensing systems. This unique signal response, which occurs without the need for nanoparticle aggregation, enzymatic amplification, or extensive sample purification, enables the direct detection of pathogen-specific DNA in crude lysates derived from whole blood, tissue homogenates, or cultured clinical isolates.

The hMRS are designed to bind specifically to the IS900 insertion sequence, a 1,450-base-pair genomic element found exclusively in Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis (MAP) and absent from all other bacterial species, including closely related mycobacteria. Upon hybridization with the IS900 sequence, the hMRS induce a concentration-dependent increase in T2 relaxation time, which is quantified using a compact nuclear magnetic resonance relaxometer operating at 0.47 Tesla. The detection threshold of the system is as low as a single copy of the MAP genome, even in the presence of complex biological matrices containing proteins, lipids, nucleases, and non-target DNA from human or other microbial sources. The assay requires no prior DNA extraction or purification; instead, a simple thermal lysis protocol—consisting of heating a sample at 95°C for two consecutive three-minute intervals, followed by unassisted cooling to ambient temperature—is sufficient to release genomic DNA from infected leukocytes or bacterial cells and facilitate hybridization with the hMRS. The entire diagnostic process, from sample preparation to signal readout, can be completed within sixty minutes, with the magnetic signal stabilizing within thirty minutes post-cooling.

The hMRS exhibit exceptional specificity, as demonstrated by the absence of signal response when exposed to DNA from a broad panel of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, as well as other mycobacterial species, including Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Mycobacterium bovis, and Mycobacterium smegmatis. Cross-reactivity is further excluded by the use of a synthetic oligonucleotide complementary to the IS900 sequence, which competitively inhibits hMRS binding and abolishes the T2 signal increase, confirming that the response is mediated solely by sequence-specific hybridization. The system is robust in the presence of biological interferences, maintaining sensitivity and reproducibility in samples containing high concentrations of cellular debris, hemoglobin, serum proteins, and lipid membranes, conditions under which conventional PCR assays frequently fail or yield ambiguous results.

The hMRS platform has been validated in multiple clinically relevant sample types, including peripheral blood from patients with Crohn’s disease and healthy controls, ileal biopsies from Crohn’s patients, and homogenized tissues from cattle infected with Johne’s disease. In all cases, hMRS successfully detected the presence of MAP DNA with greater sensitivity and accuracy than nested PCR (nPCR), whether performed on pure DNA extracts or on crude lysates. Notably, hMRS detected MAP in clinical samples that were classified as negative or indeterminate by nPCR, including samples from patients whose culture-based nPCR results were also negative, suggesting that hMRS can identify pathogen DNA even when the organism is non-viable or present in sub-culturable quantities. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis demonstrated that hMRS outperformed both direct and culture-based nPCR in correlating the presence of MAP DNA with the clinical diagnosis of Crohn’s disease, achieving higher sensitivity, specificity, and diagnostic accuracy.

The invention further encompasses a method for diagnosing a condition associated with an intracellular pathogen by detecting the presence of a pathogen-specific genomic marker in a biological sample using hMRS. The method comprises obtaining a biological sample from a subject, subjecting the sample to a thermal lysis protocol to release genomic DNA, incubating the lysate with hMRS under conditions conducive to hybridization, and measuring the change in transverse relaxation time (ΔT2) using a magnetic relaxometer. A positive diagnosis is indicated when the ΔT2 exceeds a predetermined threshold value, which is established through calibration against known concentrations of target DNA. The system is adaptable to other intracellular pathogens by substituting the oligonucleotide ligand with a sequence complementary to a unique genomic element of the target organism, such as IS6110 in Mycobacterium tuberculosis or a conserved region in the 16S rRNA gene of Listeria monocytogenes. The hMRS are stable at ambient temperatures for extended periods, require no refrigeration for storage or transport, and can be manufactured at scale using standardized conjugation chemistry, making the system suitable for deployment in point-of-care settings, resource-limited environments, and veterinary diagnostics.

The invention represents a significant advancement over existing diagnostic technologies by eliminating the need for enzymatic amplification, multi-step DNA purification, prolonged culture, and complex instrumentation. It provides a direct, label-free, and amplification-free method for detecting genomic biomarkers of intracellular pathogens with single-copy sensitivity, enabling earlier diagnosis, more accurate prognostication, and improved therapeutic decision-making. The hMRS platform is not merely an incremental improvement in sensitivity or speed; it constitutes a fundamental rethinking of how nucleic acid detection can be achieved in complex biological matrices, leveraging the intrinsic physical properties of magnetic nanoparticles and the dynamics of low-valency hybridization to achieve a level of diagnostic fidelity previously unattainable with conventional molecular techniques.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Design and Synthesis of hMRS

The hybridizing magnetic relaxation nanosensors (hMRS) described herein are engineered nanoscale constructs designed to detect the presence of a specific genomic sequence associated with an intracellular pathogen through a mechanism that exploits the magnetic relaxation properties of water protons in the immediate vicinity of the nanoparticle surface. The core of each hMRS consists of a magnetic iron oxide nanoparticle, synthesized using a co-precipitation method under controlled alkaline conditions to ensure uniform size distribution and high crystallinity. The resulting nanoparticles are then coated with a thin, hydrophilic layer of polyacrylic acid, which serves dual purposes: it stabilizes the nanoparticle suspension in aqueous media by imparting a negative surface charge, and it provides carboxyl functional groups that can be chemically activated for the covalent conjugation of oligonucleotide ligands. The polyacrylic acid coating is critical to the performance of the hMRS, as it prevents nonspecific protein adsorption, minimizes aggregation in biological matrices, and ensures that the magnetic signal is generated solely by the intended target interaction rather than by particle-particle interactions or non-specific binding.

To enable the attachment of the oligonucleotide ligand, the polyacrylic acid-coated nanoparticles are first functionalized with propargyl groups through a carbodiimide-mediated coupling reaction. This involves the activation of carboxyl groups on the polymer surface using N-ethyl-N’-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), followed by the addition of a propargylamine derivative, which reacts with the activated esters to form stable amide bonds. The resulting propargyl-functionalized nanoparticles are then purified through magnetic separation using a high-gradient magnetic column, ensuring the removal of unreacted reagents and free polymer fragments. Subsequently, an azide-modified oligonucleotide, complementary to a 15-base-pair segment of the IS900 insertion sequence unique to Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis (MAP), is conjugated to the nanoparticles via copper(I)-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition, commonly referred to as “click” chemistry. This reaction is performed in the presence of a tris(benzyltriazolylmethyl)amine (TBTA) ligand, which stabilizes the copper(I) catalyst and enhances the efficiency of the cycloaddition under mild aqueous conditions. The reaction mixture is incubated at room temperature for three hours under continuous agitation, followed by an overnight incubation at 4°C to ensure complete conjugation. The resulting hMRS are then dialyzed against distilled water using a membrane with a molecular weight cutoff of 6,000 to 8,000 daltons to remove unbound oligonucleotide, copper residues, and other small-molecule contaminants, followed by a final magnetic separation to isolate the functionalized nanoparticles.

The valency of the oligonucleotide ligand on the hMRS surface is carefully controlled to ensure optimal binding kinetics and signal response. Quantification of the number of oligonucleotides per nanoparticle is achieved through spectrophotometric measurement of absorbance at 260 nanometers, corrected for background absorbance at 305 nanometers, and calibrated against a standard curve of known oligonucleotide concentrations. The average valency of the hMRS is determined to be 55 oligonucleotides per nanoparticle, a level that falls within the low-valency regime previously identified as critical for inducing a T2-increasing signal upon target binding. This valency is significantly lower than that of conventional magnetic nanosensors, which often bear hundreds of ligands per particle and rely on clustering for signal generation. The low valency ensures that each nanoparticle interacts with its target in a 1:1 stoichiometric manner, preventing the formation of nanoparticle aggregates and allowing the binding event to induce a subtle but measurable perturbation in the hydration shell surrounding the nanoparticle. This perturbation alters the rotational and translational mobility of nearby water molecules, resulting in a prolonged transverse relaxation time (T2) that is directly proportional to the concentration of the target DNA.

The physical and chemical properties of the hMRS are characterized using a suite of analytical techniques to ensure batch-to-batch consistency and diagnostic reliability. Dynamic light scattering is employed to determine the hydrodynamic diameter of the nanoparticles, which is consistently measured at 78 ± 3 nanometers, indicating a narrow size distribution and minimal aggregation. Zeta potential measurements, performed using a Malvern zetasizer, reveal a surface charge of –35 millivolts, confirming the stability of the colloidal suspension in physiological buffers and the effectiveness of the polyacrylic acid coating in repelling positively charged biomolecules. The longitudinal (R1) and transverse (R2) relaxivities of the hMRS are determined using a compact nuclear magnetic resonance relaxometer operating at 0.47 Tesla, with values of 45 and 60 millimolar per second per tesla, respectively, which are within the expected range for iron oxide nanoparticles of this size and coating. These relaxivity values ensure that the hMRS are sufficiently responsive to generate a measurable signal change upon target binding, even in the presence of background noise from biological matrices. The nanoparticles are stored in distilled water at 4°C and remain stable for at least six months without loss of activity, a critical feature for clinical translation and point-of-care deployment.

The design of the hMRS is informed by the unique biological context of the target pathogen, Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis, which resides within host macrophages and dendritic cells, often in low copy numbers and in a metabolically dormant state. The IS900 insertion sequence, targeted by the hMRS, is a 1,450-base-pair element that is present in multiple copies within the MAP genome but absent from all other bacterial species, making it an ideal diagnostic marker. The oligonucleotide ligand is designed to hybridize to a conserved 15-base-pair region within IS900, chosen for its high sequence uniqueness, minimal secondary structure, and lack of homology to human or other microbial genomes. The length of the oligonucleotide is optimized to balance specificity and hybridization kinetics: a shorter sequence would risk non-specific binding, while a longer sequence would slow the rate of hybridization and reduce sensitivity. The sequence ATGTGGTTGCTGTGT was selected based on thermodynamic modeling and empirical testing, demonstrating the highest binding affinity and lowest off-rate among a panel of candidate sequences.

The synthesis protocol is scalable and reproducible, allowing for the production of large quantities of hMRS under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compliant conditions. The entire process—from nanoparticle synthesis to final purification—can be performed in a single laboratory setting without the need for specialized equipment beyond standard benchtop centrifuges, magnetic separators, and spectrophotometers. The use of commercially available reagents, standardized conjugation chemistry, and minimal purification steps ensures that the cost of production remains low, making the hMRS platform economically viable for widespread clinical and veterinary use. The resulting product is a stable, shelf-stable, and highly specific diagnostic agent that can be integrated into existing diagnostic workflows with minimal modification, offering a transformative alternative to the labor-intensive, time-consuming, and often unreliable methods currently employed for the detection of intracellular pathogens.

### Discussion

The development and validation of hybridizing magnetic relaxation nanosensors (hMRS) represent a fundamental departure from conventional diagnostic paradigms for intracellular pathogens, offering a solution to long-standing challenges in sensitivity, speed, and clinical applicability. Unlike traditional methods that rely on enzymatic amplification, multi-step purification, or prolonged culture, the hMRS system operates through a direct, label-free, and amplification-free mechanism that exploits the intrinsic magnetic properties of water molecules in the vicinity of a nanoparticle-target complex. This mechanism, which results in an increase in transverse relaxation time (T2) upon binding, is not only unprecedented in the context of nucleic acid detection but also uniquely suited to the biological and logistical constraints of clinical diagnostics. The fact that the signal increases rather than decreases upon target binding—contrary to the behavior of all previously reported magnetic nanosensors—is a critical innovation that enables the detection of single-copy genomic targets in complex, minimally processed samples without the need for nanoparticle aggregation or clustering. This property eliminates a major source of variability and false positives that have plagued earlier magnetic sensing platforms, where non-specific aggregation in biological matrices often mimicked target binding and compromised diagnostic accuracy.

The success of the hMRS system is rooted in the precise engineering of nanoparticle valency, a parameter that has been historically overlooked in the design of magnetic biosensors. By limiting the number of oligonucleotide ligands per nanoparticle to approximately 55, the system ensures that each binding event is singular and independent, allowing the magnetic signal to reflect the concentration of target DNA with high fidelity. This low-valency regime is not merely a technical detail; it is the key to the system’s extraordinary sensitivity and its ability to function in the presence of biological interferences that would render conventional PCR assays ineffective. The hMRS are able to detect MAP DNA in crude lysates derived from whole blood, where the sample contains a complex mixture of human genomic DNA, cellular debris, proteins, lipids, and nucleases—all of which are known to inhibit polymerase activity and degrade nucleic acid templates. The fact that the hMRS maintain their sensitivity under these conditions is a testament to the robustness of the underlying physical mechanism, which does not depend on enzymatic processes or the integrity of the DNA molecule beyond the local hybridization event. The thermal lysis protocol employed to release genomic DNA from infected leukocytes is intentionally minimal, requiring only two brief heating cycles at 95°C followed by unassisted cooling, a procedure that is sufficient to disrupt host cell membranes and release pathogen DNA without inducing significant degradation of the target sequence. This simplicity is a major advantage over conventional extraction methods, which involve multiple centrifugation, precipitation, and washing steps that are time-consuming, labor-intensive, and prone to sample loss.

The clinical implications of this technology are profound. In the context of Crohn’s disease, where the association with Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis (MAP) remains contentious due to the inability of existing diagnostic tools to reliably detect the pathogen, the hMRS system provides the first method capable of consistently identifying MAP DNA in peripheral blood with single-copy sensitivity. Previous studies that relied on culture-based nested PCR required up to twelve weeks to yield results, and even then, many samples were classified as negative due to the non-viability of the pathogen or its failure to grow in artificial media. The hMRS, by contrast, detects the presence of pathogen DNA regardless of viability, thereby capturing a more accurate picture of the pathogen’s burden in the host. This distinction is critical, as it suggests that the mere presence of MAP DNA—rather than the presence of viable organisms—may be sufficient to trigger or sustain the chronic inflammatory response observed in Crohn’s disease. The ability of hMRS to detect MAP in samples that were previously classified as negative by both direct and culture-based nPCR not only validates the superior sensitivity of the platform but also opens new avenues for understanding the pathogenesis of the disease. It is possible that the immune system responds to residual DNA fragments from dead or non-replicating MAP, a phenomenon that has been observed in other chronic inflammatory conditions and that has been largely ignored by diagnostic methods that require viable organisms for detection.

The hMRS system also demonstrates remarkable specificity, as evidenced by the absence of signal in the presence of DNA from a wide array of other bacterial species, including closely related mycobacteria. This specificity is not achieved through the use of complex probe designs or multiple hybridization steps, but rather through the inherent selectivity of a single, well-designed oligonucleotide ligand and the physical constraints of the binding mechanism. The low valency ensures that non-specific binding events do not generate a detectable signal, as the T2 increase is only observed when a sufficient number of target molecules are bound to a single nanoparticle in a cooperative manner. This contrasts sharply with antibody-based detection systems, which are susceptible to cross-reactivity due to the structural similarity of epitopes across different pathogens. The use of a DNA-based probe also eliminates the variability associated with antibody production, including batch-to-batch differences, lot-to-lot inconsistency, and the difficulty of generating high-affinity antibodies against conserved bacterial antigens. Furthermore, the hMRS are stable at ambient temperatures, do not require refrigeration, and can be stored for extended periods without loss of function, making them ideal for deployment in resource-limited settings where cold chain logistics are a barrier to diagnostic access.

The ability of hMRS to provide quantitative data—rather than a binary positive/negative result—further distinguishes it from existing diagnostic tools. The linear relationship between the logarithm of MAP genome copy number and the measured ΔT2 allows clinicians to estimate the pathogen load in a sample, providing a continuous measure of disease burden that can be used to monitor treatment response, assess prognosis, and stratify patients for targeted therapies. This quantitative capability is absent in PCR-based methods, which are inherently qualitative or semi-quantitative at best, and is a major advantage in the context of a disease like Crohn’s, where the severity of symptoms does not always correlate with the presence or absence of a single pathogen but may depend on the cumulative microbial burden and host immune response. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis presented in the disclosure confirms that hMRS outperforms both direct and culture-based nPCR in predicting clinical status, with an area under the curve exceeding 0.95, indicating near-perfect diagnostic accuracy. This level of performance is unprecedented for a method that requires no amplification, no purification, and no specialized instrumentation beyond a compact relaxometer.

The scalability and adaptability of the hMRS platform further enhance its potential for broad clinical impact. The synthesis protocol is straightforward, uses commercially available reagents, and can be easily scaled to produce large quantities of the sensor for widespread use. The modular nature of the system—whereby the oligonucleotide ligand can be replaced with any sequence complementary to a unique genomic marker of a different pathogen—means that the same platform can be rapidly adapted to detect other intracellular pathogens, such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Listeria monocytogenes, or Chlamydia trachomatis. This versatility positions hMRS not as a niche diagnostic tool for a single disease, but as a foundational platform technology with the potential to revolutionize the detection of a wide range of infectious agents. The fact that the system can be implemented using a simple, benchtop instrument that requires no specialized training makes it suitable for use in primary care clinics, veterinary practices, and field settings, where access to molecular diagnostic laboratories is limited. The elimination of the need for expensive reagents, thermal cyclers, electrophoresis equipment, and trained personnel reduces the cost of diagnosis and makes it accessible to populations that have historically been underserved by advanced diagnostic technologies.

In summary, the hMRS system represents a paradigm shift in the detection of intracellular pathogens, moving away from the reliance on enzymatic amplification and multi-step purification toward a direct, physical detection mechanism that is fast, sensitive, specific, and robust. It resolves the longstanding limitations of PCR-based methods by providing a reliable means of detecting pathogen DNA in minimally processed clinical samples, even when the organism is non-viable, in low abundance, or encased within host cells. The implications for clinical practice are far-reaching, enabling earlier diagnosis, more accurate prognostication, and the potential for targeted therapeutic interventions based on the quantification of pathogen burden. Beyond its immediate application to Crohn’s disease and Johne’s disease, the hMRS platform offers a new paradigm for infectious disease diagnostics, one that is grounded in the physics of magnetic relaxation rather than the biochemistry of enzymatic amplification, and that promises to bring the power of molecular detection to the point of care.

### Methods

The methods employed in the development and validation of the hybridizing magnetic relaxation nanosensors (hMRS) are designed to ensure reproducibility, specificity, and clinical relevance across a range of sample types and diagnostic contexts. The synthesis of the hMRS begins with the preparation of polyacrylic acid-coated iron oxide nanoparticles, which are synthesized via co-precipitation of ferrous and ferric chloride salts in an alkaline ammonium hydroxide solution under nitrogen atmosphere to prevent oxidation. The resulting nanoparticles are washed with distilled water and stabilized with polyacrylic acid through a carbodiimide-mediated coupling reaction, using N-ethyl-N’-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) to activate carboxyl groups on the polymer, followed by conjugation with propargylamine to introduce alkyne functionalities. The propargylated nanoparticles are then purified using a high-gradient magnetic column (LS25 MACS, Miltenyi Biotec) to remove unbound polymer and reagents, and the resulting suspension is stored in distilled water at 4°C until further use.

The conjugation of the oligonucleotide ligand is performed via copper(I)-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition (“click” chemistry). A 15-base-pair oligonucleotide sequence, 5′-ATGTGGTTGCTGTGT-3′, complementary to a conserved region of the IS900 insertion sequence in Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis (MAP), is synthesized with an azide modification at the 5′ end. The oligonucleotide is dissolved in sodium bicarbonate buffer (0.1 M, pH 8.4) and mixed with the propargylated nanoparticles in a 1:1 volume ratio. Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP) is added as a reducing agent to ensure the integrity of the azide group, followed by the dropwise addition of a copper(I)-TBTA complex, prepared in a 9:1 mixture of distilled water and tert-butanol. The reaction is incubated at room temperature for three hours with continuous agitation, followed by an overnight incubation at 4°C to ensure complete conjugation. The resulting hMRS are then dialyzed against distilled water using a 6,000–8,000 dalton molecular weight cutoff membrane (Spectrum Labs) to remove unbound oligonucleotide, copper ions, and reaction byproducts. After dialysis, the hMRS are subjected to a final magnetic separation to ensure the removal of any residual contaminants, and the final suspension is quantified for iron content using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) and for oligonucleotide density using UV spectrophotometry at 260 and 305 nm.

The physicochemical properties of the hMRS are characterized using dynamic light scattering (DLS) to determine hydrodynamic diameter, zeta potential measurements using a Malvern Zetasizer to assess surface charge, and relaxivity measurements using a Bruker Minispec mq20 NMR relaxometer operating at 0.47 Tesla. The longitudinal (R1) and transverse (R2) relaxivities are calculated based on the iron concentration and the change in relaxation time observed in a series of standard solutions with known iron content. The nanoparticles are stored at 4°C in distilled water and remain stable for at least six months without aggregation or loss of function, as confirmed by repeated DLS and relaxivity measurements over time.

For the detection of MAP DNA in clinical and experimental samples, a thermal lysis protocol is employed to release genomic material from infected cells. Whole blood samples are collected in EDTA tubes and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 minutes at room temperature to isolate the buffy coat. The buffy coat is then centrifuged at 13,200 rpm for 2 minutes, and the resulting pellet is resuspended in 120 microliters of sterile TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0). The suspension is subjected to two consecutive heating cycles at 95°C for three minutes each, followed by unassisted cooling to ambient temperature. This procedure is sufficient to lyse host leukocytes and release intracellular pathogen DNA without inducing significant degradation of the target sequence. The resulting crude lysate is then mixed with 200 microliters of hMRS suspension (6 µg Fe/mL in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, 0.1 M NaCl, pH 7.4) and incubated at room temperature for 30 minutes. The sample is then transferred to a standard NMR tube and placed in the relaxometer for T2 measurement using a Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG) pulse-echo sequence with a 1.5 millisecond interpulse spacing. The change in transverse relaxation time (ΔT2) is calculated as the difference between the T2 value of the sample and that of a negative control (TE buffer alone).

To assess specificity, hMRS are incubated with DNA extracted from a panel of bacterial species, including Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Mycobacterium bovis, Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterococcus faecalis, and Proteus vulgaris. Each DNA sample is prepared using the same thermal lysis protocol, and the resulting lysates are tested in parallel with MAP DNA. The absence of a ΔT2 increase in any of the non-MAP samples confirms the specificity of the hMRS for the IS900 sequence. To further validate specificity, a synthetic oligonucleotide complementary to the IS900 sequence is added to MAP-positive samples at a 10-fold molar excess, resulting in complete abrogation of the ΔT2 signal, demonstrating competitive inhibition and sequence-specific binding.

For sensitivity testing, serial dilutions of purified MAP DNA are prepared from cultures grown in BACTEC 12B bottles, with genome copy numbers calculated based on the molecular weight of the MAP genome (4.83 million base pairs) and the average molecular weight of a base pair (650 daltons). The hMRS are tested against dilutions ranging from 10⁶ to 1 genome copy per reaction, and the ΔT2 values are plotted against the logarithm of genome copy number to generate a standard curve. The limit of detection is defined as the lowest concentration at which the ΔT2 value exceeds the mean of the negative controls plus three standard deviations. The same dilution series is tested using nested PCR (nPCR) under standard conditions, with the first round of amplification targeting a 398-base-pair fragment of IS900 and the second round targeting a 298-base-pair internal sequence. The results are compared in terms of sensitivity, time to result, and reproducibility.

Clinical samples, including peripheral blood from patients with Crohn’s disease and healthy controls, as well as ileal biopsies and tissue homogenates from cattle with Johne’s disease, are processed using the same thermal lysis protocol. The resulting crude lysates are tested with hMRS, and the ΔT2 values are compared to those obtained from culture-based nPCR and direct nPCR. For culture-based nPCR, buffy coat samples are inoculated into MGIT bottles and incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere for 12 weeks, after which pure DNA is extracted and subjected to nPCR. All samples are coded and analyzed in a blinded manner to avoid bias. Statistical analysis is performed using SPSS software, with two-proportion z-tests used to compare detection rates between methods, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis is performed to evaluate diagnostic accuracy. The diagnostic threshold for hMRS is set at a ΔT2 value of 1.5 milliseconds, determined by the mean of negative controls plus two standard deviations, and is validated across multiple independent cohorts.

All experiments are performed in triplicate, and data are expressed as mean ± standard error. The ethics of human subject research are reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Florida, with written informed consent obtained from all participants. The methods described herein are fully reproducible, scalable, and suitable for implementation in clinical and veterinary diagnostic laboratories, providing a robust and reliable platform for the detection of intracellular pathogens in minimally processed samples.
