# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to novel modified bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) with enhanced resistance to inhibition by the endogenous antagonist noggin, and to methods for their production, purification, and therapeutic use. More specifically, the invention encompasses genetically engineered variants of BMP-7, BMP-2, and other BMP family members that exhibit substantially reduced susceptibility to noggin-mediated suppression of signaling activity, while retaining or even enhancing their native osteoinductive properties. These modified BMPs are particularly useful in applications requiring robust and sustained bone formation, including spinal fusion, fracture repair, craniofacial reconstruction, and treatment of non-union bone defects. The invention further includes nucleic acid sequences encoding such modified BMPs, expression vectors containing these sequences, host cells capable of expressing the modified proteins, and pharmaceutical compositions comprising the modified BMPs for clinical administration. The modified proteins disclosed herein represent a significant advancement over naturally occurring BMPs, which are often rendered ineffective in vivo due to rapid neutralization by endogenous noggin and related inhibitors, thereby necessitating supraphysiological dosing that increases the risk of adverse effects such as ectopic bone formation, inflammation, and edema.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) are members of the transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) superfamily and play essential roles in embryonic development, tissue homeostasis, and skeletal regeneration. Among the most clinically utilized members are BMP-2 and BMP-7, which have been approved for use in orthopedic and spinal fusion procedures due to their potent ability to induce mesenchymal stem cell differentiation into osteoblasts and stimulate de novo bone formation. Despite their therapeutic utility, the clinical application of these proteins is severely limited by their susceptibility to inhibition by endogenous antagonists, particularly noggin, a secreted glycoprotein that binds with high affinity to BMPs and prevents their interaction with cognate cell surface receptors. Noggin is itself induced by BMP signaling, forming a classic negative feedback loop that tightly regulates the spatial and temporal extent of BMP activity. In the context of exogenous BMP administration, this feedback mechanism becomes a major impediment to efficacy, as the administered protein rapidly triggers its own neutralization, necessitating the delivery of doses far exceeding physiological levels to achieve a therapeutic outcome.

Clinical studies have demonstrated that the required doses of BMP-2 and BMP-7 for successful bone healing often exceed 1.5 mg per site, resulting in high costs, increased risk of complications, and inconsistent clinical outcomes. The underlying molecular basis for this inefficiency has been poorly understood, with prior efforts focused primarily on improving protein stability, delivery scaffolds, or pharmacokinetics without addressing the fundamental issue of antagonist-mediated inhibition. Recent investigations have revealed that not all BMPs are equally susceptible to noggin. In particular, BMP-6, a closely related family member sharing approximately 73% amino acid identity with BMP-7, exhibits markedly greater resistance to noggin inhibition in vitro and in vivo, despite inducing less noggin expression than BMP-7. This paradoxical observation—where a BMP that induces less of its inhibitor nonetheless remains more active in the presence of that inhibitor—suggested the existence of intrinsic structural features within BMP-6 that confer resistance to noggin binding or functional interference, independent of transcriptional regulation.

Structural analyses of BMP-6 and BMP-7 have revealed subtle but functionally significant differences in the conformation of their receptor-binding interfaces, particularly in the region surrounding residue 60 of the mature domain. In BMP-7, this position is occupied by a glutamic acid residue, which participates in a salt bridge with arginine at position 48 and forms direct contacts with noggin during complex formation. In contrast, BMP-6 possesses a lysine residue at the homologous position, which alters the electrostatic and steric landscape of the binding interface, reducing the stability of the BMP-noggin complex and enhancing the ability of BMP receptors to compete for binding. This discovery provided a critical insight: that a single amino acid substitution at this site could be exploited to engineer BMP variants with superior resistance to noggin, thereby increasing their functional potency without requiring higher concentrations.

Prior attempts to modify BMPs for enhanced activity have focused on mutations that improve receptor binding affinity, increase half-life, or reduce immunogenicity. However, no prior art has identified or disclosed a specific amino acid residue whose substitution confers resistance to noggin inhibition across multiple BMP family members. Nor has any prior invention described the generation of BMP variants with enhanced resistance to noggin that retain full osteoinductive capacity, nor has any patent or publication disclosed the use of such variants in therapeutic contexts where endogenous noggin levels are known to be elevated, such as in chronic inflammation, aging, or post-traumatic bone repair environments. Furthermore, no prior art has demonstrated that the noggin-resistance phenotype conferred by a lysine at position 60 can be transplanted from BMP-6 to other BMPs such as BMP-7 or BMP-2, thereby creating a new class of engineered molecules with predictable and reproducible improvements in biological activity under physiologically relevant conditions of antagonist presence.

The present invention fills this critical gap by providing a novel class of modified BMPs that are specifically engineered to evade noggin-mediated inhibition through targeted substitution of key residues in the receptor-binding interface. These modified proteins exhibit significantly enhanced signaling potency in the presence of physiological concentrations of noggin, enabling therapeutic efficacy at substantially lower doses than those required for wild-type BMPs. The invention further provides the molecular tools necessary for the scalable production of these proteins, including recombinant expression systems, purification protocols, and formulations suitable for clinical deployment. The resulting compositions represent a transformative advance in regenerative medicine, offering the potential for safer, more cost-effective, and more reliable bone repair therapies with reduced side effects and improved patient outcomes.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel class of modified bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) that exhibit substantially increased resistance to inhibition by the endogenous antagonist noggin, while maintaining or enhancing their native osteoinductive properties. These modified BMPs are characterized by the presence of a lysine residue at position 60 of the mature domain, as defined by the numbering system of human BMP-6, or its functional equivalent in other BMP family members. This single amino acid substitution disrupts the high-affinity interaction between noggin and the BMP molecule, thereby reducing the stability of the BMP-noggin complex and allowing the modified BMP to more effectively compete for binding to its cognate type I and type II receptors on the surface of target cells. As a result, the modified BMPs elicit stronger and more sustained activation of downstream signaling pathways, including phosphorylation of Smad1/5/8, induction of osteogenic transcription factors such as Id-1, Dlx-5, and Msx-2, and ultimately, enhanced alkaline phosphatase activity and bone matrix mineralization, even in the presence of physiological concentrations of noggin.

In a preferred embodiment, the invention encompasses a modified BMP-7 protein in which the glutamic acid residue at position 60 of the mature domain is replaced by a lysine residue, resulting in a BMP-7 E60K variant that demonstrates a greater than tenfold increase in resistance to noggin inhibition compared to wild-type BMP-7. This variant retains full structural integrity and receptor-binding capacity, as confirmed by surface plasmon resonance, receptor cross-linking assays, and functional reporter gene assays. In another embodiment, the invention includes a modified BMP-2 protein in which the proline residue at the position homologous to BMP-6 lysine 60 is substituted with lysine, yielding a BMP-2 P36K variant that exhibits significantly enhanced resistance to noggin-mediated suppression of signaling activity. Additional embodiments include modified forms of BMP-4, BMP-5, and other BMP family members in which the corresponding residue is substituted with lysine or another positively charged amino acid, such as arginine, to confer noggin resistance. The invention further encompasses chimeric BMP proteins in which a segment of the mature domain from BMP-6, encompassing the region around residue 60, is grafted into the sequence of another BMP, such as BMP-7, to transfer the noggin-resistant phenotype. In particular, a chimera designated 80-1, in which residues 45–80 of BMP-7 are replaced with the corresponding region from BMP-6, exhibits resistance to noggin inhibition equivalent to that of native BMP-6.

The invention further provides nucleic acid sequences encoding the modified BMPs described above, including codon-optimized variants for enhanced expression in mammalian, bacterial, yeast, or insect expression systems. These nucleic acids are operably linked to appropriate promoters, enhancers, signal peptides, and polyadenylation signals to ensure efficient transcription and secretion of the modified proteins. The invention further encompasses expression vectors containing these nucleic acids, including plasmids, viral vectors, and episomal constructs suitable for transient or stable transfection in host cells. Host cells capable of expressing the modified BMPs include mammalian cell lines such as HEK293T, CHO, and COS-7, as well as microbial systems such as E. coli and Pichia pastoris, provided that appropriate post-translational modifications are achieved. The modified BMPs are secreted into the culture medium and can be purified using standard chromatographic techniques, including nickel-affinity chromatography, ion-exchange chromatography, and reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography, to yield proteins of high purity and biological activity.

The invention further provides pharmaceutical compositions comprising the modified BMPs in combination with a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, excipient, or delivery matrix. These compositions may be formulated as lyophilized powders, aqueous solutions, or impregnated into biocompatible scaffolds such as collagen sponges, hydroxyapatite ceramics, or synthetic polymers for localized delivery to bone defect sites. The compositions are suitable for administration via injection, implantation, or topical application in the treatment of a variety of skeletal disorders, including non-union fractures, spinal fusion, osteoporotic fractures, craniofacial defects, and critical-sized bone defects. In a preferred embodiment, the modified BMPs are administered at doses ranging from 10 to 500 micrograms per site, significantly lower than the milligram-range doses required for wild-type BMP-2 or BMP-7, thereby reducing the risk of adverse effects and lowering treatment costs.

The invention further provides methods for producing the modified BMPs, comprising the steps of: (a) introducing a nucleic acid encoding the modified BMP into a host cell; (b) culturing the host cell under conditions conducive to expression of the modified BMP; (c) recovering the modified BMP from the culture medium or cell lysate; and (d) purifying the modified BMP to homogeneity. The invention further provides methods for enhancing bone formation in a subject in need thereof, comprising administering to the subject an effective amount of a modified BMP as described herein. The invention further provides methods for screening for compounds that enhance the resistance of BMPs to noggin inhibition, comprising measuring the ability of a test compound to restore BMP signaling activity in the presence of noggin using a reporter gene assay or alkaline phosphatase activity assay. The invention further provides diagnostic kits for detecting the presence of noggin-resistant BMP variants in biological samples, comprising antibodies specific for the modified epitope surrounding residue 60.

The modified BMPs disclosed herein represent a paradigm shift in the design of recombinant growth factors for regenerative medicine. By targeting a single, structurally defined residue that governs susceptibility to a major endogenous inhibitor, the invention enables the creation of BMP variants with superior therapeutic profiles, characterized by enhanced potency, reduced dosage requirements, improved safety, and greater consistency of clinical response. These innovations overcome the limitations of prior BMP therapies and open new avenues for the treatment of skeletal diseases with unprecedented precision and efficacy.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Bone Morphogenetic Proteins

Bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) constitute a subgroup of the transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) superfamily and are critical regulators of skeletal development, tissue repair, and cellular differentiation. These secreted signaling molecules function as dimeric ligands that bind to specific serine/threonine kinase receptors on the surface of target cells, initiating a conserved intracellular signaling cascade that ultimately leads to the transcriptional activation of genes involved in osteoblast differentiation, chondrogenesis, and matrix mineralization. The human BMP family comprises at least fifteen distinct members, including BMP-2, BMP-4, BMP-5, BMP-6, BMP-7, BMP-8, BMP-9, BMP-10, BMP-11, BMP-12, BMP-13, BMP-14, BMP-15, BMP-16, and BMP-17, each with overlapping yet distinct biological roles. Among these, BMP-2 and BMP-7 have been the most extensively studied and clinically utilized due to their potent osteoinductive properties. Both proteins are synthesized as precursor polypeptides consisting of an N-terminal signal peptide, a pro-domain, and a C-terminal mature domain. The mature domain, which is responsible for receptor binding and biological activity, is released through proteolytic cleavage of the pro-domain by furin-like convertases during secretion. The mature domain folds into a characteristic cystine-knot structure stabilized by conserved disulfide bonds, which presents specific surface epitopes for interaction with type I and type II receptor complexes.

The biological activity of BMPs is tightly regulated by a network of extracellular antagonists, including noggin, chordin, gremlin, and follistatin, which bind directly to the ligand and prevent its engagement with cell surface receptors. Of these, noggin is the most potent and specific inhibitor of BMP-2, BMP-4, BMP-6, and BMP-7, and its expression is itself induced by BMP signaling, forming a negative feedback loop that limits the duration and intensity of BMP activity. This regulatory mechanism is essential during embryonic development to prevent ectopic bone formation, but it becomes a significant barrier to the therapeutic application of exogenous BMPs in adult patients, where high levels of endogenous noggin are often present at sites of injury, inflammation, or degeneration. The clinical inefficiency of wild-type BMP-2 and BMP-7, which require supraphysiological doses to overcome this inhibition, has prompted extensive efforts to engineer more potent variants. However, previous attempts have focused on modifications that enhance receptor affinity, prolong half-life, or improve solubility, without addressing the fundamental issue of antagonist-mediated neutralization.

The present invention identifies a previously unrecognized structural determinant of noggin resistance within the mature domain of BMP-6, specifically a lysine residue at position 60, which confers a unique ability to evade inhibition despite maintaining high affinity for its receptors. This residue is not conserved in other BMPs such as BMP-7, which contains a glutamic acid at the homologous position, or BMP-2 and BMP-4, which contain proline. Structural modeling and functional analyses reveal that the lysine residue at position 60 in BMP-6 stabilizes the receptor-binding interface through intramolecular hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions that reduce conformational flexibility, thereby limiting the ability of noggin to induce a high-affinity binding conformation. In contrast, the glutamic acid residue in BMP-7 forms a salt bridge with arginine at position 48, which stabilizes the noggin-bound state and facilitates tight complex formation. The substitution of glutamic acid with lysine at this position in BMP-7 disrupts this interaction, destabilizes the BMP-noggin complex, and enhances the kinetic competition between noggin and receptors for ligand binding. This discovery reveals that resistance to noggin is not simply a function of binding affinity, but rather a consequence of dynamic structural properties that govern the stability of the ligand-inhibitor complex under physiological conditions.

The invention further demonstrates that this structural principle is transferable across the BMP family. By introducing the lysine residue at the homologous position in BMP-2, BMP-4, and other BMPs, it is possible to generate variants with significantly enhanced resistance to noggin inhibition, while preserving their native receptor-binding specificity and signaling potency. These modified BMPs exhibit a marked increase in their ability to activate downstream signaling pathways, including Smad1/5/8 phosphorylation, in the presence of physiological concentrations of noggin, as demonstrated in multiple cell types including C2C12 myoblasts, ROS 17/2.8 osteosarcoma cells, and primary human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells. Importantly, these modifications do not impair the ability of the BMPs to bind to their cognate receptors, as confirmed by receptor cross-linking assays and surface plasmon resonance studies. The modified BMPs retain their ability to induce alkaline phosphatase activity, osteocalcin expression, and matrix mineralization, and in vivo studies demonstrate that they promote more robust and rapid bone regeneration in critical-sized defect models compared to their wild-type counterparts. This represents a fundamental advance in the field, as it enables the design of BMP therapeutics that are not merely more stable or longer-lasting, but inherently less susceptible to the primary physiological mechanism that limits their efficacy.

### Interactions Between BMPs and Their Antagonists

The biological activity of bone morphogenetic proteins is exquisitely modulated by a network of extracellular antagonists that bind with high specificity and affinity to prevent ligand-receptor interactions. Among these, noggin stands out as the most potent and selective inhibitor of BMP-2, BMP-4, BMP-6, and BMP-7, acting as a molecular decoy that sequesters the ligand in a non-signaling complex. Noggin is a secreted glycoprotein composed of two tandem cystine-knot domains that form a clamp-like structure capable of enveloping the BMP ligand at its receptor-binding interface. Structural studies have revealed that noggin binds to BMPs in a 1:1 stoichiometry, with its concave surface making extensive contacts with the wrist epitope of the BMP dimer, which includes key residues from the pre-helix loop, helix α1, and the finger 1 and finger 2 regions. The binding interface is dominated by hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, and electrostatic complementarity, with the precise nature of these interactions varying between different BMP-noggin pairs.

In the case of BMP-7, the interaction with noggin is exceptionally tight, with a dissociation constant (Kd) in the low picomolar range, as determined by surface plasmon resonance. This high affinity is driven by a combination of favorable enthalpic contributions from hydrogen bonding and van der Waals contacts, as well as a slow dissociation rate that results in prolonged complex stability. A critical residue in this interaction is glutamic acid at position 60 of the mature BMP-7 domain, which forms a salt bridge with arginine at position 48 and simultaneously engages in direct hydrogen bonding with phenylalanine 54 of noggin. This triad of interactions stabilizes a conformation of BMP-7 that is highly complementary to the noggin binding surface, effectively locking the ligand into a non-functional state. The resulting complex is so stable that even in the presence of excess receptor, displacement is kinetically unfavorable, leading to profound inhibition of downstream signaling. This mechanism explains why BMP-7, despite its potent osteoinductive capacity, is rendered largely ineffective in environments where noggin is expressed, such as at fracture sites or in aged bone marrow.

In contrast, BMP-6, which shares 73% amino acid identity with BMP-7, exhibits markedly reduced susceptibility to noggin inhibition, despite exhibiting a similar or even higher apparent binding affinity in some in vitro assays. Functional assays reveal that while BMP-6 binds noggin with high affinity, the resulting complex is significantly less stable than that formed by BMP-7, as evidenced by a faster dissociation rate and a greater propensity for receptor-mediated displacement. This discrepancy arises from a single amino acid substitution: lysine at position 60 in BMP-6 replaces the glutamic acid found in BMP-7. The presence of a positively charged lysine residue at this position disrupts the salt bridge with arginine 48 and eliminates the hydrogen bonding network with noggin phenylalanine 54. Instead, lysine 60 forms intramolecular hydrogen bonds with asparagine 65 and other residues within the finger 1 and finger 2 loops, increasing the rigidity of the BMP-6 structure and reducing its conformational plasticity. This structural constraint prevents BMP-6 from adopting the precise orientation required for optimal noggin binding, thereby reducing the thermodynamic stability of the complex. The result is a ligand that, while still capable of binding noggin, does so with lower affinity and greater kinetic lability, allowing cell surface receptors to effectively compete for ligand binding even in the presence of high noggin concentrations.

This phenomenon is not merely an artifact of in vitro binding assays but has profound functional consequences. In primary human mesenchymal stem cells, BMP-6 induces robust expression of osteogenic markers such as Id-1, Dlx-5, and Msx-2 even in the presence of saturating concentrations of noggin, whereas BMP-7-mediated induction of the same genes is nearly abolished. Similarly, in reporter gene assays using BRE-luc constructs, BMP-6 retains over 80% of its activity when exposed to 1000 ng/ml of noggin, whereas BMP-7 activity is reduced by more than 95%. This differential sensitivity is not due to differences in receptor expression or downstream signaling components, as both BMPs activate the same Smad pathway and induce equivalent levels of phosphorylated Smad1/5/8 in the absence of noggin. Rather, the difference lies entirely in the ability of the ligand to evade inhibition. The discovery that a single amino acid substitution can fundamentally alter the kinetic and thermodynamic properties of ligand-inhibitor interactions provides a powerful mechanistic insight: that resistance to inhibition is not a passive property conferred by low affinity, but an active structural feature that governs the dynamics of molecular recognition.

The invention exploits this insight by engineering BMP variants that mimic the structural properties of BMP-6 at the noggin-binding interface. By replacing the glutamic acid residue in BMP-7 with lysine, or the proline residue in BMP-2 with lysine, the resulting proteins adopt a conformation that is less compatible with high-affinity noggin binding, while preserving the structural integrity required for receptor activation. This approach represents a paradigm shift from traditional protein engineering strategies, which have focused on enhancing receptor affinity or increasing half-life, to a novel paradigm centered on disrupting inhibitor binding through targeted structural modulation. The resulting modified BMPs are not only more potent in the presence of noggin, but also exhibit greater consistency of response across diverse physiological environments, making them ideal candidates for clinical applications where endogenous inhibitor levels are unpredictable or elevated.

### Generating Modified BMPs

The generation of modified bone morphogenetic proteins with enhanced resistance to noggin inhibition is achieved through precise molecular engineering of the mature domain, specifically at the residue corresponding to position 60 in human BMP-6. This residue, which lies within the finger 1 region of the BMP dimer and is part of the receptor-binding interface, has been identified as the critical determinant of noggin sensitivity. In wild-type BMP-7, this position is occupied by a glutamic acid residue, which participates in a stabilizing salt bridge with arginine at position 48 and forms direct hydrogen bonds with noggin. In BMP-6, the homologous position is occupied by a lysine residue, which disrupts these interactions and confers resistance to inhibition. The invention encompasses the substitution of this residue in BMP-7, BMP-2, BMP-4, and other BMP family members with lysine, arginine, or other positively charged amino acids to confer the desired noggin-resistant phenotype.

The process of generating these modified BMPs begins with the selection of a parental BMP sequence, typically derived from human cDNA, and the identification of the homologous residue based on sequence alignment. For BMP-7, the glutamic acid at position 60 is replaced with lysine through site-directed mutagenesis, yielding the BMP-7 E60K variant. For BMP-2, the proline at position 36 (homologous to BMP-6 position 60) is substituted with lysine to generate the BMP-2 P36K variant. Similar substitutions are made in BMP-4, BMP-5, and other BMPs using the same structural rationale. The mutagenesis is performed using standard molecular biology techniques, including the QuikChange method, overlap extension PCR, or gene synthesis, depending on the complexity of the desired modification. In each case, the nucleic acid sequence encoding the modified BMP is cloned into an appropriate expression vector under the control of a strong constitutive or inducible promoter, such as the cytomegalovirus immediate early promoter or the metallothionein promoter, to ensure high-level expression in mammalian host cells.

In addition to single amino acid substitutions, the invention encompasses the generation of chimeric BMP proteins in which larger segments of the mature domain from BMP-6 are grafted into the sequence of another BMP. For example, a chimera designated 80-1 is generated by replacing residues 45–80 of BMP-7 with the corresponding region from BMP-6, which includes the lysine at position 60 as well as surrounding residues that contribute to structural rigidity. This chimera is produced using a PCR-based assembly method in which overlapping fragments of BMP-7 and BMP-6 DNA are amplified separately and then fused together through overlapping primers. The resulting chimeric construct is cloned into the expression vector and transfected into HEK293T cells for secretion. The 80-1 chimera exhibits noggin resistance equivalent to that of native BMP-6, demonstrating that the resistance phenotype is not solely dependent on the single residue at position 60, but can be fully transferred through the inclusion of a contiguous structural module. Other chimeras, such as 40-1 and 90-1, which replace residues 1–40 and 90–120, respectively, are also generated and tested, but these variants retain sensitivity to noggin inhibition, confirming that the critical region for resistance lies within the 45–80 segment.

The invention further includes the generation of triple mutants and combinatorial variants in which multiple residues implicated in noggin binding are simultaneously modified. For instance, a BMP-7 variant containing the E60K substitution along with R48Q and Y65N mutations is generated to further destabilize the noggin-binding interface. While the E60K mutation alone confers substantial resistance, the addition of these secondary mutations does not significantly enhance the phenotype, indicating that the lysine substitution is the primary driver of the effect. This finding underscores the precision of the structural mechanism and confirms that the invention does not require extensive, non-specific mutagenesis to achieve the desired outcome. All modified BMPs are verified by DNA sequencing, and their expression is confirmed by Western blot analysis using antibodies specific for the mature domain or for epitope tags such as hemagglutinin or His-tag. The proteins are then purified and tested for biological activity using alkaline phosphatase assays, reporter gene assays, and receptor phosphorylation assays to confirm that the modifications do not impair receptor binding or downstream signaling.

The modified BMPs are further characterized for their structural integrity using circular dichroism spectroscopy, thermal denaturation assays, and mass spectrometry to ensure that the substitutions do not induce misfolding or aggregation. In all cases, the modified proteins exhibit melting temperatures and secondary structure profiles comparable to those of wild-type BMPs, confirming that the lysine substitution is structurally tolerated and does not compromise protein stability. The invention thus provides a robust and reproducible method for generating a family of modified BMPs with predictable and consistent improvements in noggin resistance, enabling their development as next-generation therapeutics for bone regeneration.

### Preparing Nucleic Acids Encoding Modified BMPs

The preparation of nucleic acid sequences encoding the modified bone morphogenetic proteins described herein is carried out through a combination of molecular cloning, gene synthesis, and codon optimization techniques designed to ensure high-level expression in a variety of host systems. The starting material for these constructs is typically a cDNA sequence encoding the full-length precursor form of the parental BMP, such as human BMP-7 (GenBank accession NM_001719), which includes the signal peptide, pro-domain, and mature domain. The nucleic acid sequence encoding the mature domain is then modified at the specific residue corresponding to position 60 of BMP-6 to introduce the desired amino acid substitution, such as lysine in place of glutamic acid in BMP-7 or lysine in place of proline in BMP-2. This modification is introduced using site-directed mutagenesis techniques, including the QuikChange method, which employs complementary oligonucleotide primers containing the desired nucleotide change to amplify the entire plasmid template in a PCR reaction, followed by digestion of the methylated parental strand with DpnI endonuclease to enrich for the mutated product.

Alternatively, for more complex modifications such as the generation of chimeric BMP proteins, overlapping PCR is employed to assemble large DNA fragments from multiple templates. In this method, three separate PCR reactions are performed: one amplifying the 5′ portion of the BMP-7 sequence upstream of the target region, another amplifying the homologous region from BMP-6, and a third amplifying the 3′ portion of BMP-7 downstream of the target region. The resulting fragments are designed with overlapping ends of 15–25 base pairs that are complementary to adjacent fragments. These fragments are then mixed and subjected to a second round of PCR without external primers, allowing the overlapping regions to anneal and serve as templates for extension. The full-length chimeric sequence is then amplified using external primers and cloned into the expression vector. This approach enables the seamless replacement of entire structural domains without introducing unwanted restriction sites or linker sequences.

To enhance expression efficiency in mammalian cells, the nucleic acid sequences encoding the modified BMPs are codon-optimized for human expression, replacing rare codons with those most frequently used in highly expressed human genes. This optimization is performed using bioinformatics tools that analyze codon usage bias across the human genome and adjust the nucleotide sequence accordingly while preserving the encoded amino acid sequence. The codon-optimized sequences are further modified to remove cryptic splice sites, destabilizing RNA motifs, and repetitive sequences that could interfere with transcription or translation. The resulting synthetic genes are synthesized de novo using solid-phase DNA synthesis and assembled into plasmid vectors containing a strong promoter, such as the cytomegalovirus immediate early promoter, a Kozak consensus sequence for efficient translation initiation, and a polyadenylation signal derived from the bovine growth hormone gene to ensure proper mRNA processing.

In addition to mammalian expression systems, nucleic acid constructs are prepared for expression in bacterial, yeast, and insect cell systems. For Escherichia coli expression, the signal peptide is removed and replaced with a bacterial secretion signal, such as the pelB or ompA leader sequence, and the mature domain is fused to an N-terminal His-tag for purification. For yeast expression in Pichia pastoris, the construct is cloned into a vector containing the alcohol oxidase 1 promoter and the α-factor secretion signal to direct secretion into the culture medium. For insect cell expression using baculovirus systems, the gene is cloned into a transfer vector under the control of the polyhedrin promoter and co-transfected with linearized baculovirus DNA to generate recombinant virus. In each case, the nucleic acid sequence is verified by sequencing, and the resulting plasmids or viral constructs are propagated and stored for subsequent transfection or infection.

The invention further includes the preparation of expression cassettes for viral delivery, including lentiviral, adenoviral, and adeno-associated viral vectors, which can be used for in vivo gene therapy applications. In these constructs, the modified BMP gene is placed under the control of a tissue-specific promoter, such as the osteocalcin promoter for bone-specific expression, and is flanked by viral packaging signals and inverted terminal repeats as required. These vectors are packaged into viral particles and titered to ensure sufficient transduction efficiency for therapeutic use. The nucleic acid constructs described herein are therefore not limited to recombinant protein production but extend to gene-based therapies for sustained local expression of noggin-resistant BMPs in bone defect sites, offering a novel strategy for long-term bone regeneration without repeated dosing.

### Expression Vectors

The invention encompasses a variety of expression vectors designed for the efficient production of modified bone morphogenetic proteins in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic host systems. These vectors are engineered to provide high-level, stable, and secreted expression of the modified BMPs while ensuring proper folding, post-translational modification, and biological activity. The core components of these vectors include a strong promoter, a signal peptide sequence for secretion, the modified BMP coding sequence, and a polyadenylation signal for mRNA stability. In mammalian expression systems, the cytomegalovirus immediate early promoter is preferred due to its ability to drive constitutive, high-level transcription in a wide range of cell types, including HEK293T, CHO, and COS-7 cells. Alternative promoters, such as the elongation factor 1-alpha (EF1α) promoter or the phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK) promoter, may be used for applications requiring more moderate or sustained expression. The signal peptide sequence is derived from the native BMP precursor or from heterologous sources such as tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) or immunoglobulin heavy chain, to ensure efficient translocation of the nascent polypeptide into the endoplasmic reticulum for proper folding and disulfide bond formation.

The modified BMP coding sequence is inserted downstream of the signal peptide in-frame and is typically fused to a C-terminal epitope tag, such as a hexahistidine (His6) tag, FLAG tag, or hemagglutinin (HA) tag, to facilitate detection and purification. In some embodiments, the tag is positioned immediately after the mature domain and is separated from it by a short protease cleavage site, such as the thrombin or TEV protease recognition sequence, to allow for removal of the tag after purification without altering the native protein sequence. The vector also contains a polyadenylation signal, such as the SV40 late polyA or bovine growth hormone polyA signal, to ensure proper 3′ end processing of the mRNA and enhance transcript stability. Selection markers, such as neomycin phosphotransferase (NeoR) for G418 resistance or hygromycin B phosphotransferase (HygroR) for hygromycin resistance, are included to enable the selection of stably transfected cell lines.

For bacterial expression systems, the vectors are designed to accommodate the absence of endoplasmic reticulum machinery and are therefore modified to exclude the signal peptide and instead include a bacterial secretion signal, such as the pelB or ompA leader, or are constructed as cytoplasmic fusion proteins with solubility-enhancing tags such as maltose-binding protein (MBP) or glutathione S-transferase (GST). These vectors are typically based on the pET or pQE plasmid backbones and contain the T7 or lac promoter, respectively, for inducible expression in E. coli strains such as BL21(DE3). For yeast expression, the vectors are based on the pPICZ or pYES2 backbones and contain the alcohol oxidase 1 (AOX1) promoter for methanol-inducible expression in Pichia pastoris, along with the α-factor secretion signal to direct the modified BMP into the culture medium. For insect cell expression, the modified BMP gene is cloned into a baculovirus transfer vector such as pFastBac1 under the control of the polyhedrin promoter, which drives very high levels of expression during the late phase of viral infection.

The invention further includes episomal vectors for transient expression in mammalian cells, such as the pcDNA3.1 and pCMV6 plasmids, which are suitable for rapid protein production without the need for stable cell line generation. These vectors are particularly useful for screening multiple modified BMP variants or for producing small quantities of protein for functional characterization. For long-term, high-yield production, stable cell lines are generated by transfecting CHO or HEK293T cells with the expression vector and selecting clones using antibiotic selection. Clones are screened for high-level secretion of the modified BMP using ELISA or Western blot analysis, and the highest-producing clones are expanded and cryopreserved for large-scale culture. The vectors may also be engineered to include elements that enhance protein secretion efficiency, such as the human immunoglobulin kappa chain leader sequence or the human serum albumin signal peptide, which have been shown to improve the yield of secreted proteins in mammalian systems.

In addition to plasmid-based systems, the invention encompasses viral vectors for gene delivery, including lentiviral, adenoviral, and adeno-associated viral constructs, which are designed to deliver the modified BMP gene directly to target tissues in vivo. These vectors are packaged into viral particles using standard methods and are engineered to include tissue-specific promoters, such as the osteocalcin promoter for bone-specific expression, to restrict transgene expression to osteoblasts and mesenchymal stem cells. The inclusion of such regulatory elements ensures that the modified BMP is expressed only at the site of injury or implantation, minimizing off-target effects. The vectors are further modified to include introns, WPRE elements, and polyadenylation signals to enhance mRNA stability and translation efficiency. The resulting expression vectors represent a comprehensive toolkit for the scalable production of modified BMPs, enabling their use in both recombinant protein manufacturing and gene therapy applications.

### Transfection/Transformation

The introduction of nucleic acid constructs encoding the modified bone morphogenetic proteins into host cells is accomplished using a variety of transfection and transformation techniques tailored to the specific host system and the intended application. In mammalian cell lines such as HEK293T, CHO, COS-7, and A549, transient transfection is performed using lipid-based reagents such as FuGENE 6 or FuGENE HD, which form non-covalent complexes with the plasmid DNA and facilitate its uptake through endocytosis. The transfection is carried out according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with DNA-to-reagent ratios optimized for each cell line to maximize transfection efficiency while minimizing cytotoxicity. Typically, cells are seeded at a density of 1–2 × 10^5 cells per well in 6-well plates or at higher densities in larger culture vessels, and transfection is performed 24 hours after seeding, when cells are in mid-logarithmic growth phase. After 24 to 48 hours, the culture medium is replaced with serum-free or low-serum medium to enhance protein secretion and reduce interference from serum proteins during downstream purification.

For the generation of stable cell lines, transfected cells are selected using antibiotics such as Geneticin (G418) or hygromycin B, which are added to the culture medium 48 hours post-transfection. Selection is maintained for 10 to 14 days, during which time non-transfected cells are eliminated, and surviving colonies are isolated, expanded, and screened for high-level expression of the modified BMP using ELISA or Western blot analysis. Clones exhibiting the highest secretion levels are cryopreserved and used for large-scale production. In some cases, dual selection systems are employed, using vectors that contain both a neomycin resistance gene and a puromycin resistance gene to allow for the selection of cells that have integrated multiple copies of the transgene, thereby increasing protein yield.

In bacterial systems, transformation is performed using chemically competent E. coli cells, such as DH5α or BL21(DE3), which are incubated with the plasmid DNA on ice for 30 minutes, followed by a brief heat shock at 42°C for 45 seconds to facilitate DNA uptake. The cells are then allowed to recover in nutrient-rich medium for one hour before being plated on agar containing the appropriate antibiotic, such as ampicillin or kanamycin, to select for transformants. Colonies are screened by colony PCR or restriction digest to confirm the presence of the correct insert. For protein expression, selected colonies are inoculated into liquid culture and grown to mid-log phase, at which point expression is induced by the addition of isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) to a final concentration of 0.1 to 1.0 mM. Cells are harvested after 4 to 16 hours of induction, depending on the expression level and solubility of the target protein.

For yeast expression in Pichia pastoris, transformation is performed using electroporation or lithium acetate-mediated methods, with linearized plasmid DNA introduced into competent cells. Transformants are selected on YPD agar plates containing zeocin or G418, depending on the selectable marker used in the vector. Positive clones are screened for methanol utilization and high-level secretion by culturing in buffered methanol-containing medium and analyzing the supernatant for the presence of the modified BMP. For insect cell expression, the baculovirus transfer vector is co-transfected with linearized baculovirus DNA into Sf9 or High Five cells using a transfection reagent such as Cellfectin II. Recombinant virus is harvested from the supernatant after 72 hours, amplified through multiple rounds of infection, and titrated to determine the optimal multiplicity of infection for large-scale protein production.

In addition to these standard methods, the invention encompasses the use of advanced transfection technologies such as nucleofection for hard-to-transfect primary cells, or magnetofection for enhanced delivery efficiency. For in vivo gene delivery, the nucleic acid constructs are packaged into viral vectors and administered via direct injection into the bone defect site or systemic delivery, depending on the therapeutic goal. The choice of transfection or transformation method is dictated by the host system, the scale of production, and the intended application, but in all cases, the goal is to achieve high levels of expression of the modified BMP with minimal cellular stress and maximal secretion into the culture medium or extracellular space.

### Hosts for Expression of Modified BMPs

The production of modified bone morphogenetic proteins with enhanced resistance to noggin inhibition requires the use of appropriate host systems capable of supporting the complex post-translational modifications necessary for biological activity. The invention encompasses a range of host organisms, including mammalian, bacterial, yeast, and insect cell lines, each offering distinct advantages depending on the scale, purity, and functional requirements of the final product. Mammalian cell lines, particularly human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK293T) and Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells, are the preferred hosts for the production of modified BMPs intended for clinical use due to their ability to perform human-like glycosylation, disulfide bond formation, and proteolytic processing of the pro-domain. These cells are capable of secreting fully processed, biologically active BMPs into the culture medium, allowing for straightforward purification and minimizing the risk of immunogenic modifications.

HEK293T cells are particularly advantageous due to their high transfection efficiency, rapid growth rate, and robust secretion capacity. When transfected with plasmids encoding the modified BMPs, these cells consistently produce high yields of secreted protein, with concentrations reaching several micrograms per milliliter in serum-free medium. The conditioned medium is collected 48 to 72 hours post-transfection and subjected to purification without the need for cell lysis, preserving the integrity of the secreted protein. CHO cells, while slower growing, offer superior scalability for large-scale industrial production and are widely used in the biopharmaceutical industry for the manufacture of therapeutic proteins. Stable CHO cell lines expressing the modified BMPs can be maintained in bioreactors under controlled conditions, enabling continuous production over extended periods. These cell lines are particularly useful for generating clinical-grade material for human trials, as they meet regulatory standards for protein purity, consistency, and absence of adventitious agents.

Bacterial hosts, such as Escherichia coli, are employed for the rapid, low-cost production of modified BMPs for research and preclinical applications. While E. coli lacks the machinery for glycosylation and cannot process the native BMP signal peptide, the invention enables the production of functional BMPs by removing the signal peptide and expressing only the mature domain as a soluble fusion protein. The mature domain is fused to solubility-enhancing tags such as maltose-binding protein or glutathione S-transferase, which prevent aggregation and facilitate purification. After expression, the fusion protein is purified using affinity chromatography, and the tag is cleaved off using a site-specific protease, leaving the mature BMP intact. Although the resulting protein lacks post-translational modifications, it retains full biological activity in cell-based assays, making it suitable for in vitro studies and screening applications.

Yeast systems, particularly Pichia pastoris, offer a middle ground between bacterial and mammalian systems, providing high-level secretion and the ability to perform some post-translational modifications, including disulfide bond formation and limited glycosylation. The modified BMPs are secreted into the culture medium under the control of the alcohol oxidase 1 promoter, which is induced by methanol. The resulting protein is purified using ion-exchange and size-exclusion chromatography, and its activity is validated using alkaline phosphatase and reporter gene assays. Yeast-produced BMPs are particularly useful for applications requiring large quantities of protein at lower cost than mammalian systems, such as scaffold impregnation or animal studies.

Insect cell systems, such as Sf9 or High Five cells infected with recombinant baculovirus, are used to produce modified BMPs with complex disulfide bonding and high yields. The baculovirus system supports the expression of full-length precursor proteins, including the pro-domain, which can be processed in vitro using furin or trypsin to generate the mature domain. This system is particularly valuable for producing modified BMPs that require precise folding and multimerization, as the insect cell environment closely mimics the endoplasmic reticulum of mammalian cells. The resulting proteins are purified from the culture supernatant and exhibit biological activity comparable to those produced in mammalian cells.

The invention further encompasses the use of transgenic animals or plants as alternative expression platforms, although these are not preferred for clinical applications. In all cases, the choice of host system is guided by the intended use of the modified BMP, with mammalian systems reserved for therapeutic applications and bacterial, yeast, or insect systems used for research, screening, or non-clinical purposes. The modified BMPs produced in these hosts are functionally equivalent, demonstrating that the noggin-resistant phenotype is an intrinsic property of the amino acid sequence and is not dependent on the host-specific post-translational modifications.

### Expression Methods

The expression of modified bone morphogenetic proteins is carried out under carefully controlled conditions to ensure optimal protein folding, secretion, and biological activity. In mammalian cell systems such as HEK293T and CHO cells, expression is initiated by transfecting cells with plasmid DNA encoding the modified BMP using lipid-based reagents such as FuGENE 6 or FuGENE HD. Transfection efficiency is monitored by co-transfection with a GFP-expressing plasmid, and conditions are optimized to achieve transfection rates exceeding 80% without inducing significant cytotoxicity. Twenty-four hours after transfection, the culture medium is replaced with serum-free or low-serum medium containing 0.5% fetal bovine serum to reduce background protein contamination and enhance the recovery of the secreted modified BMP. The cells are then incubated for an additional 48 to 72 hours, during which time the modified BMP is secreted into the medium.

For large-scale production, cells are cultured in 150 cm² or 225 cm² flasks, with volumes ranging from 50 to 100 ml per flask, and the conditioned medium is collected and pooled. In bioreactor systems, CHO cells are grown in stirred-tank or wave bioreactors under controlled pH, temperature, and dissolved oxygen conditions to maintain cell viability and maximize protein yield. The medium is supplemented with trace elements, antioxidants, and anti-clumping agents to prevent aggregation and maintain cell health. The conditioned medium is harvested periodically, filtered through 0.22 µm membranes to remove cellular debris, and concentrated using ultrafiltration devices with molecular weight cutoffs of 10 kDa or less to retain the modified BMP while removing smaller contaminants.

In bacterial systems, expression is induced by adding isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) to cultures of E. coli grown to mid-log phase, with induction typically occurring at an optical density of 0.6 to 0.8 at 600 nm. The temperature is lowered to 18–25°C to promote proper folding and reduce inclusion body formation, and induction is maintained for 16 to 24 hours. Cells are harvested by centrifugation, lysed by sonication or French press, and the soluble fraction is collected by centrifugation. The modified BMP is then purified from the lysate using affinity chromatography, typically via immobilized metal ion affinity chromatography (IMAC) if a His-tag is present.

In yeast systems, Pichia pastoris cultures are grown in buffered glycerol medium until the cells reach high density, at which point methanol is added to induce expression via the alcohol oxidase 1 promoter. Induction is maintained for 72 to 96 hours, with methanol replenished every 24 hours to sustain expression. The culture supernatant is harvested, filtered, and concentrated, and the modified BMP is purified using ion-exchange and size-exclusion chromatography.

In insect cell systems, Sf9 or High Five cells are infected with recombinant baculovirus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1 to 5, and the culture is maintained for 72 to 96 hours post-infection. The supernatant is collected and processed as described above. In all systems, the expression conditions are optimized to minimize proteolytic degradation, aggregation, and oxidation of the modified BMP. The resulting protein is analyzed for purity by SDS-PAGE and silver staining, and for biological activity by reporter gene assays, alkaline phosphatase assays, and receptor phosphorylation assays. The modified BMPs produced by these methods exhibit consistent and reproducible activity, confirming that the expression system does not alter the fundamental noggin-resistant phenotype conferred by the amino acid substitution.

### Purification

The purification of modified bone morphogenetic proteins from culture supernatants or cell lysates is a critical step in ensuring the safety, efficacy, and consistency of the final therapeutic product. The process is designed to isolate the modified BMP with high purity, remove contaminants such as host cell proteins, nucleic acids, endotoxins, and aggregates, and preserve the structural integrity and biological activity of the protein. The purification strategy is tailored to the expression system and the presence of affinity tags, but generally follows a multi-step chromatographic approach that includes capture, intermediate purification, and polishing steps.

For proteins expressed in mammalian cells, the conditioned medium is first clarified by centrifugation and filtration through 0.22 µm membranes. The medium is then concentrated using ultrafiltration devices with a molecular weight cutoff of 10 kDa to reduce volume and increase protein concentration. The concentrated supernatant is loaded onto a nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) affinity column if the modified BMP contains a hexahistidine (His6) tag at the C-terminus. The column is washed with buffer containing 20 mM HEPES, 0.5 M NaCl, and 10 mM imidazole to remove weakly bound contaminants, and the modified BMP is eluted with buffer containing 20 mM HEPES, 0.5 M NaCl, and 250–500 mM imidazole. The eluate is immediately dialyzed against a low-salt buffer to remove imidazole and reduce ionic strength prior to further purification.

The Ni-NTA-purified fraction is then subjected to ion-exchange chromatography, typically using a cation-exchange resin such as SP Sepharose or Source 15S, which separates the modified BMP from residual host proteins based on charge differences. The protein is eluted using a linear gradient of sodium chloride from 0.1 M to 0.5 M over 20 column volumes. Fractions containing the modified BMP are identified by SDS-PAGE and Western blot analysis, pooled, and concentrated again by ultrafiltration. The sample is then subjected to size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) using a Superdex 75 or Superdex 200 column to remove aggregates, dimers, or misfolded species and to exchange the buffer into a formulation suitable for storage or administration. The SEC-purified protein is analyzed for monomeric content by analytical SEC and for purity by SDS-PAGE under reducing and non-reducing conditions, with purity typically exceeding 95%.

For proteins expressed in bacterial systems, the soluble fraction is loaded directly onto a Ni-NTA column, followed by ion-exchange and SEC as described above. If the protein is expressed without a tag, heparin-affinity chromatography is employed, as BMPs bind strongly to heparin due to their positively charged surface. The heparin column is washed with low-salt buffer and eluted with a salt gradient, and the eluate is further purified by SEC. In all cases, the final purified protein is analyzed for endotoxin content using the Limulus amebocyte lysate (LAL) assay, with levels required to be below 0.1 endotoxin units per microgram of protein for clinical use. The protein is then lyophilized or formulated in a sterile, isotonic buffer containing 10 mM histidine, 150 mM sodium chloride, and 0.01% polysorbate 80, pH 6.5, for long-term storage at -80°C.

The purified modified BMPs are validated for biological activity using a panel of functional assays, including the A549-BRE luciferase reporter assay, the ROS 17/2.8 alkaline phosphatase assay, and Smad1/5/8 phosphorylation in C2C12 cells. The specific activity of the purified protein is determined by comparing its potency to that of commercially available wild-type BMPs, and the modified BMPs consistently demonstrate equal or greater activity per unit mass, confirming that the purification process does not impair function. The final product is sterile-filtered, tested for sterility and mycoplasma contamination, and stored under controlled conditions to ensure stability for clinical use.

## EXAMPLES

### BMP Induction of Alkaline Phosphatase Activity

The osteogenic potency of a panel of bone morphogenetic proteins was systematically evaluated by measuring their ability to induce alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity in the rat osteosarcoma cell line ROS 17/2.8, a well-established model for early osteoblast differentiation. Cells were seeded at a density of 3.7 × 10⁴ cells per well in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight in F-12 medium supplemented with 0.2% bovine serum albumin. The following day, cells were stimulated with increasing concentrations of recombinant human BMP-2, BMP-4, BMP-5, BMP-6, BMP-7, GDF-5, and GDF-6, each ranging from 0.1 ng/ml to 1000 ng/ml, in triplicate. After 48 hours of stimulation, cells were lysed with 2% Triton X-100, and ALP activity was quantified by adding p-nitrophenyl phosphate (pNPP) substrate and measuring absorbance at 405 nm after 10 minutes of incubation at 37°C. The reaction was stopped with sodium hydroxide, and ALP activity was normalized to total protein content as determined by the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay.

The dose-response curves revealed a striking hierarchy of potency, with BMP-6 exhibiting the highest activity, achieving half-maximal stimulation at an EC₅₀ of 14 ng/ml, followed by BMP-7 at 45 ng/ml, BMP-5 at 80 ng/ml, BMP-4 at 120 ng/ml, and BMP-2 at 150 ng/ml. GDF-5 and GDF-6 showed minimal activity, with EC₅₀ values exceeding 500 ng/ml. The slopes of the dose-response curves for BMP-2 and BMP-4 were notably shallower than those for BMP-5, BMP-6, and BMP-7, suggesting the presence of stronger negative regulatory mechanisms for these ligands. The superior potency of BMP-6 was further confirmed in C2C12 myoblasts, where ALP induction at 100 ng/ml was significantly greater than that induced by BMP-7, despite the fact that both proteins are structurally homologous and share 73% amino acid identity. These results established that BMP-6 is a more potent inducer of osteoblast differentiation than BMP-7, a finding that was unexpected given their high sequence similarity and suggested the presence of functional differences not attributable to receptor affinity alone.

### Noggin Inhibition of a Panel of Exemplary BMPs and Related Proteins

To determine the susceptibility of various BMPs to inhibition by the endogenous antagonist noggin, a series of dose-response experiments were conducted in which the ability of BMP-2, BMP-4, BMP-6, and BMP-7 to induce ALP activity in ROS 17/2.8 cells was measured in the presence of increasing concentrations of recombinant noggin-Fc fusion protein. Cells were seeded as described above and stimulated with a fixed concentration of each BMP (100 ng/ml) in the presence of noggin concentrations ranging from 0.1 ng/ml to 10,000 ng/ml. After 24 hours, ALP activity was measured and normalized to total protein. The percentage inhibition was calculated using the formula: [(A - B)/A] × 100, where A represents ALP activity in the absence of noggin and B represents activity in the presence of noggin. The IC₅₀ values, defined as the concentration of noggin required to inhibit 50% of BMP activity, were derived from non-linear regression analysis of the dose-response curves.

The results revealed a dramatic difference in noggin sensitivity among the BMPs tested. BMP-4 was the most sensitive, with an IC₅₀ of 12 ng/ml, followed by BMP-2 at 45 ng/ml and BMP-7 at 800 ng/ml. In stark contrast, BMP-6 exhibited exceptional resistance to noggin inhibition, with an IC₅₀ exceeding 5,000 ng/ml, representing a more than sixfold increase in resistance compared to BMP-7. This finding was confirmed in a parallel assay using the A549-BRE luciferase reporter cell line, where BMP-6 retained over 80% of its activity at 1,000 ng/ml noggin, whereas BMP-7 activity was reduced by more than 90%. The resistance of BMP-6 was not due to lower expression levels, as Western blot analysis confirmed equivalent secretion of all BMPs from transfected HEK293T cells. These data established that the resistance to noggin inhibition is an intrinsic property of BMP-6 and not an artifact of expression or assay conditions. The magnitude of this difference was unprecedented among BMP family members and suggested that a structural feature unique to BMP-6 confers protection against this major physiological inhibitor.

### BMP-6 Induction of Downstream Genes in Primary Human Bone Marrow-Derived Mesenchymal Stem Cells is Less Susceptible to Noggin Inhibition

To determine whether the resistance of BMP-6 to noggin inhibition extends to primary human cells relevant to bone regeneration, primary human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) were isolated from healthy donors and cultured in osteogenic medium. Cells were stimulated with 50 ng/ml of either BMP-6 or BMP-7 in the presence or absence of 1,000 ng/ml of recombinant noggin-Fc. After 24 hours, total RNA was extracted, reverse transcribed, and analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR for the expression of key osteogenic target genes, including Id-1, Dlx-5, Msx-2, and noggin itself. Gene expression was normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and expressed as fold induction relative to unstimulated controls.

BMP-7 robustly induced the expression of all four genes, with Id-1 increasing by 12-fold, Dlx-5 by 8-fold, Msx-2 by 6-fold, and noggin by 15-fold. However, in the presence of noggin, the induction of Id-1, Dlx-5, and Msx-2 by BMP-7 was reduced by 80–90%, and the induction of noggin itself was nearly abolished. In contrast, BMP-6 induced Id-1 by 14-fold, Dlx-5 by 10-fold, Msx-2 by 7-fold, and noggin by 18-fold in the absence of noggin. When noggin was added, the induction of Id-1 and Dlx-5 by BMP-6 remained at 85–90% of control levels, Msx-2 was reduced by only 20%, and noggin induction was still elevated by 12-fold. These results demonstrated that BMP-6 is uniquely capable of maintaining robust transcriptional activation of osteogenic genes even in the presence of high concentrations of its own inhibitor, a property not shared by BMP-7 or other BMPs tested. The finding that noggin fails to suppress its own induction by BMP-6 further suggests that the resistance mechanism operates at the level of ligand-receptor interaction rather than transcriptional feedback, highlighting the potential of BMP-6 as a superior therapeutic agent in environments where noggin levels are elevated.

### BMP-7 Variant Proteins with Marked Resistance to Noggin Inhibition

To determine whether the noggin-resistant phenotype of BMP-6 could be transferred to BMP-7, a series of site-directed mutants of BMP-7 were generated, with the glutamic acid residue at position 60 substituted with lysine (E60K), arginine (E60R), asparagine (E60N), or glutamine (E60Q). The mutant constructs were transfected into HEK293T cells, and the conditioned medium was collected and purified using Ni-NTA affinity chromatography. The purified proteins were then tested for their ability to induce luciferase activity in A549-BRE reporter cells in the presence of increasing concentrations of noggin. The residual activity was calculated as the ratio of luciferase activity in the presence of noggin to that in its absence.

The BMP-7 E60K variant exhibited a dramatic increase in resistance to noggin inhibition, retaining 75% of its activity at 1,000 ng/ml noggin, compared to less than 10% for wild-type BMP-7. The E60R variant also showed significant resistance, retaining 65% activity, while E60N and E60Q retained only 20–30% activity, indicating that a positively charged residue at this position is essential for the phenotype. The E60K variant was further tested in primary hMSCs, where it induced Id-1 and Dlx-5 expression at levels comparable to BMP-6, even in the presence of noggin. The modified protein was also tested in vivo using a rabbit ulnar critical-sized defect model, where implantation of 100 µg of BMP-7 E60K on a collagen sponge induced bone bridging at 4 weeks, whereas the same dose of wild-type BMP-7 showed only partial calcification. By 8 weeks, micro-CT analysis revealed that BMP-7 E60K induced 1.8-fold more bone volume than wild-type BMP-7, demonstrating that the single amino acid substitution not only enhances resistance to noggin in vitro but also translates into superior bone regeneration in vivo. These results established that the lysine residue at position 60 is both necessary and sufficient to confer noggin resistance to BMP-7, and that this modification can be used to generate a novel class of therapeutic BMPs with enhanced efficacy.

### Example 5

The invention further encompasses the generation of a modified BMP-2 protein in which the proline residue at position 36, homologous to position 60 in BMP-6, is substituted with lysine (BMP-2 P36K). This variant was produced using the same site-directed mutagenesis and purification methods as described for BMP-7 E60K. The purified BMP-2 P36K protein was tested for its resistance to noggin inhibition using the A549-BRE reporter assay and the ROS 17/2.8 ALP assay. In the presence of 1,000 ng/ml noggin, wild-type BMP-2 retained less than 15% of its activity, whereas BMP-2 P36K retained 55% of its activity, representing a more than threefold improvement in resistance. The modified protein was also tested in a murine ectopic bone formation model, where 50 µg of BMP-2 P36K implanted in the quadriceps muscle of C57BL/6 mice induced the formation of mature, mineralized bone nodules at 4 weeks, whereas the same dose of wild-type BMP-2 induced only fibrous tissue and minimal calcification. Histological analysis confirmed the presence of osteoblasts, osteoid, and trabecular bone in the BMP-2 P36K implants, whereas wild-type BMP-2 implants showed only inflammatory infiltrates and collagen deposition. These results demonstrate that the noggin-resistance phenotype conferred by the lysine substitution at the homologous position is transferable across the BMP family and can be used to enhance the therapeutic potential of other BMPs beyond BMP-6 and BMP-7. The invention thus provides a generalizable strategy for engineering a new generation of BMP therapeutics with superior resistance to endogenous inhibition and enhanced clinical efficacy.
