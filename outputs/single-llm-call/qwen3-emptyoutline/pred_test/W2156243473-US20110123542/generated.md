# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of oncology and molecular medicine, specifically to novel therapeutic strategies for the treatment of cancer through the modulation of chemokine signaling pathways. More particularly, the invention concerns methods and compositions for inhibiting tumor growth, invasion, and vascularization by targeting the interaction between the chemokine CCL20 and its receptor CCR6, as well as the upstream regulatory relationship between the chemokine receptor CXCR4 and the production of CCL20 in malignant cells. The invention encompasses the use of neutralizing antibodies, small molecule inhibitors, RNA interference agents, and other biologically active compounds directed against CCL20, CCR6, or their signaling intermediates, for the treatment of a broad spectrum of human cancers including but not limited to prostate cancer, colon cancer, acute myeloid leukemia, and other malignancies characterized by aberrant co-expression of CXCR4, CCL20, and CCR6. The invention further includes diagnostic methods for identifying patients likely to benefit from such therapies based on the expression profile of these chemokine components in tumor tissues. The therapeutic approaches disclosed herein are grounded in the discovery that CCL20 functions as a critical autocrine and paracrine growth and survival factor for cancer cells expressing CCR6, and that its expression is directly upregulated by CXCR4 signaling, thereby establishing a previously unrecognized signaling axis that drives tumor progression independently and synergistically of the canonical CXCL12/CXCR4 pathway.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide, and despite advances in surgical, radiological, and pharmacological interventions, many solid and hematological malignancies continue to exhibit poor prognoses due to their capacity for invasion, metastasis, and resistance to conventional therapies. The tumor microenvironment plays a pivotal role in facilitating these aggressive behaviors, and among its most influential components are chemokines—small secreted signaling proteins that orchestrate the migration and activation of immune and non-immune cells. While traditionally studied for their roles in inflammation and immune cell trafficking, chemokines have emerged as central mediators of tumor progression, influencing angiogenesis, immune evasion, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and metastatic seeding. Among the most extensively studied chemokine receptors in oncology is CXCR4, which is overexpressed in a wide array of human cancers, including breast, prostate, pancreatic, glioblastoma, and hematological malignancies. Its ligand, CXCL12, is frequently expressed in common metastatic sites such as the lymph nodes, bone marrow, liver, and lungs, creating a chemotactic gradient that directs cancer cell dissemination. The CXCR4/CXCL12 axis has been validated as a therapeutic target, and multiple inhibitors have entered clinical trials, though their efficacy has been limited by compensatory signaling pathways and tumor heterogeneity.

In parallel with the investigation of CXCR4, other chemokine systems have been implicated in cancer biology, but their roles have often been viewed as secondary or context-dependent. One such system involves the CC chemokine CCL20 and its sole receptor, CCR6. CCL20 is known to be constitutively expressed in barrier tissues such as the skin and intestinal mucosa, where it recruits immature dendritic cells, memory T cells, and Th17 lymphocytes to maintain immune surveillance. In the context of cancer, CCL20 has been detected in multiple tumor types, including colorectal, pancreatic, and prostate carcinomas, yet its functional significance has remained controversial. Some studies suggest that CCL20 may enhance antitumor immunity by attracting dendritic cells, while others report that its presence correlates with increased tumor aggressiveness and poor clinical outcomes. This dichotomy has hindered the development of targeted therapies against the CCL20/CCR6 axis, as the underlying mechanisms driving its pro-tumorigenic effects have not been clearly defined. Moreover, no prior work has established a direct molecular link between CXCR4 and the transcriptional or translational regulation of CCL20 in cancer cells, nor has any study demonstrated that CCL20 functions as an autocrine growth factor for cancer cells expressing CCR6 in a manner that is independent of classical G-protein-coupled receptor signaling.

The existing literature has largely focused on the role of CCR6 in immune cell recruitment within the tumor microenvironment, with little attention paid to its expression on the cancer cells themselves. While some reports have noted CCR6 expression on certain tumor cell lines, the functional consequences of this expression have not been systematically explored. In particular, there is no prior evidence that CCL20 directly stimulates cancer cell proliferation, adhesion to extracellular matrix components, or tumor vascularization through autocrine signaling. Furthermore, the notion that CXCR4, a well-characterized G-protein-coupled receptor, can regulate the expression of a chemokine ligand that acts on a distinct receptor expressed on the same cell line has not been described. The absence of such a mechanistic framework has left a critical gap in our understanding of how chemokine networks cooperate to drive tumor progression. Consequently, therapeutic efforts have remained narrowly focused on blocking CXCL12-CXCR4 interactions, without addressing the downstream effectors that may sustain tumor growth even when this primary axis is inhibited. This limitation is particularly relevant in clinical settings where tumors develop resistance to CXCR4 antagonists through alternative signaling pathways. The discovery that CXCR4 upregulates CCL20 expression, and that CCL20 in turn promotes tumor cell survival and invasion via CCR6, reveals a novel and previously unrecognized signaling cascade that operates in parallel to, and in some cases independently of, the canonical CXCL12/CXCR4 pathway. This finding fundamentally alters the conceptual understanding of chemokine-driven oncogenesis and provides a compelling rationale for the development of therapies targeting CCL20 or CCR6 as a means to suppress tumor growth, inhibit metastasis, and overcome resistance to existing CXCR4-targeted agents.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive and novel therapeutic strategy for the treatment of cancer by targeting the CCL20/CCR6 signaling axis, which is shown herein to be a critical downstream effector of CXCR4-mediated tumor progression. The invention is based on the groundbreaking discovery that the chemokine receptor CXCR4, when activated by its canonical ligand CXCL12 or through ligand-independent mechanisms, directly upregulates the expression and secretion of the chemokine CCL20 in a variety of human cancer cell types, including prostate, colon, and leukemic malignancies. This upregulation occurs at both the transcriptional and translational levels and is mediated through a JAK/STAT signaling pathway that is distinct from the classical G-protein-dependent pathways traditionally associated with CXCR4 activation. Importantly, the invention demonstrates that CCL20 is not merely a bystander molecule in the tumor microenvironment but functions as a potent autocrine and paracrine growth factor for cancer cells that express its receptor, CCR6. The co-expression of CCL20 and CCR6 on the same tumor cells creates a self-sustaining signaling loop that drives proliferation, enhances adhesion to extracellular matrix components such as collagen type I, and promotes tumor vascularization and invasion in vivo. This autocrine loop is independent of CXCL12, as neutralizing antibodies against CXCL12 fail to fully suppress CCL20 secretion in cells with high CXCR4 expression, and the pathway remains active even in the presence of pertussis toxin, which inhibits G-protein signaling. The invention further establishes that the inhibition of CCL20, either through neutralizing antibodies, RNA interference, or competitive receptor antagonists, significantly suppresses tumor growth, reduces vascularization, and inhibits invasion in multiple preclinical models of cancer, including xenografts derived from prostate, colon, and leukemic cells. The invention also demonstrates that neutralization of CCL20 is effective not only in tumors that overexpress CCL20 directly but also in tumors that upregulate CCL20 via CXCR4, thereby providing a mechanism to overcome resistance to CXCR4-targeted therapies. The invention further includes diagnostic methods for identifying patients whose tumors exhibit co-expression of CXCR4, CCL20, and CCR6, thereby enabling patient stratification for targeted therapy. The compositions and methods disclosed herein represent a paradigm shift in cancer therapeutics by targeting a previously unrecognized chemokine signaling axis that operates as a central driver of tumor progression across multiple cancer types, offering a broadly applicable and highly specific therapeutic approach with significant potential to improve clinical outcomes.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a detailed and comprehensive framework for the therapeutic targeting of the CCL20/CCR6 signaling axis in cancer, grounded in the novel discovery that the chemokine receptor CXCR4 regulates the expression of CCL20 in a manner that is independent of its canonical ligand CXCL12 and that CCL20, in turn, functions as a critical autocrine mediator of tumor cell proliferation, adhesion, and vascularization through its receptor CCR6. This signaling cascade, which has not been previously described in the scientific literature, constitutes a fundamental mechanism by which cancer cells exploit chemokine networks to promote their own survival and dissemination. The invention encompasses a wide range of therapeutic modalities designed to disrupt this axis at multiple points, including the use of neutralizing antibodies against CCL20 or CCR6, small molecule inhibitors of CCR6, RNA interference constructs targeting CCL20 or CCR6 mRNA, and agents that interfere with the upstream signaling pathways responsible for CXCR4-mediated CCL20 induction, particularly the JAK/STAT pathway. The invention further includes diagnostic methods for identifying patients who are most likely to benefit from these therapies based on the immunohistochemical or molecular profiling of tumor tissues for the co-expression of CXCR4, CCL20, and CCR6. The therapeutic efficacy of these interventions has been validated in multiple in vitro and in vivo models, demonstrating that inhibition of CCL20 or CCR6 leads to a significant reduction in tumor growth, decreased vascular density, and impaired invasion into surrounding tissues, even in tumors that are resistant to CXCR4 blockade. The invention is not limited to any single cancer type but is broadly applicable to any malignancy characterized by the co-expression of CXCR4 and CCL20, with or without concomitant CCR6 expression on tumor cells. The discovery that CCL20 can act as an autocrine growth factor for cancer cells expressing CCR6 represents a paradigm shift in our understanding of chemokine biology in oncology, as it reveals that chemokines can function not only as chemoattractants for immune cells but also as direct mitogens for malignant cells, thereby blurring the traditional distinction between immune mediators and tumor growth factors. The invention further demonstrates that the CCL20/CCR6 axis operates independently of the classical G-protein-coupled receptor signaling pathways, as pertussis toxin, which inhibits Gi protein signaling, fails to fully suppress CCL20 secretion in CXCR4-overexpressing cells, indicating that CXCR4 signals through alternative pathways such as JAK2 and STAT3 to induce CCL20 transcription. This finding is further supported by the observation that pharmacological inhibition of JAK2 with AG-490 completely abrogates CCL20 production, while inhibition of the MEK/ERK pathway results in a paradoxical increase in CCL20 secretion, suggesting a complex regulatory network in which multiple signaling cascades converge to modulate chemokine expression. The invention further establishes that the autocrine loop formed by CCL20 and CCR6 is dose-dependent, with low to moderate concentrations of CCL20 promoting tumor cell proliferation and adhesion, while higher concentrations lead to receptor desensitization and reduced responsiveness, a phenomenon consistent with the known behavior of chemokine receptors upon prolonged ligand exposure. This biphasic effect has important implications for therapeutic dosing, as it suggests that sustained, low-level inhibition of CCL20 may be more effective than acute, high-dose blockade. The invention further demonstrates that the CCL20/CCR6 axis is not restricted to prostate cancer but is operational in a variety of other malignancies, including colon carcinoma, acute myeloid leukemia, and potentially other cancers that express both CXCR4 and CCR6. The co-expression of these molecules in human tumor tissues has been confirmed through immunohistochemical analysis of clinical specimens, revealing that the majority of advanced prostate cancers and a significant proportion of colon cancers exhibit concurrent expression of CXCR4, CCL20, and CCR6, with the highest levels correlating with higher Gleason scores and more aggressive disease phenotypes. The invention further provides evidence that neutralizing antibodies against CCL20 can suppress tumor growth not only in models where CCL20 is overexpressed directly but also in models where CCL20 is induced by CXCR4, thereby establishing a dual mechanism of action that can be leveraged to treat both CXCR4-dependent and CXCR4-independent tumors. The therapeutic potential of this approach is further enhanced by the fact that CCL20 is not expressed at significant levels in most normal adult tissues, suggesting that targeting this axis may have a favorable safety profile with minimal off-target effects. The invention further includes methods for the production of high-affinity monoclonal antibodies against human CCL20 or CCR6, as well as recombinant fusion proteins, peptide inhibitors, and small molecule antagonists that can be optimized for pharmacokinetic properties suitable for systemic administration. The invention further encompasses the use of these agents in combination with existing cancer therapies, including chemotherapy, radiation, immunotherapy, and other targeted agents, to enhance their efficacy and overcome resistance. The invention further provides detailed protocols for the detection and quantification of CCL20 and CCR6 expression in tumor biopsies, including immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry, real-time PCR, and ELISA, enabling the development of companion diagnostics to guide patient selection. The invention further discloses that the inhibition of CCL20 leads to a reduction in tumor vascularization, as evidenced by decreased microvessel density and impaired oxygen delivery within the tumor mass, as measured by functional MRI, indicating that CCL20 plays a direct role in promoting angiogenesis. This effect is not mediated by the recruitment of endothelial progenitor cells but rather by the direct stimulation of vascular endothelial cells or by the induction of other angiogenic factors such as VEGF, which are upregulated in response to CCL20 signaling. The invention further demonstrates that the CCL20/CCR6 axis contributes to tumor invasion by enhancing the adhesion of cancer cells to collagen type I, a major component of the extracellular matrix found in stromal tissues, thereby facilitating local infiltration and metastatic spread. The invention further provides evidence that the inhibition of CCL20 results in increased tumor necrosis, likely due to the loss of pro-survival signaling and the failure to establish functional vasculature, further supporting the notion that CCL20 is essential for maintaining tumor viability. The invention further includes the use of gene silencing technologies, such as siRNA or shRNA, to specifically knock down CCL20 or CCR6 expression in tumor cells, either through direct intratumoral delivery or via systemic administration of nanoparticle-encapsulated constructs, thereby providing an alternative therapeutic strategy for patients who are not candidates for antibody-based therapies. The invention further encompasses the use of CRISPR/Cas9 technology to permanently disrupt the CCL20 or CCR6 genes in cancer cells, either ex vivo for adoptive cell therapies or in vivo through targeted delivery systems. The invention further provides evidence that the CCL20/CCR6 axis is conserved across multiple species, allowing for the development of preclinical models in rodents that accurately recapitulate the human disease, thereby facilitating the translation of these findings into clinical applications. The invention further includes the use of biomarkers derived from the CCL20/CCR6 axis, such as serum levels of CCL20 or the ratio of CCL20 to CXCL12, as prognostic indicators for disease progression and response to therapy. The invention further discloses that the inhibition of CCL20 does not lead to compensatory upregulation of other chemokines, suggesting that this axis is not redundant within the broader chemokine network and that its blockade is unlikely to be bypassed by alternative signaling mechanisms. The invention further provides evidence that the CCL20/CCR6 axis is active in cancer stem cells, which are known to be responsible for tumor recurrence and therapy resistance, thereby offering a strategy to target the root cause of disease persistence. ## EXAMPLES

### Experimental Procedures

All experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for the ethical use of animals and human tissues, and all protocols were approved by the relevant institutional review boards and animal care committees. Human cancer cell lines, including PC3, LNCaP, 22RV1, DU145, NB4, HL60, and HT-29, were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection and maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 2 mM L-glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination using a commercial PCR-based detection kit and were found to be negative. For the generation of stable cell lines overexpressing CXCR4 or CCL20, parental PC3 cells were transfected with pcDNA3.1 vectors encoding full-length human CXCR4 or human CCL20 cDNA using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transfected cells were selected with 800 µg/mL G418 for two weeks, and single-cell clones were isolated by limiting dilution. Clones with the highest expression of the transgene were selected for further experiments based on flow cytometry and ELISA analysis. For the generation of CCL20-overexpressing clones, supernatants were collected after 48 hours of culture, and CCL20 concentration was measured using a commercially available human CCL20 ELISA kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN). Clones with CCL20 secretion levels exceeding 500 pg/mL were selected for in vivo studies. For RNA interference experiments, Dicer-substrate siRNAs targeting human CXCR4 (sense: 5′-UAAAAUCUUCCUGCCCACCAUCUAC-3′; antisense: 5′-GUAGAUGGUGGGCAGGAAGAUUUUAUU-3′) or non-targeting control siRNA were purchased from IDT (Coralville, IA). PC3-CXCR4.5 cells were transfected with 200 nM siRNA using Oligofectamine reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) in serum-free medium for 6 hours, followed by replacement with complete medium. Cells were harvested 48 hours post-transfection for analysis of CXCR4 and CCL20 expression by flow cytometry and ELISA. For pharmacological inhibition experiments, cells were treated with the JAK2 inhibitor AG-490 (1 µM, Calbiochem), the MEK inhibitor PD98059 (20 µM, Calbiochem), or pertussis toxin (5 µg/mL, List Biological Laboratories) for 24 to 48 hours prior to stimulation with CXCL12 (5–1000 ng/mL, PeproTech EC, London, UK). For neutralization experiments, cells were treated with monoclonal anti-human CCL20 antibody (MAB360, R&D Systems) or isotype-matched control IgG1 (10 µg/mL) for 24 to 48 hours prior to functional assays. Cell proliferation was assessed by seeding cells at 2×10⁴ cells per well in 24-well plates in serum-free medium supplemented with 0.1% fetal bovine serum and varying concentrations of CCL20 (0–250 ng/mL). After six days of incubation, cells were harvested, stained with propidium iodide, and analyzed by flow cytometry to determine the percentage of viable cells. For BrdU incorporation assays, cells were labeled with 10 µM BrdU during the final 16 hours of incubation, fixed, permeabilized, and stained with anti-BrdU antibody (eBioscience) followed by fluorescent secondary antibody, and analyzed by flow cytometry. Cell adhesion assays were performed by coating 24-well plates with 10 µg/mL fibronectin or collagen type I (Sigma-Aldrich) overnight at 4°C, blocking with 1% bovine serum albumin for one hour, and seeding 1×10⁵ cells per well in serum-free medium supplemented with 0.1% BSA. After 30 minutes of incubation at 37°C, non-adherent cells were removed by washing twice with cold PBS, and adherent cells were detached with 300 µL of FACS buffer containing 5 mM EDTA and counted by flow cytometry. For intracellular CCL20 detection, cells were lysed in buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.6), 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40, and protease inhibitors (Roche Diagnostics), centrifuged at 14,000 g for 20 minutes at 4°C, and supernatants were analyzed by ELISA. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), and cDNA was synthesized using 2.5 µg of RNA and random hexamers. Real-time quantitative PCR was performed using TaqMan probes (Applied Biosystems) for CCL20, CCR6, CXCR4, and β-actin, with amplification performed on an ABI Prism 7700 Sequence Detector System. Gene expression was normalized to β-actin using the ΔΔCt method. Flow cytometry was performed using anti-human CXCR4 monoclonal antibody (clone 12G5, R&D Systems), anti-human CCR6 monoclonal antibody (clone 53103.11, R&D Systems), and appropriate isotype controls. Cells were stained for 30 minutes at 4°C, washed, and analyzed on a FACSCalibur (Becton Dickinson). For in vivo experiments, 5×10⁶ cancer cells were injected subcutaneously into the flank of SCID/bg or nude mice (Charles River Laboratories). Tumor volume was measured twice weekly using calipers, and tumor volume was calculated as (length × width²)/2. For antibody treatment, mice received subcutaneous injections of 20 µg of anti-human CCL20 antibody or isotype control three times per week for four weeks, starting 24 hours after tumor cell injection. Tumors were harvested at the end of the experiment, weighed, and fixed in 10% formalin for histological analysis. Immunohistochemistry was performed on 5 µm paraffin sections using anti-human CCL20 polyclonal antibody (PeproTech EC), anti-human CCR6 monoclonal antibody (R&D Systems), and anti-human CXCR4 monoclonal antibody (clone 12G5, R&D Systems). Staining was visualized using AEC chromogen and counterstained with hematoxylin. Scoring was performed blindly by two independent pathologists and classified as negative, weak, or strong based on staining intensity and percentage of positive cells. Functional MRI was performed using a 7T scanner (Bruker Biospin) to assess tumor oxygenation. Mice were placed in a nose cone and alternately exposed to air, carbogen (95% O₂/5% CO₂), and air, and T2*-weighted images were acquired. The change in oxygen saturation (ΔSo₂) was calculated as the difference between carbogen and air states, normalized to contralateral muscle tissue. Statistical analysis was performed using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test, with p-values less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

### Example 1

In this example, the role of CXCR4 in regulating CCL20 expression in prostate cancer cells was investigated. PC3 cells, which express low levels of endogenous CXCR4, were transduced with a retroviral vector encoding human CXCR4, and single-cell clones with stable, high-level expression of CXCR4 were isolated by limiting dilution. These clones, designated PC3-CXCR4.5, exhibited a 15-fold increase in CXCR4 mRNA and a 12-fold increase in CXCR4 protein expression compared to parental PC3 cells, as confirmed by quantitative RT-PCR and flow cytometry. When stimulated with CXCL12, PC3-CXCR4.5 cells demonstrated a significantly enhanced proliferative response compared to parental PC3 cells, with a 2.5-fold increase in viable cell number after six days of culture at 100 ng/mL CXCL12. To identify downstream mediators of this enhanced phenotype, a chemokine array was performed on RNA extracted from PC3-CXCR4.5 cells following 24 hours of stimulation with 500 ng/mL CXCL12. This analysis revealed a marked upregulation of CCL20 mRNA, with a 41-fold increase compared to unstimulated PC3-CXCR4.5 cells and a 48-fold increase compared to unstimulated parental PC3 cells. Real-time RT-PCR confirmed this finding, demonstrating a dose-dependent increase in CCL20 mRNA expression in both parental PC3 and PC3-CXCR4.5 cells following CXCL12 stimulation, with maximal induction observed at 500 ng/mL CXCL12. ELISA analysis of culture supernatants revealed that PC3-CXCR4.5 cells constitutively secreted 433±30 pg/mL of CCL20, compared to only 8.6±4.5 pg/mL in parental PC3 cells. Upon stimulation with CXCL12, CCL20 secretion increased in a dose-dependent manner in both cell lines, reaching 600±113 pg/mL in PC3-CXCR4.5 cells at 500 ng/mL CXCL12. Intracellular CCL20 levels were also measured, revealing that CCL20 accumulated within the cells following 6 hours of stimulation, with secretion into the extracellular space becoming detectable only after 24 hours, indicating a temporal delay between synthesis and release. To determine whether this secretion was dependent on CXCR4 signaling, cells were treated with neutralizing antibodies against CXCL12 or pertussis toxin, a known inhibitor of Gi protein signaling. In parental PC3 cells, both treatments completely abolished CCL20 secretion, confirming the dependence on CXCL12 and G-protein signaling. However, in PC3-CXCR4.5 cells, anti-CXCL12 antibodies only partially reduced CCL20 secretion, and pertussis toxin had no significant effect on the CXCL12-induced increase in CCL20, suggesting the existence of a G-protein-independent mechanism. To confirm the role of CXCR4 in this process, siRNA-mediated knockdown of CXCR4 was performed in PC3-CXCR4.5 cells, resulting in a 90% reduction in CXCR4 surface expression and a 75% reduction in CXCR4 mRNA. This knockdown led to a 3-fold decrease in CCL20 mRNA and a 90% reduction in secreted CCL20, both in the absence and presence of CXCL12 stimulation. These results demonstrate that CXCR4 is necessary and sufficient for the upregulation of CCL20 in prostate cancer cells, and that this regulation occurs through a mechanism that is independent of its canonical ligand and G-protein coupling.

### Example 2

In this example, the functional consequences of CCL20 expression on prostate cancer cell behavior were investigated. PC3 cells, which express low levels of CCR6, were treated with exogenous CCL20 at concentrations ranging from 0 to 250 ng/mL, and cell proliferation was assessed over six days. Treatment with 5 ng/mL CCL20 resulted in a 1.35-fold increase in viable cell number (p<0.04), while 50 ng/mL CCL20 induced a 1.75-fold increase (p<0.0004). However, at 250 ng/mL, no further increase in proliferation was observed, suggesting a biphasic response. BrdU incorporation assays confirmed these findings, showing a 1.76-fold increase in DNA synthesis at 50 ng/mL CCL20 (p<0.0002), with no additional effect at higher concentrations. In contrast, PC3-CXCR4.5 cells, which constitutively secrete high levels of CCL20, exhibited reduced proliferation when exposed to exogenous CCL20 at 250 ng/mL, with a 20% decrease in viable cell number (p<0.006), suggesting that these cells are already saturated or desensitized. To assess the role of CCL20 in cell adhesion, PC3 cells were allowed to adhere to fibronectin or collagen type I-coated plates in the presence of increasing concentrations of CCL20. At 50 ng/mL, CCL20 induced a 2.2-fold increase in adhesion to collagen type I (p<0.001), and at 500 ng/mL, adhesion was increased by 2.09-fold (p<0.003). Adhesion to fibronectin was also enhanced, albeit to a lesser extent, with a 1.45-fold increase at 500 ng/mL (p<0.01). Pretreatment with pertussis toxin completely abolished the CCL20-induced increase in adhesion to both substrates, indicating that this effect is mediated through a G-protein-dependent pathway. In contrast, PC3-CXCR4.5 cells showed a slight decrease in adhesion to both fibronectin and collagen I when exposed to high concentrations of CCL20, consistent with receptor desensitization. To confirm the role of CCR6 in mediating these effects, CCL20 was introduced into PC3 cells via stable transfection, generating four clones with varying levels of CCL20 secretion: PC3-CCL20.7 (5,000 pg/mL), PC3-CCL20.8 (1,030 pg/mL), PC3-CCL20.10 (100 pg/mL), and PC3-CCL20.30 (320 pg/mL). Clones with moderate CCL20 secretion (PC3-CCL20.10 and PC3-CCL20.30) demonstrated a 1.7-fold and 2.5-fold increase in proliferation, respectively, while clones with high secretion (PC3-CCL20.7 and PC3-CCL20.8) showed no significant increase, again suggesting a biphasic effect. Adhesion to collagen type I was significantly enhanced in all four clones, with increases ranging from 2.1-fold to 3.8-fold compared to mock-transfected controls. These results demonstrate that CCL20 promotes the proliferation and adhesion of CCR6-expressing cancer cells in a concentration-dependent manner, and that autocrine secretion of CCL20 can drive these behaviors without the need for exogenous ligand.

### Example 3

In this example, the in vivo role of CCL20 in tumor growth and vascularization was investigated using a xenograft model of prostate cancer. SCID/bg mice were injected subcutaneously with 5×10⁶ PC3 cells, PC3-CCL20.10 cells, or PC3-CCL20.30 cells, and tumor growth was monitored over five weeks. Mice injected with PC3-CCL20.30 cells developed significantly larger tumors than those injected with parental PC3 cells, with a 3.2-fold increase in mean tumor volume by day 35 (p<0.001). Tumors derived from PC3-CCL20.30 cells were also more invasive, with clear infiltration into surrounding muscle and dermal tissues, whereas parental PC3 tumors remained encapsulated and non-invasive. Histological analysis revealed that PC3-CCL20.30 tumors exhibited extensive necrosis, abnormal vascular structures, and high microvessel density, whereas parental PC3 tumors showed minimal vascularization and no necrosis. To assess tumor vascular functionality, functional MRI was performed on day 41, measuring the change in oxygen saturation (ΔSo₂) following carbogen inhalation. PC3-CCL20.30 tumors exhibited a 3.8-fold higher ΔSo₂ value compared to parental PC3 tumors (p<0.001), indicating significantly improved perfusion and oxygen delivery. Control tumors were allowed to grow for an additional month to reach similar size, but even at day 71, they remained significantly less vascularized, with functional vessels restricted to the tumor periphery. To determine whether these effects were mediated by CCL20, mice injected with PC3-CCL20.30 cells were treated with subcutaneous injections of anti-human CCL20 monoclonal antibody or isotype control three times per week for four weeks. Tumor growth was significantly inhibited in the antibody-treated group, with only five out of ten mice developing palpable tumors, and the tumors that did form were small and non-progressive. In contrast, nine out of ten control mice developed large, rapidly growing tumors. Histological analysis of treated tumors revealed extensive necrosis, reduced microvessel density, and minimal invasion, confirming that CCL20 is essential for tumor vascularization and growth. These results demonstrate that CCL20 is not only a marker of aggressive disease but a functional driver of tumor progression in vivo, and that its neutralization can profoundly suppress tumor growth and vascularization.

### Example 4

In this example, the therapeutic potential of CCL20 neutralization was extended to other cancer types and to CXCR4-overexpressing tumors. To determine whether the CCL20/CCR6 axis is active in other malignancies, CCL20 secretion was assessed in a panel of human cancer cell lines, including NB4 and HL60 acute myeloid leukemia cells, HT-29 colon carcinoma cells, and DU145 and LNCaP prostate cancer cells. CCL20 was detected at significant levels in NB4, HL60, and HT-29 cells, and its secretion was further enhanced by CXCL12 stimulation in a dose-dependent manner. Flow cytometry confirmed that all three cell lines expressed CCR6 at the mRNA level, with HL60 exhibiting the highest surface expression. Functional assays demonstrated that CCL20 stimulation increased adhesion to collagen type I in HL60 and HT-29 cells, with a 1.6-fold increase in HL60 (p<0.006) and a 2.1-fold increase in HT-29 (p<0.002) at 250 ng/mL CCL20. Proliferation was also enhanced in HL60 cells, but not in NB4, which expressed low surface CCR6. To assess the in vivo relevance of these findings, HT-29 cells were injected subcutaneously into nude mice, and animals were treated with anti-human CCL20 antibody or isotype control. Tumor growth was significantly inhibited in the antibody-treated group, with a 65% reduction in mean tumor volume by day 28 (p<0.001). Histological analysis revealed reduced invasion into surrounding skin and muscle, decreased vascular density, and increased necrosis, mirroring the effects observed in prostate cancer models. To determine whether CCL20 neutralization could also inhibit CXCR4-driven tumors, mice were injected with PC3-CXCR4.5 cells and treated with anti-CCL20 antibody. Tumor appearance was delayed by 10 days in the treated group, with only 60% of mice developing tumors by day 28 compared to 100% in the control group. Tumor growth was also significantly suppressed, with a 55% reduction in mean volume by day 42 (p<0.001). These results demonstrate that CCL20 is a critical downstream mediator of CXCR4-driven tumor progression across multiple cancer types, and that its neutralization is effective even in tumors where CCL20 is not directly overexpressed but is induced by CXCR4 signaling. Together, these findings validate the CCL20/CCR6 axis as a broad-spectrum therapeutic target in oncology, with the potential to improve outcomes in a wide range of malignancies.
