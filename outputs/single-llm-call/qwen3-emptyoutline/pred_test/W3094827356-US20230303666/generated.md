# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to a novel, integrated, cell-free platform for the engineering of high-affinity, single-domain antibody binders, specifically designed for the rapid discovery and optimization of nanobodies derived from camelid heavy-chain variable domains. The invention encompasses a comprehensive system for the generation, selection, and computational analysis of ultra-diverse synthetic nanobody libraries with fully randomized complementarity-determining regions (CDRs), enabling the identification of functional binders against a broad spectrum of target antigens without reliance on immunization or cellular systems. The platform integrates a precisely engineered linear DNA library construction method, an optimized ribosome display selection cycle with built-in background suppression, and a computational clustering algorithm that identifies distinct binding families from high-throughput sequencing data. This system is particularly suited for applications requiring the discovery of binders to challenging targets, including viral epitopes, conformationally dynamic proteins, and epitopes that are inaccessible to conventional antibodies. The invention further extends to methods for affinity maturation of selected nanobodies through iterative error-prone mutagenesis and selection, enabling the generation of therapeutic candidates with picomolar binding affinities and enhanced neutralization potency. The platform is fully amenable to automation and scalable production, making it suitable for high-throughput antibody discovery in drug development, diagnostic reagent engineering, and rapid response to emerging pathogens.

## BACKGROUND

The discovery and development of therapeutic antibodies has long been a cornerstone of modern biomedicine, yet traditional methods relying on animal immunization remain time-consuming, costly, and often fail to yield binders against conserved, non-immunogenic, or structurally complex targets. While phage display and yeast display systems have provided significant advances in in vitro antibody engineering, they are inherently limited by the capacity of cellular systems to maintain library diversity, constrained by transformation efficiency, cloning bias, and the physiological constraints of host organisms. These limitations restrict the exploration of vast sequence spaces necessary to identify rare, high-affinity binders, particularly those targeting epitopes with low sequence conservation or those requiring unusual structural features for binding. Furthermore, the reliance on cellular systems introduces variability in expression, folding, and stability, complicating reproducibility and scalability.

Nanobodies, derived from the single-domain variable regions of camelid heavy-chain-only antibodies, have emerged as compelling alternatives to conventional antibodies due to their small size, exceptional stability, high solubility, and ability to access cryptic epitopes. However, the generation of synthetic nanobody libraries for in vitro selection has historically been hampered by suboptimal design principles. Previous approaches have attempted to mimic the amino acid composition of naturally occurring nanobodies by biasing CDR randomization toward residues commonly observed in camelid repertoires, under the assumption that such biases enhance functional fitness. Yet, the extent to which natural sequence profiles are essential for in vitro binding fitness has never been rigorously tested, and such biases may inadvertently exclude novel, non-natural binding motifs with superior properties. Moreover, existing library construction methods often employ multi-step cloning procedures that are labor-intensive, prone to bias, and incapable of achieving the ultra-high diversity (>10¹⁰ unique variants) required for comprehensive exploration of the binding landscape.

In vitro display technologies such as ribosome display offer a promising solution by decoupling genotype-phenotype linkage from cellular constraints, enabling the selection of libraries with theoretical diversities exceeding 10¹³. However, practical implementation has been hindered by several persistent challenges: inefficient recovery of functional sequences due to ribosomal stalling, RNA degradation, and non-specific binding; the emergence of sequence shuffling during PCR amplification between selection rounds, which disrupts CDR pairing and compromises binding integrity; and the absence of robust computational methods to interpret the complex, high-dimensional output of next-generation sequencing data following selection. Consequently, even when high-diversity libraries are generated, the identification of true binders remains a bottleneck, often requiring laborious screening of hundreds to thousands of individual clones, many of which are false positives or low-affinity binders.

Additionally, the biophysical properties of in vitro-generated nanobodies—particularly their propensity for aggregation, disulfide bond mispairing, and thermal instability—have raised concerns regarding their suitability for therapeutic applications. While some nanobodies exhibit remarkable stability, the impact of fully random CDR sequences, particularly those containing non-natural amino acid compositions such as elevated cysteine content, on folding and function has not been systematically evaluated. Furthermore, the potential for immunogenicity due to camelid-specific framework residues remains a significant hurdle for clinical translation, and no systematic strategy has been developed to guide the humanization of in vitro-derived nanobodies without compromising binding affinity.

There is therefore a critical and unmet need for a fully integrated, cell-free platform that enables the unbiased generation of ultra-diverse nanobody libraries, selects for high-affinity binders with minimal background, computationally identifies functional binding families from massive sequencing datasets, and provides a robust framework for affinity maturation and humanization—all while ensuring the production of nanobodies with excellent biophysical properties and therapeutic potential. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing CeVICA, a novel and self-contained system that synergistically combines optimized molecular design, streamlined biochemical selection, and intelligent computational analysis to overcome each of these longstanding limitations.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel, integrated, cell-free platform for the discovery and optimization of high-affinity nanobodies, termed CeVICA, which enables the rapid, scalable, and comprehensive identification of functional binders against virtually any target antigen without the use of living cells. The platform comprises four interdependent components: (1) a linear DNA library encoding fully randomized complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) of nanobody variable domains, constructed using a three-stage PCR and ligation process that preserves sequence diversity and mimics the natural structural architecture of camelid nanobodies; (2) an optimized ribosome display selection cycle that minimizes sequence shuffling, suppresses non-specific binding, and enriches for full-length, functional nanobodies through iterative rounds of target binding and RNA recovery; (3) a computational clustering algorithm that identifies distinct binding families from high-throughput sequencing data by analyzing pairwise CDR sequence similarity scores, thereby reducing the number of candidates for experimental validation from tens of thousands to hundreds while retaining high sensitivity for rare, high-affinity binders; and (4) a method for affinity maturation that introduces random mutations across the entire nanobody sequence and selects for improved binding and neutralization through stringent, iterative selection cycles, enabling the generation of nanobodies with picomolar affinities and enhanced therapeutic potency.

The DNA library is designed based on a comprehensive analysis of natural nanobody sequences, defining CDR boundaries and lengths according to observed distributions in over a thousand camelid-derived variable domains. Each nanobody sequence in the library contains three CDRs—CDR1, CDR2, and CDR3—fully randomized using NNB codons, which encode all 20 amino acids with equal probability, thereby maximizing sequence diversity and avoiding any preconceived biases toward naturally occurring residues. The library is constructed in a hierarchical manner, with CDR2 randomized first, followed by CDR1, and then CDR3, reflecting the natural diversity hierarchy observed in camelid nanobodies. The library contains upstream T7 promoter elements for in vitro transcription, a 3×Myc tag for initial enrichment, and a spacer sequence downstream of the nanobody coding region that prevents ribosome release, enabling stable ribosome-nanobody-RNA complexes essential for ribosome display. The resulting library exhibits a diversity of greater than 3.68 × 10¹¹ full-length sequences per microgram of DNA, far exceeding the capacity of cell-based systems.

The ribosome display selection cycle is performed in a closed, cell-free system using PURExpress in vitro transcription-translation reagents, with each round of selection involving binding of ribosome-nanobody complexes to immobilized target antigens, stringent washing to remove non-specific binders, RNA extraction from bound complexes, reverse transcription to generate cDNA, and amplification by PCR using a polymerase mixture that minimizes template switching and CDR shuffling. A pre-selection enrichment step using anti-Myc antibody selection significantly reduces the proportion of out-of-frame and truncated sequences, increasing the proportion of full-length, functional nanobodies from approximately 25% to over 50% prior to target-specific selection. After three rounds of selection against a target antigen, the recovered RNA is sequenced to generate a comprehensive profile of enriched sequences.

The computational clustering algorithm analyzes the sequencing data by calculating ungapped BLOSUM62 alignment scores between all pairs of CDR sequences across the three CDR regions. Sequences with a combined CDR1 and CDR2 match score exceeding a predefined threshold are grouped into clusters, each representing a distinct binding family. This approach identifies not only the most abundant binder families but also rare clusters representing low-frequency, high-affinity binders that would be missed by random sampling or conventional clustering methods. The algorithm further assigns a representative sequence to each cluster based on maximal similarity to all other members, minimizing the impact of sequencing errors and ensuring that the selected candidate is representative of the true binding motif.

The invention further provides a method for affinity maturation, wherein error-prone PCR is applied to the DNA sequences of selected nanobodies to introduce random mutations across the entire coding region, generating a new library with an average of three mutations per sequence. This library is subjected to three additional rounds of stringent ribosome display selection under reduced binding time to favor higher-affinity variants. Post-selection sequencing enables the identification of beneficial mutations by comparing amino acid frequency shifts between pre- and post-maturation libraries. These mutations are then rationally incorporated into the parental sequence to generate optimized variants, which are validated for binding and neutralization. The method has been successfully applied to generate nanobodies with dissociation constants as low as 2.18 nM and pseudovirus neutralization IC₅₀ values as low as 0.329 nM, surpassing the potency of previously described animal-derived nanobodies.

The nanobodies produced by this platform exhibit exceptional biophysical properties, including high thermal stability (melting temperatures exceeding 70°C), efficient refolding after complete denaturation, and minimal aggregation, even when containing multiple cysteines in their CDRs. Notably, the presence of cysteines in CDRs does not invariably lead to dimerization or loss of function, and in some cases, disulfide bond formation enhances binding affinity. Furthermore, affinity maturation has revealed that certain camelid-specific framework residues can be converted to their human homologs without loss of binding function, providing a pathway for humanization and reduced immunogenicity. The platform’s seamless integration of library design, selection, computational analysis, and maturation enables the end-to-end generation of therapeutic-grade nanobodies within weeks, without the need for animal immunization, making it a transformative tool for antibody discovery and development.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE EXAMPLE EMBODIMENTS

### General Definitions

For the purposes of this patent application, the term “nanobody” refers to a single-domain antibody derived from the variable region of a heavy-chain-only antibody naturally found in camelids, comprising a single immunoglobulin variable domain (VHH) that retains full antigen-binding capability without the need for a light chain. The term “complementarity-determining region” or “CDR” refers to the hypervariable loops within the VHH domain that directly contact the antigen, typically designated as CDR1, CDR2, and CDR3, which are flanked by conserved framework regions. The term “fully randomized” in reference to CDRs means that each amino acid position within the CDR is encoded by codons that allow for the incorporation of all 20 canonical amino acids with approximately equal probability, without bias toward residues commonly observed in natural nanobodies. The term “NNB codon” refers to a degenerate codon sequence comprising any combination of nucleotides at the first and second positions and a mixture of nucleotides at the third position, such that all 64 codons are represented with near-equal frequency, thereby encoding all 20 amino acids without restriction. The term “ribosome display” refers to an in vitro display technology in which a nascent polypeptide chain remains tethered to its encoding mRNA via a stalled ribosome, enabling direct linkage between genotype and phenotype without cellular transformation. The term “in vitro transcription and translation” refers to a cell-free system that synthesizes RNA from a DNA template and translates that RNA into protein using purified components, including ribosomes, tRNAs, translation factors, and energy sources, without the presence of living cells. The term “sequence shuffling” refers to the non-templated recombination of DNA fragments during PCR amplification that results in the creation of chimeric sequences with mismatched CDR pairings, thereby disrupting the native binding architecture. The term “binding family” refers to a cluster of nanobody sequences that share high sequence similarity in their CDR regions and are inferred to bind to the same epitope or structural motif on the target antigen. The term “affinity maturation” refers to the iterative process of introducing random mutations into a lead nanobody sequence and selecting for variants with improved binding affinity, neutralization potency, or other functional properties. The term “pseudotyped lentivirus” refers to a replication-incompetent viral particle in which the envelope glycoprotein of one virus (e.g., SARS-CoV-2 spike protein) is displayed on the surface of a lentiviral core derived from another virus (e.g., HIV-1), enabling safe and high-throughput measurement of neutralization activity. The term “biophysical stability” refers to the resistance of a protein to aggregation, denaturation, or degradation under physiological or stress conditions, including thermal denaturation, prolonged storage, and exposure to proteases. The term “humanization” refers to the modification of a non-human protein sequence to increase its similarity to human protein sequences, thereby reducing its potential to elicit an immune response in human subjects. The term “dissociation constant” or “K_D” refers to the equilibrium constant for the dissociation of a ligand-receptor complex, expressed in units of concentration, where lower values indicate higher binding affinity. The term “IC₅₀” refers to the concentration of an inhibitor required to reduce a biological response by 50%, commonly used to quantify neutralization potency in virological assays. The term “full-length sequence” refers to a nanobody coding sequence that is intact, in-frame, and encodes a complete VHH domain without premature stop codons, frameshifts, or truncations.

### OVERVIEW

The CeVICA platform represents a paradigm shift in the field of antibody discovery by enabling the end-to-end generation of high-affinity nanobodies entirely in vitro, without the need for immunization, cellular cloning, or hybridoma technology. The system is structured as a closed-loop workflow that begins with the synthesis of a linear DNA library encoding a diverse array of nanobody sequences, each containing three fully randomized CDRs designed to mirror the natural architecture of camelid variable domains. This library is subjected to multiple rounds of ribosome display selection against a target antigen, during which only those nanobodies capable of folding correctly and binding with sufficient affinity remain associated with their encoding mRNA. The bound complexes are isolated, their RNA is recovered, reverse transcribed into DNA, and amplified using a polymerase mixture that preserves sequence integrity and minimizes recombination artifacts. The resulting DNA pool is subjected to high-throughput sequencing, yielding millions of full-length nanobody sequences. These sequences are then analyzed using a novel computational clustering algorithm that groups them into binding families based on pairwise CDR similarity scores, effectively distilling a library of tens of thousands of sequences into a manageable set of representative candidates for experimental validation. This approach not only accelerates the discovery process but also uncovers rare, high-affinity binders that would be statistically unlikely to be identified through random screening. Once lead candidates are identified, the platform enables affinity maturation through error-prone mutagenesis of the parental sequence, followed by iterative rounds of stringent selection to enrich for variants with improved binding kinetics or neutralization potency. The resulting optimized nanobodies demonstrate exceptional biophysical properties, including thermal stability, refolding capacity, and resistance to aggregation, even when engineered with non-natural amino acid compositions such as elevated cysteine content. Importantly, the platform has revealed that the amino acid profiles of natural nanobodies are not a prerequisite for functional binding, as fully randomized CDRs yield binders whose sequence profiles are more closely aligned with the input library than with natural repertoires, suggesting that natural selection has not optimized for binding fitness per se but rather for other biological constraints. Furthermore, the platform facilitates the identification of human-compatible framework residues through affinity maturation, enabling the rational design of nanobodies with reduced immunogenic potential. The entire process—from library construction to validated therapeutic candidate—can be completed in less than six weeks, with minimal manual intervention, and is fully scalable to accommodate high-throughput screening against multiple targets simultaneously. This integrated, automated, and computationally driven approach transforms nanobody discovery from a labor-intensive, low-yield endeavor into a robust, reproducible, and highly efficient technology platform suitable for industrial-scale antibody development.

### Therapeutic Antibodies or Binding Fragments of an Antibody

The therapeutic antibodies or binding fragments of an antibody generated by the CeVICA platform are single-domain nanobodies derived from camelid heavy-chain variable domains, each comprising a single immunoglobulin variable region (VHH) capable of binding with high specificity and affinity to a target antigen. These nanobodies are characterized by their small molecular weight (approximately 12–15 kDa), exceptional stability under extreme conditions, high solubility, and the ability to penetrate dense tissues and bind cryptic epitopes that are inaccessible to conventional antibodies. Unlike traditional monoclonal antibodies, which require both heavy and light chains for antigen recognition, these nanobodies function as monomeric entities, eliminating the risk of chain mispairing and simplifying production and formulation. The nanobodies produced by CeVICA are encoded by synthetic DNA sequences that contain three complementarity-determining regions (CDR1, CDR2, and CDR3) that are fully randomized using NNB codons, allowing for the exploration of an unprecedented breadth of amino acid combinations without bias toward naturally occurring residues. This design enables the discovery of binders with novel binding modes, including those that engage targets through non-canonical interactions, such as deep pocket penetration, extended surface contacts, or conformational stabilization of dynamic epitopes. The nanobodies are expressed in Escherichia coli with a C-terminal 6×His tag for purification and are typically isolated in high yield and purity using cobalt-affinity chromatography, yielding soluble, monomeric proteins with minimal aggregation. The binding specificity of these nanobodies is determined through a combination of enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA), surface plasmon resonance, and biolayer interferometry, which confirm high-affinity interactions with target antigens such as the receptor-binding domain of SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, enhanced green fluorescent protein, and other disease-relevant targets. The nanobodies demonstrate exceptional neutralization potency in pseudovirus assays, with some variants achieving IC₅₀ values below 0.5 nM, surpassing the potency of clinically approved monoclonal antibodies. Importantly, these nanobodies retain their structural integrity and binding function after complete thermal denaturation and refolding, a property that is rare among antibodies and suggests an intrinsic folding robustness conferred by the selection environment of ribosome display, which favors sequences capable of folding correctly in the absence of cellular chaperones. The nanobodies are further amenable to engineering into multivalent formats, including dimers and trimers, which significantly enhance their neutralization potency through avidity effects, without compromising solubility or stability. The platform also enables the identification of human-compatible framework residues through affinity maturation, allowing for the substitution of camelid-specific hallmark residues with their human homologs, thereby reducing the potential for immunogenicity in human therapeutic applications. The resulting nanobodies are suitable for a wide range of therapeutic modalities, including intravenous or intranasal administration, inhalation, topical delivery, and fusion with Fc domains or half-life extension technologies. Their small size and stability make them ideal candidates for crossing biological barriers, such as the blood-brain barrier, and for use in diagnostic imaging, targeted drug delivery, and intracellular inhibition of protein-protein interactions. The CeVICA-generated nanobodies represent a new class of therapeutic agents that combine the specificity and affinity of antibodies with the simplicity, stability, and manufacturability of small molecules, offering a transformative approach to the treatment of infectious diseases, cancer, autoimmune disorders, and neurodegenerative conditions.

### EXAMPLES

The following examples illustrate the implementation and utility of the CeVICA platform in the discovery and optimization of nanobodies against specific target antigens, including the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein and enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP). In the first example, a synthetic nanobody library was constructed using a three-stage PCR and ligation process, wherein CDR2 was randomized first using NNB codons, followed by CDR1, and then CDR3, resulting in a library with a diversity of 3.68 × 10¹¹ full-length sequences per microgram of DNA. The library was subjected to three rounds of ribosome display selection against immobilized RBD fused to a 3×Flag tag, with each round involving binding of ribosome-nanobody complexes to anti-Flag-coated magnetic beads, stringent washing, RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and PCR amplification using a polymerase mixture that suppressed sequence shuffling. Following selection, the recovered DNA was sequenced using Illumina MiSeq, yielding over 19,000 unique nanobody sequences. Computational clustering based on pairwise CDR similarity scores identified 862 distinct binding families, of which 14 representative nanobodies were synthesized, expressed, and validated by ELISA and pseudovirus neutralization assays. Three nanobodies (SR1, SR2, SR12) demonstrated strong binding to RBD, while six others (SR4, SR6, SR8, SR3, SR7, SR11, SR13, SR14) showed weak binding, and three showed no binding. Notably, six of the fourteen nanobodies inhibited pseudovirus infection by more than 30% at a concentration of 1 μM, including all three strong binders and three of the weak binders, demonstrating that binding affinity does not always correlate with neutralization potency. In a second example, a nanobody cluster with only five sequences (SR38) was identified as a low-frequency binder that exhibited preferential binding to the N501Y and E484K RBD variants and neutralized pseudoviruses carrying these mutations more effectively than previously described animal-derived nanobodies, highlighting the platform’s ability to uncover rare, high-value binders that would be missed by conventional screening. In a third example, affinity maturation was performed on six lead nanobodies using error-prone PCR to introduce random mutations across the entire coding sequence, followed by three rounds of stringent selection under reduced binding time. Post-selection sequencing revealed an average of two mutations per sequence, and a computational analysis of amino acid frequency shifts identified between eight and twenty-five putative beneficial mutations per parental sequence. These mutations were incorporated into the parental nanobodies to generate multiple variants, several of which outperformed the benchmark nanobody VHH72 in both binding affinity and neutralization potency. One variant, SR6c3, achieved an IC₅₀ of 62.7 nM in pseudovirus neutralization, comparable to potent human monoclonal antibodies. In a fourth example, a second round of affinity maturation was performed on SR6c3, yielding a variant, SR6v15, with a dissociation constant of 2.18 nM as measured by biolayer interferometry. When engineered as a dimer (SR6v15.d), this variant achieved an IC₅₀ of 0.329 nM, rivaling the potency of the most potent nanobody therapeutics reported to date. Biophysical characterization of the nanobodies revealed that over 90% of each purified sample existed as a monomer, and that nanobodies containing up to two cysteines in their CDRs retained monomeric structure and function, even after prolonged storage. Notably, one nanobody, SR6c3, exhibited increased binding affinity after thermal denaturation and refolding, likely due to accelerated disulfide bond formation, demonstrating an unexpected and beneficial consequence of CDR cysteine content. Thermal stability assays showed melting temperatures of 72°C for both SR6c3 and SR6v15, and refolding efficiency after complete denaturation exceeded that of benchmark nanobodies, with SR6v15 achieving a refolding ratio of 0.72 compared to 0.33 for VHH72. These results collectively demonstrate that the CeVICA platform enables the discovery of nanobodies with exceptional binding properties, stability, and therapeutic potential, without reliance on natural sequence biases or cellular systems. The platform’s ability to identify rare binders, perform affinity maturation, and generate human-compatible variants positions it as a transformative technology for the rapid development of next-generation antibody therapeutics.
