# DESCRIPTION

## PRIORITY APPLICATIONS

This patent application claims the benefit of priority under 35 U.S.C. § 119(e) to U.S. Provisional Patent Application No. 63/435,789, filed on January 20, 2023, entitled “Multi-Matrix Color Calibration for LED-Based Virtual Production Stages,” which is hereby incorporated by reference in its entirety. The present invention builds upon the technical disclosures and experimental validations presented in the provisional application, extending the scope of protection to include the full operational framework, mathematical formulations, and system implementations necessary for achieving simultaneous optimization of foreground color fidelity and background color accuracy in virtual production environments utilizing RGB LED panels. The invention described herein is not merely an incremental improvement to existing calibration techniques but constitutes a novel architectural approach to the fundamental problem of spectral mismatch between LED-based illumination and natural lighting conditions. The priority application established the foundational empirical evidence that conventional primary-based calibration methods, while effective for rendering in-camera backgrounds, produce unacceptable color shifts in illuminated subjects due to the narrowband spectral characteristics of RGB LEDs. This patent application expands upon that foundation by introducing a three-matrix calibration architecture that decouples the color correction objectives for foreground and background elements, enabling a level of photorealistic consistency previously unattainable without spectral measurement equipment or additional light sources. The inventive framework described herein is operable within existing virtual production pipelines, requiring no modification to hardware beyond the software-controlled application of color transformation matrices, and is compatible with industry-standard rendering engines such as Unreal Engine and proprietary systems used in high-end film and television production. The priority application demonstrated the feasibility of the core concept through controlled experiments with seven distinct lighting environments, including incandescent, daylight, sodium vapor, and artificial LED sources, and established the quantitative and qualitative superiority of the multi-matrix approach over baseline calibration methods. This patent application formalizes those findings into a legally enforceable claim structure that covers the entire sequence of operations, from the acquisition of calibration imagery to the real-time application of inverse and forward color transforms during production, ensuring that the invention’s novel combination of elements is protected in its full operational context.

## BACKGROUND

The field of virtual production has undergone a dramatic transformation in recent years, driven by the increasing adoption of large-scale LED volume stages that replace traditional green screen techniques with immersive, real-time rendered backgrounds. These volumes, composed of high-resolution LED panels arranged to encircle actors and sets, allow cinematographers to capture in-camera backgrounds with unprecedented fidelity, eliminating the need for extensive post-production compositing and rotoscoping. The ability to photograph subjects against dynamically changing virtual environments in real time has revolutionized the workflow for feature films, television series, and commercial productions, offering enhanced actor immersion, natural eyelines, and immediate visual feedback to directors and crew. However, while the visual realism of these backgrounds has improved significantly, a persistent and often overlooked challenge remains: the accurate reproduction of lighting conditions that match those of the intended environment. Unlike conventional lighting setups where physical lamps can be selected for their spectral properties, LED volumes rely on the additive mixing of red, green, and blue light-emitting diodes to simulate ambient illumination. This method, while sufficient for displaying imagery on a screen, is fundamentally inadequate for replicating the continuous spectral distributions of natural or incandescent light sources. The resulting illumination exhibits pronounced spectral gaps, particularly in the cyan, yellow, and orange regions of the spectrum, leading to systematic and visually jarring color shifts in skin tones, fabrics, and materials. Lighter skin tones appear unnaturally pink, darker skin tones shift toward red, yellow materials darken, and cyan materials exhibit an exaggerated blue tint. These artifacts are not merely aesthetic concerns—they compromise the photorealism of the final product, introduce inconsistencies across shots, and create significant challenges for color grading and visual effects integration. Industry professionals have begun to document these issues in detail, noting that even when the white point of the scene is accurately matched through standard calibration procedures, the chromatic fidelity of non-neutral surfaces remains severely degraded. This phenomenon arises because traditional calibration methods, such as those employed by Unreal Engine and other major virtual production platforms, rely on a single 3×3 color matrix derived from capturing the camera’s response to pure red, green, and blue patches displayed on the LED panels. These methods assume that if the primary colors are accurately reproduced, then all derived colors—including white—will be accurately rendered as well. This assumption holds true only under the condition that the spectral response of the display and the camera are linearly related and that the goal is purely visual display fidelity. However, when the LED panels are used as a light source to illuminate physical subjects, the underlying spectral mismatch between the LED emission profile and the target illuminant becomes critically apparent. The human visual system, as well as the spectral sensitivity curves of modern digital cinema cameras, respond nonlinearly to these spectral discontinuities, causing materials with complex reflectance spectra to appear dramatically different under LED illumination than they would under the intended lighting. For instance, a fabric that reflects a broad spectrum of wavelengths in daylight will appear desaturated or distorted under the peaky, narrowband output of RGB LEDs, even when the overall color temperature appears correct. This issue is further exacerbated by the fact that LED panels are typically optimized for gamut coverage and brightness rather than spectral continuity, and manufacturers have shown little incentive to incorporate additional spectral channels such as white or cyan LEDs, as these would reduce the achievable color gamut for display purposes. As a result, the industry has been left with a technological gap: a system capable of rendering photorealistic backgrounds but incapable of reproducing photorealistic lighting. Existing attempts to mitigate these issues have been limited in scope and practicality. Some researchers have proposed adding broad-spectrum white LEDs to the panel architecture, which improves color rendition but at the cost of reduced color gamut, increased hardware complexity, and higher power consumption—factors that are commercially unviable for most production studios. Others have suggested spectral measurement of the LED panels, camera sensors, and materials to construct detailed color correction models, but these approaches require expensive spectroradiometers, specialized expertise, and lengthy calibration procedures that are incompatible with the fast-paced nature of film production. Furthermore, these methods do not address the fundamental architectural limitation: the inability to simultaneously optimize both the lighting of physical subjects and the appearance of the displayed background. Current workflows treat the LED volume as a single display surface, applying one calibration matrix to all content, which inevitably forces a compromise between foreground and background fidelity. When the matrix is tuned to improve the appearance of actors and costumes, the in-camera background becomes color-shifted and inaccurate. When the matrix is tuned to preserve background accuracy, the subjects appear unnaturally saturated and distorted. This trade-off has persisted as an unsolved problem in the field, leaving cinematographers with no practical solution other than to accept the color artifacts or resort to labor-intensive post-production corrections. The present invention resolves this longstanding dilemma by introducing a novel multi-matrix calibration architecture that decouples the color correction objectives for foreground and background elements, enabling both to be optimized simultaneously without requiring additional hardware, spectral measurements, or changes to existing production pipelines. This innovation represents a paradigm shift in virtual production calibration, moving from a monolithic approach to a dual-path, context-aware system that recognizes the distinct roles played by the LED volume—as both a display and a light source—and addresses each role with a tailored mathematical transformation. The invention is grounded in a deep understanding of photometric principles, camera sensor response, and material reflectance, and it leverages the superposition of light to simulate the appearance of any lighting environment using only four calibration images captured with the principal photography camera. By introducing a pre-correction matrix for out-of-frustum content, a post-correction matrix for the final image, and a derived pre-correction matrix for in-frustum content, the invention achieves a level of color fidelity that was previously unattainable, effectively closing the gap between virtual and real-world lighting without compromising the efficiency or scalability of the virtual production workflow.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a novel method and system for calibrating an RGB LED-based virtual production stage to achieve simultaneous, near-optimal color fidelity for both illuminated subjects and in-camera backgrounds, without requiring spectral measurements or additional hardware. The invention operates by decomposing the color calibration process into three distinct, interdependent 3×3 linear transformation matrices, each assigned a specific role in the imaging pipeline: a first matrix for correcting out-of-frustum content to ensure accurate lighting of physical subjects, a second matrix for post-processing the final recorded image to correct residual color distortions, and a third matrix for correcting in-frustum content to preserve the accuracy of the displayed background. The method begins with the acquisition of four calibration images using the principal photography camera: a first image capturing the camera’s response to pure red, green, and blue patches displayed on the LED panels; and three additional images capturing the response of a color chart illuminated individually by each of the red, green, and blue spectral channels of the LED volume. From these images, a first 3×3 matrix is computed to map the target scene’s pixel values to the LED panel’s output such that the camera perceives the displayed primaries as identical to the intended primaries. This matrix, while sufficient for background display, produces excessive saturation and hue shifts when used to illuminate physical subjects. To correct these distortions, a second 3×3 matrix is computed by comparing the simulated appearance of the color chart under the LED illumination—derived from the individual spectral channel images and scaled by the diffuse integral of the target lighting environment—with the actual appearance of the same chart in the original real-world environment. This second matrix, applied as a post-process to the final recorded footage, desaturates overly pink skin tones, reduces red shifts in orange and yellow materials, and corrects cyan and blue distortions. However, applying this second matrix to the entire image would also alter the appearance of the in-camera background, introducing unacceptable color shifts. To resolve this conflict, a third 3×3 matrix is derived by multiplying the first matrix by the inverse of the second matrix, producing a pre-correction matrix specifically tailored for the in-frustum content. This third matrix effectively inverts the post-correction’s effect on the background, ensuring that the displayed environment appears visually consistent with the target HDR environment, while the post-correction matrix continues to correct the foreground. The system further incorporates a black level subtraction step to compensate for the non-zero albedo of the LED panels, which causes unwanted light reflection onto the in-camera background. This is achieved by measuring the average RGB value of the panels when illuminated by out-of-frustum content and subtracting a scaled version of this value from the in-frustum content prior to display. The entire process requires no spectral measurements of the LED panels, camera sensors, or materials, relying instead on the camera’s native response and the geometric relationship between the LED panels and the color chart. The invention enables a virtual production stage to reproduce the color characteristics of any lighting environment—whether natural daylight, incandescent, sodium vapor, or artificial LED—with unprecedented accuracy, simultaneously preserving the integrity of both the illuminated subjects and the in-camera background. The method is compatible with existing virtual production pipelines, requires no hardware modifications, and can be implemented using standard shader operations within rendering engines such as Unreal Engine. The invention represents a significant advancement over prior art, which either sacrifices foreground fidelity for background accuracy or vice versa, and provides a practical, scalable, and industry-ready solution to one of the most persistent challenges in modern digital filmmaking.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EXEMPLARY EMBODIMENTS

### EXAMPLE EMBODIMENTS

The invention is implemented as a multi-stage calibration and rendering pipeline that operates within the context of a virtual production environment comprising an array of RGB LED panels arranged to form a volumetric enclosure around a physical set and actors. The system is designed to function with standard digital cinema cameras, industry-standard HDR image-based lighting (IBL) environments, and commercial rendering engines such as Unreal Engine, without requiring any specialized hardware beyond what is already present in a typical LED volume setup. The calibration process begins with the acquisition of four distinct images using the principal photography camera that will be used during filming. The first image is captured while the LED panels display three pure color patches—red, green, and blue—within the in-camera frustum region, meaning the area of the panels that will be visible to the camera during production. These patches are displayed at maximum intensity, and the camera records the pixel values corresponding to each color. The out-of-frustum panels are turned off during this capture to prevent any reflected light from interfering with the measurement. From these pixel values, a 3×3 matrix, denoted as [SL], is constructed, where each column represents the camera’s response to one of the three LED spectral channels, and each row corresponds to the camera’s red, green, and blue color channels. This matrix encodes the linear relationship between the pixel values displayed on the LED panels and the pixel values recorded by the camera under ideal conditions. The next step involves computing the first pre-correction matrix, M, which is derived by inverting the [SL] matrix. This matrix ensures that when the target scene’s RGB pixel values are transformed by M and then displayed on the LED panels, the camera will record the same RGB values as those in the original content. This step is identical to the standard primary-based calibration used in current virtual production workflows, and it guarantees that the in-camera background will appear visually accurate when the HDR environment is displayed. However, this matrix alone is insufficient for lighting physical subjects, as it does not account for the spectral mismatch between the LED output and the target illumination. To address this, the second phase of calibration involves capturing three additional images of a standardized color chart, such as a ColorChecker, placed at a fixed distance from the LED volume. For each of these images, only one spectral channel of the LED panels is activated at a time—first the red, then the green, then the blue—while the other two channels remain off. A 1m × 1m square of LED panels is used for each capture, and the color chart is positioned one meter from the center of the square, with the camera’s optical axis oriented at a 45-degree angle to the chart’s surface to ensure consistent diffuse reflectance sampling. The resulting images capture how the color chart responds to each individual spectral channel of the LED volume. These images are then scaled by a geometric factor, β, which accounts for the difference in solid angle subtended by the 1m × 1m square compared to the full sphere of illumination that would be required for a complete virtual environment. This factor is computed by constructing a cube map environment with a 54-pixel-wide square representing the active LED panel region on a 90-pixel-wide cube face, and calculating the diffuse convolution of the frontal direction, which yields β ≈ 0.311. The calibration images are then multiplied by the reciprocal of this factor to simulate the appearance of the color chart under a uniform, omnidirectional illumination of the same spectral channel. The next step involves determining the diffuse integral of the target HDR environment, which is represented by the RGB pixel value w_avg, derived from the white square of the color chart photographed in the original real-world environment. This value is scaled by the pre-correction matrix M to account for the fact that the LED panels are already being pre-corrected for display, yielding Mw_avg. This scaled value is then used to weight the three individual spectral channel images, producing a simulated appearance of the color chart under the target lighting environment. This simulated appearance is compared to the actual appearance of the same color chart in the real-world environment, represented by a target vector p. The difference between the simulated and target appearances is minimized using a least-squares optimization to solve for the second 3×3 matrix, Q, which, when applied as a post-process to the final recorded image, brings the color rendition of the illuminated subjects into alignment with the real-world reference. This matrix is computed by solving a system of linear equations derived from the reflectance properties of each of the 24 color patches on the chart, with each patch contributing three equations—one for each color channel—resulting in a 72-equation system for nine unknowns in the Q matrix. The solution is obtained using standard linear algebra techniques, such as singular value decomposition, to ensure numerical stability. Once Q is determined, the third matrix, N, is derived by multiplying the first matrix M by the inverse of the second matrix Q, yielding N = M × Q⁻¹. This matrix is then applied to the in-frustum content displayed on the LED panels, effectively counteracting the color shifts introduced by the post-correction matrix Q on the background. Because the background pixels contribute minimally to the illumination of the subjects—due to the low-angle, grazing incidence of light reflecting off the panels and the Fresnel effect that suppresses spectral modulation in specular reflections—the color distortion introduced by the background lighting is negligible compared to the direct illumination from the out-of-frustum panels. Thus, by applying N to the in-frustum content, the background appears visually consistent with the target HDR environment, while the post-correction matrix Q continues to correct the foreground. The system further incorporates a black level subtraction step to compensate for the non-zero albedo of the LED panels, which causes a small but perceptible amount of incident light to be reflected back onto the in-camera background. This reflection, typically ranging from 4% to 10% of the incident intensity, introduces a color bias that can shift the white balance of the background. To correct for this, a per-lighting-environment black level measurement is taken by turning on all out-of-frustum content with the pre-correction matrix M applied, turning off the in-frustum content, and capturing the average RGB value of a region on the background panels that is not occluded by any physical objects. This value, denoted as b_camera, represents the residual illumination reflected onto the background. To determine the appropriate offset to subtract from the in-frustum content, the system computes the camera’s response to a full [1,1,1] pixel value by summing the calibration images from the primary calibration step, yielding w_camera. The black level offset is then calculated as b_camera divided by w_camera, and this value is subtracted from the in-frustum pixel values prior to display. This step ensures that the background remains neutral and free from unintended color shifts. The entire calibration process is completed in under thirty minutes and requires no spectral measurement equipment, no additional lighting hardware, and no changes to the physical setup of the LED volume. Once calibrated, the system operates in real time during production: the out-of-frustum content is pre-corrected using matrix M, the in-frustum content is pre-corrected using matrix N, and the final recorded footage is post-corrected using matrix Q. The matrices are applied as shader operations within the rendering engine, ensuring minimal computational overhead and compatibility with existing workflows. The invention has been tested across seven distinct lighting environments, including incandescent, daylight, sodium vapor, and artificial LED sources, and has consistently demonstrated superior color fidelity compared to conventional calibration methods. In each case, the skin tones of subjects appear natural and consistent with the real-world reference, yellow and orange materials retain their intended hue without darkening, cyan materials do not exhibit exaggerated blue shifts, and the in-camera background remains visually accurate. The invention is particularly effective in challenging environments such as sodium vapor lighting, where the narrowband emission spectrum of the real-world source cannot be replicated by RGB LEDs. In such cases, the post-correction matrix Q desaturates the entire color chart to produce a near-monochromatic appearance, closely matching the real-world effect, even though the underlying spectral energy is fundamentally different. The system also handles extreme dynamic ranges, as the HDR environments are pre-processed using energy-preserving dilation algorithms to prevent clipping of bright light sources while maintaining the overall spectral balance. The invention’s robustness is further demonstrated by its theoretical validation, in which simulated appearances of the color chart, computed using the derived matrices and spectral response functions, closely match the empirical results obtained during filming. The residual color errors observed in the experimental results are not attributable to calibration inaccuracies but rather to the inherent limitations of using only three spectral channels to approximate continuous spectra, a fundamental constraint of RGB LED technology. The invention does not attempt to overcome this limitation through spectral interpolation or machine learning, but rather accepts it as a given and optimizes the linear transformation matrices to extract the maximum possible color fidelity within those constraints. The result is a system that delivers industry-leading color accuracy for both foreground and background elements, enabling filmmakers to achieve photorealistic lighting without compromising workflow efficiency or requiring costly hardware upgrades. The invention is scalable to any size of LED volume, compatible with any digital cinema camera, and requires no specialized training to implement, making it immediately deployable in commercial production environments. The method is also extensible to computer-generated lighting environments, as long as a color chart is photographed in a real-world scene that matches the desired lighting conditions, allowing the same calibration process to be applied to entirely synthetic environments. This flexibility ensures that the invention is not limited to real-world HDR captures but can be seamlessly integrated into any virtual production pipeline, regardless of the source of the lighting environment. The invention thus represents a complete, end-to-end solution to the problem of color fidelity in LED-based virtual production, providing a practical, mathematically rigorous, and commercially viable method for achieving unprecedented levels of photorealism in real time.
