## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to wireless communication systems, and more specifically to the use of electrically reconfigurable antennas in multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) ad hoc networks to enhance spectral efficiency, mitigate co-channel interference, and increase overall network throughput without requiring centralized control or extensive channel feedback. The invention pertains to the integration of compact, pattern-reconfigurable antenna architectures into distributed wireless networks where nodes operate autonomously, dynamically adapt their radiation characteristics in response to changing channel conditions, and collectively optimize network performance through localized decision-making. The invention is particularly applicable to portable, battery-powered devices operating in dense, interference-limited indoor environments such as office buildings, conference centers, and smart homes, where spatial constraints limit the deployment of traditional multi-antenna arrays and where the dynamic nature of user mobility and environmental scattering necessitates adaptive physical layer solutions. The invention further encompasses methods for selecting optimal antenna configurations in real-time using distributed algorithms that require only local channel state information, thereby avoiding the overhead and latency associated with global network coordination or closed-loop feedback mechanisms. The invention is also directed to the characterization of reconfigurable antenna performance metrics—such as pattern diversity, radiation efficiency, and inter-configuration correlation—and their predictive relationship to achievable network capacity, enabling the systematic design of reconfigurable antenna systems tailored for multi-link MIMO applications.

## BACKGROUND

Traditional wireless communication systems rely on fixed-geometry antennas that provide static radiation patterns, limiting their ability to adapt to dynamic channel conditions, user mobility, or interference environments. In ad hoc networks, where nodes are self-organizing, lack centralized infrastructure, and frequently operate in close proximity, interference between simultaneous transmissions becomes a dominant factor degrading network performance. To mitigate this, researchers have explored directional antennas, such as phased arrays and switchable parasitic element antennas, which can focus energy in specific directions to reduce interference and improve signal-to-noise ratios. However, these solutions require multiple physical antenna elements, which are impractical for compact, portable devices due to spatial constraints and increased power consumption. Alternative approaches have employed multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) techniques to exploit spatial diversity and multiplexing gains, but these too typically require multiple fixed antennas spaced apart to achieve sufficient channel decorrelation, which is not feasible in small form-factor devices.

Reconfigurable antennas have emerged as a promising solution to bridge the gap between the benefits of directional beamforming and the spatial limitations of portable platforms. These antennas can alter their radiation patterns through electrical means—such as switching PIN diodes, varactor diodes, or MEMS elements—thereby changing their effective aperture, directivity, and polarization without requiring additional physical elements. Prior work has demonstrated that reconfigurable antennas can enhance single-link MIMO capacity by generating multiple distinct radiation patterns, increasing the rank and condition number of the channel matrix. However, these studies have been largely confined to point-to-point links under controlled laboratory conditions, and none have addressed the complex, multi-user interference dynamics inherent in real-world ad hoc networks.

Moreover, existing literature on reconfigurable antennas has not systematically investigated the interplay between antenna configuration selection and network-wide interference. When a node changes its transmit configuration, it alters the interference profile experienced by neighboring links, which in turn affects their optimal configuration choices. This creates a coupled, non-linear optimization problem that cannot be solved by independent, greedy decisions without iterative coordination. While centralized approaches that exhaustively search all possible configuration combinations across the entire network can theoretically achieve optimal performance, they are impractical in decentralized environments due to the prohibitive communication overhead, latency, and computational complexity required to gather and process global channel state information.

Furthermore, prior designs of reconfigurable antennas have not adequately balanced the competing objectives of maximizing the number of available configurations, ensuring high pattern diversity between those configurations, and maintaining consistent radiation efficiency across all states. Many designs exhibit significant efficiency variations between modes—such as high efficiency in low-order modes and poor efficiency in high-order modes—leading to unpredictable performance gains and potential capacity degradation under certain configurations. Additionally, the correlation between radiation patterns generated by different configurations has been insufficiently quantified in the context of multi-link interference, despite its direct impact on the ability of the channel matrix to support spatial multiplexing.

There remains a critical need for a system and method that enables autonomous, distributed selection of antenna configurations in MIMO ad hoc networks using compact reconfigurable antennas, while accounting for the mutual interference between links, the efficiency variations among antenna states, and the practical constraints of real-time operation. There is also a need to establish design guidelines that relate measurable antenna parameters—such as spatial correlation, number of configurations, and radiation efficiency—to achievable network capacity, thereby enabling engineers to optimize antenna architectures for specific network environments rather than relying on empirical trial-and-error. Finally, there is a need for a configuration selection strategy that achieves near-optimal network performance without requiring feedback from receivers to transmitters, thereby reducing protocol complexity and enabling seamless integration into existing ad hoc network standards.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel system and method for enhancing the spectral efficiency of MIMO ad hoc networks through the use of electrically reconfigurable antennas and distributed configuration selection algorithms that operate without centralized control or channel feedback. The invention comprises a network of wireless nodes, each equipped with a compact, two-element MIMO transceiver incorporating a reconfigurable antenna architecture capable of generating multiple distinct radiation patterns through electrical switching. Each node independently selects its transmit and/or receive antenna configuration in real-time based on locally measured channel state information, including the channel matrix between itself and its intended partner and the interference-plus-noise covariance matrix resulting from transmissions by neighboring nodes. The selection process is performed iteratively and autonomously, allowing each node to adapt its configuration in response to changes in the interference environment caused by other nodes’ configuration updates, thereby converging toward a stable, high-capacity network state.

Two distinct reconfigurable antenna architectures are disclosed: a reconfigurable printed dipole array (RPDA) and a reconfigurable circular patch antenna (RCPA). The RPDA comprises two microstrip dipole elements, each of which can be electrically lengthened or shortened via PIN diode switches, resulting in four distinct radiation pattern configurations per node. The RCPA comprises a single circular patch antenna with two feed points and a set of PIN diodes that enable switching between two distinct electromagnetic modes—TM₃₁ and TM₄₁—thereby producing two distinct radiation patterns per node. Both architectures are designed to operate within the 2.4–2.5 GHz ISM band and are characterized by full azimuthal coverage, high port isolation, and compatibility with standard MIMO signal processing techniques.

The invention further introduces three operational modes for configuration adaptation: double-side reconfigurable antennas (DSRA), in which both transmitter and receiver of each link can adapt their configurations; transmitter-side reconfigurable antennas (TXRA), in which only the transmitter adapts while the receiver remains fixed in its most efficient configuration; and receiver-side reconfigurable antennas (RXRA), in which only the receiver adapts while the transmitter remains fixed. The RXRA mode is particularly advantageous, as it eliminates the need for feedback from receiver to transmitter, reduces the search space for configuration selection, and renders the distributed selection process equivalent to the centralized optimal solution, since maximizing individual link capacity under RXRA also maximizes the total network sum capacity.

The invention includes a distributed configuration selection algorithm that enables each node to compute its optimal configuration by evaluating the capacity of all possible configuration combinations for its own link, given the current interference environment as estimated from the interference-plus-noise covariance matrix. The algorithm operates iteratively: each node updates its configuration based on its local channel measurements, which causes a change in the interference experienced by neighboring nodes, prompting them to update their own configurations in turn. This process continues until convergence is achieved or a maximum number of iterations is reached, at which point the network settles into a stable configuration state that approximates the global optimum without requiring global knowledge.

The invention further provides a methodology for predicting the achievable network capacity based on measurable antenna parameters, including the number of available configurations, the spatial correlation between radiation patterns generated at different configurations, and the relative radiation efficiency of each configuration. It is demonstrated that higher network capacity is achieved when the antenna design provides a large number of configurations with moderate to low inter-configuration correlation and balanced radiation efficiency across states. The invention further shows that the number of configurations is a more significant factor than pattern diversity alone, as demonstrated by the superior performance of the RPDA over the RCPA despite the RCPA’s lower inter-configuration correlation, due to the RPDA’s four-fold greater number of available states.

The invention also introduces a novel normalization technique for isolating the effect of pattern correlation from radiation efficiency, enabling direct comparison of antenna architectures with differing efficiency profiles. By normalizing each configuration combination independently to ensure equal received power across all states, the invention reveals that low correlation between radiation patterns significantly enhances network capacity, even when efficiency differences are eliminated. This finding establishes a new design criterion for reconfigurable antennas: to maximize the number of distinct, low-correlation radiation patterns while maintaining consistent radiation efficiency across all operational states.

The invention further demonstrates that the distributed RXRA mode achieves a near-optimal balance between network performance and practical implementation complexity, offering substantial capacity gains over non-reconfigurable systems—ranging from 14% to 31% depending on the environment—while requiring no feedback, minimal computational overhead, and no synchronization between nodes. The convergence properties of the distributed algorithm are shown to be rapid, with over 99% of network states converging within ten iterations, making the invention suitable for real-time operation in dynamic environments.

The invention is applicable to any wireless network employing MIMO transceivers in interference-limited, infrastructure-less environments, including but not limited to wireless sensor networks, vehicular ad hoc networks, emergency response communication systems, and next-generation Wi-Fi mesh networks. The disclosed antenna architectures, configuration selection algorithms, and performance prediction methodologies collectively enable the design of compact, high-capacity, self-organizing wireless systems that outperform conventional fixed-antenna MIMO systems without requiring additional hardware, power, or protocol complexity.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF ILLUSTRATIVE EMBODIMENTS

### I. RECONFIGURABLE ANTENNA ARCHITECTURES

The invention employs two distinct reconfigurable antenna architectures, each designed to meet the stringent spatial, power, and performance requirements of compact MIMO transceivers operating in dense ad hoc networks. These architectures are not merely variations of existing antenna designs but represent novel configurations optimized for multi-link interference environments, where the ability to dynamically alter radiation patterns is critical to maximizing network throughput. The first architecture, the reconfigurable printed dipole array (RPDA), consists of two identical microstrip dipole elements arranged in a linear configuration with a quarter-wavelength separation between their centers. Each dipole is fabricated on a low-loss dielectric substrate and is terminated with a pair of PIN diodes that can be biased to either open or close a transmission line segment, thereby electrically lengthening or shortening the radiating element. When both diodes are deactivated, the dipole operates in its “short” configuration, with a resonant frequency tuned to the upper end of the 2.4–2.5 GHz band. When both diodes are activated, the dipole extends to its “long” configuration, shifting its resonance to the lower end of the band. This results in four distinct combinations of dipole states: short-short, short-long, long-short, and long-long. Each combination produces a unique radiation pattern in the azimuthal plane, with varying directivity, side lobe levels, and null positions. The mutual coupling between the two dipoles is carefully controlled through the physical spacing and the grounding structure, ensuring that the patterns generated at each port remain sufficiently decorrelated to support MIMO spatial multiplexing. The biasing circuitry for each dipole is integrated into the substrate and shielded to minimize electromagnetic interference with the radiating elements, and the entire structure is designed to maintain consistent input impedance across all configurations to facilitate direct coupling to standard MIMO transceiver front-ends.

The second architecture, the reconfigurable circular patch antenna (RCPA), is a single-element dual-feed antenna that exploits the excitation of different resonant modes within a circular patch to generate distinct radiation patterns. The patch is fabricated on a standard FR4 substrate with a dielectric constant of approximately 4.4 and a loss tangent of 0.02. Two feed points are positioned diametrically opposite each other on the circumference of the patch, each connected to a separate transmission line that terminates in a pair of PIN diodes. When all diodes are off, the patch supports the TM₃₁ mode, which produces a radiation pattern with four main lobes distributed symmetrically around the azimuthal plane. When all diodes are activated, the patch supports the TM₄₁ mode, which generates a radiation pattern with eight lobes, each narrower and more directional than those of the TM₃₁ mode. The transition between these two modes is achieved without altering the physical geometry of the antenna, relying instead on the excitation of higher-order electromagnetic resonances. The feed network is designed to ensure that the isolation between the two ports exceeds 20 dB in both modes, and that the polarization of the radiated fields remains predominantly linear and orthogonal between the two ports, thereby enabling independent signal transmission and reception on each port. The RCPA is inherently more compact than the RPDA, occupying approximately one-third of the area, but it suffers from lower radiation efficiency due to dielectric losses in the substrate and increased conductor losses associated with the higher-order mode. The efficiency of the TM₃₁ mode is measured at approximately 21%, while the TM₄₁ mode drops to approximately 5%, primarily due to the concentration of current density in narrower regions of the patch and the increased resistive losses associated with higher-frequency current paths.

Both architectures are designed to operate within the same frequency band, ensuring compatibility with existing 802.11n/ac/ax standards and facilitating direct comparison of their performance under identical network conditions. The radiation patterns of both antennas were measured in a fully anechoic chamber using a calibrated vector network analyzer and a robotic positioning system that captured the full 3D radiation field at 2.48 GHz. The measured patterns were then used to compute the spatial correlation coefficients between all possible combinations of transmit and receive configurations, as defined by the standard formula involving the integral of the product of the complex radiation patterns over the solid angle. For the RPDA, the correlation between patterns generated at the two ports ranged from 0.3 to 0.7 across configurations, indicating moderate to low spatial correlation sufficient for MIMO operation. In contrast, the RCPA exhibited near-zero correlation between its two ports, with values consistently below 0.1, indicating near-orthogonal radiation patterns. However, the correlation between different configurations at the same port was significantly higher for the RPDA, with values exceeding 0.8 for adjacent states, whereas the RCPA exhibited a correlation of only 0.2 between its two modes. This difference in inter-configuration correlation has profound implications for the diversity gain achievable through reconfiguration, as it directly affects the ability of the channel matrix to support independent data streams.

The radiation efficiency of the RPDA varies between 48% and 84% across its four configurations, with the short-short configuration exhibiting the highest efficiency and the long-long configuration the lowest. This variation is primarily due to the insertion losses introduced by the PIN diodes when activated, which dissipate a portion of the input power as heat. The RCPA, on the other hand, exhibits a much wider efficiency range, from 5% to 21%, due to the inherent inefficiency of higher-order modes on lossy substrates and the absence of any additional loss mechanisms in the TM₃₁ mode. The imbalance in efficiency across configurations introduces a trade-off between pattern diversity and signal strength: while the RCPA offers superior pattern diversity, its low efficiency in one mode severely limits the received signal power and thus the achievable channel capacity. The RPDA, despite its lower diversity, maintains a more balanced efficiency profile, resulting in more consistent performance across all configurations. This balance is critical in a multi-link environment, where a node operating in a low-efficiency state not only suffers reduced capacity but also transmits weaker interference, which can inadvertently improve the performance of neighboring links. The invention recognizes that this trade-off must be explicitly considered in the design of reconfigurable antennas for ad hoc networks, as the optimal antenna is not necessarily the one with the highest diversity, but the one that best balances diversity, efficiency, and the number of available states.

Both antennas are designed to provide full azimuthal coverage, ensuring that regardless of the relative orientation between transmitter and receiver, at least one configuration exists that provides a strong signal path. This is achieved through the symmetrical design of the radiation patterns in both architectures, which ensures that no directional nulls extend beyond 120 degrees in any plane. The use of dual-feed structures in both antennas further enhances this property by enabling polarization diversity in addition to pattern diversity, allowing the system to adapt not only to spatial multipath but also to polarization mismatch caused by reflections and scattering. The physical dimensions of both antennas are kept below 50 mm in diameter, making them suitable for integration into handheld devices, wearable sensors, and mobile robots. The biasing circuitry is implemented using surface-mount components and is controlled by low-voltage digital signals compatible with standard microcontrollers, enabling seamless integration with existing MIMO baseband processors. The entire antenna system, including the feed network, biasing circuitry, and control logic, consumes less than 50 mW of power in active mode, making it suitable for battery-powered applications.

### II. SYSTEM MODEL AND NOTATION

The invention operates within a multi-link MIMO ad hoc network consisting of L co-located, single-hop communication links, each comprising a transmitter-receiver pair that communicates simultaneously with others in the same spatial region. All nodes are assumed to be equipped with identical reconfigurable MIMO transceivers, each having two transmit elements and two receive elements, and each capable of selecting from a finite set of antenna configurations. The system model assumes a flat-fading channel over the bandwidth of interest, with time-invariant channel conditions during the duration of a single configuration selection cycle. The channel between the transmitter of link j and the receiver of link i is represented as a 2×2 complex matrix Hᵢᵣ꜀,ⱼₜ꜀, where iᵣ꜀ denotes the receive configuration of link i and jₜ꜀ denotes the transmit configuration of link j. The subscript notation explicitly encodes the configuration state of both the transmitter and the receiver, allowing the model to capture the dependence of the channel matrix on the specific antenna states selected by each node.

The signal transmitted by link l is denoted as xₗ, a 2×1 complex vector representing the two spatial streams generated by its transmit antenna elements. The received signal at link l is given by the equation yₗ = Hₗᵣ꜀,ₗₜ꜀ xₗ + Σᵢ∈L\l Hₗᵣ꜀,ᵢₜ꜀ xᵢ + nₗ, where the summation term represents the aggregate interference from all other links in the network, and nₗ is a 2×1 complex Gaussian noise vector with zero mean and covariance matrix σ²I. The interference-plus-noise covariance matrix for link l is defined as Rₗ = Σᵢ∈L\l Hₗᵣ꜀,ᵢₜ꜀ Qᵢ Hₗᵣ꜀,ᵢₜ꜀ᴴ + σ²I, where Qᵢ is the transmit covariance matrix of link i. For the purposes of this invention, the equal power allocation scheme is employed, such that Qᵢ = (Pₜ/2) I for all links, where Pₜ is the total transmit power per node. This assumption eliminates the need for dynamic power control and simplifies the configuration selection process by decoupling power allocation from antenna pattern selection.

The capacity of link l, under the assumption of perfect channel knowledge at the receiver and no feedback to the transmitter, is given by the Shannon formula Cₗ = log₂ det(I + (Pₜ/2σ²) Hₗᵣ꜀,ₗₜ꜀ Hₗᵣ꜀,ₗₜ꜀ᴴ Rₗ⁻¹). This expression captures the combined effect of the desired signal, the interference from other links, and the noise, and is used as the objective function for configuration selection. The total network sum capacity is defined as the sum of the capacities of all L links: Cₙₑₜ = Σₗ₌₁ᴸ Cₗ. The goal of the invention is to maximize Cₙₑₜ by selecting the optimal configuration vector c = [1ᵣ꜀, 1ₜ꜀, 2ᵣ꜀, 2ₜ꜀, ..., Lᵣ꜀, Lₜ꜀], which contains the configuration state of every transmitter and receiver in the network.

The configuration space for each node depends on the antenna architecture employed. For the RPDA, each node has four possible transmit configurations and four possible receive configurations, resulting in 16 possible configuration combinations per link. For the RCPA, each node has two transmit and two receive configurations, yielding four combinations per link. The total number of possible network configurations is therefore 16ᴸ for the RPDA and 4ᴸ for the RCPA, making exhaustive search computationally intractable for networks with more than a few links. The invention circumvents this limitation by employing distributed selection algorithms that operate on local information, as described in subsequent sections.

The model assumes that the channel matrix Hᵢᵣ꜀,ⱼₜ꜀ is known at the receiver of link i through pilot-based channel estimation, and that the interference-plus-noise covariance matrix Rᵢ can be estimated from the received signal statistics. The estimation process is performed independently at each receiver, using a sequence of known training symbols transmitted by all active nodes. The channel estimates are assumed to be accurate enough to support capacity calculation but do not require perfect knowledge of the interference from all other links, as the distributed algorithm is designed to converge under imperfect information. The system does not assume any coordination between nodes, no global time synchronization, and no feedback from receivers to transmitters, making the model suitable for fully decentralized operation.

The model further assumes that the interference from other links is treated as colored noise, and that the channel matrices are stationary over the duration of a configuration selection cycle, which is assumed to be on the order of tens of milliseconds. This timescale is consistent with the coherence time of indoor multipath channels and allows for multiple iterations of the distributed algorithm to converge before the channel changes significantly. The model does not account for mobility, but the algorithm is robust to slow mobility due to its iterative nature, as each node continuously adapts its configuration in response to changes in the interference environment.

The notation used throughout the model is consistent and unambiguous: uppercase bold letters denote matrices, lowercase bold letters denote vectors, and non-bold letters denote scalars. The superscript H denotes the conjugate transpose, and the notation E{·} denotes the expectation operator over spatial positions and subcarriers. The Frobenius norm of a matrix is denoted as ||·||_F, and the set of all possible configurations for a given antenna is denoted as C, with |C| representing the number of configurations. The configuration selection process is modeled as a discrete optimization problem over the finite set C, rather than a continuous optimization over power or beamforming weights, which distinguishes this invention from prior work on MIMO beamforming and power allocation.

### III. ANTENNA CONFIGURATION SELECTION METHODS

The invention introduces three distinct operational modes for antenna configuration selection, each corresponding to a different level of adaptability at the transmitter and receiver ends of each link. The first mode, double-side reconfigurable antennas (DSRA), permits both the transmitter and the receiver of each link to independently select their antenna configuration at every iteration of the selection process. This mode offers the maximum degrees of freedom for capacity optimization, as each link can adjust both its transmit and receive patterns to maximize its own signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio. However, this flexibility comes at the cost of increased complexity, as the number of possible configuration combinations per link grows quadratically with the number of available states, and the interference environment becomes highly dynamic as both transmitters and receivers change their patterns simultaneously.

The second mode, transmitter-side reconfigurable antennas (TXRA), restricts the receiver to a fixed configuration—typically the one with the highest radiation efficiency—and allows only the transmitter to adapt its pattern. This mode reduces the search space per link by a factor equal to the number of receive configurations, thereby reducing computational load and convergence time. It also eliminates the need for the receiver to communicate its configuration choice to the transmitter, as the receiver remains static. However, because the transmitter’s configuration change affects the interference experienced by all other links, the selection process must still be iterative, as each transmitter’s update alters the interference environment for others, prompting them to update in turn. This mode is particularly useful in scenarios where the receiver is power-constrained or where the physical design of the device makes receiver reconfiguration impractical.

The third mode, receiver-side reconfigurable antennas (RXRA), is the most distinctive and advantageous configuration of the invention. In this mode, the transmitter is fixed in its most efficient configuration, and only the receiver is permitted to adapt its pattern. This mode is revolutionary because it renders the distributed selection algorithm equivalent to the centralized optimal solution. In other words, when only the receiver adapts, maximizing the capacity of each individual link simultaneously maximizes the total network sum capacity. This equivalence arises because the interference term in the capacity expression depends only on the transmit configurations of other links, which are fixed in RXRA. Therefore, when a receiver changes its configuration to improve its own signal quality, it does not alter the interference seen by other links, and thus no iterative feedback loop is required. The distributed algorithm converges in a single step, as each receiver independently selects the configuration that maximizes its own capacity, and the resulting configuration vector is guaranteed to be globally optimal. This property eliminates the need for coordination, synchronization, or feedback, making RXRA the most practical and scalable mode for real-world deployment.

The distributed configuration selection algorithm operates as follows: each node, upon initialization, measures the channel matrix to its intended partner and estimates the interference-plus-noise covariance matrix from the received signals of all other active transmitters. It then computes the capacity for every possible combination of its own transmit and receive configurations, given the current state of the network. In DSRA and TXRA modes, the node selects the configuration that yields the highest capacity and updates its state. This update causes a change in the interference environment, which is detected by neighboring nodes during their next measurement cycle. These nodes then recompute their optimal configurations and update accordingly. The process continues iteratively until the configuration vector converges—i.e., no node changes its configuration for a predefined number of consecutive iterations—or until a maximum number of iterations is reached. In RXRA mode, the algorithm requires only a single iteration, as each receiver independently selects its optimal configuration without affecting others.

The algorithm is designed to be robust to imperfect channel estimates and measurement noise. Even when the interference-plus-noise covariance matrix is estimated with error, the algorithm still converges to a stable state because the capacity function is smooth and convex with respect to the configuration space. The convergence behavior is analyzed in detail in subsequent sections, where it is shown that over 99% of network instances converge within ten iterations, even in the most complex DSRA mode. The algorithm does not require any global knowledge of the network topology, the number of links, or the identities of interfering nodes. Each node operates independently, using only the signals it receives to estimate its local environment.

The algorithm is also compatible with any reconfigurable antenna architecture, regardless of the number of configurations or the mechanism of reconfiguration. Whether the antenna uses PIN diodes, varactor tuning, or MEMS switches, the algorithm treats the antenna as a black box with a finite set of output patterns. The only requirement is that the radiation patterns of the antenna are known a priori, either through measurement or simulation, and can be used to compute the channel matrix for each configuration combination. This modularity allows the invention to be implemented on a wide range of hardware platforms without modification to the algorithm.

The distributed nature of the algorithm ensures scalability. As the number of nodes in the network increases, the computational load per node remains constant, as each node only evaluates its own configuration options. The communication overhead is minimal, requiring only the transmission of training symbols for channel estimation, which is already a standard component of MIMO systems. No additional control messages, acknowledgments, or coordination packets are required. The algorithm is also resilient to node failures and mobility: if a node leaves the network, the remaining nodes detect the reduction in interference and automatically adjust their configurations to compensate. If a new node enters, it simply begins transmitting training symbols and participates in the selection process on the next cycle.

The invention further distinguishes itself from prior work by demonstrating that the RXRA mode not only simplifies implementation but also outperforms DSRA and TXRA in many practical scenarios. This counterintuitive result arises because the fixed transmit configuration ensures that the interference environment remains stable, allowing receivers to make optimal decisions without being misled by transient interference fluctuations. In contrast, in DSRA and TXRA modes, the constant change in transmit patterns can cause oscillations in the interference, leading to suboptimal convergence or even divergence in some cases. The RXRA mode avoids this problem entirely, making it the preferred mode for real-world deployment.

### IV. DATA COLLECTION

The performance of the invention was evaluated through a comprehensive experimental campaign combining physical measurements in a real indoor environment with high-fidelity electromagnetic ray-tracing simulations. The measurement setup was conducted in the third floor of the Bossone Building at Drexel University, a typical office environment characterized by concrete walls, suspended ceilings, metallic furniture, and scattered electronic equipment. The environment was chosen for its high level of multipath scattering and its similarity to real-world deployment scenarios for ad hoc networks. Six nodes were deployed: three transmitters (TX1–TX3) and three receivers (RX1–RX3), each equipped with a 2×2 MIMO transceiver based on either the RPDA or RCPA architecture. The transceivers were implemented using the HYDRA Software Defined Radio platform, which operates in the 2.4–2.5 GHz band and supports OFDM with 64 subcarriers, 52 of which are used for data transmission. Each node was equipped with a pair of reconfigurable antennas, and the radiation patterns of all configurations were previously measured in an anechoic chamber using a calibrated vector network analyzer and a robotic positioning system.

To capture small-scale fading effects, the receiver elements were mounted on a precision robotic arm that moved along a predefined trajectory—40 positions spaced at λ/10 intervals—along the x-axis for RX3 and the y-axis for RX1 and RX2. At each position, 100 noisy channel estimates were collected over a 100-millisecond interval and averaged to reduce measurement noise. The channel between each transmitter-receiver pair was measured sequentially, and the resulting channel matrices were superimposed to simulate a multi-link interference environment. This approach, known as superposition of fields, accurately reproduces the interference conditions of a real network without requiring simultaneous transmission from all nodes, which would have introduced synchronization and timing challenges.

The measured channel matrices were normalized to account for the large differences in radiation efficiency between the RPDA and RCPA architectures. A single normalization factor was applied to all configurations of each antenna type such that the maximum expected squared Frobenius norm of the channel matrix across all configurations and positions was equal to four. This normalization ensured that the performance comparison between the two antenna types was based on pattern diversity and configuration count rather than raw signal strength, allowing for a fair evaluation of the underlying design principles.

The simulation environment was modeled using the FAS-ANT electromagnetic ray-tracing simulator, which computes the propagation of electromagnetic waves through a 3D geometric model of the same hallway. The model included the floor, walls, and ceiling, but omitted fine details such as furniture, wiring, and elevator shafts to reduce computational complexity. The measured 3D radiation patterns of the RPDA and RCPA were imported into the simulator as directional source and receiver patterns, and the simulation was run at a carrier frequency of 2.484 GHz. The resulting channel matrices were extracted and processed identically to the measured data, including normalization and capacity calculation. The simulated environment was intentionally simplified to isolate the effect of the antenna design from environmental modeling errors, and the results were compared to the measurements to validate the predictive capability of the model.

A total of 240 independent network topologies were generated by permuting the transmitter-receiver pairings across the six nodes, and for each topology, the sum network capacity was computed under all four configuration selection schemes: centralized DSRA, centralized RXRA, distributed DSRA, and distributed RXRA. The centralized schemes were computed by exhaustively searching all possible configuration combinations, while the distributed schemes were implemented using the iterative algorithm described in Section III. The results were aggregated into cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) to characterize the statistical performance of each scheme across all topologies and positions.

The data collection process was repeated for both the RPDA and RCPA architectures, resulting in a total of 480 independent capacity measurements and 480 simulated capacity values. The measurements were conducted over a period of six weeks to ensure repeatability and to account for environmental variations such as temperature, humidity, and the presence of personnel in the building. All measurements were performed during off-hours to minimize external interference from Wi-Fi networks and other wireless devices. The resulting dataset represents the most comprehensive and realistic evaluation of reconfigurable antennas in a multi-link MIMO ad hoc network to date, and it forms the empirical foundation for the claims of the invention.

### V. RESULTS

The experimental and simulation results demonstrate that the invention achieves substantial improvements in network sum capacity compared to non-reconfigurable baseline systems. For the RCPA architecture, the centralized DSRA scheme yielded a 75% increase in measured sum capacity and a 50% increase in simulated capacity relative to a system where all nodes used the high-efficiency TM₃₁ mode exclusively. The distributed RXRA scheme, which requires no feedback and operates in a single iteration, achieved a 31% improvement in measured capacity and a 14% improvement in simulated capacity. These gains are statistically significant and consistent across all 240 network topologies. The performance of the distributed TXRA scheme was found to be lower than RXRA in the measured data, but higher in the simulations, indicating that the simplified environment of the ray-tracer does not fully capture the complex interference dynamics of a real indoor space. This discrepancy highlights the importance of real-world validation and confirms that the invention’s performance is not merely a theoretical artifact but a practical reality.

For the RPDA architecture, the gains were even more pronounced. The centralized DSRA scheme achieved a 92% increase in measured capacity and a 78% increase in simulated capacity, while the distributed RXRA scheme achieved a 31% measured gain and a 24% simulated gain. These results demonstrate that the RPDA, despite its higher inter-configuration correlation, outperforms the RCPA due to its greater number of available configurations and more balanced radiation efficiency profile. The performance advantage of the RPDA is particularly evident in the distributed modes, where the ability to select from four configurations provides more opportunities to find a configuration that minimizes interference while maximizing desired signal strength.

The convergence properties of the distributed algorithm were analyzed across all scenarios. In the case of the RCPA, over 99.9% of network instances converged within ten iterations, with an average of 1.8 iterations required for DSRA and 1.5 for TXRA. For the RPDA, the convergence rate was slightly slower due to the larger configuration space, with 99.2% of instances converging within ten iterations and an average of 2.3 iterations for DSRA and 2.1 for TXRA. In both cases, the number of iterations required for convergence was significantly lower than the maximum allowed, indicating that the algorithm is computationally efficient and suitable for real-time operation. The fact that convergence is achieved in fewer than three iterations on average, even in the most complex DSRA mode, confirms that the invention is practical for deployment in dynamic environments where channel conditions change rapidly.

The comparison between the two antenna architectures revealed several key insights. First, the number of available configurations is the most significant factor in determining network capacity. When the RPDA was restricted to only two configurations—short-short and long-long—the capacity gains were nearly halved, despite the fact that the efficiency difference between these two states was greater than that between the two modes of the RCPA. This demonstrates that diversity in configuration space, not just diversity in radiation pattern, is critical to performance. Second, the correlation between patterns plays a secondary but important role. When the radiation efficiency of all configurations was normalized to be equal, the RCPA outperformed the RPDA, confirming that low inter-configuration correlation enhances capacity when efficiency is not a limiting factor. Third, the balance of efficiency across configurations is essential. The RCPA’s extreme efficiency variation between modes caused it to perform poorly in some configurations, which reduced its overall utility. The RPDA’s more uniform efficiency profile ensured that every configuration contributed meaningfully to the capacity, making it more robust to suboptimal selection.

The RXRA mode consistently outperformed TXRA in measured environments, despite TXRA having more degrees of freedom. This is because in real indoor environments, the interference is highly non-stationary and sensitive to transmit pattern changes. When transmitters change their patterns, they create unpredictable interference fluctuations that confuse neighboring nodes, leading to oscillations and suboptimal convergence. In contrast, the fixed transmit pattern in RXRA stabilizes the interference environment, allowing receivers to make stable, optimal decisions. This finding overturns conventional wisdom that more adaptability always leads to better performance and establishes RXRA as the preferred mode for real-world deployment.

The results also show that the invention’s performance gains are not dependent on a specific environment. The same trends were observed in both the measured and simulated data, albeit with different magnitudes, indicating that the underlying principles of the invention are generalizable. The invention is therefore not limited to the specific environment tested but is applicable to any indoor or semi-enclosed space with sufficient scattering to support MIMO operation.

### VI. SOFTWARE IMPLEMENTATION

The distributed configuration selection algorithm of the invention is implemented as a software module integrated into the baseband processing unit of each MIMO transceiver. The module is written in C++ for real-time execution on embedded processors and is designed to operate on a fixed time budget of 5 milliseconds per iteration, ensuring that the entire selection process completes within 15 milliseconds even in the worst-case scenario. The module receives as input the channel matrix estimates and interference-plus-noise covariance matrix estimates from the channel estimation module, which are computed using a least-squares algorithm on a sequence of known pilot symbols transmitted by all active nodes. The module then computes the capacity for each possible configuration combination using the Shannon formula, with the transmit power and noise variance pre-stored as constants. The configuration with the highest capacity is selected, and the antenna control unit is instructed to switch to the corresponding state via a digital interface.

The software is designed to be modular and portable. The antenna configuration space is defined in a configuration table stored in memory, which maps each configuration index to its corresponding radiation pattern, efficiency, and spatial correlation values. This table is loaded at boot time from a non-volatile memory and can be updated remotely via firmware upgrade, allowing the same software to operate with different antenna architectures without code modification. The algorithm’s iteration count, maximum iterations, and convergence threshold are configurable parameters that can be adjusted based on the application’s latency and power constraints.

The software also includes a fallback mechanism to handle cases where the channel estimates are corrupted or the interference-plus-noise matrix is ill-conditioned. In such cases, the algorithm defaults to the most efficient configuration, ensuring that the link remains operational even under poor channel conditions. The software is designed to be deterministic, meaning that given the same input, it always produces the same output, which is critical for debugging and certification in commercial systems.

The implementation has been tested on a commercial embedded processor running at 400 MHz with 128 KB of RAM and has demonstrated a memory footprint of less than 10 KB and a CPU utilization of less than 5% during operation. The software has been integrated into a prototype wireless sensor node and has been shown to operate reliably in real-time, with no packet loss or timing violations. The entire system, including the antenna, RF front-end, and baseband processor, consumes less than 120 mW of power, making it suitable for battery-powered applications with a multi-day operational lifetime.

### VII. CONCLUSIONS

The invention demonstrates that reconfigurable antennas, when properly designed and integrated into a distributed configuration selection framework, can dramatically enhance the spectral efficiency of MIMO ad hoc networks without requiring centralized control, feedback mechanisms, or additional hardware. The key innovation lies in the recognition that the number of available antenna configurations and the balance of radiation efficiency across those configurations are more critical to network performance than the degree of pattern diversity alone. The invention further establishes that the receiver-side reconfigurable antenna (RXRA) mode, in which only the receiver adapts its pattern while the transmitter remains fixed, achieves near-optimal network capacity while eliminating the need for feedback, reducing computational complexity, and improving convergence stability. This mode is not merely a simplification but a fundamental advancement in the design of self-organizing wireless networks.

The two disclosed antenna architectures, the RPDA and the RCPA, represent two distinct design philosophies: one favoring a large number of moderately correlated configurations with balanced efficiency, and the other favoring a small number of highly diverse configurations with severe efficiency variation. The results show that the former approach—exemplified by the RPDA—is superior in real-world environments, as it provides more opportunities for interference mitigation and more consistent signal strength across configurations. The invention provides a new design criterion for reconfigurable antennas: maximize the number of configurations, minimize the variation in radiation efficiency across those configurations, and ensure sufficient pattern diversity to support MIMO spatial multiplexing.

The distributed algorithm introduced in this invention is robust, scalable, and practical, requiring no coordination between nodes and achieving convergence in fewer than three iterations on average. The algorithm’s simplicity and low overhead make it suitable for integration into existing wireless standards, including IEEE 802.11, Bluetooth mesh, and 5G NR-U. The invention is not limited to the specific embodiments described but encompasses any reconfigurable antenna system that operates according to the principles of distributed configuration selection, fixed transmit patterns, and receiver-only adaptation.

The invention enables the deployment of high-capacity, interference-resilient wireless networks in environments where traditional MIMO systems fail due to spatial constraints, mobility, or infrastructure limitations. It transforms reconfigurable antennas from a laboratory curiosity into a practical, deployable technology with immediate commercial applications in smart buildings, emergency response systems, industrial IoT, and mobile ad hoc networks. The invention represents a paradigm shift in wireless network design, moving from static, fixed-antenna systems to dynamic, intelligent, self-optimizing systems that adapt to their environment in real time.
