# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of anomaly detection has long been a cornerstone of machine learning systems deployed in critical domains such as manufacturing quality control, cybersecurity, financial fraud prevention, medical diagnostics, and industrial monitoring. The ability to identify rare, atypical, or malicious patterns within vast streams of data remains indispensable for maintaining system integrity, ensuring regulatory compliance, and preventing catastrophic failures. Traditional approaches to anomaly detection have broadly fallen into two opposing paradigms: fully supervised and fully unsupervised methods. Fully supervised methods require comprehensive labeling of both normal and anomalous instances during training, enabling the construction of binary classifiers that distinguish between the two classes with high precision. These methods, while effective in controlled environments, are severely constrained by the prohibitive cost and logistical difficulty of acquiring accurate, exhaustive labels—particularly in domains where anomalies are rare, diverse, or constantly evolving. In manufacturing, for instance, the emergence of novel defect types may occur unpredictably, rendering previously labeled datasets obsolete before they can be fully annotated. In financial systems, fraudsters continuously adapt their tactics, making it impractical to label every new form of illicit activity as it arises. The reliance on exhaustive labeling thus introduces a fundamental bottleneck that limits scalability and real-world applicability.

At the opposite extreme, fully unsupervised anomaly detection methods operate without any labeled data, relying instead on assumptions about the structure of normal data to infer deviations. Techniques such as Isolation Forest, One-Class Support Vector Machines (OC-SVM), and autoencoder-based reconstruction models attempt to characterize the normal data manifold and flag instances that lie far from it as anomalies. While these methods eliminate the need for labeled examples entirely, they suffer from significant performance degradation in real-world settings. Without any guidance from known anomalies, unsupervised models often fail to distinguish between benign outliers and true malicious deviations, resulting in high false positive rates or, worse, missed detections of novel threats. The performance gap between supervised and unsupervised methods is well-documented, with empirical studies consistently showing that the absence of even minimal labeled information leads to substantial drops in detection accuracy, particularly in high-dimensional or heterogeneous data spaces. This dichotomy—between the high accuracy of supervised methods and the practical feasibility of unsupervised ones—has motivated the pursuit of semi-supervised approaches that seek to bridge the gap by leveraging a small set of labeled examples alongside a much larger pool of unlabeled data.

However, the prevailing assumptions underlying most semi-supervised learning frameworks are fundamentally incompatible with the realities of anomaly detection in dynamic environments. Conventional semi-supervised methods, including those based on consistency regularization, pseudo-labeling, or self-training, operate under the implicit assumption that labeled and unlabeled data are drawn from the same underlying distribution. This assumption, while mathematically convenient and widely adopted in image classification, natural language processing, and other domains, is rarely valid in anomaly detection applications. In practice, the process of labeling data is inherently biased. Human annotators, whether domain experts or crowd workers, tend to focus on samples that are easily interpretable, visually distinct, or confidently classifiable, leaving behind the ambiguous, subtle, or complex cases as unlabeled. This results in a systematic divergence between the distribution of labeled data—which is skewed toward prototypical, high-confidence examples—and the distribution of unlabeled data—which contains the majority of edge cases, novel variations, and difficult-to-classify instances. Moreover, in time-evolving systems such as fraud detection or industrial production lines, the data distribution itself changes over time. Labeled data may reflect historical patterns, while unlabeled data captures new, unseen anomalies that have emerged since the labeling process concluded. In such cases, the labeled set becomes a static snapshot of a past state, while the unlabeled set represents the current, evolving reality. This temporal drift further exacerbates the distribution mismatch, rendering standard semi-supervised techniques ineffective.

The consequences of this mismatch are profound. When pseudo-labeling is applied under distributional disparity, the model’s predictions become heavily biased toward the characteristics of the small, non-representative labeled set. The resulting pseudo-labels, derived from a classifier trained on this biased sample, propagate and amplify the initial bias across the unlabeled data, leading to a feedback loop that reinforces incorrect assumptions. For example, if the labeled data contains only a subset of known anomaly types, the model will fail to recognize novel anomalies present in the unlabeled data, treating them as normal due to their deviation from the learned prototype. Similarly, if labeled data consists predominantly of easy-to-identify normal samples, the model may misclassify complex, rare normal instances as anomalies. This phenomenon is particularly acute in positive-unlabeled (PU) learning scenarios, where only anomalous examples are labeled and all other data is treated as unlabeled. In such cases, the unlabeled set contains both normal and unannotated anomalous samples, creating a fundamental ambiguity that standard methods cannot resolve without explicit modeling of the distributional gap.

Existing attempts to address distributional shifts in semi-supervised learning—such as domain adaptation, transfer learning, or reweighting schemes—have shown limited success in the context of anomaly detection. These approaches typically assume the availability of sufficient labeled data from the source domain to learn meaningful representations or alignment functions. However, in anomaly detection, the labeled data is intentionally minimal by design, often comprising less than 5% of the total training set. Under such extreme data scarcity, domain adaptation techniques struggle to learn robust mappings between source and target distributions, leading to poor generalization. Furthermore, many of these methods rely on validation sets to tune hyperparameters such as regularization strength, learning rates, or threshold values. In real-world applications, however, acquiring a separate validation set from the same distribution as the labeled data is often impossible without sacrificing an already scarce portion of the labeled budget. This creates a catch-22: the very mechanism used to optimize model performance—validation-based hyperparameter tuning—is rendered infeasible by the constraints that motivate the semi-supervised approach in the first place.

The result is a critical gap in the literature and in practical deployment: there exists no robust, scalable, and data-efficient framework for semi-supervised anomaly detection that can operate effectively under the pervasive and unavoidable condition of distribution mismatch. Current methods either ignore the problem entirely, leading to degraded performance, or attempt to mitigate it through indirect means that are insufficient under real-world constraints. The need for a novel paradigm that explicitly accounts for distributional discrepancies, leverages minimal labeled data without overfitting, and eliminates reliance on validation sets for hyperparameter selection has never been more urgent. This invention addresses this gap by introducing a comprehensive framework that redefines the architecture of semi-supervised anomaly detection through a synergistic combination of ensemble one-class classifiers, partial distribution matching, and joint supervised-self-supervised learning—all designed to function optimally under the challenging conditions of real-world deployment.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a novel framework for semi-supervised anomaly detection that overcomes the fundamental limitations of prior art by explicitly addressing the challenge of distribution mismatch between labeled and unlabeled data. Unlike conventional approaches that assume homogeneity between labeled and unlabeled data distributions, this invention operates under the realistic premise that such distributions are inherently divergent due to labeling biases, temporal evolution of anomalies, or selective sampling protocols. The framework, referred to as Semi-supervised Pseudo-labeler Anomaly Detection with Ensembling (SPADE), integrates four core components: an encoder, a predictor, a pseudo-labeler, and a projection head, all trained in a unified, iterative optimization loop that dynamically adapts to distributional shifts without requiring external validation data or assumptions of data homogeneity.

The encoder transforms raw input features into a latent representation space, serving as the foundation for all subsequent processing. The predictor, a binary classifier operating on the encoded representations, is trained exclusively on labeled data and on unlabeled data that have been assigned high-confidence pseudo-labels, ensuring that model updates are grounded in reliable information. The pseudo-labeler, a novel and central innovation, is constructed as an ensemble of multiple one-class classifiers, each independently modeling the distribution of normal or anomalous data without direct supervision from the labeled set. These one-class classifiers collectively vote on the label of each unlabeled sample, assigning a pseudo-label of anomalous, normal, or unknown based on unanimous agreement among all ensemble members. This consensus-based mechanism ensures that pseudo-labels are only generated when the ensemble exhibits high internal consistency, effectively filtering out ambiguous or uncertain predictions that would otherwise introduce noise and bias into the training process. By decoupling the pseudo-labeling mechanism from the predictor, the framework prevents the propagation of biases from the small, potentially skewed labeled set into the broader unlabeled data, thereby enhancing robustness to distributional shifts.

A critical innovation of this invention is the method for automatically determining the decision thresholds used in the pseudo-labeling process. Rather than relying on a validation set—a resource often unavailable in low-label regimes—the framework employs a partial matching technique grounded in optimal transport theory to estimate the marginal distributions of anomaly scores within the unlabeled data relative to the labeled data. Specifically, the Wasserstein distance between the anomaly score distributions of labeled normal samples and unlabeled samples is minimized to identify the threshold for assigning negative pseudo-labels, while the same principle is applied to labeled anomalous samples to determine the threshold for positive pseudo-labels. This approach enables the framework to autonomously calibrate its decision boundaries without consuming any additional labeled data, preserving the integrity of the limited labeling budget and ensuring optimal performance even in extreme data scarcity scenarios.

To further enhance the quality of the latent representations and improve generalization, the framework incorporates a self-supervised learning module via a projection head. This module is trained using auxiliary tasks such as contrastive learning, reconstruction, or data augmentation-based objectives, depending on the data modality. These self-supervised signals provide additional regularization and encourage the encoder to learn representations that are invariant to irrelevant variations, thereby improving the separability of normal and anomalous instances in the latent space. The entire system—encoder, predictor, projection head, and pseudo-labeler—is optimized jointly using a composite loss function that combines supervised binary cross-entropy on labeled and pseudo-labeled data with a self-supervised loss on the full dataset. The relative weighting of these components is fixed and empirically validated, eliminating the need for hyperparameter tuning via validation sets.

This invention achieves state-of-the-art performance across a diverse array of real-world benchmarks, including tabular datasets representing financial and medical anomalies and image datasets capturing manufacturing defects. In scenarios involving novel anomaly types not present in the labeled set, the framework demonstrates up to a 10.6% improvement in AUC over existing methods. In positive-unlabeled settings, where only anomalous samples are labeled, it outperforms specialized PU learning techniques by a significant margin. In time-evolving fraud detection systems, where the distribution of anomalies shifts over time, SPADE maintains consistent performance without re-labeling, while competing methods degrade substantially. Crucially, the framework achieves these gains without requiring any validation data, making it uniquely suited for deployment in environments where labeling is costly, time-consuming, or logistically infeasible. The invention thus provides a complete, end-to-end solution for semi-supervised anomaly detection that is not only more accurate but also fundamentally more practical than any prior approach.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention relates to a novel framework for semi-supervised anomaly detection that enables high-accuracy identification of anomalous patterns in data under conditions of severe label scarcity and inherent distributional mismatch between labeled and unlabeled datasets. The framework, designated as Semi-supervised Pseudo-labeler Anomaly Detection with Ensembling (SPADE), is designed to operate in real-world environments where labeled data is limited, costly to obtain, and systematically biased, while unlabeled data reflects the true, evolving, and often heterogeneous distribution of the operational environment. The invention is not merely an incremental improvement over existing methods but constitutes a paradigm shift in how semi-supervised anomaly detection is conceptualized, implemented, and deployed. It achieves this by integrating four interdependent components—an encoder, a predictor, a pseudo-labeler, and a projection head—into a unified, iterative learning architecture that dynamically adapts to distributional shifts without requiring external validation data or assumptions of data homogeneity.

The encoder serves as the foundational component of the system, responsible for transforming raw input features into a compact, semantically meaningful latent representation. This transformation is critical, as the quality of the latent space directly determines the separability of normal and anomalous instances in subsequent stages. The encoder may be implemented using any neural network architecture appropriate to the data modality. For tabular data, such as financial transaction records, medical diagnostic profiles, or industrial sensor readings, a multi-layer perceptron with two hidden layers is employed, where the dimensionality of the hidden layers is set to half the original feature space to encourage dimensionality reduction and noise filtering. For image data, such as defect inspection in manufacturing or medical imaging, a convolutional neural network, specifically a ResNet-18 architecture, is utilized to capture spatial hierarchies and local patterns. The encoder is initialized with random weights and is trained end-to-end alongside the other components of the system. Its parameters are updated through backpropagation based on the composite loss function derived from both supervised and self-supervised signals. Importantly, the encoder is not pre-trained on external datasets or frozen during training; instead, it is co-optimized with the predictor and projection head to ensure that the latent representations are tailored specifically to the anomaly detection task at hand, rather than being generic or transferable features.

The predictor is a binary classifier whose function is to assign a probability score indicating the likelihood that a given sample is anomalous. This classifier operates on the latent representations produced by the encoder and outputs a scalar value between zero and one, interpreted as the anomaly score. The predictor is implemented as a fully connected neural network with a single output neuron and a sigmoid activation function, ensuring that its outputs are interpretable as probabilities. The key constraint governing the predictor’s training is that it is only updated using samples for which the label is known with high confidence: namely, the original labeled data and the unlabeled data that have been assigned pseudo-labels by the pseudo-labeler. This selective training mechanism is a deliberate design choice to prevent the model from being corrupted by unreliable or biased predictions derived from uncertain unlabeled samples. The predictor is trained using binary cross-entropy loss, which measures the discrepancy between the predicted anomaly scores and the true or pseudo-labeled ground truth. The loss is computed separately for the labeled set and the pseudo-labeled set, and both contributions are aggregated into the overall training objective. The predictor does not have access to unlabeled samples that have been marked as unknown by the pseudo-labeler, thereby insulating it from the most ambiguous and potentially misleading instances in the data.

The pseudo-labeler is the most novel and critical component of the invention. Unlike conventional semi-supervised methods that use the predictor itself to generate pseudo-labels—a practice that propagates the predictor’s inherent biases into the unlabeled data—the pseudo-labeler in this invention is a completely independent module composed of an ensemble of multiple one-class classifiers. Each one-class classifier is trained exclusively on either the labeled normal samples or the labeled anomalous samples, without any knowledge of the other class or the unlabeled data. In the preferred embodiment, five Gaussian Distribution Estimators (GDEs) are employed as the base one-class classifiers, chosen for their computational efficiency, interpretability, and suitability for high-dimensional data. Each GDE models the distribution of its assigned class (normal or anomalous) as a multivariate Gaussian, estimating the mean and covariance matrix from the labeled data. The ensemble operates by computing the anomaly score for each unlabeled sample with respect to each of the five GDEs. A sample is assigned a pseudo-label of anomalous only if all five GDEs unanimously agree that its score exceeds a predefined threshold η_p, indicating that the sample is an outlier with respect to the normal class. Similarly, a sample is assigned a pseudo-label of normal only if all five GDEs unanimously agree that its score falls below a threshold η_n, indicating that it is consistent with the anomalous class. If the ensemble fails to reach unanimous agreement—meaning that some classifiers classify the sample as anomalous while others classify it as normal—the sample is marked as unknown and excluded from training the predictor. This unanimity requirement is a deliberate safeguard against overconfidence and ensures that pseudo-labels are only generated when the ensemble exhibits consensus, thereby significantly reducing the risk of introducing false positives or false negatives into the training process. The use of an ensemble of one-class classifiers, rather than a single classifier or a binary classifier, is essential to the robustness of the framework. One-class classifiers are inherently less susceptible to overfitting on small labeled sets because they do not attempt to model the full complexity of both classes simultaneously. Their independence from the predictor ensures that the pseudo-labeling process is not contaminated by the predictor’s biases, allowing the framework to generalize effectively even when the labeled data is a non-representative subset of the true data distribution.

The projection head is an auxiliary module designed to enhance the quality of the latent representations learned by the encoder through self-supervised learning. This component is not directly involved in the final anomaly detection decision but plays a crucial role in regularizing the encoder’s training and improving the discriminative power of the latent space. The projection head is implemented as a lightweight neural network that maps the latent representations to a higher-dimensional space for the purpose of applying a self-supervised objective. The specific form of the self-supervised task depends on the data modality. For image data, the CutPaste augmentation technique is employed, wherein patches of the input image are randomly cut and pasted to different locations, creating synthetic anomalies. The projection head is trained to reconstruct the original image from the augmented version, encouraging the encoder to learn features that are invariant to such local perturbations. For tabular data, a contrastive learning objective is used, wherein pairs of samples are generated by applying small random perturbations to the input features, and the projection head is trained to bring the representations of these augmented pairs closer together in the latent space while pushing apart representations of unrelated samples. This self-supervised signal provides additional supervision that is independent of the labeled data, helping the encoder to learn more robust and generalizable features that are less likely to overfit to the biases present in the labeled set. The projection head is jointly optimized with the encoder and predictor using the same training loop, ensuring that all components evolve in concert.

The training of the entire system is governed by a composite loss function that combines three distinct components: supervised binary cross-entropy on labeled data, supervised binary cross-entropy on pseudo-labeled data, and a self-supervised loss on the full dataset. The supervised loss on labeled data ensures that the model learns from the ground truth information available in the small labeled set. The supervised loss on pseudo-labeled data allows the model to leverage the high-confidence predictions generated by the pseudo-labeler, effectively expanding the labeled set without introducing unreliable annotations. The self-supervised loss, derived from the projection head, provides an additional regularization signal that improves the quality of the latent representations. These three losses are combined into a single objective function using fixed weighting coefficients α and β, both set to 1.0 in the preferred embodiment. The use of fixed weights, rather than tunable hyperparameters, is a deliberate design choice that eliminates the need for validation data during training. The entire system is trained iteratively, with the pseudo-labeler being re-computed at the end of each epoch based on the current state of the encoder and the one-class classifiers. This dynamic re-computation ensures that the pseudo-labels evolve in tandem with the model’s understanding of the data, creating a feedback loop that gradually refines both the representations and the decision boundaries. Training continues until the composite loss converges, which is determined by monitoring the loss over a sliding window of five consecutive epochs. If no improvement in the loss is observed over this window, training is terminated, ensuring that the model does not overfit to the limited data.

A critical innovation of this invention is the method for determining the decision thresholds η_p and η_n used by the pseudo-labeler. Conventional approaches to anomaly detection typically require a validation set to tune these thresholds, either through grid search, Bayesian optimization, or manual inspection. However, in the context of semi-supervised anomaly detection with minimal labeling, acquiring a separate validation set is often impossible without sacrificing a portion of the already scarce labeled data. The invention overcomes this limitation by employing a partial matching technique based on optimal transport theory, specifically the Wasserstein distance. The Wasserstein distance measures the minimal cost required to transform one probability distribution into another, providing a principled way to align the anomaly score distributions of the labeled and unlabeled data. To determine η_p, the anomaly scores of the labeled anomalous samples are treated as a reference distribution, and the unlabeled data is scanned to identify the subset whose anomaly score distribution minimizes the Wasserstein distance to the labeled anomalous distribution. The threshold η_p is then set to the value that separates this matched subset from the rest of the unlabeled data. Similarly, to determine η_n, the anomaly scores of the labeled normal samples are used as a reference, and the unlabeled data is matched to identify the subset whose distribution is most similar to the normal class. The threshold η_n is set accordingly. This approach effectively uses the unlabeled data itself to calibrate the decision boundaries, without requiring any labeled samples beyond those already used for training the one-class classifiers. In cases where only one class of labels is available—for example, in positive-unlabeled learning scenarios where only anomalous samples are labeled—the framework employs Otsu’s method to automatically determine the threshold for the unlabeled class. Otsu’s method identifies the threshold that minimizes the intra-class variance of the anomaly scores, effectively segmenting the unlabeled data into two homogeneous groups based on their internal structure. This ensures that the framework remains fully functional even in the most challenging labeling scenarios.

The framework is designed to be modular and adaptable to a wide range of data types and application domains. For tabular data, the use of multi-layer perceptrons and Gaussian Distribution Estimators ensures computational efficiency and scalability to high-dimensional feature spaces. For image data, the integration of convolutional architectures and augmentation-based self-supervision enables the capture of complex spatial patterns and textures. The framework is agnostic to the specific nature of the anomalies being detected, whether they are manufacturing defects, fraudulent transactions, network intrusions, or medical abnormalities. The only requirement is the availability of a small set of labeled examples and a larger pool of unlabeled data, which is the norm in virtually all real-world applications. The training process is entirely self-contained, requiring no external validation set, no manual hyperparameter tuning, and no assumptions about the homogeneity of the data distribution. This makes the framework uniquely suited for deployment in environments where labeling is expensive, time-consuming, or logistically infeasible, such as continuous manufacturing lines, real-time financial monitoring systems, or remote sensor networks.

The performance of the framework has been validated across multiple benchmark datasets spanning both tabular and image modalities. On tabular datasets such as Covertype, Thyroid, and Drug, the framework achieves up to a 10.6% improvement in AUC over the best competing methods, particularly in scenarios involving novel anomaly types not present in the labeled set. On image datasets such as MVTec and Magnetic Tile, the framework demonstrates a 3.6% improvement in AUC, with particularly strong gains in detecting subtle, previously unseen defects. In positive-unlabeled learning settings, where only anomalous samples are labeled, the framework outperforms specialized PU learning techniques by a significant margin, demonstrating its ability to handle extreme label imbalance. In time-evolving fraud detection scenarios, where the distribution of anomalies shifts over time, the framework maintains consistent performance without requiring re-labeling, while competing methods exhibit substantial degradation. These results are achieved without the use of any validation data, confirming the robustness and practicality of the framework in real-world conditions.

The invention also includes a detailed computational analysis demonstrating that the framework is efficient enough for deployment in production environments. Training on tabular datasets requires at most one hour per experiment on a single V100 GPU, while training on image datasets requires at most four hours, primarily due to the computational overhead of the CutPaste augmentation. The pseudo-labeler, which is re-computed once per epoch, is implemented using shallow one-class classifiers (GDEs) to minimize computational cost. The entire system is designed to be trained end-to-end in a single pass, with no need for multi-stage pipelines or external tools. This efficiency, combined with the framework’s high accuracy and robustness to distributional shifts, makes it uniquely suitable for deployment in latency-sensitive, resource-constrained, or continuously evolving environments.

In summary, the invention provides a complete, end-to-end solution for semi-supervised anomaly detection that is superior to all prior art in terms of accuracy, robustness, and practicality. By decoupling pseudo-labeling from prediction, enforcing consensus through ensemble one-class classifiers, automating threshold selection via partial matching, and incorporating self-supervised learning without requiring validation data, the framework overcomes the fundamental limitations of existing methods. It enables high-performance anomaly detection in the most challenging real-world conditions—where labels are scarce, biased, and outdated—thereby unlocking the potential of machine learning in domains where it has previously been impractical to deploy.
