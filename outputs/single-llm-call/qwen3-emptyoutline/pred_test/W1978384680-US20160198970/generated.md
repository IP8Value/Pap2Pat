# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging systems and methods, specifically to a novel technique for determining optimal cardiac gating windows during magnetic resonance coronary angiography. The invention provides a high-temporal-resolution, one-dimensional motion monitoring sequence—referred to herein as the Septal Scout—that measures longitudinal displacement of the ventricular septum to identify periods of minimal myocardial motion, known as diastasis, with greater precision than conventional cine cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) methods. This technique is particularly suited for non-contrast-enhanced, three-dimensional steady-state free precession (SSFP) imaging of the coronary arteries, where motion artifacts arising from cardiac contraction significantly degrade image quality. By leveraging the intrinsic correlation between basal interventricular septal motion and global ventricular diastolic quiescence, the Septal Scout enables the derivation of subject-specific, heartbeat-resolved gating windows that adapt to heart rate variability during breath-hold acquisitions. The invention eliminates reliance on low-temporal-resolution cine-CMR for gating calibration, thereby overcoming the temporal blurring and data binning errors inherent in conventional methods. The resulting improvement in spatial fidelity of coronary vessel boundaries enhances diagnostic confidence for detecting stenoses, plaques, and other pathologies of the coronary arterial tree without exposing patients to ionizing radiation or invasive catheterization. The method is compatible with standard clinical MRI hardware and requires no additional contrast agents, making it readily translatable to existing cardiovascular imaging workflows.

## BACKGROUND

Magnetic resonance coronary angiography (MRCA) has long been recognized as a non-invasive alternative to conventional x-ray angiography for the evaluation of coronary artery disease. Unlike catheter-based techniques, MRCA avoids the risks of vascular injury, contrast-induced nephropathy, and exposure to ionizing radiation, while providing three-dimensional volumetric visualization of the coronary lumen. However, the clinical utility of MRCA has been severely limited by the need for extremely high spatial resolution—typically on the order of 1.5 mm isotropic or finer—to resolve the small, tortuous, and dynamically moving coronary arteries. This requirement necessitates prolonged data acquisition windows that span multiple cardiac cycles, during which cardiac motion introduces severe blurring and ghosting artifacts that obscure vessel boundaries and compromise diagnostic accuracy. To mitigate these motion artifacts, prospective cardiac gating has become the standard of care, wherein image data acquisition is synchronized with specific phases of the cardiac cycle, most commonly the diastasis period, when coronary motion is minimal. Diastasis, defined as the interval of relative quiescence between the early (E) and late (A) ventricular filling waves, typically lasts between 65 and 220 milliseconds depending on the coronary segment and individual physiology, and is best identified through precise temporal alignment with the electrocardiogram (ECG).

Current clinical practice relies on a two-step process for gating window determination. First, an ECG signal is used to detect the R-wave, which marks the onset of ventricular systole. Second, a separate cine-CMR acquisition is performed prior to the actual MRCA scan, typically in a four-chamber long-axis orientation, to retrospectively determine the timing of diastasis relative to the R-peak. This cine acquisition is acquired using a balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) sequence with a fixed repetition time (TR), usually between 3 and 5 milliseconds, and a limited number of k-space lines acquired per heartbeat, known as lines per segment (LPS). The temporal resolution of such a cine acquisition is determined by the product of TR and LPS, and in typical clinical protocols, this resolution is insufficient to resolve the rapid motion of the coronary arteries during the transition into and out of diastasis. For instance, a cine acquisition with a TR of 4 ms and LPS of 16 yields a true temporal resolution of 64 ms, which is inadequate to capture the sub-millisecond motion dynamics of the right coronary artery, which may move at velocities exceeding 10 cm/s during early diastole. Consequently, the cine-CMR method often misidentifies the onset and termination of diastasis, leading to the inclusion of motion-corrupted data within the gated acquisition window and the exclusion of quiescent intervals, thereby degrading image sharpness and introducing systematic blurring.

Moreover, cine-CMR gating is inherently vulnerable to heart rate variability (HRV), which commonly occurs during breath-hold acquisitions due to physiological stress, respiratory effort, or autonomic fluctuations. Because cine-CMR requires the acquisition of multiple cardiac cycles to reconstruct a full cardiac phase series, any variation in heart rate during the scan causes temporal misregistration of data bins, resulting in motion artifacts that are not merely localized but propagated across the entire reconstructed volume. This problem is exacerbated by the fact that the diastasis window itself is not static—it shifts in time with each heartbeat as heart rate changes, yet conventional cine-CMR assumes a single, averaged gating window for the entire breath hold. As a result, even modest HRV of 5 to 10 beats per minute can lead to significant degradation in image quality, particularly in small distal branches where motion sensitivity is greatest.

Attempts to improve gating accuracy have included the use of tissue Doppler echocardiography (TDE) to measure septal motion, which has demonstrated high correlation with coronary motion patterns. However, TDE is not compatible with MRI environments due to the presence of strong magnetic fields and radiofrequency interference, rendering it unsuitable as a direct replacement for cine-CMR in MRCA workflows. Other approaches, such as respiratory navigators for diaphragmatic motion tracking, have been successfully integrated into cardiac MRI protocols but are not designed to resolve cardiac motion with the required temporal fidelity. Similarly, real-time motion tracking using 2D or 3D navigator echoes has been explored, but these methods suffer from low signal-to-noise ratio, long acquisition latencies, or interference with the main imaging sequence. Furthermore, many of these techniques require continuous interleaving with the imaging sequence, which prolongs scan time and introduces magnetization state perturbations that destabilize the SSFP signal, a critical component for high-quality non-contrast MRCA.

There remains, therefore, a critical unmet need in cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging for a gating calibration technique that provides high-temporal-resolution, motion-specific, and heart-rate-adaptive determination of the diastasis window without requiring additional hardware, contrast agents, or complex post-processing. Such a technique must be compatible with standard clinical MRI systems, require minimal operator intervention, be robust to physiological variability, and deliver measurable improvements in coronary vessel sharpness. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing the Septal Scout—a novel, one-dimensional projection-based motion monitoring sequence that acquires high-frame-rate temporal data of the basal interventricular septum with a temporal resolution of 10 milliseconds or less, enabling the precise identification of diastasis on a per-heartbeat basis. Unlike cine-CMR, which averages motion over multiple cardiac cycles, the Septal Scout captures the dynamic evolution of septal motion throughout the breath hold, allowing for the construction of a multi-beat gating window that reflects actual physiological variation. This innovation not only improves image quality but also enhances the reproducibility and reliability of MRCA in clinical practice.

## SUMMARY

The present invention is directed to a method and system for improving the quality of magnetic resonance coronary angiography by determining a cardiac gating window with superior temporal precision and physiological adaptability compared to conventional cine cardiovascular magnetic resonance techniques. The invention comprises a novel imaging sequence, termed the Septal Scout, which is implemented as a pre-scan acquisition immediately preceding the main coronary angiography sequence. The Septal Scout acquires a one-dimensional projection of the basal interventricular septum along the long-axis direction of the heart using a modified steady-state free precession sequence with phase encoding disabled, thereby achieving a temporal resolution of approximately 10 milliseconds or less per frame. This high temporal sampling rate allows for the detection of subtle, rapid displacements of the septal tissue that correlate directly with the onset and termination of ventricular diastasis, the period of minimal coronary motion critical for high-resolution imaging.

The method involves prescribing a thin, planar slice oriented perpendicular to the long-axis of the heart and aligned with the interventricular septum, such that the resulting projection captures the motion of the septal wall as it moves toward and away from the imaging plane during the cardiac cycle. The acquired one-dimensional signal is then processed to generate a time-intensity plot representing the motion of the septum over multiple cardiac cycles. A gradient-based optical flow algorithm is applied to this intensity plot to compute a pseudo-displacement function, from which a pseudo-velocity function is derived via temporal differentiation. The inflection points of this velocity function, corresponding to the transition from the E-wave to the diastasis period and from diastasis to the A-wave, are automatically identified as the start and end times of the optimal data acquisition window. This window is determined independently for each heartbeat, and the intersection of these windows across all measured heartbeats defines a robust, heart-rate-adaptive gating window that accounts for physiological variation during the breath hold.

The invention further includes a system for implementing the Septal Scout method, comprising a magnetic resonance imaging scanner equipped with a multi-channel cardiac phased-array coil, a pulse sequence controller configured to execute the modified SSFP sequence with phase encoding turned off, and a processing unit running a software module that performs the optical flow analysis and inflection point detection. The system is designed to operate without the need for additional hardware, contrast agents, or external sensors, and is fully compatible with existing clinical MRI platforms. The gating window derived from the Septal Scout is then applied prospectively to trigger the acquisition of a high-resolution, three-dimensional, fat-suppressed SSFP coronary angiography sequence, resulting in images with significantly improved vessel edge definition and reduced motion blurring compared to those acquired using gating windows determined by conventional cine-CMR.

The invention has been validated in a cohort of healthy human volunteers, demonstrating that the Septal Scout-derived gating windows produce coronary angiograms with statistically significant improvements in both qualitative and quantitative measures of image quality. Subjective assessments by blinded radiologists revealed higher sharpness scores for both large and small coronary vessel segments when imaged using the Septal Scout method. Quantitative analysis using full width at half maximum (FWHM) measurements confirmed a significant reduction in vessel blur, with median FWHM values decreasing by approximately 12% for large vessels and 13% for small vessels compared to cine-CMR gating. Signal-to-noise ratio remained unchanged, confirming that the improvement was due to enhanced motion suppression rather than increased signal intensity. Furthermore, the Septal Scout method was shown to be highly correlated with tissue Doppler echocardiography, a known gold standard for septal motion assessment, with correlation coefficients exceeding 0.98 for both start and end times of the diastasis window.

The invention offers several advantages over existing methods. First, it eliminates the dependence on low-temporal-resolution cine-CMR for gating calibration, thereby avoiding the temporal blurring and data binning artifacts that plague conventional approaches. Second, it provides heartbeat-resolved gating windows that adapt to heart rate variability during breath holds, a capability not achievable with single-window cine-CMR methods. Third, it requires no additional hardware, contrast agents, or specialized training, making it readily implementable in any clinical MRI environment. Fourth, it is compatible with standard SSFP-based coronary angiography protocols and does not interfere with the main imaging sequence when implemented as a pre-scan. Finally, the method is fully automated, requiring no manual annotation or user intervention beyond initial plane prescription, thereby enhancing reproducibility and reducing inter-operator variability. The invention thus represents a significant advancement in the field of non-invasive coronary imaging, enabling more accurate, reliable, and clinically actionable MRCA studies without compromising patient safety or workflow efficiency.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a novel method and system for determining a cardiac gating window for magnetic resonance coronary angiography by employing a high-temporal-resolution, one-dimensional motion monitoring sequence known as the Septal Scout. The method is designed to overcome the fundamental limitations of conventional cine cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) techniques, which rely on low-frame-rate, multi-segment acquisitions to estimate the timing of diastasis—the period of minimal coronary motion critical for high-resolution imaging. The Septal Scout achieves superior temporal resolution by eliminating one dimension of spatial encoding, thereby enabling the acquisition of a continuous, high-frequency time-series signal of the basal interventricular septum’s longitudinal motion. This signal is then processed using a gradient optical flow algorithm to derive a pseudo-velocity profile that precisely identifies the boundaries of diastasis, allowing for the construction of a subject-specific, heart-rate-adaptive gating window that significantly improves the sharpness and diagnostic quality of coronary angiograms.

The method begins with the prescription of a dedicated imaging plane for the Septal Scout acquisition. This plane is oriented perpendicular to the long-axis of the heart and is positioned to intersect the basal portion of the interventricular septum, ensuring that the motion of the septal wall is captured as it moves toward and away from the imaging plane during the cardiac cycle. The plane is prescribed using a standard four-chamber long-axis cine view as a reference, which is acquired prior to the Septal Scout to ensure anatomical alignment. The thickness of the prescribed plane is maintained between 5 and 10 millimeters to ensure sufficient signal averaging while minimizing partial volume effects from adjacent structures such as the left ventricular free wall or the right ventricular outflow tract. The plane is positioned such that it remains aligned with the septum throughout the cardiac cycle, avoiding vertical misplacement that could result in signal loss due to septal motion exiting the plane. Horizontal misalignment, such as deviation from the true long-axis plane, is tolerated to a degree because the dominant motion component of the basal septum remains longitudinal, and the method is robust to minor angular deviations as long as the septal wall remains within the excitation profile.

Once the plane is prescribed, the Septal Scout sequence is initiated. This sequence is a modified version of the balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) pulse sequence, with the key modification being the deactivation of all phase-encoding gradients in the direction perpendicular to the long-axis of the heart. As a result, the acquisition becomes one-dimensional, capturing only the signal intensity along the depth axis of the prescribed plane, effectively generating a motion profile akin to M-mode ultrasound. The repetition time (TR) of the sequence is set to 10 milliseconds or less, which corresponds to a frame rate of at least 100 frames per second, a temporal resolution sufficient to resolve the rapid motion of the septum during the transition into and out of diastasis. In preferred embodiments, the TR is maintained at 10 milliseconds to balance temporal resolution with signal-to-noise ratio and to allow for efficient data processing without introducing susceptibility artifacts. The flip angle is set to 55 degrees to optimize the steady-state magnetization for high signal intensity, and the field of view is adjusted to approximately 31 centimeters to encompass the full range of septal motion without aliasing. The spatial resolution along the scout axis is set to 0.8 millimeters to ensure fine sampling of the septal boundary, and the acquisition is triggered by the R-wave of the electrocardiogram to ensure phase consistency across heartbeats.

The acquisition duration is set to five seconds, which is sufficient to capture at least four to five cardiac cycles under normal heart rate conditions, and is performed at the beginning and end of a breath hold to account for any physiological changes that may occur during the duration of the subsequent coronary angiography scan. During the breath hold, subjects typically experience a gradual increase in heart rate of 5 to 10 beats per minute due to respiratory strain or autonomic response. The Septal Scout acquisition at both the beginning and end of the breath hold captures the evolution of the diastasis window across this range of heart rates, enabling the identification of a common temporal interval during which diastasis occurs consistently across all measured heartbeats. This multi-beat intersection approach is a critical innovation of the invention, as it provides a gating window that is not an average of multiple phases, but rather a consensus period of quiescence that adapts to physiological variation. This is in stark contrast to conventional cine-CMR, which acquires data continuously over the breath hold and bins the data into a fixed number of cardiac phases, leading to temporal misregistration and motion blurring when heart rate changes.

The raw data from the Septal Scout acquisition consists of a series of one-dimensional intensity profiles, each corresponding to a single TR interval, appended along a time axis to form a two-dimensional matrix with time on one axis and spatial depth on the other. This matrix is then processed using a custom software module that performs region-of-interest (ROI) selection and signal averaging. An ROI is defined along the spatial axis to encompass a depth range of ±2.5 millimeters centered on the location of the basal septum, as identified visually from the initial projection. This ROI height is chosen to be sufficiently narrow to ensure that the tissue within the ROI behaves as a rigid structure during motion, while being wide enough to permit spatial averaging that enhances the signal-to-noise ratio without introducing motion blur. The intensities within the ROI are averaged along the spatial dimension to produce a single one-dimensional intensity-time trace that represents the motion of the septal wall over time.

This intensity-time trace is then subjected to a gradient optical flow algorithm, which computes the apparent motion of intensity patterns between consecutive frames. Unlike traditional correlation-based tracking methods, which rely on matching discrete intensity patterns and are prone to noise-induced errors due to the limited number of data points in a one-dimensional profile, the gradient optical flow method leverages the continuous spatial variation of intensity gradients to estimate motion with greater robustness. The algorithm treats the intensity-time trace as a pseudo-displacement function, where changes in intensity over time are interpreted as motion of tissue across the fixed spatial sampling points of the scout plane. The temporal derivative of this pseudo-displacement function yields a pseudo-velocity function, which exhibits characteristic peaks corresponding to the E-wave and A-wave of ventricular filling, separated by a period of near-zero velocity that corresponds to diastasis. The inflection points of this velocity function—defined as the points where the second derivative crosses zero—are automatically identified as the start and end times of the diastasis window. This method of inflection point detection is preferred over threshold-based methods because it is insensitive to absolute intensity levels and is robust to minor variations in signal amplitude or noise, which are common in one-dimensional projections.

The resulting gating window, denoted as Wsep, is defined as the intersection of the diastasis windows identified in each heartbeat across the entire acquisition. This intersection is computed by determining the temporal overlap between the start and end times of diastasis for each individual heartbeat, and the final gating window is set to the maximum temporal interval during which diastasis was consistently observed across all heartbeats. This approach ensures that the gating window excludes any heartbeats in which motion was abnormal or inconsistent, such as those occurring during arrhythmias or premature contractions, and retains only those intervals where the septum was truly quiescent. This is a significant advantage over conventional cine-CMR, which typically uses a single, averaged window derived from all heartbeats regardless of variability, thereby including motion-corrupted data in the final reconstruction.

Once the gating window Wsep is determined, it is applied prospectively to trigger the acquisition of the high-resolution, three-dimensional coronary angiography sequence. This sequence is a fat-suppressed, steady-state free precession (SSFP) acquisition with isotropic or near-isotropic spatial resolution, typically 1.5 × 1.5 × 2.0 millimeters, and is prescribed to cover the entire coronary arterial tree from the aortic ostia to the distal branches of the left anterior descending, left circumflex, and right coronary arteries. The acquisition is triggered by the R-wave of the ECG, and data is collected only during the time interval defined by Wsep, ensuring that only motion-free data is included in the final image reconstruction. The number of k-space lines acquired per heartbeat is adjusted dynamically based on the duration of Wsep, allowing for optimal sampling efficiency without compromising resolution. The sequence employs a Cartesian trajectory with an α/2 pre-pulse and a sufficient number of dummy cycles to establish steady-state magnetization, and is typically completed within a breath hold of 15 to 20 seconds.

In an alternative embodiment, the Septal Scout may be implemented as a continuous, interleaved navigator during the coronary angiography acquisition itself, rather than as a pre-scan. In this configuration, the Septal Scout sequence is executed at regular intervals between the acquisition of imaging k-space lines, providing real-time feedback on septal motion and allowing for dynamic adjustment of the gating window on a heartbeat-by-heartbeat basis. This approach, termed the Septal Navigator, would enable adaptive gating in the presence of unpredictable heart rate changes or arrhythmias, further enhancing image quality. However, implementation of this mode requires careful consideration of the potential for signal saturation in the coronary arteries due to the excitation of the septal plane, which may lie in close proximity to the target vessels. To mitigate this, a two-dimensional radiofrequency (RF) excitation pulse may be employed to selectively excite only the septal tissue, leaving the coronary arteries unaffected. Such a 2D spiral RF pulse, for example, could be designed to excite a cylindrical volume along the septal wall on the long-axis plane, preserving the high temporal resolution while minimizing interference with the main imaging sequence. This approach would require longer RF pulse durations and careful calibration of the excitation profile, but it represents a viable pathway for future integration into real-time, continuous gating systems.

The invention further includes a system for performing the method, comprising a magnetic resonance imaging scanner equipped with a multi-channel cardiac phased-array coil, a pulse sequence controller capable of executing the modified SSFP sequence with phase encoding disabled, and a processing unit running a software module that implements the optical flow algorithm and inflection point detection. The software module is configured to receive the raw one-dimensional intensity-time data from the Septal Scout acquisition, perform ROI selection and spatial averaging, compute the pseudo-displacement and pseudo-velocity functions, identify inflection points, and output the final gating window in a format compatible with the scanner’s prospective gating system. The system is designed to operate entirely within the clinical MRI environment, requiring no additional hardware, contrast agents, or external sensors, and is fully compatible with standard clinical MRI platforms from major manufacturers such as GE Healthcare, Siemens Healthineers, and Philips Healthcare.

The method has been validated in a cohort of nine healthy male volunteers, aged 30 ± 4 years, who underwent two separate coronary angiography scans: one gated using the conventional cine-CMR method and one gated using the Septal Scout method. The cine-CMR gating window was determined by acquiring a four-chamber long-axis cine with a TR of 3.9 milliseconds, LPS of 16, and 30 cardiac phases, followed by frame-to-frame correlation analysis and inflection point detection as described in prior literature. The Septal Scout method was performed as described above, with a TR of 10 milliseconds, a 10-millimeter-thick scout plane, and a five-second acquisition duration at the beginning and end of the breath hold. The resulting gating windows were applied to identical 3D SSFP coronary angiography sequences, with all other acquisition parameters held constant.

Quantitative analysis of the resulting images revealed a statistically significant improvement in vessel sharpness when using the Septal Scout gating window. The full width at half maximum (FWHM) of vessel boundaries was measured at three cross-sectional locations along each major coronary artery segment, and the mean FWHM was found to be 3.6 millimeters for large vessels and 2.1 millimeters for small vessels in the Septal Scout group, compared to 4.1 millimeters and 2.4 millimeters, respectively, in the cine-CMR group. These differences were statistically significant (p = 0.03 for both groups), indicating a consistent and reproducible improvement in image sharpness. Subjective assessment by two blinded, experienced radiologists using a five-point Likert scale further confirmed this improvement, with median sharpness scores increasing from 1.9 to 2.8 for large vessels and from 1.7 to 2.4 for small vessels in the Septal Scout group (p < 0.01 for both observers). There was no significant difference in signal-to-noise ratio between the two methods, confirming that the improvement was due to enhanced motion suppression rather than increased signal intensity.

In a sub-cohort of six subjects, the Septal Scout gating windows were compared to those derived from tissue Doppler echocardiography (TDE) of the interventricular septum, a technique known to provide high-fidelity motion data. The correlation coefficients between the start and end times of the diastasis windows determined by the Septal Scout and TDE were both greater than 0.98 (p < 0.001), demonstrating that the Septal Scout provides motion data that is not only superior to cine-CMR but also highly concordant with an established gold standard. Bland-Altman analysis revealed no systematic bias between the two methods, with limits of agreement within ±15 milliseconds, which is clinically acceptable given the temporal resolution of the acquisition.

The invention offers several advantages over existing methods. First, it provides a temporal resolution of 10 milliseconds or less, which is substantially higher than the 64 milliseconds typically achieved with conventional cine-CMR, enabling the precise identification of diastasis even in the presence of rapid septal motion. Second, it captures motion on a per-heartbeat basis, allowing for the construction of a gating window that adapts to heart rate variability, a capability absent in conventional methods that rely on averaged, fixed windows. Third, it requires no additional hardware, contrast agents, or specialized training, making it easily implementable in any clinical MRI environment. Fourth, it is fully automated, requiring no manual annotation or user intervention beyond initial plane prescription, thereby enhancing reproducibility and reducing inter-operator variability. Fifth, it is compatible with standard SSFP-based coronary angiography protocols and does not interfere with the main imaging sequence when implemented as a pre-scan. Finally, the method is robust to minor anatomical misplacement and physiological noise, and its reliance on optical flow rather than correlation-based tracking ensures superior performance in low-signal environments.

The invention also has potential applications beyond coronary angiography. The Septal Scout technique could be adapted for use in other cardiac imaging modalities that require precise motion gating, such as myocardial perfusion imaging, strain analysis, or valve motion assessment. It could also be integrated into real-time cardiac monitoring systems for patients with arrhythmias or those undergoing stress testing, where heart rate variability is pronounced. Furthermore, the method’s ability to detect subtle septal motion patterns may prove useful in the diagnosis of conditions such as constrictive pericarditis or septal bounce, where abnormal septal motion is a key diagnostic feature. Future work may explore the use of machine learning algorithms to automate the identification of septal motion patterns associated with specific pathologies, further expanding the clinical utility of the technique.

In summary, the Septal Scout represents a fundamental advancement in the field of cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging. By replacing the low-temporal-resolution cine-CMR gating method with a high-resolution, one-dimensional motion monitoring sequence that captures the true dynamics of septal motion, the invention enables the acquisition of coronary angiograms with unprecedented sharpness and diagnostic fidelity. The method is simple, robust, and fully compatible with existing clinical workflows, offering a practical and effective solution to one of the most persistent challenges in non-invasive coronary imaging. The invention thus provides a significant improvement in the quality, reliability, and clinical utility of magnetic resonance coronary angiography, paving the way for broader adoption of this non-invasive, radiation-free modality in the diagnosis and management of coronary artery disease.
