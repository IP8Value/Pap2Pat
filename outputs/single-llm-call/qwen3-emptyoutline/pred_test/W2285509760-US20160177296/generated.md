# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The global poultry industry faces persistent and escalating threats from avian influenza virus (AIV) outbreaks, which have resulted in the mass culling of millions of birds and economic losses exceeding billions of dollars annually. These outbreaks are not only devastating to agricultural economies but also pose significant zoonotic risks due to the potential for cross-species transmission to mammals, including humans. The most commonly employed preventive strategy has been vaccination; however, conventional vaccines for avian influenza suffer from multiple critical limitations that severely restrict their utility in large-scale, rapid-response scenarios. Effective vaccination typically requires subcutaneous or intramuscular administration, which is labor-intensive, time-consuming, and impractical for deployment across vast commercial flocks. Furthermore, the induction of protective humoral immunity following vaccination requires a latency period of two to three weeks, during which birds remain vulnerable to infection. This delay renders vaccination ineffective in the context of an emerging outbreak, where immediate intervention is essential to contain viral spread. Additional challenges include the necessity for strict cold-chain management, as improper storage or handling can lead to complete vaccine failure. Even when properly administered, conventional vaccines are subtype-specific, meaning that a vaccine developed for one hemagglutinin (HA) subtype—such as H5N1—offers no cross-protection against other subtypes like H7N9 or H6N2. As a result, vaccine stockpiles rapidly become obsolete as new strains emerge, necessitating continuous and costly re-development efforts. The requirement for strain-specific matching also complicates regulatory and trade protocols, as vaccinated birds may seroconvert and test positive for AIV antibodies, triggering trade restrictions even in the absence of active infection. These systemic shortcomings underscore an urgent and unmet need for a prophylactic intervention that is broadly protective across all AIV subtypes, acts rapidly upon administration, does not induce seroconversion, and can be delivered en masse in a scalable, cost-effective manner.

RNA interference (RNAi) has emerged as a promising alternative to traditional antiviral strategies, offering the potential for highly specific gene silencing through the targeted degradation of viral mRNA transcripts. Previous studies have demonstrated that small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) directed against conserved regions of the influenza virus nucleoprotein (NP) and polymerase acidic protein (PA) genes can significantly suppress viral replication in both mammalian and avian cell models. These siRNAs function by guiding the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) to complementary viral mRNA sequences, leading to their cleavage and subsequent inhibition of viral protein synthesis. Despite their demonstrated efficacy in laboratory settings, the clinical translation of siRNA-based antivirals has been hindered by the persistent challenge of intracellular delivery. Naked siRNAs are rapidly degraded by nucleases in biological fluids, poorly internalized by target cells, and unable to escape endosomal compartments once internalized. To overcome these barriers, researchers have explored two primary delivery platforms: synthetic carriers and genetically engineered viral vectors. Synthetic carriers, such as lipid nanoparticles or cationic polymers, have been used to encapsulate siRNAs and facilitate cellular uptake; however, these systems often require high dosages to achieve therapeutic effect, resulting in increased production costs and potential cytotoxicity. Moreover, their delivery efficiency remains suboptimal, particularly in mucosal tissues where the mucus barrier and epithelial tight junctions impede penetration. Viral vectors, including adenoviruses and lentiviruses, have been engineered to express short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) that are processed intracellularly into functional siRNAs. While these systems offer sustained gene silencing, they carry substantial safety risks, including insertional mutagenesis, hepatotoxicity, and immune overactivation. Reports have documented severe adverse events in animal models, including hepatocyte apoptosis, systemic inflammation, and tumorigenesis, particularly when shRNA expression is driven by strong constitutive promoters. These safety concerns have severely limited the clinical applicability of viral delivery systems, especially in food-producing animals where long-term health and product safety are paramount. Furthermore, the use of viral vectors raises regulatory and public acceptance hurdles due to the perception of genetic modification and potential for unintended environmental consequences. There exists, therefore, a critical gap in the field for a delivery platform that combines the specificity and potency of RNAi with the safety, scalability, and mucosal targeting capabilities required for practical, large-scale application in poultry.

The present invention addresses this gap through the novel application of transkingdom RNA interference (tkRNAi), a non-viral, non-integrating, bacterial delivery system engineered to deliver shRNAs directly into the cytoplasm of mucosal epithelial cells. This platform utilizes a genetically modified, nonpathogenic strain of Escherichia coli that has been rendered auxotrophic for diaminopimelic acid (Dap) and engineered to constitutively express two key proteins: invasin (Inv) and listeriolysin O (hlyA). Invasin, a surface-expressed protein derived from Yersinia pseudotuberculosis, binds with high affinity to β(1) integrin receptors abundantly present on the apical surfaces of mucosal epithelial cells, triggering receptor-mediated endocytosis. Once internalized, the bacterial vector produces listeriolysin O, a pore-forming toxin that disrupts the endosomal membrane, enabling the release of shRNA transcripts into the host cell cytoplasm. These shRNAs are then processed by the endogenous RNAi machinery into functional siRNAs that silence target viral genes. Crucially, the tkRNAi vector is non-conjugative, non-colonizing, and incapable of horizontal gene transfer, eliminating the risk of genomic integration or persistent colonization. The Dap auxotrophy ensures that the bacteria cannot replicate outside of supplemented environments, providing an additional layer of biocontainment. The system has been previously validated in mammalian models for the treatment of colorectal cancer and familial adenomatous polyposis, demonstrating safety, tolerability, and efficacy in human clinical trials. However, its application to avian mucosal epithelium and its potential for inhibiting avian influenza virus replication have not been previously explored. The invention described herein represents the first demonstration that tkRNAi can be effectively adapted to avian cells, that avian epithelial cells express sufficient levels of β(1) integrin to support vector uptake, and that shRNAs targeting conserved regions of the AIV NP and PA genes can be delivered via this platform to achieve profound inhibition of infectious viral shedding. This approach offers a paradigm shift in avian influenza control: a prophylactic that is universal in scope, rapid in onset, non-seroconverting, non-toxic, scalable through fermentation, and deliverable via aerosol or oral routes suitable for mass administration. It circumvents the limitations of vaccination, avoids the safety pitfalls of viral vectors, and surpasses the delivery inefficiencies of synthetic carriers, providing a novel, robust, and commercially viable solution to one of the most pressing challenges in global poultry health.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel prophylactic composition and method for inhibiting avian influenza virus (AIV) replication in avian species through the targeted delivery of RNA interference (RNAi) effectors using a genetically engineered, nonpathogenic bacterial vector. The composition comprises a non-conjugative, non-colonizing, diaminopimelic acid (Dap) auxotrophic strain of Escherichia coli that has been genetically modified to constitutively express at least one short hairpin RNA (shRNA) designed to target a conserved sequence within the nucleoprotein (NP) or polymerase acidic protein (PA) gene of avian influenza virus. The bacterial vector further expresses two heterologous proteins necessary for efficient intracellular delivery of the shRNA: invasin, which mediates binding to β(1) integrin receptors on avian mucosal epithelial cells, and listeriolysin O, which facilitates endosomal escape and cytoplasmic release of the shRNA. The shRNA is transcribed from a plasmid vector that is stably maintained within the bacterial host and is processed intracellularly by the host cell’s RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) into small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) that direct the degradation of complementary viral mRNA transcripts. The invention encompasses both individual shRNA constructs targeting either the NP gene or the PA gene, as well as a combination thereof, wherein the simultaneous delivery of shRNAs against both viral genes results in synergistic inhibition of viral replication and a substantial reduction in infectious viral shedding. The bacterial vector is formulated as a live, viable inoculum suitable for administration via intranasal, oral, or aerosol routes, enabling rapid, non-invasive, and scalable delivery to large poultry populations. The composition is non-toxic to avian cells at therapeutically effective concentrations, does not integrate into the host genome, does not induce seroconversion against AIV, and does not interfere with diagnostic testing for active infection. The invention further provides a method for inhibiting AIV replication in avian epithelial cells by contacting the cells with the bacterial vector at a concentration sufficient to mediate internalization and shRNA delivery, resulting in the suppression of viral gene expression and a reduction in infectious viral titers by at least one log10 unit compared to untreated controls. The invention further provides a method for prophylactically protecting poultry from AIV infection by administering the bacterial vector prior to exposure to infectious virus, thereby reducing or preventing viral shedding and transmission. The invention further provides a method for manufacturing the bacterial vector at industrial scale through fermentation under controlled conditions, followed by formulation into a stable, shelf-stable preparation suitable for long-term storage and mass deployment. The invention further provides a method for adapting the shRNA sequence to new AIV strains by modifying the target region within the plasmid construct while retaining the same delivery platform, enabling rapid response to emerging variants without requiring re-engineering of the bacterial vector or its delivery mechanism. The invention further provides a method for detecting the presence of the bacterial vector in avian tissues or excretions through culture-based or molecular methods, allowing for monitoring of vector persistence and clearance. The invention further provides a method for ensuring biocontainment through the use of Dap auxotrophy, wherein the bacterial vector is incapable of replication in the absence of exogenous diaminopimelic acid, thereby preventing environmental proliferation. The invention further provides a method for co-administering the bacterial vector with other prophylactic or therapeutic agents, including but not limited to immune modulators, antimicrobials, or nutritional supplements, to enhance overall protection against AIV and other avian pathogens. The invention further provides a method for evaluating the efficacy of the bacterial vector in reducing AIV transmission within a flock by measuring reductions in viral load in respiratory secretions, mortality rates, and clinical signs following experimental or natural exposure. The invention further provides a method for integrating the bacterial vector into existing poultry management systems, including hatchery administration, feed or water delivery, or environmental nebulization, to enable seamless adoption by commercial producers. The invention further provides a method for reducing trade barriers associated with AIV outbreaks by enabling the maintenance of AIV-negative serological status in vaccinated flocks, thereby facilitating international movement of poultry and poultry products without quarantine or testing restrictions. The invention further provides a method for reducing the economic burden of AIV outbreaks by minimizing culling, improving flock survival, and maintaining market access through the use of a non-seroconverting, broadly protective, and rapidly deployable prophylactic agent.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

### Design of siRNAs and Use of Viral Specific siRNAs to Inhibit Avian Influenza in an Avian Tissue Model

The design of the small interfering RNA (siRNA) sequences utilized in this invention was guided by the imperative to target regions of the avian influenza virus (AIV) genome that are both highly conserved across diverse subtypes and functionally critical to viral replication. The nucleoprotein (NP) and polymerase acidic protein (PA) genes were selected as targets due to their essential roles in viral RNA synthesis, nuclear import of the viral ribonucleoprotein complex, and overall replication fidelity. These genes exhibit a high degree of sequence conservation across avian, swine, equine, canine, and human influenza isolates, making them ideal candidates for the development of a universal antiviral strategy. The siRNA sequences were derived from previously published constructs that demonstrated potent silencing activity against influenza virus in mammalian systems, and their complementarity to the NP and PA genes of the H6N2 and H8N4 AIV subtypes was rigorously confirmed using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) algorithm. The sense strand of the NP-targeting siRNA was designed as GGA TCT TAT TTC TTC GGA G, corresponding to nucleotides 1453–1471 in the H6N2 NP gene and 1486–1504 in the H8N4 NP gene, with 100% sequence identity across both subtypes. The sense strand of the PA-targeting siRNA was designed as GCA ATT GAG GAG TGC CTG A, corresponding to nucleotides 2065–2083 in the H6N2 PA gene and 2077–2095 in the H8N4 PA gene, again with complete sequence identity. These sequences were selected not only for their perfect match to the target viral genes but also for their absence of significant homology to any known avian gene sequence, as determined by exhaustive BLAST searches against the chicken genome database. Particular attention was paid to the seed region of the antisense strand, spanning nucleotides 2–8, as this region is critical for target recognition and off-target silencing. No perfect complementarity was observed between the seed region of either siRNA and any annotated chicken mRNA, thereby minimizing the risk of unintended gene silencing. The siRNA sequences were incorporated into a short hairpin RNA (shRNA) expression cassette, which was cloned into a plasmid vector under the control of a constitutive promoter to ensure continuous transcription within the bacterial host. The shRNA structure included a 19-nucleotide sense strand, a 9-nucleotide loop sequence (5′-TTC AAG AGA-3′), and the complementary antisense strand, followed by a poly-T terminator. This design ensures efficient processing by the cellular Dicer enzyme into a functional siRNA duplex upon release into the cytoplasm of the target avian cell. The plasmid backbone was engineered to maintain stability in the bacterial host without requiring antibiotic selection during production, and the entire construct was verified by DNA sequencing to ensure fidelity of the shRNA insert. The use of these specific siRNA sequences enables the inhibition of a broad spectrum of AIV strains, including highly pathogenic isolates such as H5N1, H7N9, and H9N2, which share identical or near-identical sequences in the targeted regions. This universality is a critical advantage over conventional vaccines, which must be reformulated for each emerging strain. The ability of these siRNAs to silence viral gene expression was previously demonstrated using synthetic transfection methods, but their efficacy when delivered via a bacterial vector in avian cells had not been established. The present invention demonstrates that these same siRNA sequences, when expressed as shRNAs within the tkRNAi vector, retain their potency in avian epithelial cells and are capable of achieving profound suppression of infectious viral shedding. The selection of NP and PA as targets is further supported by their high conservation across decades of viral evolution and their low tolerance for mutation, as alterations in these regions often impair viral fitness. This reduces the likelihood of viral escape mutants emerging under selective pressure from RNAi, a phenomenon that has been observed with siRNAs targeting more variable regions such as the hemagglutinin gene. The invention thus provides a genetically defined, sequence-specific, and evolutionarily robust antiviral strategy that is not dependent on host immune recognition but rather on direct molecular interference with essential viral functions.

### Inhibiting Avian Influenza Replication in a Chicken Cell Model Using a Unique RNAi Delivery Technology

The inhibition of avian influenza virus replication in a chicken epithelial cell model was achieved through the innovative application of a transkingdom RNA interference (tkRNAi) delivery system, which overcomes the fundamental limitations of conventional RNAi delivery methods. The tkRNAi vector employed in this invention is a genetically modified, nonpathogenic strain of Escherichia coli that has been engineered to express two key proteins—invase and listeriolysin O—that enable the vector to breach the barriers of the avian mucosal epithelium and deliver its therapeutic payload directly into the cytoplasm. Invasin, a surface-expressed adhesin derived from Yersinia pseudotuberculosis, binds with high affinity to β(1) integrin receptors that are constitutively expressed on the apical membranes of avian epithelial cells, including the chicken hepatocellular carcinoma line LMH used in this study. This interaction triggers receptor-mediated endocytosis, allowing the entire bacterial vector to be internalized into the host cell within a membrane-bound vesicle. Once internalized, the bacterial vector produces listeriolysin O, a pore-forming toxin that disrupts the endosomal membrane, thereby facilitating the release of the plasmid-encoded shRNA transcripts into the host cytoplasm. This mechanism of delivery is fundamentally distinct from synthetic carriers, which rely on passive diffusion or endocytic uptake without efficient endosomal escape, and from viral vectors, which depend on viral entry pathways that may trigger immune recognition or genomic integration. The use of a bacterial vector eliminates the need for chemical conjugation, nanoparticle formulation, or physical transfection methods, all of which are incompatible with mass administration in commercial poultry settings. The tkRNAi vector was demonstrated to be non-cytotoxic to LMH cells at concentrations up to 7.8 × 10⁵ colony-forming units per milliliter, a threshold determined through serial dilution and assessment of cytopathic effect using crystal violet staining. At this concentration, the vector was efficiently internalized, as confirmed by fluorescent microscopy and flow cytometry using a recombinant vector co-expressing red fluorescent protein (RFP). Intracellular RFP signal was detected in 74.1% to 75.7% of LMH cells at 20 and 36 hours post-invasion, respectively, indicating a high rate of successful delivery. Importantly, the expression of β(1) integrin on LMH cells was confirmed under both uninfected and AIV-infected conditions, demonstrating that the receptor target remains accessible even during active viral infection, which is critical for therapeutic efficacy in a real-world outbreak scenario. The delivery of shRNAs targeting the NP and PA genes resulted in a significant reduction in infectious viral titers, as measured by the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID₅₀) assay in MDCK cells. When LMH cells were pre-treated with the tkRNAi vector expressing NP-targeting shRNA, infectious AIV shedding was reduced by 3.8 log₁₀ units against the H8N4 strain and 1.6 log₁₀ units against the H6N2 strain. Treatment with the PA-targeting shRNA vector resulted in reductions of 2.5 log₁₀ and 1.4 log₁₀ units, respectively. Most notably, the combination of both vectors—termed the anti-AIV cocktail—produced synergistic inhibition, achieving reductions of 4.0 log₁₀ and 1.9 log₁₀ units against H8N4 and H6N2, respectively. This level of suppression corresponds to a reduction in infectious virus of up to 10,000-fold, effectively reducing viral titers below the threshold of detection in many samples. The scramble control vector, which expresses a non-targeting shRNA, also exhibited a modest but statistically significant reduction in viral shedding, likely attributable to the innate immune-stimulating properties of bacterial components such as lipopolysaccharide, which may activate Toll-like receptor pathways and prime the host cell for antiviral defense. However, the magnitude of inhibition observed with the NP- and PA-specific vectors far exceeded that of the scramble control, confirming that the antiviral effect was sequence-specific and attributable to RNAi-mediated gene silencing. The absence of cytotoxicity, the high delivery efficiency, and the profound reduction in infectious virus collectively demonstrate that the tkRNAi platform is not only compatible with avian epithelial cells but is also highly effective in suppressing AIV replication. This represents the first successful application of tkRNAi in an avian model and establishes a foundation for its translation into practical poultry applications. The ability to achieve such potent inhibition without the use of synthetic carriers or viral vectors, and without inducing seroconversion or cellular damage, positions this technology as a superior alternative to existing approaches.

### Prophylactic Treatment of Chicken Populations with Anti-AIV Vectors

The prophylactic treatment of chicken populations with the anti-AIV vectors described herein represents a transformative approach to the prevention and control of avian influenza outbreaks, offering a solution that is rapid, scalable, universally protective, and compatible with commercial poultry production systems. Unlike conventional vaccines, which require individual administration and weeks to induce immunity, the anti-AIV vectors can be delivered en masse via aerosol, oral, or intranasal routes within minutes, providing immediate protection upon exposure. The bacterial vector is formulated as a stable, viable inoculum that can be stored at −80°C for extended periods without loss of potency and can be reconstituted in sterile saline or buffered solution for immediate application. The method of administration is tailored to the logistics of poultry farming: in hatcheries, the vector can be sprayed onto day-old chicks during processing; in grow-out facilities, it can be administered via nebulizers integrated into ventilation systems, allowing for uniform exposure across entire flocks; or it can be delivered through drinking water or feed, leveraging existing infrastructure. The non-pathogenic nature of the vector, its Dap auxotrophy, and its inability to colonize or replicate in the absence of supplemented diaminopimelic acid ensure that it poses no risk to animal health, human consumers, or the environment. The vector does not integrate into the host genome, does not produce infectious progeny, and is cleared from the gastrointestinal and respiratory tracts within days, minimizing the potential for long-term persistence. Critically, the prophylactic administration of the vector does not induce antibody production against avian influenza virus, meaning that treated birds remain seronegative in standard diagnostic tests such as hemagglutination inhibition or ELISA. This characteristic is of profound economic and regulatory significance, as it allows for the continued export of poultry and poultry products without triggering trade restrictions that are typically imposed on vaccinated flocks. The universal nature of the siRNA targets ensures that a single vector formulation is effective against all known AIV subtypes, eliminating the need for strain-specific reformulation, stockpiling of multiple vaccine types, or delays associated with antigen matching. In the event of a novel outbreak caused by a previously uncharacterized strain, the shRNA sequence can be rapidly redesigned and synthesized within days, while retaining the same bacterial delivery platform, enabling a response time that is orders of magnitude faster than traditional vaccine development. The manufacturing process is inherently scalable, as the bacterial vector can be produced in large-scale fermenters using inexpensive, non-specialized media, yielding billions of viable units per batch at a fraction of the cost of vaccine production. The formulation can be lyophilized or suspended in a stabilizing buffer to extend shelf life, and the final product can be packaged in single-use dispensers suitable for field use. The prophylactic effect is immediate and sustained for the duration of vector persistence in the mucosal tissues, which is sufficient to cover the critical window of vulnerability during the early stages of an outbreak. The invention further encompasses the co-administration of the anti-AIV vector with other biocontrol agents, such as probiotics, immunomodulators, or nutritional supplements, to enhance overall flock health and resilience. The method of prophylactic treatment does not require changes to existing biosecurity protocols, does not interfere with routine veterinary practices, and can be integrated into standard flock management routines. The reduction in infectious viral shedding observed in vitro translates directly to a reduction in transmission within a flock, as lower viral loads in respiratory secretions decrease the probability of horizontal spread. This not only protects the treated birds but also reduces environmental contamination, thereby lowering the risk of transmission to neighboring flocks and wild bird populations. The prophylactic application of this vector thus represents a comprehensive, multi-layered intervention that addresses the biological, logistical, economic, and regulatory dimensions of avian influenza control, offering a sustainable, scalable, and globally applicable solution to a problem that has eluded conventional approaches for decades.

## GLOSSARY OF CLAIM TERMS

In the context of this patent application, the following terms are used with the meanings ascribed herein to ensure clarity and consistency in the interpretation of the claims. The term “avian influenza virus” refers to any strain or subtype of influenza A virus that infects avian species, including but not limited to H5N1, H7N9, H6N2, H8N4, H9N2, and H7N1, and encompasses both low-pathogenic and highly pathogenic variants. The term “RNA interference” refers to a naturally occurring cellular mechanism by which double-stranded RNA molecules induce the sequence-specific degradation of complementary messenger RNA transcripts, thereby silencing gene expression. The term “short hairpin RNA” refers to a synthetic RNA molecule comprising a sense strand, a loop region, and an antisense strand that forms a stem-loop structure, which is processed by the cellular enzyme Dicer into a functional small interfering RNA. The term “transkingdom RNA interference” refers to a delivery system in which a genetically engineered nonpathogenic bacterium is used to produce and deliver short hairpin RNA molecules into eukaryotic host cells, enabling gene silencing across biological kingdoms. The term “invasin” refers to a surface-expressed protein derived from Yersinia pseudotuberculosis that binds to β(1) integrin receptors on eukaryotic cells, triggering receptor-mediated endocytosis and facilitating bacterial internalization. The term “listeriolysin O” refers to a pore-forming toxin derived from Listeria monocytogenes that disrupts endosomal membranes, enabling the release of intracellular cargo into the host cell cytoplasm. The term “β(1) integrin” refers to a transmembrane receptor protein expressed on the surface of mucosal epithelial cells that mediates cell adhesion and serves as the primary binding site for invasin. The term “diaminopimelic acid auxotroph” refers to a bacterial strain that is genetically incapable of synthesizing diaminopimelic acid, an essential component of the bacterial cell wall, and therefore requires exogenous supplementation for growth and replication. The term “non-conjugative” refers to a bacterial strain that lacks the genetic machinery necessary for the transfer of plasmid DNA to other bacterial cells via conjugation. The term “non-colonizing” refers to a bacterial strain that does not establish persistent or self-sustaining populations within the host organism after administration. The term “constitutive expression” refers to the continuous, unregulated transcription and translation of a gene product under the control of a promoter that is active under normal physiological conditions. The term “infectious viral shedding” refers to the release of viable, replication-competent viral particles from infected cells into the extracellular environment, measurable by assays such as the 50% tissue culture infectious dose. The term “TCID₅₀” refers to the 50% tissue culture infectious dose, a quantitative measure of infectious virus concentration defined as the dilution of a virus sample that causes cytopathic effect in 50% of inoculated cell cultures. The term “cytopathic effect” refers to visible structural changes in host cells resulting from viral infection, including cell rounding, detachment, lysis, or fusion, which are used as indicators of viral replication. The term “non-seroconverting” refers to a treatment that does not induce the production of detectable antibodies against the target pathogen in the host organism, thereby preserving the ability to distinguish vaccinated animals from naturally infected ones. The term “universal prophylactic” refers to a therapeutic agent that provides protection against multiple strains or subtypes of a pathogen without requiring strain-specific adaptation. The term “aerosol administration” refers to the delivery of a substance in the form of a fine mist or spray that is inhaled into the respiratory tract. The term “oral administration” refers to the delivery of a substance via ingestion through the mouth and gastrointestinal tract. The term “intranasal administration” refers to the delivery of a substance directly into the nasal passages. The term “fermentation” refers to the industrial-scale cultivation of microorganisms under controlled conditions to produce a biological product. The term “shelf-stable” refers to a formulation that retains its biological activity and viability under defined storage conditions for an extended period without refrigeration. The term “biocontainment” refers to the use of biological or genetic mechanisms to prevent the unintended spread or persistence of a genetically modified organism in the environment. The term “synergistic inhibition” refers to a combined effect of two or more agents that is greater than the sum of their individual effects. The term “sequence-specific silencing” refers to the targeted degradation of a messenger RNA molecule based on perfect or near-perfect complementarity to a small RNA guide. The term “nonpathogenic” refers to a microorganism that does not cause disease in its host under normal conditions. The term “genetically engineered” refers to an organism whose genome has been deliberately altered through molecular biological techniques to introduce or modify specific traits. The term “plasmid vector” refers to a circular, extrachromosomal DNA molecule capable of autonomous replication within a host cell and used to carry and express foreign genes. The term “small interfering RNA” refers to a short double-stranded RNA molecule, typically 19–25 nucleotides in length, that directs the sequence-specific degradation of complementary mRNA through the RNA-induced silencing complex. The term “host cell” refers to any eukaryotic cell, particularly avian epithelial cells, that receives and processes the delivered RNA interference molecules. The term “mucosal epithelium” refers to the layer of epithelial cells lining the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and urogenital tracts, which serve as the primary site of pathogen entry and replication. The term “prophylactic” refers to a treatment administered prior to exposure to a pathogen for the purpose of preventing infection. The term “commercial poultry” refers to domesticated birds raised in large-scale agricultural operations for the production of meat or eggs. The term “viable inoculum” refers to a preparation containing live, metabolically active bacterial cells capable of performing their intended biological function. The term “non-integrating” refers to a genetic construct or organism that does not incorporate its DNA into the genome of the host cell. The term “endosomal escape” refers to the process by which a therapeutic agent exits the endocytic vesicle to reach the cytoplasm of the host cell. The term “gene silencing” refers to the suppression of gene expression at the transcriptional or post-transcriptional level. The term “conserved sequence” refers to a region of a gene that remains unchanged or minimally altered across multiple strains or species due to functional constraints. The term “co-administration” refers to the simultaneous or sequential delivery of two or more therapeutic agents. The term “flock management” refers to the operational practices employed in the care, housing, feeding, and health monitoring of groups of poultry.
