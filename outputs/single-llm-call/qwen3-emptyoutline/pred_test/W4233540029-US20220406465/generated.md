# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to systems and methods for the passive, non-intrusive detection of mental health risk in individuals with chronic metabolic disorders, particularly diabetes mellitus, through the analysis of behavioral, physiological, and interactional data streams generated during routine engagement with digital health platforms. More specifically, the invention encompasses a machine learning-based predictive framework that leverages passive sensing signals derived from blood glucose monitoring devices, digital health applications, coaching interactions, and associated user activity logs to identify individuals at elevated risk for depression, generalized anxiety disorder, or other clinically significant mental health conditions. The system operates without requiring additional input from the user beyond their normal, self-directed use of existing diabetes management tools, thereby eliminating the burden of explicit screening, self-reporting, or sensor deployment. The invention is particularly suited for integration into remote patient monitoring systems, digital therapeutics platforms, and chronic disease management programs where continuous, real-time assessment of psychological well-being is clinically valuable but historically under-addressed due to resource constraints, stigma, and lack of scalable tools. By transforming routine, low-friction interactions with medical devices into diagnostic indicators, the invention enables early identification of mental health deterioration in a population known to experience disproportionately high rates of comorbid psychiatric conditions, thereby facilitating timely, targeted interventions that improve both psychological and metabolic outcomes. The technical innovation lies in the novel aggregation and interpretation of previously unconnected data modalities—glucometer usage patterns, emotional state responses during glucose checks, coaching engagement metrics, and digital platform interaction rhythms—into a unified probabilistic model that reliably predicts mental health risk with clinically meaningful accuracy, all while preserving user privacy and operational feasibility within existing healthcare infrastructure.

## BACKGROUND

The management of diabetes mellitus has long been recognized as a biopsychosocial challenge, requiring sustained behavioral adherence to complex regimens involving dietary control, physical activity, medication adherence, and frequent self-monitoring of blood glucose. Despite advances in pharmacological and technological interventions, a substantial proportion of individuals living with diabetes continue to experience significant psychological distress, including depressive symptoms and generalized anxiety disorder, which are prevalent at rates two to three times higher than in the general population. These mental health comorbidities are not merely incidental; they are causally linked to poorer glycemic control, reduced treatment adherence, increased hospitalization rates, and elevated healthcare expenditures. Studies have demonstrated that individuals with both diabetes and depression exhibit up to 40% lower adherence to glucose monitoring protocols, are significantly less likely to attend medical appointments, and demonstrate higher rates of diabetic complications such as neuropathy, retinopathy, and nephropathy. The economic burden of this comorbidity is staggering, with annual healthcare costs estimated to exceed $9 billion in the United States alone, attributable to increased utilization of emergency services, prolonged hospital stays, and diminished productivity. Despite clear clinical guidelines from authoritative bodies such as the American Diabetes Association and the United States Preventive Services Task Force recommending routine mental health screening for all individuals with diabetes, implementation remains inconsistent, with fewer than half of affected individuals receiving a formal diagnosis or intervention. The primary barriers to effective screening include a critical shortage of mental health professionals, limited training among primary care providers in recognizing psychological symptoms in the context of chronic disease, and the inherent limitations of traditional assessment tools that rely on periodic questionnaires, clinical interviews, or self-reported scales—all of which are subject to recall bias, social desirability effects, and low compliance rates. Furthermore, the stigma associated with mental illness often discourages patients from voluntarily disclosing emotional distress, even when prompted. As a result, mental health deterioration in this population frequently goes undetected until it reaches a crisis point, at which point intervention becomes more complex, costly, and less effective. While recent advances in digital health technologies have enabled the passive collection of behavioral data through smartphones—such as motion sensors, location tracking, call logs, and app usage patterns—these approaches have largely been confined to general population studies and have not been systematically adapted for individuals with diabetes. Moreover, reliance on smartphones as the primary data source presents challenges related to device ownership, privacy concerns, and inconsistent usage patterns that limit generalizability. Crucially, no prior system has demonstrated the capacity to detect mental health risk by analyzing data streams generated by blood glucose meters, despite the fact that these devices are used multiple times daily by nearly all individuals with diabetes, represent the most consistent point of interaction with the healthcare system, and are uniquely positioned to capture real-time physiological and emotional states through integrated contextual prompts. The invention disclosed herein addresses this critical gap by demonstrating that the temporal patterns, frequency, emotional context, and physiological variability of blood glucose measurements—when combined with complementary data from coaching interactions and digital platform engagement—form a rich, multidimensional signal set that can be modeled with high predictive validity to identify individuals at risk for mental health deterioration. This approach not only circumvents the limitations of traditional screening methods but also leverages existing infrastructure to deliver scalable, privacy-preserving, and clinically actionable insights without requiring any additional effort from the patient or provider.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a computer-implemented system and method for the passive detection of mental health risk in individuals with diabetes mellitus through the analysis of behavioral and physiological data streams generated during routine use of digital health platforms. The system comprises a data ingestion module configured to collect passive sensing signals from a plurality of sources associated with a diabetes management ecosystem, including blood glucose meter usage logs, emotional state responses recorded during glucose checks, coaching interaction records, and digital platform engagement metrics derived from mobile applications and web portals. These signals are aggregated over a defined temporal window, typically four weeks, to form a participant-period instance that encapsulates the individual’s behavioral and physiological patterns during that interval. The system further comprises a machine learning model trained to predict the likelihood of mental health risk based on these aggregated signals, wherein the model is constructed as an ensemble of gradient-boosted decision trees optimized for class imbalance and trained using a combination of undersampled control instances and diverse random subsets of the training data to enhance generalizability. The model accepts as input demographic information, temporal patterns of glucose monitoring, mean and standard deviation of glucose values, frequency and consistency of digital interactions, coaching contact outcomes, and self-reported emotional states during glucose checks, and outputs a continuous probability score between zero and one representing the individual’s risk of exhibiting clinically significant mental health symptoms. The system is designed to operate without requiring active user input beyond normal device usage, thereby enabling continuous, real-time risk assessment within existing healthcare workflows. The invention further includes a notification engine that triggers alerts to healthcare providers or care coordinators when the model’s output exceeds a predefined clinical threshold, facilitating timely intervention without disrupting the patient’s routine. The model has been validated across multiple independent test sets using medication refill data and medical claims as ground truth, achieving an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) exceeding 0.65 across all validation cohorts, with precision and recall rates significantly surpassing baseline expectations derived from population prevalence. Feature importance analysis confirms that the most predictive signals include emotional state responses during glucose checks, variation in glucose levels, frequency of coaching interactions, and temporal consistency of device usage, with demographic factors such as gender and race contributing meaningfully to predictive accuracy. The system is scalable, privacy-preserving, and compatible with existing digital health platforms, making it suitable for deployment in remote patient monitoring programs, telehealth services, and integrated care models serving large populations of individuals with chronic disease. The invention represents a paradigm shift in mental health screening by transforming passive, routine interactions with medical devices into a reliable, automated diagnostic signal, thereby enabling early, non-intrusive identification of psychological distress in a high-risk population that has historically been underserved by conventional screening methods.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is a comprehensive, computer-implemented system and method for the passive, real-time detection of mental health risk in individuals with diabetes mellitus through the analysis of behavioral, physiological, and interactional data streams generated during routine engagement with digital health platforms. The system is designed to operate seamlessly within existing diabetes management ecosystems, leveraging data that is already being collected as part of standard care without requiring any additional effort, devices, or input from the patient. The core innovation lies in the recognition that the patterns of interaction with blood glucose meters, digital applications, and coaching services—when aggregated, contextualized, and modeled with machine learning techniques—constitute a rich, multidimensional signal set that is highly predictive of underlying psychological distress. Unlike prior approaches that rely on explicit self-reporting, smartphone sensor data, or clinician-administered questionnaires, this invention extracts diagnostic information from the natural, unobtrusive rhythms of daily disease management, thereby overcoming the barriers of stigma, compliance, and scalability that have historically impeded mental health screening in chronic disease populations.

The system begins with a data ingestion module that continuously collects passive sensing signals from multiple sources within the diabetes management ecosystem. These sources include, but are not limited to, a cellular-enabled, two-way messaging blood glucose meter that records each glucose reading along with the time and date of measurement; a companion mobile application and web portal used for logging food intake, physical activity, and receiving health reminders; and a coaching service that facilitates scheduled and on-demand interactions between patients and certified diabetes care and education specialists. Each interaction with these platforms generates a discrete data point that is timestamped, anonymized, and transmitted to a secure data repository. The data ingestion module is designed to handle heterogeneous data formats, including structured numerical values, categorical responses, and unstructured text logs from coaching conversations, and normalizes these inputs into a standardized schema suitable for downstream analysis. Importantly, no data are collected for the purpose of mental health assessment; all signals are derived from activities that occur as part of routine diabetes self-management, ensuring that the system operates within the bounds of ethical data usage and preserves patient autonomy.

Once the raw data are ingested, the system aggregates them into participant-period instances, each representing a defined temporal window—typically four weeks—during which the individual’s behavioral and physiological patterns are captured in sufficient density to support meaningful analysis. This aggregation window is selected to balance data availability with clinical relevance, as shorter intervals may lack sufficient signal strength, while longer intervals may obscure acute changes in psychological state. For each participant-period instance, the system computes a comprehensive set of features derived from four primary data categories: demographics, glucometer usage, coaching interactions, and event data from digital platforms. Demographic features include age, gender, race, ethnicity, body mass index, and smoking status, all of which are known to correlate with mental health outcomes and are obtained from enrollment records. Glucometer-derived features include the total number of glucose checks performed during the period, the mean and standard deviation of glucose values, the distribution of readings across time of day (e.g., morning, afternoon, evening, night), the proportion of readings taken on weekdays versus weekends, and the frequency of responses to contextual prompts embedded within the meter interface, such as “How are you feeling today?” with options ranging from “well” to “unwell.” These emotional state responses are treated as direct, real-time indicators of affective state, distinct from retrospective self-reports, and are found to be among the most predictive features in the model. Coaching interaction features include the number of initiated and received calls, the duration of each interaction, the success rate of contact attempts (e.g., answered versus unanswered), and the frequency of follow-up messages sent by coaches in response to patient-initiated inquiries. These metrics serve as proxies for sociability and social support, which are established determinants of psychological well-being. Event data from the mobile app and web portal include the number of logins, average session duration, frequency of interactions with educational content, response rates to pop-up reminders, and patterns of activity across days of the week and times of day. Consistency of engagement—measured as the proportion of days on which any interaction occurred—is particularly informative, as abrupt declines in digital engagement have been shown to precede clinical deterioration in mood disorders.

The aggregated participant-period instances are then fed into a machine learning model that has been trained to predict the likelihood of mental health risk. The model is implemented as an ensemble of ten LightGBM gradient-boosted decision tree models, each trained on a randomly undersampled subset of the training data to mitigate the inherent class imbalance between individuals with and without clinically significant mental health conditions. The undersampling strategy involves reducing the number of control instances (those without mental health indicators) to match the number of case instances (those with evidence of mental health risk based on medication prescriptions or claims data), thereby ensuring that the model does not become biased toward the majority class. To fully utilize the available training data, multiple random subsets of the control group are generated, and each of the ten constituent models is trained on a distinct subset, allowing the ensemble to capture a broader spectrum of behavioral patterns associated with mental health risk. During inference, the output of each constituent model—a probability score between zero and one—is averaged via soft voting to produce a single, calibrated risk score for the participant-period instance. This ensemble approach not only improves predictive accuracy but also enhances robustness by reducing the variance associated with any single model’s predictions. The model is trained using a labeled dataset derived from two independent sources of ground truth: medication prescription and refill data, which serve as a proxy for clinical diagnosis and treatment initiation, and medical and pharmacy claims data, which include diagnostic codes for depression, anxiety, and related conditions. The use of multiple ground truth sources ensures that the model is not reliant on a single, potentially biased labeling mechanism and increases the generalizability of its predictions across diverse healthcare settings.

The model’s architecture is optimized for interpretability and clinical utility. Feature importance is quantified using the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method, which assigns a contribution value to each input feature based on its marginal effect on the model’s output across all training instances. This analysis confirms that the most influential features include emotional state responses during glucose checks, the standard deviation of glucose values, the frequency of coaching interactions, and the temporal consistency of device usage. Notably, the emotional state responses—particularly those indicating “unwellness”—emerge as the single most predictive signal, with a SHAP value significantly higher than all other features, suggesting that real-time affective feedback embedded within routine glucose monitoring is a powerful, previously underutilized indicator of psychological distress. The standard deviation of glucose levels, a measure of glycemic variability, also ranks highly, consistent with clinical literature linking metabolic instability to mood dysregulation. The frequency and consistency of coaching interactions reflect the individual’s engagement with social support systems, and their decline is strongly associated with increased risk. Demographic features such as gender and race contribute meaningfully to the model’s predictive power, reflecting known disparities in mental health prevalence and access to care, and are retained not as deterministic factors but as contextual variables that refine the model’s calibration across subpopulations.

The system further includes a notification engine that triggers alerts to healthcare providers, care coordinators, or digital health platforms when the model’s output exceeds a predefined clinical threshold. This threshold is calibrated based on a balance between sensitivity and specificity, informed by clinical guidelines and operational constraints, and may be adjusted dynamically based on population-level prevalence, provider workload, or intervention capacity. When an alert is triggered, the system generates a prioritized case list that includes the individual’s risk score, the key contributing features, and a summary of behavioral trends observed over the assessment period. This information is delivered through secure messaging interfaces, electronic health record integrations, or dedicated care management dashboards, enabling providers to initiate timely, personalized interventions such as referral to a mental health specialist, adjustment of diabetes care plans, or activation of peer support programs. Importantly, the system does not make clinical decisions; it provides evidence-based risk stratification that supports, rather than replaces, clinical judgment.

The invention is designed for scalability and interoperability. It can be deployed as a cloud-based service that integrates with existing digital health platforms, including commercial blood glucose meters, mobile applications, and remote monitoring systems, without requiring hardware modifications or additional sensors. The data ingestion module is agnostic to the specific brand or model of device, as long as it produces timestamped usage logs and contextual responses. The machine learning model is lightweight and can be executed on edge devices or centralized servers, depending on privacy and latency requirements. The entire system is compliant with HIPAA, GDPR, and other regulatory frameworks governing health data, with all data encrypted in transit and at rest, and individual identifiers removed prior to model training. Furthermore, the system is designed to be continuously updated through retraining on new data, allowing it to adapt to evolving behavioral patterns, demographic shifts, and emerging clinical insights without requiring manual intervention.

Validation of the system was conducted across three independent test sets, each using a different source of ground truth: medication refill data, claims data, and a hybrid dataset combining both. Across all test sets, the model achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of at least 0.65, with the highest performance observed in the medication refill cohort (AUC = 0.69). Precision and recall rates consistently exceeded baseline expectations derived from population prevalence, with precision improving by 14 percentage points over random prediction in the first test set and by 10 points in the others. The F1 score, which balances precision and recall, surpassed 0.49 in all cohorts, indicating a clinically meaningful ability to identify true positives without excessive false alarms. The model demonstrated robust performance across demographic subgroups, including age, gender, and racial categories, with no significant degradation in predictive accuracy, suggesting that the system is equitable in its application. Importantly, the model’s performance was not driven by demographic confounders alone; even after controlling for age, gender, and race, the passive sensing signals retained strong predictive power, confirming that the behavioral and physiological patterns themselves are the primary drivers of risk prediction.

The invention represents a fundamental departure from traditional mental health screening paradigms. Rather than asking patients to complete questionnaires, attend interviews, or wear additional sensors, it observes how they live with their disease—how often they check their glucose, how they respond to prompts, how they interact with their care team, and whether their routines remain consistent. This passive, non-intrusive approach is not only more acceptable to patients but also more sustainable at scale, as it requires no additional resources from providers or health systems. The system is particularly well-suited for implementation in large-scale diabetes management programs, where thousands of individuals are monitored remotely and where the burden of mental health screening has historically been prohibitive. By embedding mental health risk detection into the fabric of routine diabetes care, the invention transforms passive data into proactive care, enabling early intervention before psychological distress escalates into clinical crisis. The result is a system that is not merely predictive but preventive—capable of identifying individuals at risk, triggering timely support, and ultimately improving both mental and metabolic health outcomes in a population that stands to benefit most from such innovation. The invention is not limited to diabetes; its underlying architecture—aggregating passive signals from disease-specific devices, contextual responses, and interaction patterns into a machine learning framework—is generalizable to other chronic conditions, such as hypertension, heart failure, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, where behavioral adherence and psychological well-being are tightly interwoven. However, its first and most compelling application is in diabetes, where the ubiquity of glucose monitoring, the high prevalence of comorbid mental illness, and the profound impact of psychological distress on clinical outcomes create a uniquely fertile ground for transformative innovation.
