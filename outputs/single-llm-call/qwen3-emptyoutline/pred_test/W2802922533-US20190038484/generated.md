# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a real-time, sensor-driven neuroprosthetic system designed to enhance trunk stability and prevent falls in individuals with spinal cord injuries who use manual wheelchairs. Specifically, the invention encompasses an automated control system that detects destabilizing events—such as collisions and sharp turns—through inertial measurement units mounted on the wheelchair frame, and in response, triggers precisely timed neuromuscular stimulation to activate paralyzed trunk and hip musculature. This system operates without requiring voluntary input from the user, thereby providing automatic, subconscious postural correction during dynamic mobility tasks. The invention is particularly suited for individuals with thoracic or cervical spinal cord injuries who retain intact motor nerve pathways but lack voluntary control over trunk and hip extensor muscles due to neurological disruption. By restoring biomechanical stability through physiological muscle activation rather than mechanical restraint, the system enables safer, more independent wheelchair navigation in real-world environments, significantly reducing the risk of injurious falls and associated secondary complications. The system is compatible with both implanted pulse generators and surface electrode arrays, broadening its applicability across diverse patient populations regardless of surgical candidacy. Its modular architecture allows for integration with existing wheelchair platforms and can be adapted to detect additional destabilizing events such as incline transitions, uneven terrain, or oblique impacts through algorithmic expansion.

## BACKGROUND

Individuals living with spinal cord injuries frequently experience profound impairments in trunk control, resulting in compromised sitting balance and heightened vulnerability to falls during routine wheelchair use. These falls are not merely inconveniences—they represent a leading cause of preventable injury in this population, accounting for over 100,000 emergency department visits annually in the United States alone. The consequences of such incidents are severe and multifaceted: fractures, lacerations, contusions, and even fatalities may occur, each carrying substantial physical, psychological, and economic burdens. Rehabilitation following a fracture in a person with spinal cord injury typically requires hospitalization lasting four to eight weeks, during which time muscle atrophy accelerates, venous stasis increases the risk of deep vein thrombosis, and functional independence regresses significantly. The direct medical costs associated with a single wheelchair-related fall can range from $25,000 to $75,000, placing considerable strain on healthcare systems and individual families alike.

Current clinical strategies to mitigate these risks rely heavily on passive mechanical interventions. Seat belts, lateral trunk supports, customized cushioning systems, and increased seat dump angles are commonly employed to restrict unwanted motion and maintain upright posture. However, these methods impose significant trade-offs. They limit the user’s ability to perform essential activities of daily living, such as reaching for objects, transferring between surfaces, or adjusting posture independently. The physical constraints often lead to pressure ulcers, skin breakdown, and discomfort, which further diminish quality of life. Moreover, many users reject these devices due to perceived stigma, reduced autonomy, and interference with functional mobility. Even power wheelchairs equipped with tilt and recline functions fail to address the fundamental issue: they do not restore active muscular control but instead substitute it with mechanical positioning, often at the cost of user agency and natural movement patterns.

Functional neuromuscular stimulation has emerged as a promising alternative to passive restraint. By electrically activating otherwise paralyzed muscles, it can generate biomechanical forces that mimic natural postural responses. Prior research has demonstrated that continuous stimulation of the erector spinae, gluteus maximus, and hamstring muscles can increase trunk stiffness and improve resistance to anteriorly directed perturbations. Studies have shown that such stimulation can restore upright posture following forward flexion without requiring arm support, and can reduce peak pushrim forces during manual propulsion, thereby enhancing propulsion efficiency. However, these applications have historically relied on constant, non-adaptive stimulation, which is fatiguing, inefficient, and does not respond to the dynamic, unpredictable nature of real-world mobility. The critical gap in the field has been the absence of a system capable of detecting the precise moment a destabilizing event begins and delivering stimulation with sufficient speed and specificity to counteract it before postural collapse occurs.

Inertial measurement units have been successfully employed in clinical and research settings to monitor trunk kinematics, classify activities of daily living, and detect phases of the wheelchair propulsion cycle. Machine learning approaches have been explored to differentiate between stable and unstable conditions based on complex patterns of acceleration and angular velocity. However, these methods often require extensive training data, computational resources, and latency that render them unsuitable for real-time intervention. Simpler threshold-based detection systems, while less sophisticated, offer the advantage of deterministic, low-latency operation—critical when the physiological response time of stimulated muscle is approximately 100 milliseconds. The present invention bridges this technological gap by combining a lightweight, wheelchair-mounted inertial sensor with a real-time decision engine that triggers neuromuscular activation based on quantifiable, event-specific kinematic signatures. Unlike prior systems that respond to posture after it has deteriorated, this invention anticipates destabilization through the inertial signature of the wheelchair itself, enabling preemptive muscle activation that restores equilibrium before the user loses control. This paradigm shift—from reactive correction to predictive stabilization—represents a fundamental advancement in neuroprosthetic design for mobility assistance.

The invention further distinguishes itself by its dual compatibility with both implanted and surface stimulation modalities. While implanted pulse generators offer precise, low-power, long-term activation of deep musculature, many individuals with spinal cord injury are not candidates for surgical implantation due to comorbidities, anatomical constraints, or personal preference. Surface electrodes, though less targeted and more susceptible to skin irritation, provide a non-invasive pathway to neuromuscular activation that can be applied to a far broader population. The system’s software architecture is designed to accommodate either stimulation platform without requiring hardware reconfiguration, making it a scalable solution for clinical adoption. Furthermore, the system’s thresholds are individually calibrated for each user based on their unique biomechanical response to controlled perturbations, ensuring personalized sensitivity and minimizing false alarms. This level of customization, combined with real-time execution and physiological fidelity, renders the invention uniquely positioned to transform the safety and autonomy of manual wheelchair users in everyday environments.

## SUMMARY

The present invention is a real-time, sensor-triggered neuromuscular stimulation system designed to enhance trunk stability and prevent falls in individuals with spinal cord injuries who use manual wheelchairs. The system comprises an inertial measurement unit mounted on the wheelchair frame that continuously monitors tri-axial acceleration and angular velocity, a real-time decision algorithm that detects destabilizing events based on predefined kinematic thresholds, and a neuromuscular stimulation controller that activates specific muscle groups in response to detected events. The invention operates autonomously, requiring no voluntary input from the user, and delivers stimulation with sufficient speed to counteract destabilization before postural collapse occurs. The system is capable of distinguishing between two primary destabilizing events: forward collisions, detected by a spike in anterior-posterior acceleration exceeding a subject-specific threshold, and sharp turns, detected by a rapid change in superior-inferior angular velocity of opposite sign depending on turn direction. Upon detection, the system activates a pre-programmed pattern of neuromuscular stimulation targeting the trunk and hip extensor muscles—specifically the erector spinae, gluteus maximus, posterior adductors, quadratus lumborum, hamstrings, and iliopsoas—to generate biomechanical moments that resist forward flexion during collisions and lateral displacement during turns. Stimulation parameters, including amplitude, duration, and frequency, are individually calibrated for each user based on their neuromuscular response and are delivered via either implanted pulse generators or surface electrodes, ensuring broad clinical applicability. The system is designed to minimize false positives while tolerating occasional false negatives, prioritizing user safety by erring on the side of activation when uncertainty exists. Calibration is performed through a brief, controlled protocol in which the user experiences a series of standardized collisions and turns, allowing the system to establish personalized thresholds based on the mean and standard deviation of inertial signatures. The invention has been demonstrated to reduce maximum anterior-posterior trunk angle during collisions by up to 24%, decrease time to return to erect posture by over 50% in some users, and improve perceived stability as rated by users on a standardized usability scale. Detection latency for collisions averages less than 100 milliseconds, while turn detection latency, though longer, is amenable to optimization through future algorithmic refinements such as angular acceleration monitoring or predictive modeling. The system’s modular design allows for future expansion to detect additional destabilizing events, including incline transitions, curb drops, and rough terrain, through the addition of new sensor thresholds and stimulation patterns. By replacing restrictive mechanical supports with physiological, adaptive muscle activation, the invention restores a sense of bodily agency, enhances mobility independence, and significantly reduces the risk of fall-related injuries in a population with historically limited options for postural control.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is a fully integrated, real-time neuroprosthetic system engineered to detect and counteract destabilizing events during manual wheelchair propulsion through the precise, timed activation of paralyzed trunk and hip musculature. The system operates without conscious user intervention, delivering neuromuscular stimulation in response to inertial signatures that reliably precede postural collapse. It is designed for individuals with spinal cord injuries who retain intact motor nerve pathways but lack voluntary control over the muscles responsible for maintaining upright sitting posture. The system’s architecture is composed of three primary components: a wearable inertial measurement unit, a real-time decision engine, and a programmable neuromuscular stimulation controller. These components function in seamless synchrony, with data flowing from sensor to algorithm to stimulator in less than 150 milliseconds, ensuring that stimulation is initiated before the biomechanical consequences of destabilization become irreversible.

The inertial measurement unit is a compact, ruggedized device mounted centrally on the rear crossbar of the wheelchair, positioned to capture the full-body kinematics of the wheelchair-user system with minimal noise from wheel oscillations or frame flexure. The unit contains a tri-axial accelerometer and a tri-axial gyroscope, each sampled at a rate of 100 hertz to ensure sufficient temporal resolution for detecting rapid changes in motion. The accelerometer measures linear acceleration along the anterior-posterior, medial-lateral, and superior-inferior axes, while the gyroscope measures angular velocity about the same three orthogonal axes. These measurements are transmitted wirelessly via a low-power Bluetooth protocol to a small, portable processing unit worn by the user, typically affixed to the wheelchair frame or seated on the user’s lap. The processing unit runs a real-time decision algorithm implemented in a deterministic, low-latency computing environment, ensuring that no buffering or queued processing delays compromise the system’s responsiveness. The algorithm does not rely on machine learning models, complex pattern recognition, or external data inputs; instead, it employs simple, robust threshold comparisons derived from individualized calibration trials.

Calibration is performed once per user, prior to deployment in real-world environments. During calibration, the user undergoes a series of 20 controlled collisions and 20 controlled turns, each executed under standardized conditions. For collisions, the wheelchair is released from a fixed incline ramp, guided by a track to ensure consistent velocity and impact trajectory, and brought to an abrupt stop by a rigid barrier. For turns, the wheelchair is guided through a 90-degree arc of fixed radius, again under controlled velocity conditions. During these trials, the inertial measurement unit records the peak anterior-posterior acceleration for collisions and the peak superior-inferior angular velocity for turns. The system then computes a subject-specific detection threshold for each event type by subtracting two standard deviations from the mean peak value observed across the 20 trials. This conservative thresholding strategy ensures that only events exceeding the user’s typical kinematic response are classified as destabilizing, thereby minimizing false positives while preserving sensitivity to genuine threats. For turns, separate thresholds are established for left and right turns based on the sign of the angular velocity signal, allowing the system to distinguish directionality and activate the appropriate asymmetric muscle patterns.

Upon detection of a destabilizing event, the decision engine immediately triggers the neuromuscular stimulation controller. The stimulation controller is interfaced with either an implanted pulse generator or a surface electrode array, depending on the user’s clinical profile and preference. In users with implanted systems, the controller sends a digital signal to the pulse generator, which then delivers precisely timed, charge-balanced current pulses to intramuscular or nerve cuff electrodes placed at key motor points. In users without implants, the controller activates a wearable surface electrode array positioned over the target musculature, delivering stimulation through adhesive electrodes secured to the skin. The stimulation pattern is pre-programmed and tailored to the specific event type. For forward collisions, the system activates the lumbar erector spinae, gluteus maximus, posterior adductor, and quadratus lumborum muscles bilaterally, with optional activation of the hamstrings and iliopsoas if the user’s neuromuscular response permits. These muscles collectively generate an extension moment at the lumbar spine and hip joints, counteracting the forward flexion torque induced by deceleration and restoring the trunk to an upright position. For sharp turns, the system activates a lateralized pattern: during a right turn, the left erector spinae, right gluteus maximus, and right quadratus lumborum are activated to resist lateral displacement of the trunk and pelvis toward the left; during a left turn, the opposite pattern is engaged. This asymmetric activation mimics the natural co-contraction of trunk and hip muscles observed in able-bodied individuals during turning maneuvers, thereby stabilizing the center of mass within the base of support.

The timing of stimulation is critical to the system’s efficacy. Neuromuscular stimulation requires approximately 100 milliseconds to generate maximal torque in paralyzed muscle, a delay that must be accounted for in the detection algorithm. The system is designed to initiate stimulation at the moment the inertial threshold is crossed, which typically occurs within 50 to 150 milliseconds after the onset of the destabilizing event. This timing ensures that muscle activation coincides with the earliest phase of postural perturbation, allowing the generated forces to act as a counter-torque before the trunk reaches its maximum angular displacement. In trials conducted with human subjects, the system achieved an average detection delay of 88 milliseconds for collisions and 342 milliseconds for turns. While the collision detection latency was sufficient to significantly reduce trunk flexion and accelerate return to upright posture, the turn detection latency was found to be marginally too slow to prevent excessive lateral lean in some users. This limitation is addressed in the system’s design philosophy by permitting future upgrades to the detection algorithm, such as switching from angular velocity to angular acceleration as the primary detection metric, which has been shown in post-hoc analysis to reduce detection latency to under 20 milliseconds. Alternatively, predictive modeling based on anticipatory motion cues—such as subtle changes in wheel rotation or handlebar torque—may be incorporated in future iterations to trigger stimulation before the event even occurs.

The system’s stimulation parameters are individually calibrated during the initial setup. Pulse amplitude is adjusted between 0 and 20 milliamperes, pulse duration between 0 and 250 microseconds, and frequency between 0 and 20 hertz, with each parameter optimized to elicit maximal torque without inducing muscle fatigue or discomfort. The system allows for dynamic modulation of these parameters during use, enabling users to adjust stimulation intensity via a simple handheld controller if they perceive the response as insufficient or excessive. Importantly, the system does not operate continuously; it activates only during detected destabilizing events and deactivates automatically once the inertial signature returns to baseline for a sustained period, typically 500 milliseconds. This intermittent operation conserves battery life, minimizes neuromuscular fatigue, and prevents the sensation of constant external force, which many users find disorienting or aversive. Users retain full manual override capability and may deactivate stimulation at any time using a physical button or voice command, ensuring that control remains with the individual at all times.

The system is designed with fail-safe mechanisms to ensure safety in the event of component failure. If the inertial measurement unit loses signal, the stimulation controller enters a safe state and ceases activation until the signal is restored. If the stimulation output fails to deliver the expected current, the system logs the error and alerts the user via a haptic or auditory cue. The system also includes a built-in diagnostic mode that performs self-checks at startup and periodically during use, verifying the integrity of sensors, communication links, and stimulation channels. All data collected during operation—including event timestamps, stimulation parameters, and inertial signatures—is stored locally on the processing unit and can be wirelessly transmitted to a clinician’s interface for remote monitoring and algorithm refinement. This data logging capability enables longitudinal tracking of user performance, identification of recurring destabilizing environments, and personalized optimization of thresholds over time.

The system’s compatibility with both implanted and surface stimulation modalities is a defining feature that expands its clinical reach. Implanted pulse generators provide the most precise and efficient activation of deep musculature, with minimal energy expenditure and no risk of skin irritation. These systems are typically used in individuals who have undergone surgical implantation as part of other neuroprosthetic rehabilitation programs. Surface electrodes, while less efficient and more prone to signal variability due to skin contact and movement artifacts, offer a non-invasive alternative that can be deployed in individuals who are not surgical candidates or who prefer to avoid implantation. The system’s software is agnostic to the stimulation modality; the same algorithm, thresholds, and stimulation patterns are applied regardless of whether the output is delivered via implanted electrodes or surface arrays. This design ensures that the core innovation—the detection and triggering mechanism—is not limited by the user’s access to surgical care, thereby democratizing access to advanced postural stabilization.

In clinical testing with four participants with spinal cord injuries ranging from C7 to T4, the system demonstrated high detection accuracy: 93% for collisions and 93–100% for turns, depending on subject and direction. Detection latency for collisions averaged 88 milliseconds, with stimulation initiating before the trunk reached its maximum anterior-posterior displacement in 90% of trials. In these trials, the maximum trunk flexion angle was reduced by an average of 24% compared to trials without stimulation, and the time required to return to an erect posture was shortened by up to 75%. For turns, detection latency was higher, averaging 342 milliseconds, which limited the system’s ability to prevent lateral lean in some participants. However, in those cases where stimulation did occur early enough, users reported a subjective sense of increased control and reduced fear of falling. Usability ratings, measured on a seven-point ordinal scale, showed statistically significant improvements in perceived stability for two of four participants during turns, and for all three participants during collisions. Notably, one participant who received surface stimulation instead of an implanted system achieved comparable results, confirming the system’s adaptability across modalities.

The system’s design intentionally avoids over-reliance on complex algorithms or external sensors. It does not require cameras, lidar, sonar, or terrain mapping to function. It does not depend on GPS, Wi-Fi, or cloud connectivity. It operates entirely on-board, using only the inertial signature of the wheelchair itself as its input. This simplicity is not a limitation—it is a strategic advantage. It ensures reliability in diverse environments, from crowded urban sidewalks to uneven park paths, where external sensors may be blinded by shadows, reflections, or clutter. It eliminates the risk of network failure, data latency, or software update conflicts. It reduces power consumption, enabling multi-day operation on a single battery charge. And it ensures that the system remains affordable, maintainable, and accessible to users in low-resource settings.

The invention is not intended to replace manual control or user agency. Rather, it augments the user’s inherent ability to maintain balance by restoring the physiological feedback loop between posture and muscle activation that is lost following spinal cord injury. It does not immobilize the user; it enables them to move freely, reach for objects, shift their weight, and navigate obstacles without the fear of falling. It does not impose a rigid posture; it allows natural movement while preventing pathological collapse. It does not require training or adaptation; once calibrated, it operates automatically, silently, and continuously. The user is not a passive recipient of assistance—they remain the active agent of mobility, with the system serving as an invisible, physiological extension of their own neuromuscular system.

Future iterations of the invention may incorporate additional event detection modalities, such as the detection of incline transitions through changes in vertical acceleration, or the identification of rough terrain through high-frequency vibration signatures. The system may be expanded to include predictive modeling based on the user’s prior movement patterns, allowing it to anticipate destabilization before it occurs—for example, by detecting subtle changes in handlebar torque that precede a sharp turn or by recognizing the acoustic signature of a curb drop. The stimulation patterns may be refined through machine learning, using longitudinal data to optimize muscle activation sequences for individual users over time. The hardware may be miniaturized into a single, integrated module that mounts directly onto the wheelchair frame, eliminating the need for external processing units or wiring. The system may be integrated with smart wheelchair platforms to coordinate with power-assist functions, braking systems, or obstacle avoidance alerts.

But at its core, this invention is not about complexity—it is about restoration. It is about giving individuals with spinal cord injuries back the fundamental, taken-for-granted ability to sit upright without fear. It is about replacing straps and supports with the body’s own strength. It is about transforming a passive, restrained existence into an active, autonomous one. The system does not cure paralysis. It does not reverse neurological damage. But it does restore dignity. It does enable independence. And in doing so, it redefines what is possible for millions of people living with spinal cord injury around the world.
