# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The regulation of cell survival, proliferation, metabolism, and apoptosis is tightly controlled by a network of intracellular signaling pathways, among which the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) axis occupies a central position. This pathway is one of the most frequently dysregulated signaling cascades in human cancer, with aberrant activation observed in over half of all malignancies across diverse tissue types. The Akt family of serine/threonine kinases—comprising Akt1, Akt2, and Akt3—functions as critical downstream effectors of PI3K, integrating signals from growth factors, cytokines, and extracellular matrix interactions to modulate cellular fate. Upon activation, Akt translocates from the cytosol to the plasma membrane, where it is phosphorylated at two key regulatory sites: threonine 308 by phosphoinositide-dependent kinase 1 (PDK1) and serine 473 by the mTOR complex 2 (mTORC2). These phosphorylation events induce a conformational change that fully activates Akt, enabling it to phosphorylate a wide array of substrates involved in cell cycle progression, protein synthesis, glucose metabolism, and inhibition of apoptosis. The oncogenic potential of Akt is underscored by the fact that its hyperactivation can arise through multiple mechanisms, including amplification of Akt genes, loss-of-function mutations in the tumor suppressor PTEN, overexpression of upstream receptor tyrosine kinases, or gain-of-function mutations within Akt itself, most notably the E17K substitution in the pleckstrin homology (PH) domain of Akt1. Such mutations result in constitutive membrane localization and persistent kinase activity independent of upstream signals, rendering cells resistant to physiological controls and highly dependent on Akt signaling for survival.

Despite the clear therapeutic rationale for targeting Akt in cancer, the development of clinically effective inhibitors has proven exceptionally challenging. Early efforts focused on lipid-based molecules designed to mimic phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)-trisphosphate (PIP3), the membrane lipid that recruits Akt via its PH domain. Compounds such as perifosine and PX-316 were developed with the intent of competitively disrupting Akt-membrane interactions. However, these agents exhibited poor pharmacokinetic properties, limited bioavailability, and significant off-target effects due to their structural similarity to endogenous phospholipids, leading to broad disruption of membrane-associated signaling events. Moreover, clinical trials with perifosine in pancreatic and other solid tumors were discontinued due to unacceptable gastrointestinal toxicity and lack of demonstrable target modulation. Other approaches have targeted the ATP-binding pocket of the Akt kinase domain, yielding compounds such as triciribine (API-2), which demonstrated preclinical efficacy but suffered from insufficient selectivity and dose-limiting toxicities in early-phase human trials. The failure of these agents to achieve meaningful clinical benefit highlights a critical gap: there remains a profound unmet need for small molecule inhibitors that are not only potent and selective for Akt, but also capable of inhibiting both wild-type and constitutively active mutant forms of Akt—including those driven by PH domain mutations—without interfering with closely related kinases or upstream components of the pathway.

The structural architecture of Akt presents unique opportunities and challenges for drug discovery. The PH domain, located at the N-terminus of Akt, is responsible for membrane recruitment through its high-affinity interaction with PIP3. Unlike the highly conserved ATP-binding site found in most protein kinases, the PH domain exhibits greater sequence divergence among the Akt isoforms and among other PH domain-containing proteins, offering a potential avenue for achieving isoform-specific or pathway-selective inhibition. However, targeting protein-protein or protein-lipid interfaces with small molecules is notoriously difficult, as these interfaces are typically large, flat, and lack deep hydrophobic pockets amenable to high-affinity binding. Consequently, most kinase inhibitors developed to date have focused on the catalytic domain, often resulting in pan-kinase inhibition and associated toxicities. Furthermore, the functional consequences of inhibiting Akt are not always predictable: while suppression of Akt activity leads to apoptosis in cells dependent on its signaling, many normal tissues exhibit compensatory mechanisms that preserve viability, suggesting a potential therapeutic window. Yet, without a compound that can selectively and potently inhibit Akt without affecting other AGC family kinases such as PKA, PKC, or SGK, or upstream regulators such as PI3K or PDK1, this window remains unrealized.

The identification of a novel chemical scaffold capable of directly binding to the PH domain of Akt and preventing its membrane translocation represents a paradigm shift in the field of Akt-targeted therapeutics. Unlike ATP-competitive inhibitors, which require high concentrations to displace ATP and often lack selectivity, a PH domain-binding inhibitor operates through an allosteric mechanism that prevents the initial step in Akt activation: recruitment to the plasma membrane. This mechanism circumvents the problem of kinase domain conservation and offers the potential for exquisite selectivity. Moreover, because the PH domain mutations that drive oncogenesis—such as E17K and E40K—enhance the affinity of Akt for membrane phospholipids, an inhibitor that competes with this interaction would be expected to be particularly effective against tumors harboring these mutations. Such a compound would not only inhibit the canonical, growth factor-stimulated form of Akt but also the constitutively active, oncogenic variants that are often resistant to conventional therapies. The absence of any clinically approved agent that functions via this mechanism underscores the novelty and significance of this discovery. The development of a small molecule that achieves this precise mode of action, with favorable pharmacological properties and demonstrable in vivo anti-tumor efficacy, would represent a major advancement in targeted cancer therapy and fulfill a long-standing need in oncology drug development.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a novel class of small molecule inhibitors that selectively target the pleckstrin homology (PH) domain of Akt kinases, thereby preventing their translocation to the plasma membrane and inhibiting their activation without interfering with upstream signaling components or closely related kinases. The compound, identified as API-1, is a pyrido[2,3-d]pyrimidine derivative that binds directly and with high specificity to the PH domain of Akt1, Akt2, and Akt3, disrupting the interaction between Akt and phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)-trisphosphate (PIP3) at the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane. This binding event effectively blocks the initial step in Akt activation, resulting in the suppression of phosphorylation at both threonine 308 and serine 473, even in the presence of strong upstream stimuli such as epidermal growth factor or insulin-like growth factor 1. Unlike conventional ATP-competitive kinase inhibitors, API-1 does not inhibit the catalytic activity of Akt in vitro at concentrations that fully suppress Akt phosphorylation in living cells, indicating that its mechanism of action is entirely dependent on preventing membrane localization rather than competing with ATP or substrate peptides. This unique mode of inhibition confers exceptional selectivity, as API-1 shows no significant inhibitory activity against PI3K, PDK1, mTORC1, mTORC2, PKA, PKC, SGK, ERK, JNK, p38, or STAT3, even at concentrations far exceeding those required for Akt inhibition.

API-1 demonstrates potent anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic effects in a wide range of human cancer cell lines characterized by hyperactivation of the Akt pathway, whether due to PTEN loss, Akt amplification, or expression of oncogenic Akt mutants such as Akt1-E17K and Akt1-E40K. In these cells, API-1 induces downregulation of phosphorylated downstream targets including GSK3β, mTOR, p70S6K, Bad, and FOXO3a, leading to cell cycle arrest and caspase-mediated apoptosis, as evidenced by poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase cleavage and annexin V positivity. Importantly, cells with low basal Akt activity, such as those derived from non-transformed tissues or cancer lines without Akt pathway alterations, exhibit minimal sensitivity to API-1, indicating a favorable therapeutic index. The compound retains its efficacy against constitutively active Akt forms, including myristoylated Akt variants, suggesting that its interaction with the PH domain can override even strong membrane-targeting signals, potentially through steric hindrance or conformational stabilization that prevents lipid engagement. In vivo, API-1 administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 10 mg/kg/day significantly inhibits the growth of human tumor xenografts derived from Akt-hyperactivated cell lines such as OVCAR3 and PANC-1, achieving tumor growth suppression of up to 70% without inducing measurable toxicity, as assessed by body weight, food intake, blood glucose levels, or behavioral parameters. In contrast, tumors derived from cell lines with low Akt activity, such as OVCAR5 and COLO357, are largely unaffected by API-1 treatment, further confirming the selectivity of the compound for Akt-dependent malignancies. The ability of API-1 to inhibit both wild-type and mutant forms of Akt, its lack of effect on upstream or parallel signaling pathways, and its demonstrated efficacy in preclinical tumor models establish it as a first-in-class therapeutic agent with the potential to treat a broad spectrum of cancers driven by Akt hyperactivation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention disclosed herein pertains to a novel small molecule inhibitor designated API-1, which possesses a unique chemical structure and a distinct mechanism of action that enables selective and potent inhibition of Akt kinases through direct binding to the pleckstrin homology (PH) domain. This compound, chemically identified as pyrido[2,3-d]pyrimidine derivative NSC 177223, is structurally distinct from previously described Akt inhibitors and operates through a mechanism that fundamentally differs from ATP-competitive or lipid-mimetic strategies. API-1 does not interfere with the catalytic activity of Akt in cell-free systems at pharmacologically relevant concentrations; instead, it prevents the translocation of Akt from the cytosol to the plasma membrane, thereby blocking the spatial reorganization required for its phosphorylation and full activation. This inhibition is achieved through a direct, high-affinity interaction between API-1 and a specific region within the PH domain of Akt, which overlaps with the phosphoinositide-binding pocket normally occupied by phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)-trisphosphate (PIP3). The binding of API-1 to this site induces a conformational change or steric obstruction that renders the PH domain incapable of engaging membrane lipids, effectively sequestering Akt in the cytoplasm and rendering it inaccessible to its activating kinases, PDK1 and mTORC2. This mechanism ensures that API-1 selectively targets Akt without affecting other kinases that rely on similar activation pathways, including those in the AGC family such as PKA, PKC, and SGK, or upstream regulators such as PI3K and PDK1, which remain fully functional in the presence of the compound.

The chemical structure of API-1 is characterized by a fused tricyclic core consisting of a pyridine ring fused to a pyrimidine ring, with specific substitutions at the 2-, 4-, and 6-positions that confer its biological activity. The presence of a primary amino group at the 2-position is essential for its ability to form covalent linkages with NHS-activated affinity matrices, a property exploited in the binding assays used to identify its target. However, this amino group is not required for its cellular activity, as analogs lacking this functionality retain potency, suggesting that the binding interaction is primarily non-covalent and mediated by hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, and hydrophobic complementarity within the PH domain pocket. The compound is soluble in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and exhibits moderate solubility in aqueous buffers when formulated with non-ionic surfactants, making it suitable for both in vitro and in vivo administration. Its molecular weight is approximately 280 Daltons, placing it within the ideal range for oral bioavailability and blood-brain barrier penetration, although its current formulation for preclinical studies utilizes intraperitoneal delivery. API-1 is stable under physiological conditions, with a half-life in plasma exceeding four hours, and does not undergo rapid hepatic metabolism or renal excretion, suggesting favorable pharmacokinetic properties that support once-daily dosing regimens.

The selectivity profile of API-1 is unprecedented among Akt inhibitors. Extensive biochemical profiling across a panel of 200 kinases, including representatives from the AGC, CAMK, CMGC, TK, and STE families, revealed no significant inhibition at concentrations up to 10 micromolar, with the exception of Akt isoforms. In vitro kinase assays using recombinant PKA, PKC, and SGK demonstrated no reduction in substrate phosphorylation following API-1 treatment, even at concentrations 50-fold higher than the IC50 for Akt inhibition. Similarly, API-1 had no effect on the activity of PI3K, as measured by the production of phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)-trisphosphate in immunoprecipitated p110α complexes, nor did it alter the phosphorylation status of PDK1 at serine 241, a key autophosphorylation site required for its activation. The mTOR complexes, both mTORC1 and mTORC2, remained intact in the presence of API-1, as evidenced by co-immunoprecipitation of Raptor and Rictor with mTOR, and the phosphorylation of mTOR at serine 2841 was reduced only as a downstream consequence of Akt inhibition, not through direct targeting. These findings confirm that API-1 does not act as a broad-spectrum kinase inhibitor or a lipid analog that disrupts membrane integrity. Its specificity for Akt is further reinforced by its inability to inhibit the phosphorylation of ERK, JNK, p38, or STAT3 in multiple cancer cell lines, even after prolonged exposure, indicating that its effects are confined to the Akt signaling axis.

The mechanism of action of API-1 was elucidated through a series of biochemical and cellular experiments designed to map its interaction site on Akt. Affinity chromatography using API-1 immobilized on NHS-activated Sepharose beads successfully pulled down recombinant Akt1, Akt2, and Akt3, but not unrelated kinases or control proteins. This interaction was abolished when the PH domain of Akt was deleted or mutated at key residues involved in lipid binding, such as lysine 14, arginine 25, and glutamate 17. Truncation experiments using GST fusion proteins containing the PH domain, kinase domain, or C-terminal regulatory region demonstrated that only the PH domain consistently bound API-1, regardless of salt concentration or detergent composition, indicating a direct and robust interaction. Mutational analysis of Akt1 revealed that substitution of glutamate 17 with lysine, a mutation found in human ovarian, breast, and prostate cancers, did not abolish API-1 binding; in fact, the mutant protein retained equal or greater sensitivity to the compound, suggesting that API-1 exploits the altered conformation of the PH domain in this oncogenic variant to enhance its affinity. This finding is of critical therapeutic importance, as previous allosteric inhibitors failed to inhibit Akt1-E17K, rendering them ineffective against a clinically relevant subset of tumors. API-1, by contrast, not only inhibits the wild-type form of Akt but also suppresses the activity of myristoylated Akt variants, which are engineered to constitutively localize to the membrane through a lipid anchor. This suggests that API-1’s binding to the PH domain can override even strong membrane-targeting signals, potentially by inducing a conformational lock that prevents the PH domain from adopting the orientation necessary for lipid engagement.

The functional consequences of API-1 treatment are consistent with the suppression of Akt signaling. In cells treated with API-1, the phosphorylation of Akt at both threonine 308 and serine 473 is rapidly and dose-dependently reduced, with an IC50 of approximately 0.8 micromolar in OVCAR3 cells. This reduction occurs without any change in total Akt protein levels, confirming that the compound acts post-translationally. Downstream substrates of Akt, including glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β), mTOR, p70S6K, Bad, and FOXO3a, show corresponding decreases in phosphorylation, leading to the activation of pro-apoptotic pathways and the suppression of protein synthesis and cell cycle progression. The induction of apoptosis is particularly pronounced in cancer cell lines with elevated Akt activity, as measured by annexin V staining and cleavage of poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase. In contrast, cells with low basal Akt phosphorylation, such as MDA-MB-435s or OVCAR5, exhibit minimal apoptosis and only modest reductions in proliferation, indicating a narrow therapeutic window that favors tumor cells over normal tissues. This differential sensitivity is further validated in a xenograft model in which tumors derived from Akt-hyperactivated cells (OVCAR3, PANC-1) are profoundly suppressed by API-1 treatment, while tumors from Akt-low cell lines (OVCAR5, COLO357) show no significant change in growth rate. Importantly, no signs of systemic toxicity were observed in treated mice, including no alteration in body weight, food intake, locomotor activity, or blood glucose levels, even after prolonged daily dosing for three weeks. These findings collectively demonstrate that API-1 achieves a high degree of target specificity and therapeutic index, making it uniquely suited for clinical development in cancers defined by Akt pathway activation.

The utility of API-1 extends beyond its direct inhibition of Akt. Because it acts upstream of the kinase domain, it may be effective in tumors that have developed resistance to ATP-competitive inhibitors or those with mutations in the kinase domain that reduce drug binding. Moreover, its ability to inhibit Akt1-E17K, a mutation that confers resistance to other classes of inhibitors, positions API-1 as a potential salvage therapy for patients who have failed prior treatments. The compound’s structural simplicity and synthetic accessibility also allow for the generation of a wide array of analogs, enabling medicinal chemistry optimization for improved pharmacokinetics, oral bioavailability, and blood-brain barrier penetration. The invention encompasses not only API-1 itself but also its pharmaceutically acceptable salts, solvates, prodrugs, and derivatives that retain the core pyrido[2,3-d]pyrimidine scaffold and its ability to bind the PH domain of Akt. The invention further includes pharmaceutical compositions comprising API-1 in combination with other anti-cancer agents, such as chemotherapeutics, radiation, immune checkpoint inhibitors, or other targeted therapies, to enhance efficacy and overcome potential resistance mechanisms. Additionally, the invention covers diagnostic methods for identifying patients whose tumors harbor Akt hyperactivation, including mutations in PTEN, amplification of Akt genes, or presence of the E17K mutation, thereby enabling patient stratification and personalized treatment strategies.

### MATERIALS AND METHODS

All experiments were conducted using standardized protocols and reagents of the highest available purity, with appropriate controls to ensure reproducibility and specificity. Cell lines utilized in this study included human ovarian carcinoma OVCAR3, OVCAR5, OVCAR8, and MDA-MB-468; human breast carcinoma MCF7; human pancreatic carcinoma PANC-1 and COLO357; human cervical carcinoma HeLa; and murine fibroblast NIH3T3 lines, all obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) or previously characterized in published studies. Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 2 mM L-glutamine at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% carbon dioxide. For experiments requiring serum starvation, cells were incubated in medium containing 0.1% fetal bovine serum for 16 to 24 hours prior to stimulation or compound treatment. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling and tested monthly for mycoplasma contamination using a commercial PCR-based detection kit.

The compound API-1, identified as NSC 177223, was obtained from the National Cancer Institute’s Developmental Therapeutics Program and stored as a 10 mM stock solution in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) at −20°C. Working dilutions were prepared fresh in culture medium immediately before use, with final DMSO concentrations not exceeding 0.1% to avoid solvent-induced cytotoxicity. Vehicle controls consisted of medium containing equivalent concentrations of DMSO. Recombinant human Akt1, Akt2, and Akt3 proteins were purchased from Upstate Biotechnology and used in kinase assays without further purification. GST fusion proteins containing the pleckstrin homology domain (GST-PH), kinase domain (GST-KD), and C-terminal regulatory region (GST-CT) of Akt1 were generated by cloning the respective cDNA fragments into the pGEX-4T-1 vector (GE Healthcare) and expressing them in Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) cells. Protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside for 4 hours at 30°C, followed by purification using glutathione-Sepharose affinity chromatography and dialysis against phosphate-buffered saline. Purity was confirmed by SDS-PAGE and Coomassie staining, with protein concentrations determined by Bradford assay.

Affinity chromatography was performed by covalently immobilizing API-1 onto NHS-activated Sepharose beads (GE Healthcare). Briefly, 1 mL of beads was equilibrated in anhydrous DMSO and incubated with 1 mM API-1 and 100 mM triethylamine in a 0.5:1 volume ratio for 16 hours at room temperature with end-over-end rotation. Unreacted NHS esters were quenched by washing with 0.8 M aminoethanol in PBS, followed by alternating washes with 0.1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and 0.1 M acetate buffer (pH 4.0) containing 0.5 M NaCl. The resulting API-1-Sepharose beads were equilibrated in binding buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 0.2% Nonidet P-40, 5% glycerol, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 25 mM NaF, 1 mM Na3VO4, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 1 mM dithiothreitol, and protease inhibitors) and incubated with 400 ng of recombinant Akt or GST fusion proteins overnight at 4°C. Beads were washed four times with binding buffer, and bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 2× Laemmli sample buffer. Samples were resolved by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes for immunoblotting. Primary antibodies used included anti-Akt1 (Cell Signaling Technology, #4685), anti-GST (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-138), and anti-β-actin (Sigma-Aldrich, A5441). Secondary antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase were from Jackson ImmunoResearch. Blots were developed using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents and imaged with a digital chemiluminescence system.

In vitro kinase assays were performed using recombinant Akt protein incubated with Akt/SGK substrate peptide (Upstate Biotechnology) in kinase buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, 1 mM dithiothreitol, 20 μM ATP, and 10 μCi [γ-32P]ATP. Reactions were initiated by adding ATP and terminated after 30 minutes by spotting onto phosphocellulose paper, followed by washing in 0.5% phosphoric acid and quantification by scintillation counting. For PDK1 and PI3K assays, recombinant enzymes were incubated with their respective substrates and inhibitors under manufacturer-recommended conditions. In cellular kinase assays, cells were transfected with HA-tagged Akt constructs using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen), serum-starved, treated with API-1, and stimulated with 100 ng/mL epidermal growth factor or 100 ng/mL insulin-like growth factor 1 for 15 to 30 minutes. Akt was immunoprecipitated using anti-HA antibody (Covance, MMS-101R), washed with lysis buffer, and subjected to in vitro kinase assays as described. Phosphorylation status of Akt and downstream targets was assessed by immunoblotting using phospho-specific antibodies against p-Akt (Thr308, Ser473), p-GSK3β (Ser9), p-mTOR (Ser2448), p-p70S6K (Thr389), and p-Bad (Ser136), all from Cell Signaling Technology.

For membrane translocation studies, HeLa cells were transfected with Myc-tagged Akt1 or GFP-tagged Akt1 or GFP-PH domain constructs using Lipofectamine 2000. After 24 hours, cells were serum-starved for 16 hours and treated with API-1 or vehicle for 1 hour prior to stimulation with 100 ng/mL insulin-like growth factor 1 for 15 minutes. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and stained with anti-Myc antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #2276) followed by Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibody. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342. Images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 710) with identical acquisition parameters across all conditions. Fluorescence intensity at the plasma membrane was quantified using ImageJ software by measuring mean pixel intensity along a 5-pixel-wide line drawn along the cell periphery and normalizing to cytoplasmic intensity.

Apoptosis was assessed using annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide staining (BD Biosciences) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were harvested, washed in PBS, resuspended in binding buffer, and stained with annexin V and propidium iodide for 15 minutes in the dark. Flow cytometry was performed using a FACSCalibur instrument (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software. Cell proliferation was measured using the CellTiter 96 AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay (Promega), which quantifies metabolic activity via reduction of MTS tetrazolium compound. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates, treated with compounds for 24 to 72 hours, incubated with MTS reagent for 2 hours, and absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader.

For in vivo studies, 8-week-old female athymic nude mice (Nu/Nu, Charles River Laboratories) were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions with ad libitum access to food and water. Tumor xenografts were established by subcutaneous injection of 2 × 10^6 cells in 100 μL phosphate-buffered saline into the right and left flanks. When tumors reached an average volume of 100 mm³, mice were randomized into treatment groups (n = 8 per group) and administered API-1 (10 mg/kg/day) or vehicle (20% DMSO in saline) via intraperitoneal injection daily for 21 days. Tumor dimensions were measured every 2 to 3 days using digital calipers, and volume was calculated using the formula: volume = (length × width²)/2. Body weight and food intake were recorded daily. At the end of the study, tumors were excised, weighed, and snap-frozen for immunoblotting or fixed in formalin for histological analysis. Blood glucose levels were measured using a glucometer (Accu-Chek Performa) from tail vein blood samples collected at the time of euthanasia. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

### EXAMPLE 1

The identification of API-1 as a selective inhibitor of Akt began with a high-throughput screening campaign designed to detect compounds capable of suppressing the proliferation of cells transformed by constitutive Akt signaling while sparing non-transformed controls. A library of 2,300 small molecules from the NCI/DTP Open Chemical Repository was screened in parallel against NIH3T3 cells stably transfected with AKT2 and control NIH3T3 cells transfected with the empty LXSN vector. After 72 hours of compound exposure at a fixed concentration of 5 micromolar, cell viability was assessed using the CellTiter 96 AQueous One Solution assay. Of the 2,300 compounds tested, 32 demonstrated selective inhibition of AKT2-transformed cells, with minimal effect on control cells, indicating a potential mechanism of action specific to Akt-dependent survival. Among these, API-1 emerged as the most potent and reproducible candidate, consistently reducing viability by more than 70% in AKT2-transformed cells while maintaining over 90% viability in control cells across three independent replicates. Subsequent dose-response experiments confirmed an IC50 of approximately 1.2 micromolar for API-1 in AKT2-transformed cells, compared to greater than 20 micromolar in control cells, establishing a therapeutic window of over 16-fold. This selectivity was not attributable to differential compound uptake or metabolism, as intracellular concentrations measured by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry were equivalent between cell types.

To determine whether API-1 directly inhibited Akt kinase activity, recombinant Akt1, Akt2, and Akt3 proteins were incubated with API-1 in a cell-free kinase assay using a synthetic peptide substrate. At concentrations up to 10 micromolar, API-1 showed no significant reduction in phosphorylation of the substrate, even after prolonged incubation, whereas the pan-kinase inhibitor compound E completely abolished activity at 1 micromolar. This result indicated that API-1 does not function as an ATP-competitive or substrate-competitive inhibitor. To investigate whether API-1 affected upstream activators of Akt, the activity of PI3K was measured in HEK293 cells following stimulation with epidermal growth factor. Immunoprecipitated p110α complexes were subjected to an in vitro lipid kinase assay using phosphatidylinositol 4-phosphate as substrate. API-1 had no effect on the production of phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)-trisphosphate, whereas the PI3K inhibitor wortmannin reduced activity by over 95%. Similarly, PDK1 kinase activity was assessed using a commercial assay kit, and API-1 failed to inhibit phosphorylation of its substrate SGK, even at concentrations 50-fold higher than its effective cellular dose. Phosphorylation of PDK1 at serine 241, a marker of its autophosphorylation and activation, remained unchanged in OVCAR3 cells treated with API-1, whereas the PDK1 inhibitor UCN-01 induced a marked reduction. These findings collectively demonstrated that API-1 does not interfere with the upstream components of the Akt pathway, including PI3K and PDK1, and that its inhibitory effects are confined to Akt itself.

To determine the molecular target of API-1, affinity chromatography was employed using API-1 covalently immobilized on Sepharose beads. Recombinant Akt1 was incubated with the API-1-conjugated beads, and after extensive washing, bound proteins were eluted and analyzed by immunoblotting. A strong signal for Akt1 was detected only in the API-1-bound fraction, whereas no binding was observed with beads coupled to an unrelated compound, BMS-354825, or with uncoupled beads. To map the binding domain, a series of GST fusion proteins encompassing the PH domain, kinase domain, and C-terminal region of Akt1 were incubated with API-1-Sepharose. Only the PH domain-containing construct consistently bound API-1, regardless of the salt concentration in the buffer, indicating a direct and robust interaction. Deletion of the PH domain or mutation of key residues involved in phosphoinositide binding, such as lysine 14 and arginine 25, abolished binding, confirming that API-1 occupies the lipid-binding pocket. These results established that API-1 binds directly to the PH domain of Akt and that this interaction is necessary and sufficient for its biological activity.

To assess the functional consequence of this binding, the subcellular localization of Akt was examined in HeLa cells transfected with Myc-tagged Akt1. Upon stimulation with insulin-like growth factor 1, Akt translocated from the cytoplasm to the plasma membrane, as visualized by confocal microscopy. Pre-treatment with API-1 completely prevented this translocation, with Akt remaining diffusely distributed throughout the cytoplasm. Similar results were obtained with GFP-tagged Akt1 and GFP-tagged PH domain constructs, where API-1 blocked membrane recruitment induced by growth factor stimulation. Endogenous Akt2 in OVCAR3 cells exhibited the same behavior, with API-1 inhibiting EGF-induced membrane translocation as confirmed by subcellular fractionation and immunoblotting. These findings demonstrated that API-1 inhibits Akt by preventing its recruitment to the plasma membrane, thereby blocking its activation without altering its expression or stability. The specificity of this effect was further confirmed by the observation that API-1 did not affect the membrane localization of other PH domain-containing proteins such as Btk or GRP1, indicating that its interaction is highly selective for Akt.

### EXAMPLE 2

The ability of API-1 to inhibit Akt signaling was further validated in a panel of human cancer cell lines known to harbor varying degrees of Akt pathway activation. OVCAR3, MDA-MB-468, and PANC-1 cells were selected for their high basal levels of phosphorylated Akt, resulting from PTEN loss, Akt amplification, or upstream receptor tyrosine kinase overexpression. In contrast, OVCAR5, MDA-MB-435s, and COLO357 cells were chosen for their low Akt phosphorylation status, serving as negative controls. Treatment of these cells with API-1 at concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 10 micromolar for 24 hours resulted in a dose-dependent reduction in phosphorylation of Akt at both threonine 308 and serine 473 in the high-Akt cell lines, with maximal inhibition achieved at 5 micromolar. In the low-Akt cell lines, phosphorylation was only modestly reduced, even at the highest concentration tested. The reduction in phospho-Akt correlated with decreased phosphorylation of downstream targets, including GSK3β at serine 9, mTOR at serine 2448, p70S6K at threonine 389, and Bad at serine 136, all of which are direct substrates of Akt. These changes were accompanied by increased levels of cleaved PARP, a marker of caspase-mediated apoptosis, and increased annexin V staining, confirming the induction of programmed cell death. In contrast, the low-Akt cell lines exhibited minimal changes in these markers, even after 72 hours of continuous exposure.

Cell proliferation assays revealed a striking differential sensitivity to API-1. In OVCAR3, MDA-MB-468, and PANC-1 cells, API-1 at 10 micromolar inhibited proliferation by 60 to 75% after 48 hours, whereas in OVCAR5, MDA-MB-435s, and COLO357, proliferation was reduced by only 10 to 20%. This differential effect was not due to differences in cell cycle distribution, as flow cytometry showed no significant alteration in G1, S, or G2/M phases in low-Akt cells. Apoptosis induction was similarly selective: annexin V-positive cells increased by 9-fold in OVCAR3 and 4.3-fold in MDA-MB-468 after 24 hours of API-1 treatment, whereas in OVCAR5 and MDA-MB-435s, the increase was less than 1.5-fold. To confirm that the observed effects were mediated specifically through Akt inhibition, siRNA-mediated knockdown of Akt1 and Akt2 in OVCAR3 cells phenocopied the effects of API-1, resulting in reduced proliferation and increased apoptosis, whereas knockdown of other kinases such as ERK or JNK had no such effect. Conversely, overexpression of constitutively active Akt1 in MDA-MB-435s cells rendered them sensitive to API-1, demonstrating that Akt hyperactivation is both necessary and sufficient for sensitivity to the compound.

The specificity of API-1 was further tested in cells expressing oncogenic Akt mutants. HEK293 cells were transfected with wild-type Akt1, Akt1-E17K, Akt1-E40K, or myristoylated Akt1 (myr-Akt1), all of which are known to be constitutively active due to enhanced membrane localization. API-1 effectively suppressed the kinase activity of all four variants in immunoprecipitation-based kinase assays, despite the fact that myr-Akt1 bypasses the need for PH domain-lipid interaction. This result was unexpected, as myristoylation should theoretically render Akt insensitive to PH domain inhibitors. However, API-1 still inhibited myr-Akt1 activity by approximately 60%, suggesting that its binding to the PH domain induces a conformational change that impairs the catalytic function of the kinase domain, even when the enzyme is membrane-localized. This dual mechanism—blocking membrane recruitment and impairing catalytic efficiency—may explain the potency of API-1 against all forms of Akt, including those that are resistant to other classes of inhibitors. The inhibition of Akt1-E17K is particularly significant, as this mutation is found in approximately 4% of human ovarian, breast, and prostate cancers and is associated with aggressive disease and resistance to conventional therapies. Previous allosteric inhibitors failed to inhibit Akt1-E17K, but API-1 suppressed its activity with an IC50 comparable to that of wild-type Akt, positioning it as a promising therapeutic agent for this genetically defined subset of patients.

To assess whether API-1 affects other members of the AGC kinase family, recombinant PKA, PKC, and SGK were subjected to in vitro kinase assays in the presence of increasing concentrations of API-1. At concentrations up to 50 micromolar, API-1 had no effect on the phosphorylation of Kemptide by PKA or the SGK substrate peptide by SGK. In cells transfected with HA-tagged SGK, API-1 did not inhibit EGF-induced SGK activation, whereas the PI3K inhibitor wortmannin completely abolished it. Similarly, in OVCAR3 cells treated with API-1, phosphorylation of PKC at threonine 638 and PKA at threonine 197 remained unchanged, whereas specific inhibitors of these kinases induced robust dephosphorylation. These data confirm that API-1 does not cross-react with other kinases that share structural homology with Akt, including those that are activated by PI3K or membrane recruitment. The absence of inhibition of PKA, PKC, and SGK is critical, as these kinases are involved in essential physiological processes such as cardiac function, neuronal signaling, and glucose homeostasis. The lack of off-target effects on these pathways suggests that API-1 may be well tolerated in vivo, with minimal risk of cardiotoxicity, neurotoxicity, or metabolic disturbances.

### EXAMPLE 3

The in vivo efficacy of API-1 was evaluated in a xenograft model of human cancer using athymic nude mice implanted with tumors derived from Akt-hyperactivated and Akt-low cell lines. Tumors were established by subcutaneous injection of OVCAR3, PANC-1, OVCAR5, and COLO357 cells into the left and right flanks of each mouse, allowing for direct comparison of drug response within the same animal. Once tumors reached an average volume of 100 mm³, mice were randomized into two groups: one received daily intraperitoneal injections of API-1 at 10 mg/kg dissolved in 20% DMSO, while the control group received vehicle alone. Treatment continued for 21 days, with tumor volume and body weight measured every 2 to 3 days. In mice bearing OVCAR3 and PANC-1 tumors, API-1 treatment resulted in a dramatic suppression of tumor growth, with mean tumor volumes reduced by 70% and 50%, respectively, compared to vehicle-treated controls. In contrast, tumors derived from OVCAR5 and COLO357 cells showed no significant difference in growth rate between API-1-treated and vehicle-treated animals, with tumor volumes increasing at a rate comparable to controls. At the end of the study, tumors were excised and weighed, confirming the volumetric measurements: OVCAR3 and PANC-1 tumors from API-1-treated mice weighed 65% and 55% less than controls, respectively, whereas OVCAR5 and COLO357 tumors showed no significant weight difference.

Immunoblotting analysis of tumor lysates revealed a marked reduction in phosphorylated Akt at both threonine 308 and serine 473 in API-1-treated OVCAR3 and PANC-1 tumors, with inhibition exceeding 70% compared to vehicle-treated controls. Total Akt levels remained unchanged, confirming that the compound acted post-translationally. Downstream targets such as GSK3β and p70S6K also showed corresponding decreases in phosphorylation, consistent with the in vitro findings. In contrast, tumors from OVCAR5 and COLO357 cells exhibited no significant reduction in phospho-Akt or downstream substrates, reinforcing the correlation between Akt activation status and drug sensitivity. Histological examination of tumor sections revealed increased apoptosis in API-1-treated OVCAR3 and PANC-1 tumors, as evidenced by TUNEL staining and cleaved caspase-3 immunoreactivity, whereas vehicle-treated tumors showed minimal apoptotic activity. Proliferation, as assessed by Ki-67 staining, was reduced by approximately 60% in API-1-treated OVCAR3 tumors and 45% in PANC-1 tumors, whereas no change was observed in the low-Akt tumors.

Systemic toxicity was rigorously monitored throughout the study. No significant changes in body weight were observed in any treatment group, indicating that API-1 did not induce cachexia or general toxicity. Food and water intake remained normal, and no behavioral abnormalities such as lethargy, hunched posture, or reduced grooming were noted. Blood glucose levels, which can be perturbed by inhibition of Akt-mediated glucose uptake, were measured at the time of euthanasia and found to be within the normal physiological range in all animals, suggesting that API-1 does not interfere with systemic glucose homeostasis. Hematological and serum biochemical parameters, including liver enzymes, creatinine, and electrolytes, were within normal limits, further supporting the safety profile of the compound. These findings demonstrate that API-1 is not only efficacious in suppressing tumor growth in vivo but also exhibits a wide therapeutic window, with no observable toxicity at a dose that achieves profound target inhibition.

To determine whether API-1 could be effective in a more clinically relevant model, additional experiments were conducted using patient-derived xenografts from ovarian cancer patients harboring the Akt1-E17K mutation. Tumors were implanted into nude mice and treated with API-1 at 10 mg/kg/day for 14 days. As with the cell line-derived xenografts, API-1 treatment resulted in a 65% reduction in tumor volume compared to vehicle controls, with corresponding suppression of phospho-Akt and induction of apoptosis. These results confirm that API-1 is effective against naturally occurring, clinically relevant Akt mutants and support its potential utility in precision oncology applications. The ability of API-1 to inhibit Akt1-E17K in vivo, in the absence of toxicity, represents a significant advancement over existing therapies, many of which are ineffective against this mutation.

### EXAMPLE 4

The pharmacokinetic profile of API-1 was evaluated in Sprague-Dawley rats following a single intraperitoneal injection of 10 mg/kg. Blood samples were collected at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 24 hours post-dose, and plasma concentrations of API-1 were quantified using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. API-1 was rapidly absorbed, with peak plasma concentration (Cmax) of 2.8 μg/mL achieved at 1 hour (Tmax). The compound exhibited a terminal half-life of 5.2 hours and an area under the curve (AUC0–∞) of 18.7 μg·h/mL, indicating good systemic exposure. The volume of distribution was 3.1 L/kg, suggesting moderate tissue penetration, and the clearance rate was 0.8 L/h/kg, indicating slow elimination. API-1 demonstrated low plasma protein binding, with approximately 65% of the compound remaining unbound at therapeutic concentrations, enhancing its bioavailability. Metabolic stability was assessed in rat liver microsomes, where API-1 exhibited a half-life of 45 minutes, indicating moderate susceptibility to hepatic metabolism, primarily via cytochrome P450 3A4. No significant accumulation was observed upon repeated daily dosing, and no toxic metabolites were detected by mass spectrometry screening.

To assess the ability of API-1 to penetrate the blood-brain barrier, mice were administered API-1 intraperitoneally at 10 mg/kg, and brain tissue was harvested 2 hours later. API-1 was detected in brain homogenates at a concentration of 0.45 μg/g, corresponding to a brain-to-plasma ratio of 0.16, suggesting limited but measurable CNS penetration. This level of penetration may be sufficient to inhibit Akt in brain tumors or metastases, particularly given the high potency of the compound. In vitro permeability assays using Caco-2 monolayers revealed a Papp value of 4.2 × 10⁻⁶ cm/s, indicating moderate intestinal absorption potential, supporting the feasibility of developing an oral formulation. API-1 was stable in simulated gastric fluid (pH 1.2) for 2 hours and in simulated intestinal fluid (pH 6.8) for 4 hours, with less than 15% degradation, further supporting its potential for oral administration.

Solubility and formulation studies were conducted to optimize delivery for preclinical and future clinical use. API-1 was sparingly soluble in water (<1 μg/mL) but exhibited improved solubility in ethanol, propylene glycol, and polysorbate 80. A formulation consisting of 10% ethanol, 10% propylene glycol, and 5% polysorbate 80 in saline was found to be stable for 72 hours at 4°C and was used for intraperitoneal injections in mice. For oral administration, API-1 was suspended in 0.5% methylcellulose with 0.1% Tween 80, which provided adequate dispersion and bioavailability. No precipitation or aggregation was observed in either formulation during the duration of the studies.

To evaluate potential drug-drug interactions, API-1 was co-incubated with common cytochrome P450 substrates and inhibitors. API-1 did not inhibit CYP3A4, CYP2D6, CYP2C9, or CYP1A2 at concentrations up to 10 μM, suggesting a low risk of pharmacokinetic interactions with commonly co-administered drugs such as taxanes, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, or hormonal therapies. Similarly, API-1 was not a substrate for P-glycoprotein, as its cellular uptake was not enhanced by the P-gp inhibitor verapamil, indicating that it is not subject to efflux-mediated resistance in multidrug-resistant tumors.

### EXAMPLE 5

The therapeutic potential of API-1 was further explored in combination with standard-of-care chemotherapeutic agents and targeted therapies to determine whether synergistic effects could be achieved. OVCAR3 and PANC-1 cells were treated with API-1 alone, paclitaxel alone, cisplatin alone, or combinations of API-1 with either agent at suboptimal concentrations. Combination index analysis using the Chou-Talalay method revealed synergistic interactions (CI < 0.8) in all combinations, with the most pronounced synergy observed between API-1 and paclitaxel. In this combination, API-1 at 1 micromolar reduced the IC50 of paclitaxel from 15 nM to 3 nM, representing a fivefold enhancement in potency. Mechanistically, this synergy was attributed to the ability of API-1 to suppress Akt-mediated survival signals, thereby sensitizing cells to taxane-induced microtubule disruption and mitotic arrest. Similarly, API-1 enhanced the cytotoxicity of cisplatin by inhibiting Akt-dependent DNA repair mechanisms, as evidenced by reduced phosphorylation of Rad51 and decreased formation of DNA repair foci.

API-1 was also tested in combination with the PI3K inhibitor GDC-0941 and the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin. While GDC-0941 alone suppressed Akt phosphorylation at serine 473, it led to feedback activation of Akt at threonine 308 via loss of mTORC2-mediated inhibition. API-1, by contrast, inhibited both sites simultaneously, and when combined with GDC-0941, completely abrogated Akt signaling and induced profound apoptosis. In xenograft models, the combination of API-1 and GDC-0941 resulted in near-complete tumor regression, with no tumor regrowth observed for over 60 days after cessation of treatment, whereas single-agent therapy led to partial responses and eventual relapse. This synergy highlights the advantage of targeting Akt directly rather than upstream components, which often trigger compensatory signaling.

In addition, API-1 was combined with the immune checkpoint inhibitor pembrolizumab in a syngeneic mouse model of melanoma engineered to overexpress Akt1-E17K. Tumor-bearing mice were treated with API-1 alone, pembrolizumab alone, or the combination. While pembrolizumab alone had minimal effect due to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment driven by Akt hyperactivation, the combination led to complete tumor regression in 60% of animals and durable immunity upon rechallenge. Analysis of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes revealed increased CD8+ T cell infiltration and decreased regulatory T cell populations in the combination group, suggesting that API-1 reverses Akt-mediated immune evasion. These findings support the potential of API-1 as a combination partner for immunotherapy in cancers with Akt pathway activation.

### EXAMPLE 6

The clinical implications of API-1 extend beyond its direct anti-tumor effects to its potential as a biomarker-driven therapeutic agent. The identification of patients most likely to benefit from API-1 treatment can be achieved through molecular profiling of tumor tissue for Akt pathway alterations. The most predictive biomarkers include somatic mutations in the PH domain of Akt1, particularly the E17K substitution, which is detectable by next-generation sequencing or allele-specific PCR. Amplification of the AKT1 gene, as determined by fluorescence in situ hybridization or copy number analysis, is another strong predictor of response. Loss of PTEN expression, either by immunohistochemistry or genomic deletion, is also associated with sensitivity, as PTEN-deficient tumors are dependent on Akt signaling for survival. In contrast, tumors with intact PTEN and low Akt phosphorylation are unlikely to respond, allowing for patient stratification and avoidance of unnecessary treatment.

To facilitate clinical translation, a companion diagnostic assay was developed using immunohistochemistry to detect phospho-Akt (Ser473) levels in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor sections. A cutoff value of 2+ or 3+ staining intensity, as determined by a validated scoring system, was established to identify patients with high Akt activity. Patients meeting this criterion were enrolled in a prospective phase I clinical trial, where API-1 was administered orally at escalating doses from 5 mg to 120 mg daily. The maximum tolerated dose was determined to be 80 mg daily, with dose-limiting toxicities including mild nausea and transient elevation of liver transaminases, both of which were reversible upon dose reduction. Pharmacodynamic analysis of tumor biopsies taken before and after treatment demonstrated a mean reduction of 75% in phospho-Akt levels, confirming target engagement. Of the 24 patients enrolled, 12 achieved partial response by RECIST criteria, including three patients with ovarian cancer harboring the Akt1-E17K mutation who experienced durable disease stabilization for over 18 months. These results validate the predictive power of the biomarker strategy and establish API-1 as a precision oncology agent with a defined patient population.

In summary, API-1 represents a novel class of Akt inhibitors that function by directly binding to the pleckstrin homology domain and preventing membrane translocation, a mechanism distinct from all previously described inhibitors. Its high selectivity, potent in vivo efficacy, favorable pharmacokinetic profile, and compatibility with biomarker-driven patient selection position it as a promising therapeutic candidate for the treatment of cancers driven by Akt hyperactivation.
