## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to implantable pulse generators used for neuromodulation therapies, and more specifically to systems and methods for reducing the power consumption of electrical stimulation devices by optimizing both the electronic circuitry of the current source and the temporal shape and duration of the stimulus waveform delivered to excitable neural tissue. The invention is particularly directed to implantable medical devices designed for chronic neuromodulation applications such as spinal cord stimulation, deep brain stimulation, peripheral nerve stimulation, and cochlear stimulation, wherein energy efficiency is critical to extending battery life, minimizing the frequency of surgical interventions for battery replacement, and enhancing patient safety and quality of life. The invention integrates novel circuit design principles with biophysical insights into neural membrane dynamics to achieve substantial reductions in energy expenditure during stimulation, thereby enabling the development of next-generation implantable neurostimulators that operate with significantly lower power requirements than conventional devices. The invention further encompasses methods for dynamically adjusting the compliance voltage of the current source in real time based on the instantaneous impedance of the electrode-tissue interface and the required stimulation amplitude, as well as methods for generating non-rectangular stimulus waveforms—particularly centered-triangular waveforms—that are optimized for minimal energy consumption while maintaining effective neural activation. The invention also includes computational models and algorithms for determining the optimal pulse width for a given population of axons based on their diameter, enabling personalized stimulation parameters that further enhance energy efficiency. This invention represents a fundamental advancement in the field of bioelectronic medicine by addressing the longstanding inefficiencies inherent in traditional constant-current stimulators that rely on fixed compliance voltages and rectangular pulse waveforms, and by providing a comprehensive, integrated solution that simultaneously optimizes the electronic, biophysical, and physiological parameters of neural stimulation.

## BACKGROUND

For decades, clinical neuromodulation therapies have relied on implantable pulse generators that deliver electrical stimuli to excitable tissues using constant-current waveforms with fixed compliance voltages and rectangular pulse shapes. These devices, which include deep brain stimulators, spinal cord stimulators, and peripheral nerve stimulators, operate under the assumption that a high, fixed compliance voltage—typically ranging from 10 to 25 volts—is necessary to ensure reliable current delivery across a wide range of tissue impedances and patient-specific physiological conditions. This design philosophy, rooted in early engineering practices from the 1970s and 1980s, prioritized reliability and robustness over energy efficiency, leading to the widespread adoption of high-voltage, fixed-compliance circuits that dissipate substantial amounts of power as heat within the current source transistor, even when the actual voltage required to drive the stimulus through the tissue is far lower. The energy consumed by the stimulator is primarily determined by the product of the current amplitude, the voltage across the current source, and the duration of the pulse, with the majority of energy loss occurring in the active components of the current source rather than in the tissue itself. Conventional stimulators use rectangular waveforms because they are simple to generate with digital circuitry and have been historically assumed to be sufficient for neural activation. However, these waveforms are not optimized for energy efficiency, as they deliver current at a constant amplitude throughout the pulse duration, even though the neural membrane requires only a brief period of suprathreshold depolarization to initiate an action potential. Furthermore, the pulse width used in clinical practice is typically fixed at 60 to 90 microseconds, a value chosen based on empirical observations rather than theoretical or biophysical optimization, and often results in suboptimal energy consumption due to the non-linear relationship between pulse width and the threshold current required for neural activation. This relationship, known as the strength-duration curve, dictates that shorter pulses require exponentially higher currents to activate neurons, while longer pulses require lower currents but consume more energy due to their extended duration. The interplay between these factors creates an energy minimum at a specific pulse width, which varies depending on the diameter and myelination of the targeted axons. Despite decades of clinical use, no commercial neuromodulation device has incorporated real-time adjustment of compliance voltage or non-rectangular stimulus waveforms to exploit this energy minimum, largely due to the perceived complexity of implementing such features in a miniaturized, hermetically sealed, battery-powered implant. Additionally, the electrode-tissue interface has been treated as a passive, linear resistor in most designs, ignoring the capacitive and polarizable characteristics of the interface that contribute to voltage buildup during stimulation and further increase energy dissipation. The use of platinum or platinum-iridium electrodes, while biocompatible and widely adopted, introduces significant interfacial impedance and voltage transients that are not accounted for in traditional stimulator designs. As a result, existing devices operate with energy efficiencies that are fundamentally limited by their static, non-adaptive architectures. The consequences of this inefficiency are profound: patients require frequent surgical interventions to replace depleted batteries, which increases healthcare costs, risk of infection, and patient morbidity. Moreover, the inability to extend battery life limits the feasibility of advanced neuromodulation paradigms such as closed-loop, responsive stimulation systems that require continuous monitoring and frequent pulse delivery. There is therefore a critical and unmet need in the field for a neuromodulation system that reduces power consumption by intelligently adapting both the waveform shape and the compliance voltage to the dynamic biophysical requirements of the stimulated tissue, thereby achieving substantial energy savings without compromising therapeutic efficacy or safety.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel system and method for reducing the energy consumption of implantable neuromodulation devices by integrating three interdependent innovations: (1) a variable compliance voltage circuit that dynamically adjusts the driving voltage of the current source to the minimum level required to maintain constant current delivery, (2) a stimulus waveform generator that produces centered-triangular biphasic pulses instead of conventional rectangular pulses, and (3) a pulse width optimization algorithm that selects the duration of the stimulus pulse based on the diameter of the targeted axonal population to achieve the lowest possible energy threshold for neural activation. The invention operates by first determining the activation threshold of the target neural tissue using a low-power, iterative search protocol, then adjusting the compliance voltage of the current source to the lowest possible level that maintains the integrity of the constant current waveform during stimulation, thereby minimizing the voltage drop across the active transistor and reducing power dissipation in the current source circuit. Simultaneously, the stimulus waveform is generated as a centered-triangular pulse, which rises linearly to a peak current and then symmetrically decays back to zero, resulting in a more efficient temporal distribution of charge that requires less total energy to depolarize the neural membrane than a rectangular pulse of equivalent peak amplitude and duration. The pulse width of the stimulus is selected based on a pre-calibrated model that correlates axon diameter with the energy-optimal pulse width, enabling the system to automatically adapt its stimulation parameters to the specific neural target—whether it be small-diameter central axons in deep brain stimulation or large-diameter peripheral motor axons in spinal cord stimulation. The system further includes real-time monitoring of the electrode-tissue interface impedance and voltage transients, allowing for adaptive compensation of interfacial capacitance and polarization effects, thereby ensuring that the energy savings achieved through waveform and compliance optimization are not negated by parasitic losses at the electrode surface. The invention is implemented within an implantable pulse generator that includes a microcontroller, a programmable current source with feedback-controlled compliance voltage, a waveform generator capable of producing non-rectangular pulses, and a memory module storing calibration data for multiple axon diameter classes. The system is capable of operating in either open-loop or closed-loop modes, and may be integrated with sensing circuitry to detect neural response signals, allowing for adaptive feedback control of stimulation parameters based on real-time physiological feedback. The invention achieves energy savings of up to 90 percent compared to conventional neuromodulation devices when using the optimal combination of variable compliance, centered-triangular waveforms, and axon-diameter-matched pulse widths, and provides a scalable, clinically viable solution that can be retrofitted into existing implantable platforms or incorporated into next-generation devices without requiring changes to electrode design or surgical procedure. The invention eliminates the need for fixed, high-voltage compliance settings, reduces the thermal load on the implant, extends battery life, and enables the development of more sophisticated, energy-intensive neuromodulation therapies that were previously impractical due to power constraints. The invention also provides a method for calculating the energy-optimal stimulation parameters for any given neural target based on anatomical and biophysical data, thereby enabling personalized stimulation protocols that maximize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing energy expenditure.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a system and method for reducing the energy consumption of implantable neuromodulation devices through the synergistic optimization of electronic circuit design, stimulus waveform morphology, and pulse duration parameters based on the biophysical properties of the targeted neural tissue. The invention fundamentally redefines the paradigm of neural stimulation by abandoning the static, one-size-fits-all approach of conventional implantable pulse generators and replacing it with a dynamic, adaptive architecture that responds in real time to the electrical and physiological characteristics of the tissue being stimulated. The core innovation lies in the recognition that the energy required to activate neural tissue is not a fixed quantity determined by arbitrary clinical defaults, but rather a variable that depends on the precise interplay between the voltage supplied by the current source, the shape of the current waveform delivered to the tissue, the duration of the pulse, and the intrinsic membrane properties of the axons being targeted. By simultaneously optimizing all three of these variables—compliance voltage, waveform shape, and pulse width—the invention achieves energy savings that are not merely incremental, but transformative, reducing power consumption by as much as 90 percent compared to conventional systems. The invention is implemented in an implantable pulse generator that includes a microprocessor-controlled current source, a programmable waveform generator, a compliance voltage regulation circuit, a real-time impedance monitoring subsystem, and a memory module containing a database of axon diameter-specific energy-optimal parameters. The system operates by first establishing a baseline activation threshold for the target neural population using a low-power, iterative search protocol that incrementally increases the stimulus amplitude until a measurable physiological response—such as muscle contraction, sensory perception, or neural firing—is detected. Once the threshold is identified, the system initiates a compliance voltage optimization sequence in which the voltage supplied to the current source is gradually reduced in small increments while continuously monitoring the integrity of the output current waveform. If the current waveform begins to distort—indicating that the transistor has exited its saturation region—the compliance voltage is immediately restored to the previous level, which is then stored as the minimum viable compliance voltage for that specific stimulation condition. This process ensures that the voltage across the current source is always maintained at the lowest possible level consistent with the delivery of a stable, constant-current pulse, thereby minimizing the power dissipated in the transistor, which is the primary source of energy loss in conventional stimulators. The waveform generator then produces a centered-triangular biphasic pulse, which consists of a linear rise in current amplitude to a peak value, followed by a symmetric linear decay back to zero, separated by a brief interphase interval to prevent net charge injection and minimize tissue damage. Unlike rectangular waveforms, which deliver the full current amplitude for the entire duration of the pulse, the triangular waveform concentrates the majority of the charge delivery in the initial phase of depolarization, where the neural membrane is most sensitive to current, and then reduces the current amplitude during the latter portion of the pulse, when the membrane is already depolarized and less responsive. This temporal shaping of the current profile results in a more efficient use of charge, as less total charge is required to reach and maintain the threshold for action potential initiation. The energy savings achieved by this waveform are not merely a function of reduced peak current, but rather a consequence of the nonlinear relationship between membrane depolarization and current flow, which favors waveforms that match the time constant of the neural membrane. The pulse width of the stimulus is then selected based on a pre-stored algorithm that correlates the diameter of the targeted axons with the pulse width that minimizes energy consumption. This algorithm is derived from computational models of myelinated axons that simulate the recruitment of axonal populations under varying pulse widths and current amplitudes, and has been validated against experimental data obtained from in vivo animal models. For example, the model predicts that large-diameter axons, such as those found in peripheral motor nerves or spinal cord pathways, exhibit an energy minimum at pulse widths between 150 and 250 microseconds, whereas small-diameter axons, such as those targeted in deep brain stimulation, require pulse widths exceeding 500 microseconds to achieve optimal energy efficiency. The system automatically selects the appropriate pulse width based on the anatomical target, which may be determined preoperatively through imaging or intraoperatively through neural mapping. In cases where the exact axon diameter is unknown, the system may employ a multi-parameter search algorithm that tests a range of pulse widths and selects the one that produces the lowest energy consumption while maintaining the desired physiological response. The system further includes a real-time impedance monitoring circuit that measures the voltage across the electrode-tissue interface during stimulation, allowing for the detection of interfacial capacitance, polarization effects, and changes in tissue conductivity that may alter the optimal stimulation parameters. This information is used to dynamically adjust the waveform shape and compliance voltage to compensate for variations in tissue properties over time, such as those caused by fibrosis, inflammation, or electrode migration. The system also incorporates a feedback loop that monitors the actual current delivered to the tissue and compares it to the programmed current, adjusting the gate voltage of the current source transistor in real time to maintain precise current control even under fluctuating load conditions. This closed-loop regulation ensures that the system remains stable and reliable under all physiological conditions, including those that would cause conventional stimulators to fail. The current source is implemented using a field-effect transistor configured in a constant-current topology, with a feedback network that senses the voltage drop across a precision current-sensing resistor and adjusts the gate voltage to maintain the desired current level. The compliance voltage is supplied by a programmable voltage regulator that can be adjusted in fine increments—on the order of tens of millivolts—based on commands from the microprocessor. The regulator is designed to operate with minimal quiescent current and to respond rapidly to changes in the required compliance level, ensuring that the system does not introduce additional power losses through slow response times or excessive control overhead. The waveform generator is implemented using a digital-to-analog converter and a programmable waveform lookup table that stores pre-calculated voltage profiles for a variety of non-rectangular waveforms, including centered-triangular, Gaussian, and exponential rise-fall shapes. The system may also be configured to generate asymmetric waveforms or biphasic pulses with unequal phase durations, depending on the specific therapeutic goal and the properties of the target tissue. The entire system is powered by a low-voltage, high-efficiency power supply that is optimized for long-term operation with minimal self-discharge, and is housed within a hermetically sealed package that meets all medical device standards for biocompatibility, electromagnetic shielding, and long-term reliability. The invention further includes a method for calibrating the system to individual patients by performing a brief, low-power calibration sequence immediately following implantation, during which the system determines the minimum compliance voltage, the optimal pulse width, and the preferred waveform shape for the specific neural target and electrode configuration. This calibration process is performed automatically and requires no manual intervention, ensuring that the system is always operating at peak efficiency from the moment it is activated. The invention also includes a method for updating the calibration parameters over time, using periodic low-power re-assessments that detect changes in tissue impedance or neural responsiveness, and automatically adjust the stimulation parameters to maintain optimal energy efficiency. The system may be integrated with external programming devices that allow clinicians to override the automatic settings, select predefined stimulation profiles, or initiate manual calibration procedures, but the default operating mode is fully autonomous, requiring no user input once the initial calibration is complete. The invention is applicable to a wide range of neuromodulation therapies, including deep brain stimulation for Parkinson’s disease, spinal cord stimulation for chronic pain, vagus nerve stimulation for epilepsy and depression, sacral nerve stimulation for urinary incontinence, and cochlear stimulation for hearing loss. In each of these applications, the invention provides a means of extending battery life, reducing the size of the implantable device, and enabling the use of more complex, energy-intensive stimulation paradigms such as high-frequency burst stimulation, closed-loop responsive stimulation, and multipolar current steering. The energy savings achieved by the invention are not merely theoretical; they have been experimentally validated in vivo using a rat sciatic nerve model, where the combination of variable compliance, centered-triangular waveforms, and optimized pulse width resulted in energy consumption that was 12 percent lower than the most efficient conventional rectangular waveform and 90 percent lower than a fixed 20-volt compliance setting. The invention further demonstrates that the energy savings are most pronounced at longer pulse widths, which are typically avoided in conventional systems due to concerns about charge injection and tissue damage, but which are rendered safe and effective by the use of biphasic, charge-balanced waveforms and the ability to reduce the compliance voltage to compensate for the increased duration. The invention also provides a method for calculating the maximum safe charge density for a given electrode material and surface area, ensuring that the increased charge delivery associated with longer pulse widths and triangular waveforms remains within established safety limits. The system includes a charge monitoring module that tracks the total charge delivered per pulse and cumulatively over time, and automatically reduces the stimulation amplitude or pulse width if the charge density approaches the safety threshold for the electrode material in use. This feature ensures that the energy efficiency gains of the invention do not come at the expense of patient safety, and that the system remains compliant with all regulatory standards for neural stimulation. The invention further includes a method for predicting the remaining battery life based on the current stimulation parameters, the estimated tissue impedance, and the historical usage patterns of the device, allowing for proactive alerts to be sent to the patient or clinician when battery replacement is imminent. The system may also be configured to enter a low-power standby mode during periods of inactivity, resuming full operation only when a physiological trigger or external command is received, further extending battery life. The invention represents a paradigm shift in the design of implantable neuromodulation devices, moving away from the legacy architecture of fixed compliance and rectangular waveforms toward a new generation of intelligent, adaptive, and energy-efficient systems that are tailored to the biophysical realities of neural tissue. The invention is not merely an improvement to existing technology; it is a foundational rethinking of how electrical stimulation should be delivered to excitable tissue, and it provides a comprehensive, clinically implementable solution that addresses the most significant limitation of current neuromodulation systems: their excessive and unnecessary power consumption. The invention enables the development of smaller, longer-lasting implants, reduces the need for surgical interventions, lowers healthcare costs, and opens the door to new therapeutic applications that were previously constrained by power limitations. The system is fully compatible with existing electrode technologies and surgical techniques, requiring no changes to the implantation procedure or the materials used in the electrodes, making it a practical and scalable solution that can be rapidly adopted by the medical community. The invention has been validated through extensive in vivo experimentation and computational modeling, and its principles are grounded in well-established biophysical theories of neural excitation, membrane dynamics, and electronic circuit design. The invention is therefore not an abstract concept or a theoretical possibility, but a fully realized, clinically viable technology that delivers tangible, measurable, and transformative benefits to patients and clinicians alike.
