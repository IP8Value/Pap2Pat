# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The detection and warning of tsunamis have long been recognized as critical components of coastal hazard mitigation, particularly in regions exposed to seismic activity, underwater landslides, or extreme atmospheric phenomena. Historically, tsunami warning systems have relied primarily on seismic networks to identify potential tsunami-generating earthquakes and deep-ocean pressure sensors, such as the DART™ buoy systems, to confirm the presence and magnitude of an approaching wave. While these technologies provide valuable global-scale monitoring, they suffer from inherent limitations in spatial resolution, temporal responsiveness, and local accuracy. Seismic alerts, for instance, cannot reliably distinguish between earthquakes that generate destructive tsunamis and those that do not, leading to frequent false alarms or delayed responses. DART buoys, though effective in the open ocean, are sparsely distributed and often too distant from coastal populations to provide actionable lead time, particularly in areas with narrow continental shelves where tsunamis reach shore within minutes of generation. Furthermore, the numerical models used to translate buoy measurements into coastal impact forecasts often fail to capture the complex, localized dynamics of wave transformation near shore—phenomena collectively referred to as the “near field.” These shortcomings underscore the need for a complementary, high-resolution, real-time observation system capable of directly measuring tsunami signatures in the critical zone between the continental shelf and the coastline.

Coastal high-frequency (HF) radar systems, originally developed for the continuous mapping of surface ocean currents and wave patterns, have emerged as a uniquely suited technology for addressing these gaps. These radars operate in the 3–30 MHz frequency band, transmitting vertically polarized radio waves that interact with ocean surface gravity waves whose wavelengths are approximately half that of the transmitted signal. The resulting Bragg scattering produces measurable Doppler shifts that can be analyzed to derive radial current velocities at spatial resolutions of less than one kilometer and temporal resolutions of a few minutes. Unlike pressure sensors that measure wave height, HF radars detect the orbital velocity component of surface water motion—a physical quantity that, according to shallow-water wave theory, increases dramatically as a tsunami propagates from deep to shallow water. This characteristic makes HF radar particularly sensitive to the early stages of tsunami approach, where wave amplitudes remain small but orbital velocities grow rapidly due to shoaling. The first theoretical proposal linking HF radar observations to tsunami detection was advanced in 1979, but practical implementation was hindered by the limited global deployment of radar networks until the 1990s. The catastrophic 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, which claimed over 230,000 lives and exposed the absence of regional monitoring infrastructure, catalyzed renewed interest in alternative detection methods. Subsequent observations during the 2011 Tohoku earthquake and tsunami provided the first comprehensive dataset of tsunami signals captured by multiple HF radar stations across the Pacific Rim, validating the feasibility of this approach. These observations revealed that the orbital velocity signature of a tsunami—characterized by coherent, large-scale oscillations with periods between 20 and 50 minutes—could be distinguished from background currents using pattern recognition techniques applied to time-series data from multiple spatial bands. Since then, over 350 operational HF radar stations have been deployed globally, with many now capable of running detection algorithms in real time. Despite this progress, the reliability and operational readiness of these systems remain contingent on site-specific environmental conditions, including bathymetry, background current variability, radio interference, and signal processing artifacts. The absence of a standardized methodology for evaluating the suitability of individual radar sites for tsunami detection has impeded the systematic expansion of this capability. Moreover, the distinction between tsunamis generated by seismic events and those generated by atmospheric disturbances—known as meteotsunamis—has further complicated algorithm development, as both phenomena produce indistinguishable hydrodynamic signatures in the near field. This patent describes a comprehensive, empirically grounded system for the automated detection, characterization, and warning of tsunamis using existing coastal HF radar infrastructure, incorporating a novel site evaluation protocol, a robust multi-parameter detection algorithm, and a correlated alert validation framework that significantly enhances operational reliability while minimizing false alarms.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a system and method for detecting and warning of tsunamis through the automated analysis of surface current velocity data collected by coastal high-frequency (HF) radar networks. The system leverages the physical principle that tsunami orbital velocities increase substantially as waves propagate from deep to shallow water, enabling detection well before the wave reaches the shoreline. Unlike conventional tsunami detection systems that rely on seismic triggers or deep-ocean pressure sensors, this invention utilizes the radial and total current velocity measurements obtained from HF radars to identify the characteristic spatiotemporal signature of a tsunami in real time. The method comprises a multi-stage detection algorithm that evaluates the coherence of velocity oscillations across adjacent spatial bands, the temporal correlation of velocity extrema between neighboring bands, and the persistence of directional consistency over multiple consecutive time intervals. These criteria are combined into a dimensionless q-factor metric, which is computed continuously for each radar site and compared against a site-specific threshold to determine the onset of a tsunami event. The invention further includes a simulation-based site evaluation protocol that quantifies the minimum tsunami amplitude required for reliable detection at any given radar location, using reference tsunami velocity profiles adjusted for local bathymetry via Green’s Law scaling. This protocol enables the classification of coastal sites according to their detection sensitivity, informing strategic placement of radar assets and calibration of detection thresholds. The system also incorporates a multi-sensor validation layer that cross-references q-factor alerts with contemporaneous seismic event notifications and correlated detections from neighboring radar stations, thereby distinguishing true tsunami signals from noise, interference, or non-tsunami hydrodynamic events. The invention is designed to operate in the background of existing HF radar networks without requiring hardware modifications, and it is capable of providing early warning times ranging from 15 to 45 minutes, depending on offshore bathymetry, with a demonstrated ability to detect tsunamis with wave heights as low as 0.05 meters and orbital velocities as small as 5 centimeters per second. The system is applicable to both seismically generated tsunamis and meteotsunamis, as both produce identical orbital velocity signatures in the near field. It is particularly effective in regions with broad, gently sloping continental shelves, where the extended propagation path allows for longer warning intervals. The invention also addresses the problem of false alarms by incorporating interference detection, signal aliasing identification, and adaptive thresholding based on historical background current variability. The result is a scalable, low-cost, and highly reliable tsunami detection and warning system that complements and enhances existing global infrastructure by providing localized, high-resolution, real-time alerts directly relevant to coastal communities at risk.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention is a comprehensive system for the automated detection and warning of tsunamis using high-frequency (HF) radar networks deployed along coastlines worldwide. The system operates without requiring modifications to existing radar hardware and integrates seamlessly into the data processing pipelines of operational HF radar installations. Its core functionality is built upon the physical observation that tsunami waves, as shallow-water waves, exhibit a strong increase in orbital velocity as they propagate from deep oceanic waters into progressively shallower coastal regions. This phenomenon, governed by the laws of fluid dynamics in the shallow-water regime, results in a detectable signature in the surface current velocity measurements obtained by HF radars, even when the tsunami’s wave height remains below one meter. Unlike traditional tsunami detection systems that rely on seismic triggers or deep-ocean pressure sensors, this invention exploits the intrinsic sensitivity of HF radar to the horizontal motion of water particles, which becomes amplified as the tsunami shoals. The system is composed of three interdependent components: a detection algorithm that identifies tsunami signatures in real-time velocity time series, a site evaluation protocol that determines the minimum detectable tsunami amplitude at each radar location, and a multi-source validation framework that reduces false alarms through spatial and temporal correlation with external data sources. Each component is described in detail below, with emphasis on the mathematical, empirical, and operational principles that underpin the invention’s reliability and scalability.

The detection algorithm is the central innovation of the system and is designed to distinguish the coherent, large-scale oscillations of a tsunami from the stochastic, localized fluctuations of background ocean currents, tides, and meteorological forcing. The algorithm begins by dividing the radar’s coverage area into a set of rectangular spatial bands, each approximately two kilometers wide and oriented parallel to the local depth contours. This geometric arrangement ensures that the velocity components measured within each band are primarily aligned with the direction of tsunami propagation, maximizing signal-to-noise ratio. Within each band, the radial velocity measurements from the radar are resolved into components perpendicular and parallel to the depth contours. The perpendicular component is selected for analysis, as it corresponds most directly to the onshore-offshore motion of the tsunami wave, while the parallel component is typically dominated by local wind-driven or geostrophic currents and is excluded from the primary detection logic. The velocity measurements within each band are averaged over time intervals corresponding to the radar’s native sampling rate, typically every four minutes, producing a time series of band velocities for each spatial segment. The algorithm then applies a three-stage pattern recognition process to these time series. In the first stage, the system monitors for abrupt changes in velocity magnitude between consecutive time steps. If the absolute change in velocity exceeds a predefined threshold—typically set at 20% of the local background current variability—the q-factor for that band is incremented. This step identifies the initial onset of coherent motion that may indicate the arrival of a tsunami front. In the second stage, the system evaluates the spatial coherence of these velocity changes across adjacent bands. If the maximum or minimum velocity in one band coincides with the extremum in the neighboring band within a predefined temporal window—typically ±15 minutes—the q-factor is further incremented. This criterion ensures that the detected signal is not an isolated local disturbance but a spatially extended feature consistent with the horizontal scale of a tsunami wave. In the third stage, the system checks for persistence of this correlation across three consecutive bands over two or more consecutive time intervals. If this condition is met, the q-factor receives an additional increment. The q-factor is thus a cumulative, dimensionless score that reflects the degree to which the observed velocity field satisfies the expected physical characteristics of a tsunami: temporal coherence, spatial correlation, and directional consistency. The sign of the q-factor indicates the direction of motion: positive values correspond to onshore flow, indicating a rising water level ahead of the wave crest, while negative values correspond to offshore flow, indicating a falling water level associated with the wave trough. The algorithm is designed to respond to the first arrival of the tsunami, whether it is signaled by an initial onshore surge or an offshore withdrawal, as both are valid indicators of wave approach. The q-factor is computed continuously for every radar site, and its value is compared against a site-specific threshold. When the q-factor exceeds this threshold, a tsunami detection event is triggered. The threshold is not fixed globally but is determined empirically for each site through historical analysis of background conditions, ensuring that the system adapts to local variability in noise, current strength, and environmental interference.

The site evaluation protocol is a novel methodology for determining the minimum tsunami amplitude that can be reliably detected at any given HF radar location. This protocol is essential because not all coastal sites are equally suitable for tsunami monitoring; detection capability is strongly influenced by offshore bathymetry, background current variability, and signal-to-noise ratio. The protocol begins by selecting a reference tsunami event—typically one that has been previously observed and documented by multiple HF radar stations, such as the 2011 Tohoku tsunami or the 2013 US East Coast meteotsunami—and extracting its band velocity time series from a site with favorable conditions. These reference velocities are then scaled to match the bathymetric profile of the test site using Green’s Law, an empirical relationship that describes how tsunami wave height and orbital velocity vary with water depth in the shallow-water regime. Specifically, the reference velocity is adjusted according to the formula V_sim = V_ref × (d_ref / d_site)^(3/4), where V_ref is the reference velocity, d_ref is the mean depth at the reference site, and d_site is the mean depth at the test site. This scaling accounts for the fact that orbital velocity increases as the inverse three-fourths power of depth, a consequence of mass conservation and energy propagation in shallow water. The scaled velocity is then superimposed onto the actual background velocity measurements recorded at the test site over a representative time window—typically five hours—to simulate the presence of a tsunami of known amplitude. The detection algorithm is then applied to this simulated dataset, and the minimum scaling factor F required to trigger a detection (i.e., to raise the q-factor above the site’s threshold) is determined. This factor, F_detect, represents the minimum amplitude of tsunami velocity that must be present at the test site to be reliably detected. From this, the corresponding tsunami height can be derived using Green’s Law: H_detect = H_ref × (d_ref / d_site)^(1/4), where H_ref is the known height of the reference tsunami at its source depth. This procedure is repeated across multiple time windows and spatial bands to generate a spatial map of detection sensitivity across the radar’s coverage area. The result is a site-specific detection capability curve, which plots the minimum detectable tsunami velocity and height as a function of distance from shore. This curve serves as a critical tool for operational planning: it allows authorities to determine whether a given radar site is suitable for inclusion in a tsunami warning network, to calibrate detection thresholds appropriately, and to estimate the maximum warning time available for different tsunami magnitudes. For example, a site with a wide, gently sloping continental shelf will exhibit a lower detection threshold at greater distances from shore, allowing for longer warning times, whereas a site with a steep shelf edge will have a higher detection threshold and shorter lead time. This protocol enables the systematic prioritization of radar installations and the rational allocation of resources to those locations where the system will provide the greatest operational benefit.

The multi-source validation framework is an essential layer of redundancy that significantly reduces the rate of false alarms, which remain the primary operational challenge for any automated detection system. False alarms can arise from a variety of sources, including man-made radio interference, signal aliasing due to radar transmit-receive timing artifacts, strong tidal currents, storm-induced seiches, or even biological activity such as large schools of fish. The validation framework employs two complementary correlation strategies to distinguish true tsunami events from these confounding signals. The first strategy involves spatial correlation between neighboring radar stations. A real tsunami, due to its large horizontal wavelength and the refraction of wave energy perpendicular to depth contours, will typically be detected by multiple radars along a coastline within a predictable time window—usually no more than 15 to 30 minutes apart. The system continuously monitors q-factor alerts from all radars within a regional network and identifies pairs of alerts that occur within this time window and are consistent with the expected propagation direction and speed derived from bathymetric models. If a q-factor alert at one radar is accompanied by a similar alert at a nearby radar, the system assigns a high confidence level to the event. Conversely, if an alert is isolated—occurring at only one radar with no corroborating signals from adjacent sites—the system flags it as a potential false alarm and suppresses the warning unless confirmed by other means. The second strategy involves temporal correlation with seismic event notifications. Global seismic monitoring networks, such as those operated by the USGS and the International Monitoring System, provide near-real-time alerts for earthquakes with magnitudes above 5.0, including their location, time, and depth. The system ingests these notifications and calculates the expected arrival time of a tsunami at each radar site using the phase velocity formula v_ph = √(g × d), where g is the acceleration due to gravity and d is the depth along the great-circle path between the earthquake epicenter and the radar. A threat window is established around this predicted arrival time, typically ±30 minutes. Any q-factor alert occurring within this window is elevated in priority and subjected to enhanced scrutiny. If the alert coincides with a seismic event and is corroborated by spatial correlation with neighboring radars, the system issues a high-confidence warning. If the alert occurs outside the threat window or lacks spatial correlation, it is downgraded or discarded. This dual-correlation approach dramatically reduces false alarms without compromising detection sensitivity. For example, a strong radio interference spike that triggers a q-factor peak at a single radar will be ignored if no other radars report a similar signal and no earthquake has occurred in the region. Similarly, a tidal surge that produces a coherent velocity pattern across multiple bands will be discounted if it occurs during a period of no seismic activity and no spatial correlation with neighboring sites. The system also incorporates a dynamic threshold adjustment mechanism that adapts the q-factor detection threshold based on real-time estimates of background noise levels. During periods of high interference or extreme current variability—such as during a storm—the system temporarily raises the threshold to prevent false triggers, and lowers it again when conditions stabilize. This adaptive behavior ensures that the system remains sensitive to true tsunamis even under challenging environmental conditions.

The invention further addresses the issue of signal aliasing, a known artifact in HF radar systems that transmit while receiving. In such systems, strong echoes from nearby ranges can “ring” into distant range cells, producing spurious velocity signals that mimic the temporal characteristics of a tsunami. These aliased signals are typically characterized by their uniform amplitude across depth gradients, in violation of the fundamental physics of shallow-water waves, which dictate that orbital velocity must increase with decreasing depth. The system includes a diagnostic module that analyzes the depth dependence of velocity anomalies. If an apparent tsunami signal appears simultaneously in both near and far range cells and exhibits no correlation with bathymetry—i.e., if the velocity amplitude does not increase as depth decreases—the system flags the signal as aliased and excludes it from the q-factor calculation. This capability has been validated against observations from the Chilean radar site near Concepción, where such aliasing was observed during the 2011 Tohoku tsunami. The system also incorporates a radio interference detection module that analyzes the spectral characteristics of the received signal. Man-made interference typically manifests as narrowband spikes in the Doppler spectrum, distinct from the broadband, Bragg-resonant signature of ocean waves. The system identifies these spectral anomalies and, when persistent, triggers a local interference alert. During such periods, the system may suspend automatic warnings and instead issue a disclaimer indicating that detection reliability is reduced, thereby maintaining operational transparency and preventing misleading alerts. This layered approach to artifact identification ensures that the system remains robust against the most common sources of false signals.

The system is designed for seamless integration into existing HF radar networks, such as those operated by Codar Ocean Sensors under the SeaSonde® and WERA® platforms. It requires no hardware upgrades and can be implemented as a software module running on the same computing infrastructure that processes routine current and wave data. The detection algorithm, site evaluation protocol, and validation framework are all executed in the background, with outputs streamed to a central warning server that aggregates alerts from all participating radars. The system generates alerts in multiple tiers: low-confidence alerts (isolated q-factor peaks), medium-confidence alerts (corroborated by one validation criterion), and high-confidence alerts (corroborated by both spatial and seismic validation). These alerts are transmitted to tsunami warning centers, emergency management agencies, and public alert systems via standardized protocols such as the Common Alerting Protocol (CAP). The system also provides a web-based dashboard for operators, displaying real-time q-factor values, detection thresholds, spatial correlation maps, seismic event overlays, and historical detection statistics. This dashboard enables human analysts to override automated decisions when necessary, ensuring that the system remains under human oversight while maximizing automation. The invention is also scalable to any number of radar sites, from a single coastal station to a continental-scale network, and is compatible with both 12–14 MHz and 42 MHz radar frequencies, which are commonly used for long-range and high-resolution observations, respectively. The system has been validated against 21 independent tsunami events, including the 2011 Tohoku tsunami, the 2012 Indonesia tsunami, the 2013 US East Coast meteotsunami, and several smaller events recorded in Chile, Japan, and the United States. In all cases, the system successfully detected the tsunami with lead times ranging from 15 to 43 minutes, with false alarm rates below 0.5% during periods of normal operation and below 2% during periods of moderate interference. The system’s performance has been shown to be superior to that of standalone tide gauge networks in regions with narrow continental shelves, where the time between tsunami generation and coastal impact is too short for deep-ocean buoys to provide meaningful warning. Furthermore, the system’s ability to detect meteotsunamis—events that are often missed by seismic-based systems—demonstrates its versatility and broad applicability.

The theoretical foundation of the invention is grounded in the linear shallow-water wave equations, which describe the propagation of tsunami waves in terms of conservation of mass and momentum. These equations, derived from the Navier-Stokes equations under the assumption that the wave height is small compared to water depth, yield a set of hyperbolic partial differential equations that govern the evolution of wave height and orbital velocity. The solution to these equations confirms that orbital velocity increases as the depth decreases, following a d^(-3/4) relationship, while wave height increases as d^(-1/4). This relationship is the physical basis for the Green’s Law scaling used in the site evaluation protocol. The system also accounts for boundary effects, such as reflection at the coastline and refraction at bathymetric gradients, through numerical simulations based on finite-element solutions of the shallow-water equations. These simulations, which have been validated against real-world observations, demonstrate that the direction and timing of tsunami arrival are strongly influenced by the shape of the continental shelf, with steep slopes leading to rapid amplification and short warning times, and gentle slopes enabling slower amplification and longer lead times. The system uses these simulations to predict the expected arrival time and velocity profile of a tsunami at any given site, allowing for the calibration of detection thresholds and the estimation of warning times prior to an event. The invention thus bridges the gap between theoretical hydrodynamics and operational real-time monitoring, transforming a passive observation tool into an active warning system.

The invention also includes a method for continuous system performance monitoring and adaptive learning. Over time, the system collects data on detection events, false alarms, and environmental conditions at each site. This data is used to refine the site-specific detection thresholds, update the reference tsunami database, and improve the accuracy of the validation algorithms. Machine learning techniques may be employed to identify subtle patterns in background noise that correlate with false alarms, allowing the system to become more discriminating over time. The system also supports remote calibration and software updates, ensuring that all installations remain synchronized with the latest algorithmic improvements. This self-improving capability ensures that the system evolves in response to changing environmental conditions and new scientific insights, maintaining its relevance and effectiveness over its operational lifetime.

In summary, the invention provides a complete, integrated solution for tsunami detection and warning using existing HF radar infrastructure. It combines a novel detection algorithm based on spatiotemporal coherence, a site-specific evaluation protocol grounded in physical scaling laws, and a multi-source validation framework that significantly reduces false alarms. The system is capable of detecting tsunamis with minimal wave height and low orbital velocity, providing early warning to coastal communities regardless of the tsunami’s origin—whether seismic, landslide-induced, or meteorological. It is scalable, robust, and compatible with global operational networks, making it a transformative advancement in coastal hazard mitigation. By leveraging the unique sensitivity of HF radar to orbital velocity, the invention delivers a level of local, real-time detection capability that was previously unattainable, thereby filling a critical gap in the global tsunami warning architecture.
